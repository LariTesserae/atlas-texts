The descent ends in warm dust.

It lies only a finger deep on the last steps, fine enough to retain the seams of a bare foot. Beneath it is a dark, smooth mineral that gives back the warmth of a body almost exactly. Sitting down, you have the uneasy sensation that someone has only just stood up.

There is no daylight at the bottom. Even looking straight up the shaft, you see nothing. The stairs have turned too often, and the distance to the surface has become a fact that cannot be checked.

Ahead, the ground continues.

At first this seems to be a chamber crowded with pillars. A hand finds one upright surface, then another; a tossed pebble strikes something nearby and drops without an echo. But the pillars are not pillars. They are the edges of enormous plates, standing close together, some thicker than cities, some thin enough at their extremities to ring when struck. Between them run passages that widen gradually into countries.

The whole place is a separation deep within the crust. Its floor and ceiling pulled apart once, leaving mineral partitions between them like the stretched interior of a broken bone. The separation stopped before those partitions snapped. They still lean and branch across the gap, bearing a weight that has not shifted since.

No one has found an outer wall.

***

You cannot see the vastness directly. You learn it from the air.

A warm current moves through the lower passages, barely strong enough to lift the finest dust from a palm. It has been moving in the same direction longer than any settlement remembers. Above it, beyond reach of thrown stones, another current returns. Where the two meet, dust hangs in permanent banks. A traveler can walk beneath one of these banks for days without coming out from under it.

There are districts of clear air, districts of suspended white powder, narrow regions where black grains fall so slowly that an outstretched hand acquires a dark stippling over the course of a meal. These are the weather. They do not arrive or pass. One travels into them.

The warmth varies little. No hour is cooler than another. In certain deep cuts the ground is hot enough to soften the resin used on tool handles, and these cuts are mapped carefully by people who otherwise have little use for maps.

Water is something kept in closed vessels near the stairways. Farther in, even the memory of dampness becomes peculiar. There are no rounded streambeds, no stains of seepage, no clay. Dust spills down a slope without clumping. Cracks remain open and clean to the limits of touch.

The land has scarcely been worn.

Under the powder, edges are keen. Broken crystals retain tiny hooked points that catch cloth and split skin. A ridge crossed by generations may have a polished track along its crown, but a hand’s breadth to either side the stone is as fresh as a new fracture. Fallen slabs lie exactly beneath the scars from which they detached. Nothing has carried them away or softened their corners.

It is a young landscape that has remained young for an immense time.

***

Light, when someone makes it, is disappointing.

A shallow dish of burning mineral oil shows a patch of floor, two or three upright faces, and much more darkness than before. The rock is mostly charcoal gray, with dull cream seams and occasional surfaces the color of old teeth. Here and there a crystal catches the flame and produces a brief, alarmingly distant point.

Those points are not always crystals.

Animals occupy nearly every accessible surface.

The smallest gather in the angle between dust and stone: pale jointed bodies no longer than a fingernail, each carrying two broad plates that close over its back. They stand motionless with their mouths against the rock. When disturbed, they fold their legs beneath them and become indistinguishable from chips.

Larger creatures lie along the shelves. Their bodies are flat and overlapping, like lengths of folded belt, with a soft foot beneath each segment. You can mistake a dozen of them for a single horizontal seam. They move so slowly that you discover their movement only when a familiar gap has closed.

Across the open basins live animals with long, widely spaced legs and compact bodies suspended between them. They test the floor before taking each step. Their feet spread under pressure, gripping without scraping. A whole herd can pass within a few paces of a resting traveler and make less sound than the traveler’s breathing.

None have eyes that a lamp can find.

They know the country through contact: tremors in a plate, changes in the electrical character of a seam, the minute compression of air between a body and a wall. Some carry whiskers so long that two animals can encounter one another across a passage without either leaving its ledge.

Their abundance does not make the place busy. Most are feeding, and feeding here looks like waiting.

Beneath the dust, certain mineral boundaries sustain a faint electrical tension. The animals settle across these boundaries, placing different parts of their bodies against different stones. They take very little. What they take is restored from depths no shaft has reached.

There are grazed bands polished by soft mouths, and ungrazed bands rough with fine crystals. Predators wait where these bands narrow. One common hunter resembles an empty mineral husk until its underside unfolds around something passing beneath it. The struggle is brief and close. Afterward the hunter closes again.

Nothing cries out.

***

People live where three or four passages intersect and the floor offers a broad, unbroken shelf.

Their settlements are small because sound does not carry reliably around the plates, and a household beyond calling distance might as well be a distant village. Low walls divide sleeping places. Thin slabs laid on stone pegs make roofs, useful mostly for catching the powder that falls from overhead.

A settlement seen by lamplight appears to have been assembled from the debris immediately around it, which is usually true. There are wedges of hard stone, bone scrapers, cords made from the layered skins of the shelf animals. A few families possess copper awls. These are kept wrapped, not from moisture but from the creatures that settle on exposed metal and slowly pit it.

No one needs a hearth for warmth.

The central feature of a house is instead a smooth sounding slab. Visitors touch it with a small hammer before entering. The note travels through the floor to the people inside. Different houses are known by their notes, although to an unfamiliar ear most seem almost silent.

Paths are marked at ankle height. Three cuts in a corner mean a settlement lies ahead. A row of shallow cups means the next stretch has sharp footing. A piece of hide stretched loosely between two pegs warns of a drop: the lower air makes it tremble, and its faint tapping can be heard without spending oil.

Beyond the settlements stand occasional shelters, each scarcely large enough for two people to lie down. They contain a lamp dish, a polished hammer, and sometimes a stack of unused soles. A traveler replaces what they can.

The shelters have no doors. Animals sometimes occupy them. If the animals have settled firmly across a feeding seam, the traveler sleeps outside.

***

Far from the stairways, the ceiling descends.

It does so over such a distance that the change is first noticed in the air current, then in the pattern of dust. Eventually a traveler holding a long pole can touch the underside of the world above. Later, one can reach it with a hand.

Here the upper and lower rocks nearly meet. The passage spreads laterally into a darkness too broad to imagine while lying within it. There are places where a person must exhale to crawl forward, though the same opening continues sideways for hundreds of miles.

Small animals are especially numerous there. Their dry skins brush the wrists. Their feet rest lightly on the back of a passing hand.

People leave offerings at the entrance to these low countries: smooth pebbles, broken tools, empty lamp dishes. Nothing is expected in return. It is simply difficult to approach so much weight, held so close and so steadily apart, without setting something down.

You can remain there a long time.

No crack widens. No dust bank disperses. The returning air continues along the roof, warm and nearly imperceptible. Somewhere behind you, a many-legged animal completes a step it began before you lay down.

With the lamp extinguished, even the little shelter of your own body is no longer visible. There is only the contact of the floor beneath your ribs, the ceiling above your shoulder, and the narrow, inhabited interval extending away on every side.
