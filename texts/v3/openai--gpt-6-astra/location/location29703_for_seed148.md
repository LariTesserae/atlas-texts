The town occupies a hollow beneath a root, though the root has not yet decided which way to grow. At its lowest point the ground lies only a hand’s breadth below the surrounding plain. You could walk across the whole place in a few minutes if the streets stayed where they were.

They usually do, while you are looking.

Underfoot is a pale, firm tissue with the slight give of an unripe pear. It has been pressed into paving slabs. Fine seams divide them, and at each intersection a little knot of darker material holds four corners together. The knots tighten periodically. This is how the streets are leveled: somewhere below, a fiber contracts, a shallow depression rises, and a gutter acquires its first slope.

Nothing here has been worn smooth. The rounded corners are being made round from within. The narrow channels along the streets have never carried enough water to cut anything; they have grown in anticipation of runoff. Beside one drain, a fringe of translucent cells waits for the arrival of its first puddle.

It is cold enough that your hands become clumsy. The air smells of split stems, wet flour, and the sharp green scent left on a blade after cutting nettles.

There is very little light. High overhead, where the root lifts away from the ground, a long opening admits a grayness too weak to cast shadows. It illuminates the lip of a roof, a rail, the upper edge of a tank. Everything else must be understood by touch, by the movement of air, or by the brief interference of something passing in front of that slit.

The space feels largely empty.

Buildings stand a considerable distance apart: a pump house, a row of narrow dwellings, a municipal hall with six tall window openings. Together they would fit around a village green, but the intervals between them have the austerity of abandoned plazas. No thickets obstruct the streets. No leaves crowd the windows. Vegetation has taken the form of the place itself.

The hall’s columns are tightly furled leaves. Its walls are sheets of woody tissue stretched between them, thin enough to tremble when pressure changes. The dwellings have roofs like overlapping scales, each scale still attached to a living vein. A balcony is a stem that has flattened and learned to hold its position. In the pump house, the flywheel grew around its own axle. Its outer rim is dense heartwood, its spokes flexible green cane.

The wheel turns slowly.

Every fourth revolution it pauses, seeming to listen, then completes the next turn too quickly. A belt of braided phloem carries the motion through a slit in the floor. Below it, chambers contract in sequence, sending sap along the water main. You can follow that main down the street: a raised cord, barely thicker than your wrist, warm only at its junctions. Each dwelling has a little stopcock made from a curled thorn.

This is an industrial town with almost no heat to spare. Growth provides the pressure. Ripening supplies the torque. The factories operate on differences between one part of the plant and another—sugar here, salt there, a membrane between—and their exhaust is a faint breath of cool, sweet air.

At the far end of the square, a workshop is producing stair treads. They emerge singly from a fleshy slot, soft at first, then stiffening as fibers align. There are seven stacked on a rack. The eighth has been halfway out for some time.

Beyond the rack, stairs are already rising toward an upper floor that does not exist yet.

For long stretches, the machinery is almost silent. You hear a belt brush its guide. A valve opens with a small wet click. Somewhere inside a wall, an animal scratches three times and stops.

There are animals everywhere.

Their abundance becomes apparent only when you remain still. Tiny translucent creatures travel through the walls, feeding in the narrow space between structural layers. Their moving bodies give a panel the appearance of troubled water. Beetles occupy the bolt holes, each one with its head facing outward, holding a joint shut with hooked forelegs. Along the underside of a gutter hangs a dense row of pale, batlike animals. They sleep separately, with a finger’s width between them, all facing the same empty street.

The larger animals keep to the unlit distances.

Sometimes an entire group crosses a square without making a sound. You see the low interruption of the gray overhead slit, followed by another, and another. They have narrow bodies and feet that unfold before touching down. At the far curb they fold themselves into a doorway that should be too small. Minutes later, little mouths appear along the dwelling’s foundation and begin to graze.

A predator waits in the entrance to the hall. It resembles a loose bundle of roots until one end lifts. Its prey is a quick, many-legged creature moving beneath the pavement. You can track the hunt by the slight ridge traveling across the slabs. The predator walks parallel to it, very slowly, placing each foot on a seam. Then both disappear downward. The paving rises a fraction, settles, and resumes its unfinished pattern.

Moisture comes irregularly.

For several hours there may be nothing wet enough to leave a mark on your sleeve. Then the roof of the hollow begins to bead. Drops collect on root hairs high above the town, swell, and remain hanging beyond the point at which they ought to fall. The pumps change rhythm. Shutters close along the dwellings.

Eventually a single drop descends.

Its impact can be heard from almost anywhere: a clear tap, followed by the faint patter of small animals converging. They drink around its circumference without entering it. Other drops follow, widely spaced. The gutters carry thin, disconnected threads of water toward a cistern in the square.

The cistern is shallow and lined with folded petals. Usually it holds only a dark film. During these brief rains, its reflection deepens until you can see several stories of buildings beneath the surface.

Those buildings have lights in their windows.

Aboveground, the corresponding windows remain black.

If you kneel beside the cistern, you can watch figures working in the reflected rooms. They lean over benches, carry trays, draw something long and flexible through rollers. Their movements coincide with the machinery around you, though not precisely. A worker reaches for a lever; several moments later the pump house wheel accelerates. Sometimes the order reverses.

As the water is absorbed, the lower town rises toward the surface. Floor after floor is compressed into the dark film. The last thing visible is usually the underside of a table.

There are regulations here, but they are embodied in the materials. A door will not open while the wall around it is bearing fruit. A bridge becomes pliable if too much weight approaches, obliging traffic to wait. At a junction, one street swells slightly while another narrows; this determines the right of way. Animals obey these changes without hesitation.

A visitor learns more slowly.

You may set a bag beside a bench and find, on turning back, that the bench and bag are on opposite sides of the square. Neither has moved. The town has inserted a new interval between them. Crossing it feels ordinary, although the walk takes longer than the width of the hollow could possibly allow.

Distance is one of the workshop’s products. The pale strips drying beside the stair treads are lengths of unoccupied space. When ready, they are drawn into the seams between paving slabs. The town is small, but it is making room.

This work accelerates during the darkest part of the cycle. The overhead opening closes. With no light left, the streets extend more freely. Rails slide out of their beds, carrying empty platforms. Windows bud in blank walls. Inside the columns of the municipal hall, tightly coiled chambers begin to loosen.

You can hear the effort if you put your ear against a building: a delicate continuous crackling, like roots entering dry paper.

By the time the gray slit opens again, the new growth has stiffened. There is another doorway beside the pump house. A narrow arcade occupies a corner that was empty. The unfinished staircase now reaches a landing, where something small and warm has already made a nest.

Along the edge of the square, the paving ends in a soft white lip. Beyond it is a strip of bare, resilient tissue. This is where the next street will be.

For now, animals cross it carefully. Their feet leave shallow impressions that close behind them. At one spot, a print remains a little longer than the others. Fibers gather beneath it. A depression forms, with four fine channels leading away.

The town has begun another drain.
