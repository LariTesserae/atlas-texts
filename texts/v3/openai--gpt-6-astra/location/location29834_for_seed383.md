The city lies under a roof so broad and pale that, from its center, you can mistake it for an overcast sky.

Only at the edges does the stone show itself: low shelves descending into the distance, a black seam where the ceiling meets the floor, the blunt ends of pillars worn almost through. Daylight enters through long openings far overhead. Their rims are rounded, without a fresh fracture anywhere, and the light passes down through beds of translucent gypsum before reaching the inhabited level. At noon it is bright enough to thread a bone needle. There are few distinct shadows. A person standing alone in a square seems illuminated from several directions, as if the floor, too, remembers daylight.

The air is cool. It tastes faintly of chalk, and leaves the lips dry even where droplets hang from the ceiling.

There is no river now. The whole basin was once a river’s work, but water has spent so long here that it has nearly erased the evidence of its own passage. Channels have broadened into shallow depressions. Rapids survive as rows of low, polished knobs. What might once have been a gorge is a gentle difference in the height of the floor, discernible chiefly because the buildings on one side look slightly down upon the buildings on the other.

Across this floor the city extends for several miles.

It is built low: courts cut into the rock, waist-high boundary walls, sleeping chambers hollowed under natural shelves. In some districts the houses have no roofs of their own. In others, thin slabs rest on stone uprights, creating a little private darkness beneath the great public brightness. There are no towers. Anything that once stood high has either been taken down for its stone or worn into a shape nobody recognizes as construction.

The streets are disproportionately wide. Between groups of dwellings lie expanses of bare white ground, sometimes large enough that people crossing them diminish to moving marks. A city map would show more absence than masonry, but the absences have names and purposes. One is a crossing place. One is reserved for a migration that has not come through in nine years. Another is a settling field, where the floor must be left unweighted.

Nothing grows tall enough to stir.

Life occupies the surfaces instead: a pearl-colored film beneath damp stones, rust-brown stains in the joints of a cistern, translucent colonies spread so thinly along a wall that they are visible only when someone passes a hand before them. These feed on what the seepage brings out of the deep rock. Where water returns regularly, the mineral skin thickens and becomes soft, almost greasy. The inhabitants scrape some of it into shallow dishes and leave it to dry.

Other things arrive to eat it first.

Small six-legged grazers crowd the damp margins, their shells the color of fingernails. They move in irregular fronts, leaving clean stone behind them. Flat, jointed animals lie under slabs and emerge when the air pressure changes. There are pale swimmers in the cisterns, no longer than a thumb, which can cross a dry court by folding their fins beneath them and pulling themselves along with their mouths.

Larger animals appear singly. One resembles a low, long-bodied dog until it turns and shows a blunt, nearly eyeless head with a comb of sensitive flesh around the muzzle. It searches the cracks for the shelled grazers. Its feet are broad and soft. You may watch it cross half a square without hearing a step.

At first the place seems scarcely inhabited by anything.

Then a damp line darkens the base of a wall, and the wall begins to move.

Thousands of tiny bodies detach themselves from its surface. They stream toward the fresh moisture, so close in color to the stone that their passage is visible as a change in texture. Similar movements are happening throughout the basin: beneath thresholds, across abandoned stairways, along the rims of bowls. There is abundant life here, but much of it keeps the scale and color of grit.

The stillness is equally misleading.

Under the city lies another system of chambers, most of them too narrow to enter, through which water moves under pressure. The city’s floor is a lid over this system. In places it is many body-lengths thick; in others, little more than a crust over wet powder.

The water does not rise at regular intervals. It arrives from rain falling somewhere beyond the roof, perhaps far beyond it, and passes through too many hidden routes to keep a reliable season. A court may remain dry for a generation and fill in an afternoon. A shallow pool may drain between one meal and the next. Sometimes the pressure lifts a whole district by the width of a finger, enough to send water out of every household bowl on the same side.

The first warning is usually animal movement.

Grazers climb the walls. Swimmers leave good water for apparently useless dry ground. The long pale hunters gather on particular ridges, waiting with their muzzles raised.

Then the people begin to move.

There is very little shouting. Mats are rolled, food dishes nested, infants passed from one pair of hands to another. Low sledges slide over the polished streets. Doors are lifted off their pivots and used as carrying boards. The objects of a household are few, but almost every one has been shaped for quick removal: handles cut into stone basins, loops sewn into sleeping hides, pegs that pull free with a twist.

Along the wide streets, several neighborhoods may be in motion at once, crossing and dividing around one another. A person who seems to be walking aimlessly is watching the animals underfoot. Someone stops, lays a cheek against a paving slab, and changes direction. Behind them, the square they have left begins to shine.

Water emerges without a splash. It beads up through the floor, joins itself into threads, then into broad, shallow sheets. The sheets run over almost invisible inclines, gathering the loose white dust into moving borders. Old channels reappear. New ones cut sideways through the powder. Small islands dissolve around their edges.

Sometimes the ground folds.

A patch the size of a room develops wrinkles, then sinks, its walls sliding inward so gently that the change seems impossible. Farther away, wet sediment pushes up into a smooth dome and breaks into lobes. A flight of ancient stairs becomes an embankment. A street ends in a new oval pool.

The noise is a close, dry whisper, with an occasional soft knock transmitted through the soles of the feet. There is little height here from which anything can fall. Loose material lands in silt; running water spreads too thinly to roar. Even during an upheaval, you can hear someone beside you drawing a cord through a buckle.

The oldest parts of the city accommodate this movement rather than resist it. Walls have rounded ends. Foundations stop short of certain pale seams. Bridges are single removable slabs. Reservoirs are numerous and small, so that the loss of one need not empty a district’s water supply.

Tools are flint, bone, and a little soft metal worked and reworked until an awl may contain pieces of several much older awls. Stone is drilled with a bow. Channels are deepened by hand. The great labor is recognizing which work is worth doing. Nobody spends a year repairing a wall that the grazers have begun to climb.

There are abandoned districts, but abandonment is not always final. Once the floor settles, families return to chambers their grandparents left. They scoop silt from the hearth hollows, retrieve grinding stones, scratch new marks beside the old water lines. Elsewhere, a house occupied that morning becomes a shallow depression full of pale swimmers.

Toward evening, the brightness withdraws unevenly. The ceiling’s translucent bands remain luminous after the courts have turned gray. Condensation gathers on their cooler edges. A few drops fall through the immense clear space, widely separated, each striking the ground with a sound small enough to locate.

People set out cups beneath the reliable ones.

Beyond the occupied courts, animals are still crossing the open floor. Their paths bend toward moisture not yet visible. In one deserted square, a dark line appears between two paving stones and lengthens while the rest of the city prepares to sleep.
