The first thing to rise out of the cloud is a green edge, sharp enough to seem unfinished.

It runs across the horizon without descending: a cliff lip thick with plants, its underside black and wet, its upper surface lost in a dimness that is not quite evening. Beneath it, far down through breaks in the cloud, other edges cross at different angles. Some belong to mountains. Others are the margins of whole uplands that have risen past one another without making room.

The low country is invisible. It may no longer be attached.

Here, at a height where the air should be cold, warmth comes through the soles of your feet. The ground is dark, fine-grained, faintly yielding. In places it has split into clean six-sided plates, each plate larger than a lake; in others it is still soft enough to take the impression of a falling seed. Those impressions remain for a few hours before the surface tightens and becomes stone. The new rock has a dull, unweathered sheen. Nothing has yet rounded its corners. Even the dust is angular.

Above, cloud lies in deep horizontal shelves. Very little light passes through. What does arrive is a bruised green-grey, reflected upward from the vegetation until leaves, faces of stone, and the bellies of passing animals all seem submerged. At long intervals a narrow opening appears overhead. A column of amber daylight touches the plateau, and everything within it begins to grow faster.

The opening moves. The growth does not stop at once.

You can trace yesterday’s light through the forest: a band of taller trunks, pale new leaves, swollen fruit, creatures caught halfway into unfamiliar bodies.

***

There is no continuous jungle. There are jungles in seams.

Vegetation crowds the long joints between the stone plates, rising in walls so closely layered that you cannot tell which leaves belong to which stems. Broad, ribbed fronds lean over mats of fine branching growth. Thick vines wear smaller vines like fur. On sheltered faces, a dark velvet of spores fills every irregularity. The tops of these green seams spread across the bare rock, making canopies over dry spaces underneath.

Between them lie expanses of warm mineral pavement. A crossing may take hours. A crossing may end when the green seam on the far side opens and reveals itself to be a gorge.

Water is scarce enough to be collected with precision. The plants turn their coolest surfaces toward the cloud, presenting waxy cups and combs of silver filaments. Condensation beads along them. At a certain weight, each frond folds and pours its catch inward. You hear the occasional click of a leaf joint, then a trickle hidden in the foliage.

Under the roots are little reservoirs, crowded with life. Translucent animals hang head-down in thumb-deep water. Flat black grazers work the submerged bark. Things with too many delicate legs stand across the meniscus, each foot making a separate dent.

A single reservoir may support generations before it spills.

Rain comes less often than its perpetual promise would suggest. The cloud can sit against the trees for weeks without releasing a drop. When rain finally falls, it is warm and large-beaded, striking dust into dark stars. The exposed ground drinks almost nothing. Water races across the fresh stone, finds a crack, and vanishes into the plateau.

Much later, somewhere else, a hillside exhales.

***

There are no roads, no towers, no ruins. Nothing here has been assembled by hands.

Nevertheless, the place works.

A broken escarpment reveals galleries extending into the hot dark beneath the surface. Along their walls, mineral membranes flex in sequence. Thick fluids pass through parallel channels. Nodules turn slowly in sockets polished by their own rotation, grinding the raw interior of the mountain into powders that move upward through hollow roots.

The exposed mechanisms have the wet finish of organs and the repetition of heavy machinery.

Some are immense. Across a valley, a pale piston of stone rises from a shaft every few minutes, lifting several acres of forest. Roots stretch beneath it. Animals move out of the widening gaps with the practiced indifference of creatures avoiding a tide. The stone pauses at its greatest height; vapor seeps around its base; then it settles.

The sound arrives as pressure in your chest. Only afterward comes a low click, almost too small for the event.

Farther inland, natural banks of black ribs stand beneath the canopy. They gather charge from the hot ground and discharge it into the cloud through threads too dim to see directly. Nearby leaves show their veins in blue. Fine hairs lift from the backs of animals. A metallic taste gathers in the mouth.

The plateau manufactures its own upper weather.

Its forests supply insulating fibers, oils, tough jointed tissues. Burrowing creatures open and close passages through which warmth is distributed. Great stationary animals, half sunk into the ground, filter mineral slurries through racks inside their throats. Their waste hardens into the next layer of rock.

There is no overseer. A blockage changes the chemistry of the roots around it; the changed roots attract a feeding swarm; the swarm opens a cavity; the cavity becomes a lung. Production continues, but never produces exactly the same country twice.

***

For a place so densely inhabited, it is remarkably quiet.

You notice the animals first as interruptions of pattern. A strip of bark has feet. A fruit turns to follow you. What seemed to be a cluster of folded leaves opens a row of small, patient eyes.

In the higher branches, long-bodied browsers feed upside down, anchored by blunt gripping limbs. Their backs carry gardens of moss and red seedlings. They move only when the trees move, concealing their own weight inside the motion of the forest.

On the open stone, herds of low, many-jointed animals flow around hollows. They are the color of cooled iron. Their feet touch down softly, each cushioned by a ring of living tissue. When a shadow crosses them, they flatten together into a continuous sheet. A thousand separate creatures become a dark patch that could be mistaken for a stain.

Predators travel beneath that patch.

You see a narrow ridge move through the herd. Bodies lift and settle. One place remains empty afterward, and the rest close around it.

Above the escarpments, large flyers keep to the boundary between warm rising air and cold cloud. They have broad translucent sails, little muscle, and hanging sensory threads that can extend hundreds of feet below them. Sometimes the threads brush the canopy. The trees fold their upper leaves in a slow wave.

No alarm call follows. Information here travels readily through contact: leaf to leaf, root to root, foot to ground. Sound is an expensive way to announce oneself when so much of the landscape is listening through its skin.

There are noises, but they tend to be intimate. A dry rasp under a frond. The tiny splashing of something entering a stored cup of water. Once, from deep inside a trunk, three evenly spaced knocks.

Then quiet again, while an entire ridge changes position.

***

The instability is easiest to miss at close range.

A leaf trembles. Pebbles lean against one another. The air smells of warm resin and the damp interior of stone. Nothing in that small view seems capable of moving very far.

Look up after a few minutes, however, and a mountain that stood to the left of another now stands behind it.

The plateau does not slide across an older, fixed world. Its distances are still forming. Under stress, a stretch of country can acquire more interior than its edges previously enclosed. A narrow ravine widens inward while its mouth remains the same. A forest glimpsed through a gap between two rocks may contain several days of weather.

The animals recognize the beginning of these changes. Herds abandon comfortable feeding grounds without hurry. Burrowers seal one entrance and open another a few feet away. The great flyers gather at particular heights, their hanging threads drawn up close.

Then the ground revises itself.

Usually this happens without a crash. A cliff develops a second face. An uphill slope continues upward into a place that was, a moment ago, beneath it. A green seam divides, and between its two edges a new plain is exposed, steaming gently, already bearing the shallow marks of roots.

There is violence in these changes, though little of it is thrown into the air. Trunks are severed along perfectly smooth planes. Whole colonies disappear into contacts between adjoining slabs. Animals stranded on the wrong side of a new distance keep moving toward water they can smell but can no longer reach.

The fresh spaces fill quickly. Seeds arrive in the vapor. Small gliding things descend and begin to feed on films too thin to see. By the time the first leaves unfurl, something is waiting underneath to eat them.

***

Toward the interior, the green seams thicken until they join. The canopy becomes an uneven, nearly continuous surface, warm enough to send up its own thin fog. Here the ground is difficult to distinguish from the organisms that maintain it.

A fallen trunk can continue pumping for years. A mineral column can soften at its summit and put out leaves. Some of the animals rooted beside the hot vents have outlived the vents and now draw their sustenance from the forest grown over their backs. Their eyes remain clear. They watch the small creatures crossing them.

Beyond this region rises the central mass: an elevated darkness spanning so much of the horizon that it first looks like another layer of sky.

Cloud pours from fissures halfway up its flanks. New ridges push slowly outward below, their black tips wearing collars of torn vegetation. Along its highest visible edge are forests in silhouette, whole crowns rising and falling with the deep internal strokes of the mountain.

You cannot hear them from here.

In the foreground, a cup-leaf tips its water into a root hollow. A small animal emerges from beneath the rim, drinks, and wipes its mouth with two careful forelimbs. The rock under it is warm. A faint current passes through the leaf, making its fine hairs stand apart.

Across the immense dim upland, the same small motions are happening in uncountable numbers: water being caught, channels being opened, heat exchanged, mouths feeding, new surfaces occupied.

Then a pale line appears on the far mountain.

It lengthens from summit to foot. The forest along it bends away on either side. For several minutes nothing else seems to happen.

Warm air begins moving toward it, drawing the low fog between the trunks. Leaves turn. The hanging animals tighten their grips. Somewhere underfoot, a valve closes with a sound like a seed cracking.

The line opens.

There is already green inside.
