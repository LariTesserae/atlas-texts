The hollow is on the underside of a stone near the summit. From the exposed ridge it looks like a flake has broken away: a shallow dark recess, narrow enough to cover with a thumb. Wind passes over its mouth without entering.

Inside, the ground falls away.

Not far. The entire descent could fit between the joints of a finger. Yet the floor has the remoteness of a basin seen from a great height, and the three stones lying on it cast shadows that seem to cross several hours of walking. Scale is one of the things the hollow no longer keeps reliably. A grain near the entrance is plainly a grain; farther in, its broken surface becomes a scree slope. There is no particular point at which the change occurs.

The cold is steadier here than on the mountain. Outside, sunlight occasionally warms an exposed edge. Inside, warmth advances a short distance across the threshold, thins, and stops. A faint curve in the dust marks its greatest historical reach.

Nothing has ever grown along that curve.

The floor is a dull, pale mineral, fine enough in places to look soft. It is not soft. Pressure would find a compacted crust, then thin layers that separate with the brittle precision of old mica. Beneath these lies something dark and continuous. Where the surface has worn through, the darkness has a slight directional sheen, as if the hollow were lined with a single piece of material whose grain runs toward a place outside the stone.

There is almost no loose debris. What little exists gathers in widely separated drifts: a fan beneath a ledge, a crescent around an isolated splinter, seven or eight bright particles at the foot of a low rise. Between them are broad, empty surfaces.

The drifts were made without wind.

Occasionally, an exposed grain changes which direction it considers downward. It rolls a fraction of its width, settles, and may remain there for centuries. Across long intervals this has rounded the shelves, hollowed the bases of projections, and polished certain routes through the basin. The terrain is old enough to have accommodated these changes. Its slopes carry several incompatible gravities with the quiet competence of a mountain carrying different kinds of stone.

One ridge is worn on its underside. Another has a crest that divides into two parallel edges, with a narrow strip between them where no dust can settle. That strip is not a crack. The ground is unbroken. There is simply less agreement there about where its surface is.

Near the back of the hollow stand two small structures.

The nearer one resembles a staple made of gray ceramic, set across a shallow trench. Its uprights have no visible thickness when seen directly from the entrance. Move slightly to one side and they become square columns, pitted by age. Each pit contains a lesser version of the same trench, crossed by another pale span. The repetitions are difficult to follow because they grow dimmer without appearing smaller.

The second structure is farther uphill, though the ground between them slopes continuously down.

It consists of a ring lying almost flat against the mineral crust. One portion has sunk beneath the surface. The exposed arc is smooth and black, with three faded marks along its inner edge. They might once have been instructions. They might be the remnants of something the ring was instructed to prevent.

Neither structure gives off light or heat. There are no joints, cables, screens, or recognizable instruments. Their continued operation is apparent only in the landscape around them. Shadows near the staple remain attached to the objects that cast them. Grains within the ring have only one upper surface. Whatever these devices were made to preserve has been preserved locally, in modest patches, long after the surrounding place ceased to cooperate.

The staple’s trench contains no water. Its bottom is filled with a very fine blue powder that has never darkened with damp. Here and there the powder is arranged in little steps, each step only a few particles high. They descend beneath the span and continue up the opposite wall.

At a certain angle the steps look recently swept.

There is no one here to sweep them, and no movement except the nearly imperceptible settling of matter. No husks, nests, tracks, or dormant bodies interrupt the mineral surfaces. Even decay is absent. Things wear away, but nothing consumes them.

Light enters from the mouth as a weak silver wash. It reaches the nearer stones, the trench, and part of the ring. Beyond that, the walls possess their own dim visibility. Their color is difficult to name: charcoal seen through violet, perhaps, with an occasional powdery green where two planes meet. These colors do not brighten when the entrance brightens.

Some light arrives before its source.

A thin illumination will appear along a ledge while the mountain outside is still dark. Much later, a star rises into the position that explains it. Other ledges retain a little light from stars that no longer rise. The hollow releases these old illuminations slowly. A face of stone can spend an entire winter losing the last suggestion of an edge.

Sound scarcely enters. The great winds over the ridge become a faint pressure at the mouth, without hiss or whistle. Within, there is sometimes a single dry click. A grain has reached the end of a worn channel, or one of the structures has made an adjustment too small to see. The click has no echo. The hollow seems to accept it into its surfaces.

Behind the ring is the highest ground: a rounded shelf scarcely larger than a fingernail, bare except for one dark chip resting near its center. From below, the chip looks angular. From the shelf, it would look worn almost spherical. Its shadow lies a little uphill of where a shadow should be.

The shelf is still rising.

There is no forceful uplift, no buckling crust. Its height increases because the distance beneath it is very slowly becoming greater. The grain of the dark underlying material points in that direction. So do the faded marks on the ring.

Above the shelf, the roof comes down low. A few pale scratches cross it, made when the floor and ceiling were closer together. They have the same rounded edges as the ancient erosion channels below.

For now, there is room between them: a small, cold interval under a summit stone, with two surviving machines, three scattered rocks, and a long slope whose dust is still finding places to rest.
