The height is clearest at the edges, where you can look down onto the weather.

Clouds fill the lower valleys, pressed flat and white between mountain shoulders. Here and there a summit breaks through, dark on one side, silver on the other. None reaches the shelf on which you stand. Beyond them the air grows so clear that distance loses its usual blue softness; a ridge many miles away shows every notch, yet seems to have no more substance than a line scratched into the light.

The shelf itself is broad enough to walk across for most of a day. It tilts almost imperceptibly toward the sun. Pale stone lies exposed in long, low swells, separated by shallow basins of gray grit. There are no young, sharp breaks. Even the cliff edge has been rounded, its lip worn back into a succession of smooth knuckles. Loose stones sit in sockets they have polished for themselves. Some have not moved far enough to escape those sockets in centuries.

Nothing here casts a generous shadow.

The light enters cracks, shines off the undersides of wings, comes back up from the ground. Close your eyes and the pale country remains visible through your lids. Yet the air is cool enough to sting the nostrils. A hand resting on the rock feels warmth; lift it a few inches and the warmth is gone.

And everywhere, continuously, there is the noise.

At first it seems that an enormous waterfall must be hidden just beyond the nearest rise. The sound has that same crushing breadth, that crowded, uninterrupted force. But there is scarcely enough water on the shelf to fill a small pond. What you hear is air passing through the mountain.

The stone is honeycombed beneath its worn surface. Narrow mouths open in the basins and under the cliff lip, leading into chambers enlarged by water long before the valleys sank beneath their present clouds. Wind enters the far face of the massif and escapes here. Every opening carries its own note: a low bellow from a split broad enough to crawl into, a metallic scream from a thumb-wide bore, a heavy, fluttering percussion from a chamber with a thin stone roof. Across the shelf these sounds overlap until they become a single immense pressure.

Speech does not travel. You feel your voice in your teeth, but scarcely hear it. A pebble dropped beside your boot strikes without an audible click.

The wind that produces all this is surprisingly even. It presses clothing against the same side of the body. Along the ground, a few threads of dust extend from the same stones, lifting and settling within inches of their starting places. There are no dramatic gusts, no approaching front. The lower cloud surface rests against the mountain at the level of an old stain.

Moisture reaches the shelf by small, indirect routes. Cool vapor beads beneath overhangs. A seam in the limestone darkens for twenty paces, then disappears. In certain bowls the grit remains damp under a dry upper skin, and turning a stone reveals a black, glistening underside.

The plants live in these concessions.

There are cushions the size of a palm, so tight and hard that they resemble green stone. Their leaves are minute, folded inward, furred with white hairs. A few send roots across the rock in exposed, woody cords before finding a crack. Inside the deepest hollows grows a short, dark sedge, each patch neatly bounded by the reach of moisture. Between patches lie acres of bare mineral.

Lean close and there are flowers, too: dull purple cups no larger than the head of a nail. They open below the surface of the cushion, sheltered among the leaves. Small flies move between them on foot, reluctant to give themselves to the wind.

For a while, these seem to be the only animals.

Then the empty air above a basin rearranges itself into birds.

They have been there all along, holding position against the bright rock beyond them. Hundreds occupy the uplift over the cliff, with an astonishing amount of space between one body and the next. Their pale wings make them difficult to follow. One turns and becomes briefly dark; another vanishes without apparently going anywhere.

The cliff beneath the shelf is densely inhabited. Birds nest well back in the old water passages, beyond the strongest draft. Their entrances appear as occasional black pinholes from above, but farther down whole bands of stone are freckled with them. Feathers gather in recesses the wind cannot quite scour. Chalk-white droppings mark the ledges and feed narrow orange crusts of lichen.

Some birds descend through the cloud to feed. Others take the insects carried up against the mountain face. Long-legged, ash-colored hunters walk the shelf itself, pausing beside the damp cushions with one foot raised. They turn small stones with their bills. Beside a few favored feeding places are flat pebbles worn smooth by repeated use, each with a scatter of broken snail shells around it. No hand has arranged them.

Under almost every suitable rock there is life: translucent beetles, jointed things that close into gray beads, pale larvae grazing the films of algae. Stocky little mammals occupy cracks near the sedge, their coats the color of dry grit. You notice their blunt faces only when the eyes catch light. They keep low, moving through furrows so shallow that from a distance they seem to pass through solid ground.

All this life is absorbed by the size and brightness of the place. Nothing gathers into a herd. Nothing fills the horizon. The nearest bird may be fifty paces away, the next a quarter mile beyond it, and still there are thousands along the escarpment.

The animals rarely call. Courtship is a lifted wing, a white throat shown briefly, a sequence of taps delivered through the rock. Near burrow mouths, the small mammals rest their lower jaws against the stone. Whatever they learn arrives beneath the roar.

After a long time on the shelf, you begin to distinguish its steadiness from stillness. Wings adjust. Moisture gathers and is drunk. A beetle works around the rim of a flower. But the cloud stays at its mark, the air keeps its pressure, and the great chord of the mountain does not resolve.

There is no ruin here, no trail worn by feet, no upright object to offer scale. You can choose a distant rise and walk toward it, yet for an hour the arrangement scarcely alters: the same shallow basins, the same sparse green, the same birds suspended above the drop.

Only when you stop and put a palm on the ground do you feel how much is happening out of sight.

The stone is trembling.
