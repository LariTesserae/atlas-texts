The place fits beneath a shell caught in the underside of the ice.

The shell is no larger than a closed hand. Its owner is gone, but the two halves remain slightly parted, holding between them a pocket of seawater that does not quite share the movements of the sea outside. A lip of clear ice has grown around the opening. Through it comes a diluted blue light, enough to distinguish a pale edge from a dark one, never enough to cast a definite shadow.

Inside, everything is close.

The upper valve hangs over the lower like a low, ribbed sky. Between them rise hundreds of little chambers: white tubes, translucent cups, collars of lime, soft-sided cells that harden along their rims. Some stand alone. Most have grown into their neighbors. Their walls meet at awkward angles, making narrow passages, enclosed courts, shafts through which water rises slowly. In the deepest part of the shell, one cluster has reached the ceiling and spread sideways along it. Here there is no longer a clear distinction between floor, settlement, and roof.

Nothing has been cut or carried into position. Each inhabitant lays down a little of the place around itself.

A ring of animals with retractile crowns occupies the outer districts. Their houses lean toward the opening, each new rim extending a fraction beyond the last. Behind them, where the current has already been strained, live smaller mouths. Behind those are chambers whose occupants feed on what the others shed. The innermost rooms have no direct access to open water at all. They receive it through porous walls, after it has passed through several bodies.

There are no empty streets for long. A translucent animal moves sideways down a cleft, its legs working in pairs too fine to hold the light. It pauses beneath an overhang. Another passes above it, upside down. Between them, a threadworm draws itself through a hole that seemed solid until it entered.

The ground is still arriving.

Across the lower valve lies a thin, milky deposit, softer than chalk. At its edges, crystals appear in scattered groups, then join into low ridges. Mucus catches these grains and binds them. A worm presses a groove through the paste; by the next slow change of light, the groove has stiffened into a channel. Water takes it. More grains settle along its banks. A casual passage becomes a permanent division between neighborhoods.

There are surfaces here that have never yet been touched, though they came into being among a multitude of bodies. A new white ledge extends beneath a cluster of cups. Its underside is smooth and unoccupied. Almost immediately, something clear settles there, flattens, and begins to divide.

The cold gives all this activity a peculiar restraint. There are no sudden clouds of movement, no boiling swarms. Antennae advance by small decisions. A crown opens over the course of several breaths, if there were anything here that breathed that way. Yet look anywhere for long enough and its arrangement changes. A doorway closes. A pale gut fills. A jawed creature emerges from a joint between walls and takes a smaller animal so neatly that the surrounding legs keep moving.

Farther back, the shell curves downward into a basin of heavier water.

Salt has gathered there as the surrounding ice thickens. The basin remains liquid at a cold that would seal the upper passages. Its boundary is visible only when something crosses it: a slight wavering, a displacement of the creature’s outline. The animals that live below this boundary are squat and nearly colorless. They cling with broad feet to unfinished mineral terraces. Their eggs lie in tight patches, each patch wrapped around the base of a dwelling like a second foundation.

Above them, suspended from the roof, hangs the only vegetation: a few short brown filaments and a green smear along a ridge that catches the light. The filaments are repeatedly grazed back to blunt ends. Their oldest portions have become embedded in the growing walls, so that a dark branching line sometimes runs through a translucent partition. New tips still emerge from it. A little food grows directly out of the masonry.

Light changes without the cavity ever becoming bright. Snow shifting somewhere overhead turns the blue to gray. A passing body outside can dim the whole place at once. At such moments, the crowns withdraw in a loose sequence from the entrance inward. The response travels more slowly than the shadow. By the time the deepest inhabitants have hidden, the outer ones may already be opening again.

There is almost no sound.

The currents do not have room to gather force. Soft bodies meet soft deposits. A loose grain slips down a wall and comes to rest against an egg. Even when a partition fails, it folds rather than crashes, and its collapse is known chiefly by the new route the water takes.

That is the chief disturbance here: a change of route.

A growing tube narrows a passage. The water finds another. One court receives fresh suspended food, while another becomes still and faintly sour. Its residents lengthen their necks toward the nearest opening. Some leave their houses, dragging the vulnerable portions of themselves across occupied roofs. Others seal their doors. Their abandoned walls remain, and smaller things move into the thickness of them.

Occasionally the ice presses the valves closer together.

Then the uppermost chambers buckle. Flexible bridges bow into lower lanes. The whole dense interior adjusts by minute withdrawals and extensions, and a thin breath of water escapes through the mouth of the shell. Outside, that water loses itself immediately in the sea. Inside, its departure leaves every feeding crown tilted in the same direction.

Afterward, construction resumes in the reduced space.

A broken cup becomes a sheltered hollow. Eggs appear along its lip. Fresh lime whitens the fracture, not restoring the old shape but making a new edge from which something can grow. Beneath the low blue roof, among the crowded mouths and folded limbs, the last narrow gap is already acquiring walls.
