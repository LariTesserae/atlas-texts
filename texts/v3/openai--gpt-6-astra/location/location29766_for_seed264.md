The plain lies only a little above its own water.

From a distance it looks dry: a broad, dun-colored floor crossed by pale ribs, with low gray shoulders closing the horizon. There are no peaks. Even the largest outcrops have the shape of things worn nearly out of existence—long backs, blunted wedges, shelves with their edges rounded under. Between them lie several kilometers of open ground. Nothing stands high enough to offer much shade.

The dampness becomes apparent underfoot. Dark patches occupy the shallowest depressions, not quite puddles, more like places where the stone has absorbed all the water it can hold. A clear film covers some of the lower slabs. Elsewhere, moisture beads along seams in the rock and joins into narrow, level threads. These threads lead toward a river so shallow and broad that it is first mistaken for another change in the color of the plain.

The river has no visible current. Its water passes through rather than over the landscape, moving between overlapping plates of porous stone. Only at the lip of a shelf can you see it: a transparent thickness folded over an edge, smooth as the side of a glass vessel. It falls less than the height of a hand. Beneath it, a groove has been worn into the lower slab, exactly as wide as the falling sheet.

There are hundreds of these little falls. Together they make a noise far larger than their appearance warrants.

But the heavier sound comes from underground.

It is a continuous, low mechanical roar, with a finer vibration lodged inside it. Near certain cracks, the finer sound resolves into a hard whine. Farther away, it merges with the bass note until the ground seems to be carrying the noise of an immense works: pumps under load, turbines turning behind thick walls, water forced through pipes too large to picture comfortably.

There are no walls. No buildings, tracks, cables, discarded parts, or squared-off foundations interrupt the plain. Its industrial character belongs to the rock.

Beneath the shallow river is a bed of dark, iron-rich stone riddled with chambers. Warm water enters these chambers under pressure. Gas escapes through narrow throats. Some passages amplify the sound; others transmit it into the flat slabs above, which vibrate almost imperceptibly. A hand laid against one of them feels a steady tremor, stronger at the wrist than at the fingertips. Pebbles resting there do not jump. They have settled into small hollows where the vibration holds them snugly.

The whole basin is warm without being hot. The air has the enclosed warmth of a room containing large machinery, though the sky above it is broad and pale blue. Sunlight falls plainly on everything. The wet stone shines; the dry stone does not. There is no mist to soften the distances. Across the river, a low bank of cream-colored sediment is sharply visible, down to the black mouths of cavities along its foot.

The smell is faintly metallic, with a chalky dryness behind it. Close to the water there is another smell, like a warm coin held in the mouth.

Nothing green grows here. The cracks contain no roots. The soft-looking material gathered beneath overhangs is mineral powder, not moss; the dark veneers along the waterline are manganese, not algae. On a few stones there are translucent crusts that resemble peeled skin. They have no recognizable grain or branching pattern. Their margins follow the wetness with such precision that they seem less like separate things than another condition of the rock.

Life, where it can be confidently named, is scarce.

Under one of the wider water shelves, pale animals cling to the underside of the lip. Each is about the size of a thumbnail, flat-backed, with a narrow fringe held open in the falling water. There may be twelve along a stretch of several hundred paces. They do not crawl, close, or withdraw. Their bodies are almost the same color as the limestone, distinguished chiefly by the soft regularity of their fringes. Fine particles arrive in the water and disappear among them.

No birds attend this small source of food. There are no prints in the damp powder. No wingbeat or insect note interrupts the continuous sound.

Beyond the river, the ground rises into a field of scattered stone bosses. They are far enough apart that each seems to occupy its own portion of the plain. Water has hollowed some into bowls and pierced others laterally, leaving short tunnels through their bases. The tunnels are polished inside. Their upper surfaces remain rough, granular, and warm.

Here the underground roar acquires distinct pitches. One hollow gives a deep, open note. Another produces a thin metallic ringing. Standing between them, you can hear both without either changing. The sound has no beat and no approach or retreat. It is possible to walk out of one note and into another, as though crossing from one color of stone to the next.

At first, the place appears to be waiting for weather.

The low relief suggests a floodplain. The empty bowls suggest rain. The mineral stains record what looks like a history of rising and falling water. Yet the longer you remain, the less those expectations fit. The beads beneath the ledges stay the same size. The river keeps its exact edge. The sunlight does not slide across the shallow cavities. Even the smallest shadow remains seated in its notch.

Water is moving, but nothing becomes wetter. Heat comes out of the ground, but the air does not grow warmer. The pale animals feed without visibly enlarging. At the submerged edges of the slabs, mineral leaves the stone and returns to it in equal measure, preserving grooves that ought to deepen.

All the surfaces bear the evidence of an immense age. There is no fresh fracture anywhere, no sharp fragment waiting to be rounded. The landscape seems to have reached the shapes toward which erosion was always tending. Its channels fit their water. Its hollows fit their pressure. Each bank is precisely high enough.

In the broadest reach of the river, low islands divide the clear water into parallel sheets. Their tops are dry and the color of old bone. From there, looking back across the basin, the scattered rocks, the dark seams, and the shining shelves form no obvious design. There is plenty of room between them. Nothing asks to be approached.

The roar fills that room completely.

You can still hear a footstep, but only nearby. A voice goes a short distance and is lost. Beyond it, the warm plain continues its enormous, steady operation, with no smoke above it and nothing accumulating at the end.
