The high country is green even where it breaks.

Along the escarpments, where whole slopes have fallen away, the exposed faces show pale fibers, wet chambers, and bundles of vessels as thick as tree trunks. No stone appears beneath them. A cliff may stand a hundred paces high and still bleed clear sap from its upper edge to its foot. In the heat, these wounds smell of cut stems. Below them, the fallen material keeps growing.

From a distance, the country looks sparsely inhabited by anything tall. There are immense intervals of low, smooth vegetation, interrupted by a few columnar growths and, farther off, the profiles of raised basins. Much of the sky remains visible. You can follow a storm across several valleys before it reaches you, watching its shadow travel over the green surfaces, watching the surfaces change ahead of it.

Up close, there is no empty ground.

What seems to be turf is a tightly woven rind of minute leaves. What seems to be a path is an elongated stem, flattened across its upper side. It gives slightly under pressure, then firms as liquid moves into the tissues beneath. The rounded hummocks are folded growing tips. The dull, apparently barren patches are sealed buds, acres wide, waiting under leathery skins.

Everything has been fitted to something else. Fibers turn around openings instead of crossing them. Veins thicken beneath the rims of pools. Where a slope steepens, ribs rise through its surface at regular intervals, carrying the weight down toward broad, knuckled foundations. Even the most distant ridges have this worked character: buttressed, hollow-backed, joined. The country has no underlying terrain upon which to grow. Growth has made the terrain.

It is high enough for clouds to pass through the valleys, but the air is hot. Sunlight warms the dark green upper surfaces; deep fermentation warms the enclosed layers below. In sheltered hollows the two heats meet. Water stands there almost too warm to touch, beneath an atmosphere that presses wetly against the face. A wind from the escarpment brings relief for a moment, followed by another breath from some hidden opening, heavy with the scent of bruised leaves.

There are no forests in the ordinary sense. A forest would put too much of itself in the air.

Instead, a single upright structure may occupy the center of a broad basin. Its base spreads into a skirt of load-bearing roots; its crown is a shallow dish held above the surrounding country on a hollow green shaft. Rain collects in the dish. So does mist. Long threads hang from its edges, combing moisture out of the passing clouds. At intervals the shaft makes a deep, congested sound, and water is driven down through it into the lower growth.

These sounds carry a long way.

There is the drum of rain on taut leaf surfaces, the slow tearing of an overburdened seam, the low report of a chamber opening somewhere beyond the next ridge. Water runs out of sight with the heavy rush of water inside walls. Sometimes a whole slope gives a prolonged creak. Afterward, small sharp noises travel across it in succession, each fiber yielding its share.

Nothing calls back. No wing crosses the open intervals. No mouth drinks at the pools. A fallen sheet of green tissue may float for days without a footprint or a bite in it, until its own edges curl inward and begin turning the water beneath it into another floor.

The landscape is young. Its newest ridges are narrow enough to shine along their crests. Fresh surfaces have a waxy finish that rain has scarcely dulled, and their angles are abrupt: sudden shelves, steep-sided channels, clean apertures through which warm vapor rises. Water has begun to score them, but erosion is slow compared with repair. A channel deepened overnight may be half closed by noon. In another place, a trickle finds a tender joint and opens a ravine before the rain ends.

Maps would expire quickly here.

During a downpour, the raised basins fill in uneven order. One swells, pulling the surrounding flats toward it. Another tightens its rim and lifts its water higher. Between them the connecting tissue stretches into a saddle, then splits along a prepared line. A bright sheet of water falls through the opening. The flood strikes the valley floor, rolls it into folds, and carries those folds forward until they catch against older ribs.

By evening there may be a new terrace where the current stopped.

It will already have drainage.

The orderliness is most apparent in catastrophe. Breaks tend to follow seams. Collapsing chambers leave arches around their edges. Even violent growth is accommodated by pleats, spare lengths of coiled fiber, joints that can turn without separating. The country is perpetually exceeding its own dimensions, but it has room built into itself for the attempt.

There are failures nevertheless. Far out across one plain stands a pale, tilted wall, alone in an enormous shallow pool. It once supported something too heavy. Its lower surface has buckled; its exposed interior is pinkish and loose. Each afternoon the heat raises blisters along it. They burst with wet pops audible from the pool’s opposite shore. New green tissue is climbing the wall, but in a different arrangement, abandoning several of the old supports.

Nearby, broad openings lead beneath the plain.

Inside, the light is leaf-colored and the air scarcely moves. Columns descend into black water. Overhead, the underside of the surface is divided into shallow vaults, every vault beaded with condensation. Drops fall so regularly in some chambers that they sound like footsteps approaching along a corridor, though nothing ever arrives.

These are not ruins, nor shelters awaiting occupants. The hollow spaces belong to the construction itself. They allow a plateau to rise without filling its entire volume. They hold excess water, give expanding tissue somewhere to go, keep the deeper layers breathing. Occasionally sunlight enters through a slit high in a wall, illuminating the fine roots suspended there. Slowly, visibly, their tips bend toward the warmth.

Above ground, the afternoon becomes restless.

A hot wind advances along the heights. Low leaves turn their pale undersides outward in broad, traveling bands. Water lifts from exposed pools as steam. On the far side of the valley, a ridge that seemed solid all morning begins to unfold. First its crest separates into overlapping lengths. Then those lengths straighten, pulling a translucent membrane between them.

For a little while there is a wall of green light across the horizon.

It trembles under the wind, shading an entire reach of lower country. Water trapped in its folds descends in sudden curtains. Its supports thicken as you watch. Some hold; one bends too far, slowly at first, then with a crack that reaches you several breaths after the movement. The membrane tears. A section settles across the valley, catching on the isolated crowns below.

Rainwater begins gathering in the sag.

By sunset, roots are emerging from its underside.
