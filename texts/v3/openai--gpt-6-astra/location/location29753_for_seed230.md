The town occupies a shelf in the mountain, a little below the height where soil gives way to bare rock. It is narrow enough that, from the upper stair, you can see both its gates: one opening onto the mule path, the other onto a paved landing above the drop. Between them lie a square, three short streets, and houses built in terraces against the slope. Beyond the gates there is no scatter of outlying cottages. The construction ends cleanly. Then there is stone.

The mountain is pale gray, with darker seams that show through the masonry wherever the builders have left the bedrock exposed. In some houses, the rear wall is simply the mountain dressed flat with a chisel. Elsewhere a natural rib has been made into the side of a stair, or the foundation of a bench. It is difficult to say how much of the town was raised and how much was uncovered. The same rounded corners occur on a quarried block, a doorstep, and a boulder beyond the wall.

Nothing stands very tall. The houses have one or two rooms above their storage vaults, low parapets, and roofs of overlapping stone slabs laid on heavy timber. Their doors are narrow. Most face small paved courts rather than each other. There is a great deal of room between one door and the next, but none of that room seems accidental. A triangular space is kept clear so a loaded animal can turn. A recess holds a stone trough. A broad landing has room for six people to sit along its edges without blocking the stair.

The town has the organization of a much larger place, gathered into a few dozen buildings. There is a covered weighing bench beside the entrance, a communal oven, a cistern court, a row of shallow market bays. There are gutters beneath the paving and little square openings through which they can be cleared by hand. Even the empty spaces have been finished carefully. Along the square, a waist-high wall ends in a smooth coping wide enough to rest a basket on.

The air is cool. In the sun, the stone gives a little warmth through the seat of your clothes; in shade, it keeps the temperature of a cellar. Light falls plainly here. There is no canopy to break it up, and no permanent mist. Under a pale sky, the roofs, stairs, and retaining walls are distinct down to their smallest irregularities. Farther away, the opposite mountainside looks almost flat, its gullies drawn in blue. The depth between the two slopes becomes apparent only when you look over the landing wall.

Water is present mostly as a condition of surfaces.

At the foot of the upper retaining wall, several joints stay dark. Moisture beads beneath a projecting stone, but the beads seldom grow large enough to fall. The covered passage beside the cistern smells faintly of wet limestone. A thumb-wide runnel crosses its floor and disappears through a slit under the threshold. On either side of that runnel the paving is dry and dusty.

The cistern itself is enclosed, with a low stone hood over the drawing hole. Its rope has worn a broad, shallow groove into the lip. Beside it stands a smaller basin, no deeper than a hand, in which a little water is left for animals. A mineral line marks the level it usually holds. The basin’s dry outer face is rough; inside, below the line, the stone is smooth and dark.

The gutters are ample for the occasional rain, but there is no suggestion of a stream. No rush, no marshy margin. The town’s dampness collects in sheltered places and leaves the rest alone.

Vegetation follows those same limits. A small fern grows in a shaded seam below the cistern court. Gray-green cushions of a tough, close-leaved plant occupy cracks on the outer wall, each one separated from the next by several feet of bare masonry. There is one shallow bed of earth near the oven, bounded by upright slabs. It contains a few woody herbs, clipped close, with gravel visible between their stems.

No roots are lifting the paving. No vines are taking the houses. The plants have found places where a little soil can remain without being swept away, and they stay within them.

The stone is old enough that its wear has become part of its shape. Stair treads dip slightly in the middle. The corners at shoulder height are polished where loads have brushed them. Beneath the eaves, the original chisel marks remain sharp, while exposed blocks have a fine grain like hard bread. Repairs are small and practical: a wedge driven beneath a loose slab, a timber lintel replaced with another of the same thickness, mortar pressed into a joint with a thumb. New work does not announce itself for long.

There are few inhabitants in view. Someone may be sitting inside a doorway with a wooden bowl across their knees. A folded cloth lies on a low sill. Two carrying baskets stand beneath the market roof, where the light reaches their rims but not their bottoms. These things make the streets feel occupied without filling them. There is no abandoned quarter, no place conspicuously waiting for use.

The tools are simple and durable: iron blades darkened by handling, wooden shovels, stone weights, clay jars patched around their necks with cord. Doors turn on wooden pivots. Shutters are held open by pegs. The largest piece of machinery is the cistern windlass, whose frame fits so closely beneath its shelter that a person must stoop to work it.

Sound does not travel far among the courts. A foot placed on a stair is audible, but there is no long echo. The low roofs and rough walls break it apart. From one room comes the slow scrape of something being ground by hand. Beneath the cistern hood, the rope gives a small creak. Higher up, a bird occupies a hole in the face of the rock. Its presence is easier to find from the chalk-colored mark below the hole than from any movement or call.

The mountain shelters the town from the prevailing wind. At the outward gate, air presses coolly against the face; two steps back, beside the weighing bench, it is still. Dust rests in the joints of the square. A scrap of straw lies beneath a bench without stirring.

Everything appears to have reached its ordinary position. The damp has a margin. The paving has a settled slope. The houses bear on rock that does not yield, and the rock has already lost the pieces that were easy to loosen. Nothing is pristine, yet little seems in the process of becoming something else.

From the upper stair, the whole place can be taken in again: the roof slabs, the pale courts, the dark mouth of the cistern shelter, the small bed of herbs. The town is neither huddled nor spreading. It fits the ledge with room left for walking, for setting down a load, for standing in the unobstructed light.
