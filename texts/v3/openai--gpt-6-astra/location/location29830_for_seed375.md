The whole district fits inside a shallow depression, small enough to walk around before the light changes. From its rim you can see every roof. There are perhaps forty buildings, though the number is difficult to settle: some share a wall, some are dividing, and one at the northern edge has opened along its length, exposing a pale interior that already looks like a street.

Beyond the depression lies a level expanse of dark, fine-grained stone. Nothing approaches across it. There is no road leading in.

The buildings stand well apart. Their arrangement suggests a settlement whose central squares have widened at the expense of everything else. A few narrow passages connect enormous courtyards. Doorways face doorways across empty ground. Low arcades follow the inside of the basin, occasionally rising into a second tier, and near the middle stands a single tall, hollow structure with windows stacked almost to its crown. Its proportions are those of a bell tower, though it has neither bell nor room to hang one.

Everything is made of the same mineral: a dull violet substance, light enough at its edges to resemble the shell of an egg. Its broken surfaces are white. The walls are usually no thicker than a hand.

The place is cold in shade, cool in the open. A diffuse light comes from a broad pale band low in the sky. It casts little shadow, but the undersides of lintels remain dark, and interiors deepen through purple into black. Occasionally the pale band brightens along one end. Then the upper windows catch it in sequence, giving the impression that someone is moving through the empty tower.

There is no water here. No channels cross the basin, no discoloration gathers beneath the roofs. The sharp little hollows in the paving contain only their own shadows. What initially looks like frost on the north-facing walls is a crop of minute mineral blades, each growing separately from the surface. They remain dry when crushed.

Even the dust is scarce. This is a young place. The sills have not rounded; corners retain their cutting edges. You could follow a crack down a wall and find the two sides still matching, every tooth opposite its socket.

The district grows from below.

At the bases of the buildings, the paving thickens into low ridges. These rise very slowly, fold inward, and become hollow. Their outer surfaces harden while their interiors continue to lift. A new wall begins as a seam no higher than a fingernail. Within a few days it may become a partition; within a season, a house.

There are no builders. The ground has an aptitude for rooms.

It does not make them indiscriminately. Openings in one wall align with openings in another. A row of chambers will leave space for a lane that has yet to form. Where two growing structures approach each other, each turns aside just enough to preserve a passage. Steps appear wherever the floor rises, broad, shallow steps suited to feet, although the few living things here do not use them.

From the rim, this order is reassuring. Inside it, you begin to notice small failures of hospitality. A doorway opens into a chamber too narrow to turn around in. A stair climbs halfway up an exterior wall and stops at a smooth surface. One building consists of a ring of entrances surrounding a solid core.

The proportions are close to useful. They remain close.

Only a handful of plants occupy the district, and most are difficult to distinguish from fallen strips of roofing. They lie flat on the ground in the largest courtyards: long, charcoal-colored ribbons, thin and faintly jointed. Their roots spread over the paving rather than entering it, fastening to the mineral with little star-shaped pads.

When the sky brightens, the ribbons slowly lift their edges. The exposed undersides are a soft, bruised red.

They take what they need from the air and from the wall’s slow chemistry. Beneath each root pad, the violet mineral turns transparent. In old feeding places you can look a short distance into the stone and see parallel fibers running through it, all angled toward the nearest growing edge.

There is very little of this vegetation, but it has influence beyond its size. Walls bend away from occupied paving. A strip of ribbon-growth no longer than an arm has held a whole colonnade in a gentle curve. Another has kept a square open near the southern rim, although young ridges press toward it on three sides.

The plants move too slowly to watch with confidence. Leave a ribbon in the shadow of a wall, return after the pale band has shifted, and you may find its leading end in the light. Behind it remain a few abandoned root pads, transparent stars on the dark paving.

The animals are rarer still.

Under a low arch, something the size of a thumbnail may detach itself from the ceiling. It has six fine legs and a body like a folded shaving of black horn. It does not fall. It lets itself down through the air as if walking on a surface turned vertically.

These creatures browse on the transparent patches left by the plants. Their mouthparts work without visible haste. As they feed, the patches cloud over again.

Most of the time there is only one in sight, or none. If two meet, they flatten themselves against the paving and remain there until one becomes indistinguishable from its surroundings. The other then moves on. Whether this is camouflage, submission, or the entire business of predation is hard to tell.

All this activity proceeds in silence.

There is almost no wind in the depression. The high rim catches what little movement travels across the plain. Now and then a narrow current descends and passes through a row of aligned doors. You feel it on your face, cold and faintly abrasive, but it raises nothing and makes no sound.

Growth itself is soundless. A lintel extends from opposite sides of a doorway until its two halves meet. A roof lifts along its center seam. Thin membranes form across windows, turn opaque, then peel inward and hang against the interior walls. Their movement is supple, although the material is stone.

During the dimmest part of the cycle, the district contracts.

The change is small but widespread. Long facades develop fine vertical openings. Courtyard floors separate into polygonal plates, leaving black lines between them. The tall central structure leans toward the pale band in the sky. Its windows become slightly narrower on one side.

This is when new streets are established. A crack that begins under a house may widen until it divides the whole building, carrying the two halves apart on separate plates of ground. The exposed interiors become exterior walls. Alcoves become sheltered doorways; a private-looking chamber opens at both ends and joins the district’s network of passages.

Elsewhere, a lane closes. Its facing walls approach until only a dark slot remains, then fuse around the edges. The enclosed space persists inside the new structure. You can sometimes glimpse these older streets through a fresh fracture: a succession of doorways buried in the thickness of a wall, still facing one another across a finger’s width of darkness.

The basin limits the growth. At its boundary, the violet material encounters the dark stone of the surrounding plain and cannot advance. Buildings crowd close to the rim, turn sideways, or fold back into courtyards. There is room for the district to become more intricate, but not larger.

Near the western edge is a flat patch of bare paving. From there, with the tallest structure behind you, the place almost disappears. Only a low parapet interrupts the horizon. The sky is dim, the plain level, and the air cold against the ground.

Stay long enough and the paving beneath your feet begins to change. A fine raised line develops between two plates. It travels several inches, pauses, then branches. One branch curves away toward the nearest building. The other stops beside your shoe.

By the next brightening, there will be a threshold here.
