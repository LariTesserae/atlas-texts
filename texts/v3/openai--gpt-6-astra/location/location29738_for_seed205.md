From a distance the high country looks almost bare. Long tawny slopes rise into blunt ridges, each ridge pale along its crest and red in the folds. There are no forests standing on them. A few upright shapes break the horizon, too widely separated to give a useful sense of scale. Between them lie expanses of open ground large enough to carry several kinds of weather: a short gray rain, a white dust storm, a patch of undisturbed afternoon.

Only underfoot does the material become obvious.

The ground is wood, although not quite the wood of trunks. It has a close, flexible grain, and its outer layers break into overlapping plates with soft green undersides. A footprint depresses it slightly. In the hottest hours it yields like old leather; at dawn it is harder, and a dropped tool rings against it. Cracks expose cords, dark membranes, little chambers lined with hairs. Cut deeply enough and clear liquid wells up, then thickens to a yellow plug. Most cuts close before nightfall.

The slopes are alive all the way down.

There may be stone underneath. Stone appears in stories and in a few treasured objects, passed between settlements until their edges are polished smooth. No one here has dug far enough to find it. Below the walking surface are successive weaves of root and vessel, dry galleries, buried rind, chambers large enough to contain whole populations of animals that never see daylight. The high country has grown over its own dead substance for an immeasurable time. What looks like a range of hills may be the scar of an ancient rupture, rounded by grazing, wind abrasion, and centuries of slow repair.

It is very hot. The elevation gives long views and a hard, clean edge to distant things, but little relief. By midmorning the exposed rind is too hot for bare feet. Air rises from the broad slopes in wavering sheets. In the troughs, where the surface is darker, the heat can become almost still: something entered rather than felt arriving. Travelers cross these places before sunrise, wearing sandals with thick soles of layered husk.

The upright shapes are vents.

Some resemble ragged stumps. Others are hollow towers, split lengthwise, their walls curled back to reveal a lacquered red interior. Around each is a wide collar of folded tissue. A vent may remain shut for months, accumulating a pale crust, then open over the course of an afternoon with a succession of reports audible far beyond the horizon. Warm air comes out first, smelling of sap and old fruit. After that come insects.

The sound of the country never truly stops.

It is not the continuous, fine noise of a summer meadow. It has depth and separate distances. Immediately beneath the feet there is rasping: millions of little jaws working the undersurface of the rind. Farther down, heavy bodies strike chamber walls. Somewhere beyond the next ridge, a breeding hollow roars with the calls of animals too numerous to distinguish. Wingbeats arrive in vast rough sheets, pass overhead, and leave behind a higher, thinner din.

At times the ground conducts so much noise that a resting hand tingles.

People who live here speak close to one another’s ears. At a distance they use flat paddles painted black on one side and white on the other. Settlements have signal frames on their roofs, and the opening of a distant vent is announced by three white squares held motionless above a doorway. Bells are useless. Even a scream disappears easily into the ordinary afternoon.

Yet the view remains spacious. There are enormous intervals without a standing plant, a building, or an animal large enough to see. Vegetation occupies almost everything but rises very little above itself. Broad leaves lie fitted into the ground like paving slabs, their edges tucked beneath neighboring leaves. Some are tough and gray, armored with wax. Others are the color of dried blood and turn almost black when the sun strikes them directly. Along the ridges, fine photosynthetic bristles grow only a finger high. From afar their movement resembles wind crossing sand.

Much of the fauna lives below this surface, or is small enough to vanish against it.

A seemingly empty slope may hold more moving bodies than there are visible stars. Beetles occupy grooves in the rind, shoulder to shoulder. Flat, translucent grazers slide under the flush leaves and eat their shaded edges. Mites gather in the mouths of pores. White grubs travel through galleries made by larger grubs, with still smaller creatures riding in the folds behind their heads.

Where a plate of rind lifts, life pours out of the shade.

There are larger animals too. Long-legged browsers move in scattered lines, lowering narrow heads to shave the surface with sideways strokes of their teeth. They are pale above and dark underneath. Their feet spread as they take weight, distributing each step across a delicate fan of toes. A herd can pass over a thin roof of living tissue without breaking it.

Their predators wait beneath that roof.

One sees the attack first as a traveling crease. Then the ground opens, briefly, in several places at once. The browsers spring sideways. Dust and torn fibers obscure the movement. A few seconds later the herd is running, the crease has stopped, and something broad-backed is pressing the rind into place from below. Carrion insects descend before the surface has finished closing.

The old migration routes are shallow valleys, eaten into the land by generations of passing herds. Their floors are ribbed and polished. They wind around bitter patches, dangerous vents, and places where the substrate grows too slowly to bear repeated grazing. The channels shift, but very gradually. The country has endured its animals long enough to acquire their shapes.

Water collects in a different geography.

Most rain never forms a stream. It darkens the rind, beads along waxed edges, and vanishes through pores that open as the first drops fall. After a shower there may be twenty minutes of coolness and an intense smell of crushed stems. Then the surface shuts again. A person arriving an hour later might find only dampness under an overturned husk.

But on the shaded sides of the deepest folds, condensation feeds permanent pockets. Here the material changes. The hard tawny surface becomes dark, yielding, intricately pleated. Small pools sit in living cups whose rims contract during the day. Fronds hang over them in crowded layers, each collecting the drips from the one above. There is no open bank. Every possible foothold is a root, a swollen petiole, or a pad of slippery green tissue.

The water is warm and full of mouths.

Animals come to these pockets by different schedules. Before dawn, broad-winged drinkers cling upside down to the folds and lower extensible tongues into the pools. At noon the water belongs to armored things that emerge from the drains. After sunset the larger browsers arrive, guided by the damp smell, and stand nervously in the dark while unseen creatures click against their legs.

Some pools drain without warning. The cup contracts, the surface dips, and everything in it is drawn down through a central opening. Hours later it fills again from the rim. Experienced travelers drink only where the walls are relaxed.

Human habitation is sparse and low.

A house is usually set on a patch of old, slow-growing rind, where the ground moves less from year to year. Its walls are woven from strips peeled elsewhere and dried under tension. The roof stands apart from the walls to let heat escape. Resin seals the lashings. Inside, hammocks keep sleepers above the traffic of small animals, and storage jars hang from polished rods that most climbers cannot grip.

Around each house is a scraped ring, renewed every morning. This is not enough to keep creatures out. It makes their approach visible.

The inhabitants carry shell scrapers, hard wooden wedges, hooked knives with small metal edges worn thin by sharpening. Much of their work consists of encouraging or discouraging growth. They score channels to lead sap toward a damaged roof support. They rub bitter pulp into doorposts. They shade a strip of ground for several weeks until it softens enough to bend into a trough.

Abandoned structures are absorbed unevenly. A wall may remain recognizable for generations while its lower half thickens into the substrate. Doorways narrow. Pegs are pushed out whole. On remote slopes, one sometimes finds a row of rectangular depressions, each shallow enough to collect an afternoon’s dust. The people who made them are gone; the land is still adjusting to the weight of their rooms.

Late in the day, the heat loosens its hold on the upper ridges. Shadows extend from every raised seam. The scattered vents become dark cuts against a bright, ordinary sky.

This is when the winged animals rise.

They emerge from slit mouths and folded collars, at first in separate streams, then in broad columns that bend with the air. Some are smaller than fingernails. Some pass with the slow, heavy rowing of creatures as wide as a roof. Their shadows mingle across the ground until the surface seems to flicker.

The roar climbs through several registers. Loose bowls tremble in houses. Sleeping animals wake below the rind and answer. Across the immense open slopes, leaves begin to tilt upward, exposing their cooler undersides to the departing sun.

For a little while the country has the appearance of rough water: wave after wave of lifted edges, all rooted, all moving slightly differently.

Then the leaves settle into their night positions. Moisture gathers beneath them. The last light remains on distant ridges long after the hollows have gone dark, and the great noise continues through the ground.
