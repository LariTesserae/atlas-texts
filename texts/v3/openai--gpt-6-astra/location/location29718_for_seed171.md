The ground rises while the rain is falling.

It rises too slowly to watch, but by morning the water has changed its course. A pool that lay level beneath the broad leaves now leans toward a seam in the vegetation, pouring into it with the steady force of a river through a narrow gorge. Yesterday’s low place is a ridge. The pale growth along its crest has not yet darkened in the air.

There is no exposed stone. The ridges, the shelves above the water, the steep walls disappearing into cloud are living tissue: root welded to root, woody fiber folded through softer reservoirs, layers of thick green rind. Even the grit caught in the shallows consists of hard seeds and mineral nodules grown inside cells. Underfoot, the surface has the resilience of wet timber. It carries weight without seeming to notice it.

This is already high country. The air is cold enough to make wet skin ache, and the clouds do not pass overhead so much as move through the upper growth, whitening one slope while another darkens under rain. Beyond the nearest ridge are higher ridges, green-black and indistinct. Beyond those, at distances that make their outlines almost horizontal, whole uplands lift their shoulders above the weather.

All of them are growing.

The newest terrain is smooth. Great slopes arrive in the world without gullies, without scree, without the broken feet of cliffs. Their surfaces are tightly pleated, a deep olive velvet seen from afar. Close up, each pleat is a folded leaf several paces thick. Water runs along its edges, searching for a weakness that closes behind it. A cut may widen for an hour, exposing white fibers and amber fluid, then contract as the cells on either side swell together. The rivers have enormous volume and very little history.

They run where the land presently allows.

Rainwater lies everywhere near the surface. It beads on waxy ribs, collects in the mouths of curled fronds, floods the narrow intervals between boles. Some leaves hold lakes. Their rims extend into the mist, and their undersides are supported by a dense mesh of vertical stems descending into lower, darker growth. These elevated waters are the color of tea, bitter with dissolved compounds. On still stretches, a faint metallic film breaks into angular patterns and slowly assembles again.

Stillness is local and brief.

The noise is so complete that individual sounds take time to emerge from it. At first there is only a deep, exhausting roar. Then the ear finds rain striking leaves, water falling through shafts in the canopy, the scrape of enormous surfaces sliding against one another. Hollow trunks carry a lower note. Their internal waters rise and fall under pressure, sounding through chambers larger than valleys. Sometimes several chambers fall into the same rhythm, and the landscape begins to pulse audibly, a bass concussion felt in the jaw and chest.

Above it comes a dry, rapid cracking: seed casings opening across a slope.

Below it, a grinding murmur: fibers rearranging under the weight of new land.

There are no calls in the intervals. No wings beat free of a crown, and nothing parts the groundcover to drink. A pool may stand clear to its green floor, every submerged filament sharply visible, without a single small body moving through it. Yet its surface trembles. Beneath the water, bands of cilia on the roots sweep sediment toward feeding grooves. Other roots release fine white threads that fasten to neighboring growth and begin to draw it inward. The competition is slow enough to resemble structure, but a patch watched for several days changes ownership.

There is scarcely an unoccupied volume anywhere.

What looks like fog between two trunks resolves into suspended root hairs, each wearing a chain of droplets. What looks like a shadow beneath a leaf is a second leaf, black and thin, shaped to catch the light the first has let through. The hollows inside dead stems fill with germinating tissue before the outer wood has softened. Plants grow on plants that grow within plants, with no final surface at which the nesting stops. Even decay remains close-held, its nutrients passed directly into the surrounding mesh.

The light has traveled a long way by the time it reaches the lower slopes. It arrives green, or dull silver through a water-filled sheath, or briefly violet where the edge of a leaf separates it into colors. Broad areas seem lit from within. They are not bright enough to illuminate a face clearly, but their faint glow reveals the grain of the living walls: branching lines, translucent membranes, slow changes in the opacity of fluid.

Those membranes account for more than the glow.

Within them, molecules assemble into arrays finer and more orderly than any crystal exposed to the air. They hold light, delay it, pass its energy sideways through the growth. Signals cross the plateau by routes that do not consistently obey distance. A pressure change in one watershed may be answered almost at once beneath a ridge beyond the horizon, while the tissue between remains undisturbed.

The response is practical. A thousand kilometers of rain approaches. Reservoirs close. Stems thicken along a future storm front. Leaves that have been spread flat for decades begin to fold.

There are no instruments, housings, cables, or seams of manufacture. The intricate systems are inherited in spores and repaired by growth. Their smallest components are alive; their largest include weather. A new shoot contains the means to sample the electrical state of the clouds, borrow energy from distant foliage, and alter the behavior of the water around its roots. In its first season it uses all of this chiefly to find room.

Far below the visible surface, room is being made on a greater scale.

The oldest layers are packed almost solid. Lightless tissue supports the accumulated mass above it, passing water upward through broad, slow channels. Pressure should crush these layers. Instead, particular knots of growth spread the load into regions around themselves, changing where weight takes effect. A fallen leaf near such a knot may descend at an angle, settle against a vertical surface, and stay there under a thin wash of rain.

The effect is slight at any one place. Across a continent, it permits the plateau.

It also permits the new heights. Deep growth swells; loads shift; an entire watershed ascends through the cloud floor. Above, the plants receive more light. They thicken, reproduce, and extend the structure farther. The process has no clean division between organism and terrain. A mountain is a prolonged condition of growth, fed through its flanks and continuously revised from beneath.

Late in the dim day, a region of the upper canopy begins to turn.

The first sign is a change in the rain’s sound. Its sharp pattering becomes a softer, heavier drumming as the leaves expose their pale undersides. Then a broad gray band spreads along the distant slopes. Fronds rotate on swollen joints. Stored water spills from them in sheets. Wind pushed out of the closing canopy travels ahead of the movement, cold and fragrant with sap.

For a while the whole near landscape streams.

Water leaps from shelf to shelf and vanishes through openings that seem too small to take it. The roar climbs until it loses all texture. Mist lifts under the impact and fills the last visible distances. Nearby, a young ridge flexes. A seam opens along its flank, showing fresh tissue the faint yellow of a seed just split.

The seam widens. Rain enters it. For several minutes there is a view down into the land: shining cords under tension, clear sacs crowding one another, sheets of membrane moving with the water. Deep inside, a small succession of lights passes along a branching path.

Then the flank draws together.

By the time the upper leaves settle, the ridge stands a little higher. Water gathers against its new foot. The first floating roots are already extending into the pool.
