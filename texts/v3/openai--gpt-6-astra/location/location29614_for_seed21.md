The hollow is small enough to cross before the sweat dries on your neck. It never dries here, but that is the distance: forty or fifty paces from the split rock at the entrance to the far lip, where water slips over an edge and vanishes into cloud.

Above the cloud are other summits, blue and apparently cool. This one is hot. The sunlight has the plain, hard brightness of midday at altitude, falling through clean air onto stone that would burn a bare palm. Yet every shaded surface is wet. Water beads beneath ledges, swells along the seams of boulders, and runs down the undersides of leaves. A shallow channel crosses the hollow, clear enough that its presence is betrayed chiefly by the way it rearranges the pebbles beneath it. Now and then a bubble rises from a crack. It reaches the surface without breaking and travels downstream as a small, trembling lens.

The ground is still becoming ground.

Much of it is pale, angular rock, its fractures so fresh that their interiors have a faint blue cast. There are no rounded stones. Even the gravel consists of little blades and wedges, loosely bedded in a dark paste of wet mineral dust. Where footsteps have disturbed it, the paste slowly draws back together, though the stones remain displaced. Along the eastern wall, a seam opens by the width of a fingernail every afternoon. Water enters. By morning there is new stone at the bottom, soft at first, with the texture of a tooth just emerging through gum.

This growth accounts for the shape of the place. The hollow has not been scooped out of the mountain. It is a gap among parts of the mountain that have not yet joined.

Vegetation occupies only a few sites, but occupies them extravagantly. Beside the channel stands a thicket no larger than a table, its leaves broad, dark, and overlapped like wet hides. Their red stalks rise from a shared mound of roots. Small cream-colored flowers hang beneath the foliage, opening toward the ground, where the humidity is greatest. Farther up, on a shelf of black mineral crust, grows a single tuft of grass with blades as long as a person’s arm. Each blade is furred along one edge. Condensation gathers in the fur and travels toward the root in bright, orderly pulses.

Between these green islands there is open stone, water, and hot light.

The richest growth fills a crack beside the far lip. Ferns crowd out of it in a thick, soft cascade, stopping abruptly where their roots can no longer reach shelter. Their youngest fronds are copper-colored and tightly folded. Beneath them, in almost complete shade, a translucent film spreads across the rock. It looks like clear slime until a cloud passes over the sun; then thousands of tiny vertical vanes rise from it, all facing the same direction. They stand for perhaps a minute. When the light returns, they collapse so neatly that no trace of their structure remains.

There are only three things here that are unmistakably made.

One is a short stair cut into the entrance rock. Its seven steps lead up from empty air. Whatever path once approached them lies far below, or has yet to arrive.

One is a shelter on three narrow legs, scarcely large enough for two people sitting close. Its roof is a sheet of dull gray material with no visible thickness at the edge. Rain passes through it. Sunlight does not. Beneath it, the heat becomes bearable, and water falling through the roof lands cool on the skin. A shallow depression in the floor holds a little rainwater and a single shed seed husk. There is nowhere to fasten a door.

The third is a ring set flush into the rock near the center of the hollow. It is wide enough to stand inside. Its surface resembles tarnished silver, except that the tarnish moves: slow dark bands passing around the circumference, dividing and rejoining.

At irregular intervals the ring takes the weight from everything directly above it.

Loose grit rises first. Then a fallen leaf lifts, turning once. Water caught within the circle swells into a transparent dome. The effect reaches higher than the eye can follow, but its boundary is exact; a long grass blade leaning across it bends sharply upward at the point of entry. Nothing shines. Nothing announces the change. After a few breaths, weight returns, and the water comes down with a slap that is, for a moment, the loudest sound in the hollow.

The plants nearest the ring have adapted to this. Their roots grip sideways into fissures. Their seeds are flat and thin, able to hang in the rising column long after heavier things have fallen. Some drift out across the basin. Others disappear upward.

The ring is old. The stone around it is new. Mineral growth has covered portions of its rim, and a hair-fine dark line extends from it toward each of the three legs of the shelter. When the ring lifts, those lines become briefly dry. Water draws back from them, leaving the faint smell of heated metal.

Most of the time, the place is quiet enough to hear a droplet detach.

There is no steady insect noise. No bird calls from the cloud. Wind passes overhead, lifting vapor beyond the rim, but barely enters the hollow. The sounds belong to short events: a root fiber snapping inside a widening crack; the click of a flake breaking from hot stone; a sudden patter when one broad leaf sheds all its collected water onto the next.

Late in the afternoon, a small animal sometimes appears on the western wall. It has six feet, a pale belly, and a back covered in overlapping scales the color of green glass. It moves carefully over the wet surface, licking at mineral deposits. Its shadow does not quite keep pace. When the animal stops, the shadow advances another inch, waits, and draws back beneath it.

There may be several of these animals. There is never more than one visible.

Heat accumulates until even the channel seems tired. Its current slows. Clear threads begin to rise from its surface, too thick to be vapor and too delicate to be streams. They reach upward, wavering, and break into droplets that remain suspended at the height of a person’s face. For a while the hollow fills with widely spaced beads of water. Each contains a tiny inverted piece of the place: a fern, a white wall, the shelter’s dark roof.

Then the mountain gives a single low knock.

You feel it through your feet more clearly than you hear it. The suspended drops fall. Water surges through the channel and spills over the lip. Along the eastern seam, a fresh strip of blue-white stone is exposed.

At the entrance, the lowest stair has risen a little farther from the clouds.

By evening, the sun has left the hollow but the rock still radiates heat. The flowers under the broad leaves open wider. Their scent is faintly peppery, most noticeable close to the ground. A few seeds descend from somewhere above and settle onto the wet mineral paste.

Beyond the lip, the cloud opens briefly. Far below lies a dark slope with a pale scar across it, roughly the size and shape of this place. Then vapor closes over the view, and there is only the small warm basin again, its scattered plants, its waiting shelter, and water finding new routes across a floor that has changed since morning.
