The mountains are under water, but only just.

Their highest ridges run a few fathoms beneath the surface, white and sharp enough to be seen from above as long, broken seams. Between them, the blue goes down for kilometers. There are no shores. The water continues beyond the mountain province and around the curvature of the world, carrying the same low, close-packed waves beneath a pale sky.

Sunlight reaches the summits with almost ordinary brightness. You can see individual fractures in the stone, bolts in the scattered ironwork, the shadows of fish passing over a ridge. Only the motion of those shadows gives the light away. It folds and unfolds across surfaces that have never known wind.

This was high country before it was drowned. It is high country still: an immense uplifted tableland, its outer cliffs descending into water too deep to take a color. The tableland alone could hold several continents. Its valleys are broad enough that, from one side, the other disappears into blue water rather than distance haze. Much of the place consists of that blue interval. A ridge, then an hour of nothing. A cable crossing the field of vision, then nothing again.

The water is painfully clear and close to freezing.

There is very little sediment. The mountains are young, raised along faults that left clean offsets in the pale rock. Some faces look newly split with a hammer. Others have the dark, glossy skin of lava chilled under pressure, broken into enormous pillows whose cracks have not yet filled with silt. Fallen blocks lie directly beneath the places they broke from. Even far down the slopes, their edges remain sharp.

Nothing roots in the cracks. No weed softens the outlines. There are no submerged forests, no drowned meadows, none of the wavering green that might make the shallows feel inhabited. Here and there a stain of bacteria gives a stone the color of tarnished brass. In sheltered fissures, a translucent film wrinkles when something passes close enough to disturb it.

Mostly there is rock, water, and light.

And the noise.

It begins before any machinery is visible: a pressure in the jaw, a vibration along the teeth. Then the separate layers become audible. A low continuous thunder. A higher grinding note that seems to come from directly behind the head. Over both, an immense metallic ringing, without beginning or decay.

The whole mountain province is sounding.

At intervals of hundreds of kilometers, industrial stations are anchored across narrow saddles. They are small against the terrain: a few pressure cylinders, a gantry, a cluster of intake bells. Heavy cables vanish from them into the valleys. Their paint has gone chalky, but their housings are intact. Through inspection windows, the red lamps of instruments burn in dry air.

These stations draw power from water passing through the mountain.

The flow itself is almost imperceptible. Across the open plateau, a loose flake of stone dust may take days to drift beyond the rock from which it fell. But deep inside the young faults, the water is forced through narrow passages, and there it moves with enough force to turn buried turbines. The engineers bored into those passages and enlarged some of them. They did not know how far the sound would carry.

Now every hollow in the plateau contributes. Caverns reinforce the turbine frequencies. Faults carry them sideways. The open valleys return them delayed and thickened. A station too far away to see can be louder than one overhead. In certain places, moving a hand’s breadth changes the roar from a crushing bass to a shriek.

The machinery never winds down.

The pressure difference that feeds it is maintained by the deep ocean’s slow circulation, and the cold water holds nearly the same temperature everywhere. There is no daily surge, no seasonal interruption. The stations run at fixed speed. Their governors make adjustments too small to hear.

Around this unchanging noise, the animals have arranged their lives.

At first the water seems empty. Then a silver point moves against the rock, and resolves into a fish. A second appears much farther down. Beyond it, near the limit of vision, a thousand small bodies turn together and briefly catch the sun.

Most living things keep to narrow levels. There are shoals stretched along the ridges like smoke that has forgotten how to disperse. Pale crabs occupy the undersides of ledges. Transparent animals with ribbed bells hang in the sheltered water behind stone spurs. Their organs look like fragments of dark thread caught in clear glass.

Below them, life becomes larger and farther apart.

Long white swimmers travel through the valleys with scarcely a movement of their tails. A single one may be visible for twenty minutes, dwindling into the blue without ever seeming to recede. They feed on curtains of minute animals concentrated where the deep water rises. Those curtains are almost invisible head-on. Seen obliquely, they turn the water faintly milky for kilometers.

There is food enough, but it is spread thinly through an enormous volume.

The smallest organisms feed on compounds leaking from the new rock, particularly around the warm seams far below. Their descendants and their dead drift upward with the circulation. Nothing in the bright summit water grows toward the sun. The light illuminates an ecology supplied from beneath.

Sound has shaped the animals more obviously than cold. Few have organs that would serve as ears. Many carry rows of pits along their flanks, sensitive to nearby motion but closed against the constant vibration. Some shelter in the acoustic shadows behind ridges. Others gather along the standing waves, where suspended food forms faint, fixed bands.

At the loudest crossings, fish hang nose into nothing visible, holding position as precisely as iron filings in a magnetic field.

The stations are the only places with interiors.

Inside, the floors tremble. Tools are wired to their racks. Enamel cups sit in spring clips beside switchboards labeled in a language still easy to read. There are bunks, lockers, maintenance schedules beneath glass. The thick windows make the ocean look like a brightly lit room adjoining this one, though beyond the nearest rock there may be three kilometers of empty water.

The noise is worse in the dry compartments. Every panel supplies its own rattle. Speech disappears before it reaches another person. Messages are written on slates, or tapped into the little green displays beside the doors.

On one station, a repair cradle still hangs outside an access hatch. Its floodlights are on. They cast hard white cones over a slope already lit by the sun. Small fish turn through the beams, exposing their bones.

Nothing suggests an imminent failure. The cables are taut. The pumps draw evenly. There is no dramatic rust, no great rent admitting the sea. Replacement parts wait sealed in their grease. Whatever purpose required this much power may continue somewhere beyond the plateau.

Here, the work is simply continuous.

Even the weather has acquired a steady form. Cold fog lies over the same broad upwellings. A belt of low cloud rests against the distant mountain tops that almost, but never quite, break the surface. The prevailing wind presses the water into long ranks of waves. From below, these travel across the sky like sheets of hammered glass.

A fish changes direction. A bubble slips from a valve and rises, expanding. Light runs once along a fracture.

Such events are easy to notice here.

The landscape itself remains: the bright, unweathered ridges, the immense blue vacancies between them, and the engines buried in the mountain, turning the same cold water through the same dark passages, making a sound so great that after a while it no longer resembles something happening.

It is simply where you are.
