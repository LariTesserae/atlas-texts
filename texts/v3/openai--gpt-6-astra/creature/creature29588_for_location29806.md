The people who live here still say “up home” when they mean the villages around the rim. Some have been saying it for months. They say it while sitting in doorways they built themselves, with their children asleep behind them and all their possessions hanging from the rafters.

Others have stopped. If asked where they come from, they give the name of a street.

There is already disagreement about the names.

The woman who sells cooked grain calls her lane Willow Lane, although the willows are some distance away. The men carrying gravel call it Long Carry. A child sent to fetch someone must know both names, and sometimes the name of the person who first laid a foundation there, even if that person has since left.

Children learn this quickly. They are also the first to discover which passages have become impassable since yesterday.

One girl, Sela, carries meals to the southern gate. Her father works there on alternate nights, sharing the watch with a man whose hearing is poor but whose eyesight remains good. Sela’s father hears a change in the water before he gets up to look. The other man can distinguish the wet edge on a measuring stake in the last light, when everyone else sees a dark stick.

Neither thinks much of the other’s method.

They have made a bench under the shelter from a board too warped for a door. On it they keep a cup, a spare wedge, and a smooth piece of wood they pass between them when the watch changes. The wood means that one man may go. Until it is taken from his hand, he stays.

Sela likes to be there when they raise the gate. She is not allowed near the lever, but she stands where she can see the trapped surface begin to bend toward the opening. Bits of reed that have floated motionless for hours turn slowly and then move away together. Sometimes a fish goes through.

Her father watches the banks of the channel. She watches what escapes.

He used to mend baskets in an upland village. He still does, during a quiet watch, holding the work between his knees. People leave damaged baskets at the shelter and collect them with a little grain or a piece of dried fish tucked inside. His hands are broad and rough along the outer fingers. When he touches Sela’s cheek, he uses the backs of his knuckles.

He has brought his family down because there is work here through more of the year. He does not say that the work is safe.

***

The inhabitants have not come for the same reason.

A pair of brothers have come because the basin clay makes sound bricks if they let it dry slowly enough. A widow has come because her husband’s relatives kept finding uses for the room she occupied. Three young men sleep in the storehouse they are building, earning part of their pay in food and the rest in the promise of a room elsewhere. One will leave before that room is ready. One will marry into a household two lanes away. The third keeps a careful count of what is owed to him, cutting small notches in a stick he hides in the thatch.

There are people who have arrived with enough timber for a roof, and people who arrived carrying a cooking pot in a blanket.

This difference survives the descent into the hollow. A roof beam can be lent at a price. So can a dry corner beneath an existing roof. Those with relatives outside the basin receive bundles of fuel and baskets of earth for planting. Those without them pick the usable sticks out of deliveries of brushwood before the rest goes under a walkway.

Yet the widow, who has almost nothing, possesses a flat stone on which grain grinds particularly well. Several households use it. She knows who has grain before the sellers do, and who is stretching the last of it with bitter leaves.

She is not universally kind. She dislikes the young men in the unfinished storehouse and makes them wait. She is tender with a boy who drops things. When he breaks a bowl, she tells him to bring every piece; she has uses for pieces.

Her house is small enough that she can reach the lintel without leaving her sleeping mat. In the village above, she had more space. Here no one enters without asking.

***

Some of the older people knew the lake.

One man can indicate a former fishing place by pointing into a row of houses. He does this often enough that the people living there have ceased to find it interesting. His younger sister listens when he speaks of the water, but corrects him about distances.

“You never cast that far.”

“The boat was farther out.”

“Then you are pointing at the wrong house.”

They once gathered reeds together along this shore. She now sorts reeds for builders, rejecting stems with almost no visible defect. The bundles she approves cost a little more. She sits beneath a projecting roof with her legs extended and a cloth over her feet. Each stem turns once between her fingers before she puts it down.

Her brother spends time at the pool. He catches an occasional fish with a small basket trap and insists there are larger ones beneath the mud-dark water. He is probably right.

He has moved into his daughter’s house rather than remain alone above. He complains about the damp, the closeness, the smell of everyone else’s cooking. When his daughter suggests he might sleep at his old house for a few nights, he says there is too much to do here.

In the morning he takes her youngest child to the willows. He lifts the child over the exposed roots rather than let him tread on them.

There are things he misses that he cannot show the child now: the sound of a boat against a landing stake, the way the shore seemed to move when wind passed through all the reeds. Instead he shows him snail eggs, a patch of pale beads under a leaf.

***

The children who live in the basin are cleanest on their upper halves.

Clay dries behind their knees and between their toes. Their mothers wash them, and they step out of the doorway, and the work must eventually be done again. They learn to judge a surface before putting weight on it. The youngest use a stick. Older children stamp once, decisively, and sometimes sink to the ankle.

They play at building houses, although this is forbidden near the actual foundations. Their little walls are badly placed and often block something. They also play at the gate, appointing a watcher who has to sit still while everyone else goes away. No one wants that part for long.

Sela keeps fragments of colored cord. She knots them into a length that is of no use for measuring and too short for tying a load. She wears it around her wrist until her father objects that a loose loop may catch on something. Then she hangs it inside her sleeping alcove.

Her closest friend is a boy whose family may not stay. Everyone discusses this in front of him, as though staying were a matter of timber and grain alone. He has already hidden possessions in three places around the town: a smooth fish bone, half a spindle whorl, and a small clay animal with one leg missing.

If they leave, he will have to retrieve them without anyone noticing how much it matters.

***

Not all the creatures here have arrived with the builders.

Worms rise in cut banks after rain. Small burrowing things leave holes that close as the exposed clay dries. Larvae live in the water caught beside a foundation whose owner says, every morning, that he will empty it before evening.

The wagtail has learned the distance at which each sort of person becomes troublesome. A person carrying a loaded basket deserves little attention. A child with empty hands deserves more.

The dog from above begins by visiting the food stalls. Later it finds the widow’s doorway. She throws water at it twice, then allows it to sleep outside because it keeps rats away from the grain brought to her stone. After a week she puts down a broken bowl for its water.

She says it is not her dog.

The rats are recent arrivals. They come with stored food and bundles brought down from older settlements, and find that the new walls already offer gaps enough. They travel beneath the reed foundations where a person cannot follow. At night a sleeper hears a faint working sound close to the floor and cannot tell whether it is an animal or something in the house settling.

Cats have not yet established themselves. Someone says they will bring one. Someone else says a cat would leave.

***

The people are becoming attentive in ways they did not expect.

A brickmaker notices which neighbor’s washing remains damp longest. A woman kneading dough feels a faint vibration through the bench when a loaded sledge passes outside; three weeks ago she did not. The man whose door catches has begun laying a round seed on the threshold each morning to see whether it rolls.

They exchange these observations at meals and while waiting to cross a narrow bridge. Much of the advice is contradictory. Open a trench. Close the trench before it draws more water. Add weight. Take weight off. Wait.

Waiting is the hardest instruction to charge for, and often the best.

One of the brothers who make bricks is patient with clay and impatient with people. The other can persuade a customer to wait but turns the bricks too soon. They quarrel at the drying ground, then eat from the same bowl without speaking. Both have begun to stoop, although neither is old.

They are proud of a wall on the eastern lane. It has dried evenly, and the doorway remains square. Sometimes they take a longer route home to pass it.

There are evenings when people sit outside simply because the day’s work is finished. Someone sings. Two women argue over whether a borrowed jar was already cracked. A young man washes his arms carefully before going to a house where he claims to be helping with a lintel. The cooking woman saves the browned grain from the bottom of her pot for herself.

Above the roofs, the fields are still bright.

At the southern shelter, Sela finds her father asleep sitting up, his unfinished basket resting against his chest. The other watcher has the smooth piece of wood, so she knows she may wake him and take him home.

She touches his knee. He opens his eyes, looks first toward the gate, then at her.

On the way back he carries the empty meal bowl. She walks ahead, choosing the crossings. At one corner she stops him because a log that was steady that morning has begun to rock.

He tests it with his foot. Together they move it a little farther onto its support, and then they go on.
