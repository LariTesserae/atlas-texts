The first part of it you are likely to see is a hand beneath a leaf.

Not quite a hand. There are four short divisions, with a translucent web running almost to their tips, and no nails. It holds the edge of the leaf without bending it. For a long time this is all that shows: a small, deliberate grip, pale against the silver underside.

Then the leaf turns in a breeze, and the grip goes with it.

The rest of the animal is lying along the branch behind the leaf. It is larger than the branch ought to conceal. Its body has settled around the wood, hanging a little on either side, and the wet moss comes up against its flanks without a clear boundary. What looked like another bed of moss is partly its back. What looked like exposed bark is the narrow fold beneath its throat.

It does not look toward you. There is not yet an obvious place from which it could look.

A drop gathers at the leaf-tip. The little hand shifts closer. Just before the drop falls, a crease opens between two of the fingers and takes it in.

It feeds this way for much of the afternoon.

There is a head, though it becomes a head chiefly when the animal uses it. At rest it is tucked into the forward margin of the body, a blunt wedge with the same pale, finely grained surface as the shoulders. Extended, it shows a soft throat and two eyes set far apart beneath overhanging folds. The eyes are dark brown, almost black, and surprisingly small. Their wetness gives them away before their shape does.

The mouth is underneath. It can press its whole mouth against a surface, but usually only a narrow portion opens, following a seam in bark or the lip of a water-filled pit. Inside are many short, flexible ridges. It works these over the stone with patient, almost imperceptible movements, taking the fine growth that slicks the hollows.

The seeds remain.

Sometimes it moves one out of a pit, cleans beneath it, and puts it down a little farther along the slope. This is done with the same four-parted tips that hold the leaves. They are mouths as well as hands, or hands with some of the mouth’s abilities. A bruised frond stem can occupy it for an hour. It holds the broken end, tastes what seeps out, loosens its grip, and waits for more.

Fully unfolded, it would cover most of the empty oval between the roots. But it seldom gives so complete an account of itself. There are six limbs, unequal in length, tucked into grooves along the underside. On a branch it uses the front four. On open stone it spreads all six and carries the middle of its body just clear of the wet surface.

Its underside is dark.

You see this when it crosses a ridge, lifting itself higher than usual. There is a whole sheltered wetness beneath it: overlapping folds the color of soaked fern stems, flushed here and there with a dull red. The folds open as it rises and close when it settles. For several moments afterward, the place where it rested smells more strongly of the root beds.

Across the back, by contrast, its skin is cream-colored and firm, interrupted by green in the shallow creases. A few minute plants have established themselves near the rear edge, where it cannot easily reach. They lean in different directions after it has climbed the tree.

It can move quickly. Once, when a flake of stone breaks under a rear foot, the whole animal gathers itself and is beneath a descending branch before the fragment stops sliding. The movement is so compact that afterward its usual slowness seems chosen, though perhaps quickness is simply expensive.

Most journeys are made a limb at a time.

It takes the dry ridges, but not to remain dry. Water clings freely to it. Rather, it seems reluctant to trail its underside across exposed stone. At each stretch between rooted islands, it pauses with two feet still in shelter and extends the others, feeling for purchase. Its reach is longer than expected. Sometimes it appears to have put down a foot well ahead of itself when what has touched the rock is only the first joint.

It knows where each branch comes low enough to receive it. A limb that has sagged since yesterday causes a small delay.

The oval is where it sleeps.

Before entering, it stops at the uphill end and presses the dark underside of its throat to the stone. The rushing continues. A hard little note sounds somewhere below, and a fold along its side tightens.

It waits.

A second note may come immediately, or not for several minutes. Then it advances, lowers itself, and draws in its feet.

The fit is not exact. There is room along one side for a narrow crescent of water. Its body is a little shorter than the clear space, too, although old pale marks at the margin suggest that it has lain in other positions. The roots turn away from the places its limbs reach while sleeping.

Settled there, it is very difficult to distinguish from the floor. Its back relaxes into a shallow depression. Water gathers on it, joining separate beads until there is a continuous skin through which the cream-colored grain remains visible. The eyes close sideways. The head withdraws.

A passing cloud is reflected across the animal and the stone without interruption.

It does not seem to sleep through the low, rounded sound at the center of the hollow so much as within it. Changes too slight for an upright listener to notice pass through its body. Now a foot opens beneath the surface. Now the throat swells. Occasionally, after one of the clear notes, its back lifts enough to send a little water over the edge.

Whether it makes any of those notes is hard to establish. When it is in the tree, they still occur. When it is resting, a faint click can sometimes be heard close by, smaller and drier than the sound below. The click is followed by a long stillness.

There are places on its body that have healed badly. One rear limb ends before its final division. Along the left flank is an indentation into which water collects even when it stands. These injuries are old; the skin within them has acquired the same grain as the rest.

Near evening it often leaves the oval and goes beneath the low tree. It pushes its head into the warm debris at the roots, not searching at first, merely resting there with its eyes covered. The back remains in the open air. Moisture begins to show along its edges.

If the snail is crossing nearby, it may extend a foot toward it. The foot touches the shell, stays for a moment, and withdraws. The snail stops. Eventually both resume what they were doing.

On some evenings the animal climbs no farther than the fallen limb. It lies where the living strip of bark joins the tree, with two hands resting among the new leaves. As the air cools, its folds loosen. Its body becomes less like stone, fuller and softer at the margins.

Long after the rim has gone dark, one hand is still raised beneath a leaf-tip.

The drop there is too small to take yet.
