At the tram stops, the benches have backs but no seats.

This is not immediately obvious when they are occupied. The people waiting rest against them with their legs folded underneath, coats hanging almost to the paving. A traveler might suppose that the seat was concealed by clothing. Then a tram arrives, and one of the passengers rises higher than expected, unfolding a second long section of leg.

They board carefully. Each puts a hand against the doorframe, not for balance but to feel whether the carriage has been standing in the sun.

The shady side fills first.

They are narrow through the upper body and rather broad where the legs join. Their arms look ordinary inside sleeves, although the wrists turn farther than a human wrist would turn, and their fingers close in two successive movements: first making a cage around an object, then fitting themselves to its surface. A worker can carry six little fittings in one hand, each held separately, without their touching.

Their faces are less unfamiliar than their hands. There is a brow, a mouth, a pair of dark, recessed eyes. The face moves when they speak. Displeasure tightens the skin beside the mouth; attention lifts the head.

Only after some time do you notice that the eyes never shine.

Their exposed skin resembles unglazed porcelain that has been handled for years. Not white, usually, but umber, gray, a muted orange, sometimes a deep red-brown. Around the joints it breaks into fine overlapping scales. Across the cheeks and forehead it is smooth enough to take a polish. Old people have bright places beside their mouths where affectionate hands have touched them.

***

One of the engine caretakers lives in a court behind an anchor station.

Her name, as it appears on maintenance documents, is Saret. In conversation it has another sound at the end, made with the mouth closed, which can be felt through a bench if she is sitting beside you.

She is responsible for three courtyards, nine inspection rooms, and a linkage tunnel whose far entrance is in another postal district. She walks the tunnel twice in each warm season. The rest of the time she has instruments, reports from the opposite station, and a familiarity with the machinery that occasionally makes her intolerable to younger technicians.

She can hear a fault in the engine through the handle of her brush.

This is what her neighbors say. Saret says that anyone could hear it if they would stop replacing the brush handles with hollow ones.

She wears a pale coat with heavy hems. The hems keep the cloth from lifting away from her body in the dense wind. Inside are pockets for strips of gauge metal, a folded meal, two little brushes, and a domestic fuse she has been meaning to exchange for several weeks.

Her house is warm even by local standards. She has sealed the wrong ventilation opening, according to her niece. According to Saret, her niece has never understood which side of the court gets the evening draft.

They have had this disagreement for eleven years.

***

Their bodies do not use blood to carry warmth from place to place.

Beneath the skin lies a close arrangement of yielding plates, porous tissues, and slow chemical reservoirs. Heat passes through them unevenly. The hands can be ready for work while the shoulders remain stiff from sleep. A person returning from a cold shaft journey may speak clearly and recognize everyone, yet need help stepping out of their boots.

Morning rooms contain warming rails.

People lean their forearms on these while reading notices, preparing food, or saying very little to their families. Children crowd too close and are told to turn around before they become hot on one side. Guests are offered a place at the rail before they are offered anything to eat.

Their mouths are soft inside, but dry. Food is crushed against ridged surfaces at the sides of the jaw and mixed with a clear, tacky secretion. They eat mineral preparations, cultured films grown on porous trays, and thick pastes made from the internal tissues of some of the smaller animals. A good meal has several textures and a carefully managed succession of reactions. Some ingredients are kept apart until the instant they enter the mouth.

Street vendors sell paired wafers in little hinged wrappers.

You put one wafer on each side of the tongue and bring them together. They warm sharply, then become soft. Children enjoy the cheap ones that release enough gas to interfere with speech.

Saret buys these too, although she says they are for her niece’s children.

Water is a workshop substance. It arrives sealed, is stored away from food, and is handled according to its purity and intended use. Most inhabitants have seen it only behind glass. They know that somewhere in the lower workings it gathers naturally, and that certain materials change completely in its presence.

A school demonstration involves a drop of it and a curl of pale compound.

The compound writhes.

This is remembered with delight or disgust for the rest of a person’s life.

***

They are not a slow people in quite the way their streets suggest.

Their bodies favor sustained effort, and their machinery favors patience, but they become impatient with one another at perfectly ordinary speeds.

A cage may take most of a day to ascend. Waiting for it is no particular hardship. Waiting for someone to finish an explanation that was already understood is another matter.

At household meals, several conversations proceed at once. Their speech has a dry, close sound, full of little closures made behind the jaw. Alongside it runs a quieter vocabulary of vibrations. A palm resting on the table can interrupt, reassure, or ask a private question without disturbing the spoken conversation.

Tables in eating houses are consequently set on separate footings.

Cheap establishments neglect this. An argument at one table travels into the elbows of everyone nearby.

Lovers sometimes complain that they have been given public words and private silence. Children discover early that a foot against a chair leg can carry a remark their parents will hear, even if the visiting adult does not.

Saret’s niece taps the table twice when she wants the children to stop encouraging their great-aunt.

Saret taps back.

***

They raise relatively few children, but the children occupy a great deal of the district.

Their early outer plates are flexible and dark. They acquire their adult color unevenly, beginning along the shoulders and the fronts of the legs. For several years they look as though they have been working in pale dust and have not washed it off.

A young child can sleep curled into a space that later seems impossibly small. Families keep the old sleeping boxes. They become drawers for tools, document chests, planters for food cultures. An adult may have to move aside their own first bed to reach the household isolation switch.

Children run.

They run across courts, along the shaded undersides of overhangs, between tram supports. Their feet make a clattering sound that adults find both reassuring and exhausting. Games involve hiding things at enormous distances. A conspicuous stone a kilometer away can be home base. There is always an argument about whether a player went behind the transformer house or merely passed through its shadow.

Near the escarpment, the rules are severe.

No child is allowed beyond the second row of paving joints without an adult. The parapets are high, but height itself is not the only danger. On certain afternoons the air changes direction close to the ground, and a light body can lose its footing before anything overhead appears to move.

Every district has someone whose job includes going out to retrieve toys.

This is not regarded as a trivial public service.

***

The small pale animals are tolerated almost everywhere.

They rasp deposits from thresholds, nest behind household conduits, and gather on the warm lids of street cabinets. Most residents distinguish several kinds that an outsider would take to be identical. Some are useful. Some spoil stone. One variety gives off a sour electrical smell when disturbed and must be removed from sleeping rooms.

People keep favored individuals without quite admitting that they do.

Saret has a large one with a chipped corner on its shell. It lives beside the engine station’s west door. She shifts it with her foot before sweeping and shifts it back afterward.

When a visiting inspector suggests replacing the sill, she tells him the old stone is sound.

It is not entirely sound. There is a hollow underneath it, and the animal spends the cool part of the day there.

The tall pipe grazers are another matter. They learn routes, recognize people, and can be obstinate about gates. A herd will wait beside a closed gate for days if it has previously been fed on the other side. Municipal crews have learned to leave temporary gates open after moving them, so that the animals may discover for themselves that the passage no longer leads anywhere.

One elderly grazer follows the morning tram for two stops.

Nobody remembers who first fed it through a window. Several passengers now do so, each under the impression that they are the only one.

***

Not everyone works with machines.

There are cooks, survey clerks, makers of soft linings, people who cultivate the amber films used in wound dressings. There are advocates who specialize in settlements emerging from dust, and who can determine whether a roof now used by animals remains the property of a family living six hundred kilometers away.

Such cases are bitter.

A place may have been abandoned with the expectation of return. The expectation survives in documents long after it has ceased to survive in anyone’s practical plans. Then a ventilation stack appears, a crew finds serviceable equipment beneath it, and descendants who have never seen the district arrive with keys.

The keys sometimes fit.

There are also households that stay too long in failing districts. They dislike the offered replacement homes. They have relatives in the wall chambers. They insist that the tram service will be restored if only enough people remain to use it.

Some are eventually proved right.

Others spend years watching the copper vanes above empty neighboring houses.

***

Their dead do not rapidly disappear.

The body cools. Internal separations fail. Resins set in places where they once yielded. For a while the person looks merely stiff, in need of the warming rail.

This resemblance is distressing to children.

Before burial, the joints are arranged in a resting posture and the face is covered with a thin, uncolored plate. Families may keep the body at home for several days, but not indefinitely. There are practical reasons, and there are reasons nobody attempts to make practical.

The wall chambers lie well inside stable strata, away from the engines’ anchor beds. They are dry, dark, and densely occupied.

A person visiting does not expect a reply. They may nevertheless rest a hand against the chamber door while speaking.

Saret visits her brother at the beginning of the cool season. She takes the household accounts, because he used to keep them and because she has never found a satisfactory way of telling him about her life without beginning with some small error he would have noticed.

She does not believe he is correcting her arithmetic.

She checks it before going.

***

There are people below, too.

They come up in the elevator cages wearing insulated wraps, carrying cases with pressure seals. Their skin has a different finish. They stand in open courtyards and look uncomfortable under all that sky.

Upper families who have relatives in the workings prepare inner rooms for visits. They close shutters, reduce the heat, put away foods whose sharp reactions are poorly tolerated by someone newly arrived.

Children are warned not to ask visitors whether they have touched water.

They ask other questions that amount to the same thing.

Some young people go down and stay. Others take work at remote switching stations, or join the crews following the slow fronts of dust. There are districts that have lost almost everyone between the ages of apprenticeship and household formation. Their public rooms are beautifully maintained. Their notices offer generous terms.

Saret’s niece once intended to leave for a newly opened settlement on the second rise.

She still has the appointment papers.

Her children have drawn on the back of one of them.

***

In the evening, Saret finishes her inspection at the west door.

The chipped animal is out on the sill. She bends, runs a finger along the edge of its shell, and feels it close against the cooling air.

Across the court, her niece is taking down a sunshade. One of the children has climbed onto the warming rail indoors and can be seen through the open shutters, much too close to the upper heating surface.

Saret calls a warning.

Her niece calls the same warning at almost the same time.

For a moment they are in complete agreement.

Beyond the court, the roof animals are lifting from the elevator houses. A departing membrane catches the light and becomes briefly visible from edge to edge, every fine rib and old repair showing. Then it passes beyond the buildings.

Saret stays in the doorway until it has gone.

She has lived here long enough to recognize some of the roof animals by their scars. She has watched thousands of departures. Still, when one steps off into the open air, she stops what she is doing to see whether it will rise.
