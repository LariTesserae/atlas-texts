The white animal does not go far into the frost hollow.

It stops with its forefeet on a gray patch and its hindfeet still among the leaves. For a moment it seems to have caught itself in an awkward step. Then it lowers its chin until the fur beneath its jaw touches the ground.

Under that fur is a bare strip of skin, ridged across its width. The strip rests against several leaves at once. Each leaf is moving a little differently. One carries the tightening of a vessel below; another trembles where its stalk passes through a layer of old roots. Together they tell the animal something it cannot learn by looking.

It withdraws one forefoot. Waits. Places it slightly to the left.

When the green ground ripples, it is already on the other side.

Up close, it is less white than it appeared. Its back has the dull translucence of frost that has begun to thaw. Fine hairs lie almost flat against one another, holding a shallow layer of air. The longer hairs along its flanks are stained yellow at the tips. Its belly is dark, as are its feet, and when it squeezes between root plates the white disappears with surprising completeness.

It is about the length of a rabbit, but there is little rabbit in the way it moves. Its shoulders and hips can turn independently. Its ribs overlap rather than meeting in a rigid cage. Beneath the loose coat, the body is narrow enough to enter a gap that would scarcely admit its head.

The head goes first, sideways. Then a shoulder. Then the animal seems to pour through, although there are bones in it, and it can be caught.

There are places beneath these valleys where white hair remains pinched between layers that have not separated again.

***

Most of its life is spent within a few body-lengths of the surface.

Deeper down, the temperature is more constant and there is more moisture, but the passages are dangerous. A cavity may remain open through a whole season and close in the course of a morning. A dry tunnel may become a supply channel. Some of the larger vessels develop openings wide enough to enter; their smooth interiors offer no purchase once the flow begins.

The animal keeps to the untidy layer where living stems pass through dead leaves. Here there are many small routes rather than a few large ones. It can usually retreat, or turn, or force a new passage with its wedge-shaped forehead.

Its feet have five short toes. Four bear curved nails. The fifth turns inward and ends in a broad, soft pad that can close against the others. With these feet it grips fibers, tests leaf stalks, and holds food. On exposed ground it spreads the toes so widely that each step looks deliberate. Underground, it folds them and pulls itself forward by the nails.

The dark skin of the pads is crowded with shallow pits. Damp tissue seals against them briefly. A foot placed on a stem can feel the stem growing taut before there is movement enough to see.

This is why the animal pauses so often.

An observer might take it for timidity. Sometimes it is. But sometimes it is waiting for a route to open.

***

It does not eat much of the green surface. The leaves are tough, bitter, and poor food for something that must keep itself warm. It chews their swollen bases when thirsty, choosing the ones that have just unfolded. The bitten stalks draw shut behind it.

Its real food is found beside the working channels.

There, in the warm damp seams around the valves, live soft-bodied grazers no larger than grains of barley. They are nearly transparent except for a green-brown line through the middle. They scrape the tender lining with comb-shaped mouths. In small numbers they leave pale stipples. In large numbers they turn a valve edge ragged, so that it no longer seals.

The white animal can hear a leaking valve through its jaw.

It follows the irregular pulse, digs with short alternating strokes, and opens a hole just large enough for its muzzle. Its tongue is narrow, dark, and rough along one edge. It works the tongue around the valve rim, taking the grazers and their eggs. Sometimes it braces all four feet and pulls out a fat, blind larva that has lodged crosswise in a side channel.

The hole it leaves usually closes within a day.

Around places where these animals feed often, the living tissue grows unusually smooth. Whether this is a response to their saliva, to the repeated injury, or simply to the removal of grazers is difficult to tell. The animals return to such places. Their claws have made pockets in the dead layers nearby, and those pockets contain shed hair, droppings, husks, and the small hard mouthparts of things they have eaten.

They do not maintain all the channels. They scarcely touch most of them.

A valley can swell and drain without a single animal in it. Yet in an abandoned feeding ground the grazers sometimes multiply until several neighboring valves fail. The surface above grows wet, then red, then brown. Eventually the damaged run is sealed off and another develops elsewhere.

By then the animals have moved.

***

They are not quite solitary.

At dusk, four may emerge from openings along the same ridge, each separated from the next by a considerable distance. They do not call. One stands briefly on stiff forelegs and taps a root with its hindfoot.

The tapping is too faint to carry through the air.

Farther along the ridge, another lowers its chin.

There are only a few common signals: an occupied passage, food at a shallow depth, a disturbance that is approaching rather than passing underneath. The signals work best through old roots. In loose leaf beds they break apart. Across an active basin they can be distorted beyond use.

Two animals meeting in a tunnel touch jaws, then shoulders. They spend some time examining one another’s feet. The soles hold the odors of the places each has crossed, including the sharp scent of newly exposed tissue and the sourness of stagnant chambers.

One may follow the other after this inspection.

Or it may turn back.

During the coldest weeks they gather in cavities beneath the ridge crests. The outermost animal lies with its white back toward the opening; the others press their dark bellies together. They change places through the night. Even asleep, one usually has a foot against the wall.

A sleeper that feels a slow increase in pressure begins to work its toes. The movement wakes its neighbor before it wakes the animal itself. In a few moments the whole group may be leaving, still clumsy with sleep, dragging the youngest by the loose skin behind their heads.

Sometimes nothing happens to the chamber.

They do not wait there to find out.

***

Their young are born dark and almost naked.

For the first few days they attach themselves to the mother’s belly so firmly that she can climb upside down without losing them. Later she leaves them in a shallow pocket lined with stripped fibers. The lining is dry, but the pocket is never sealed. She keeps two exits open, and often a third that is too narrow for her.

There may be only one young animal in a litter, or two. A third rarely survives the first long freeze.

Their earliest movements are searching movements of the chin. Before their eyes are fully open, they press their jaws against the floor and lift them again. The mother sometimes lies across the entrance and drums softly with a foot. The young become still.

Learning to be still is not enough. A pressure pulse that means danger in one layer may mean an opening passage in another. A stiff surface may carry weight safely, or it may be a thin frozen skin over a draining hollow.

When the young first follow their mother outdoors, they step where she has stepped. Their little feet fit within the damp marks of hers. She crosses slowly and doubles back often, touching each one under the jaw.

Later they begin making mistakes at a distance from her.

An inexperienced animal will sometimes run from a moving patch onto one that looks quiet. If the quiet patch is being emptied underneath, it sinks with its own weight. The leaves part around its legs. Pulling upward only brings more of the loose surface with it.

The mother approaches flat on her belly. She catches the young one by a forelimb or the side of its head and works backward, inch by inch. For a while afterward, both lie still on the nearest old root.

The young animal’s coat dries into points. Small leaves remain caught in it.

***

In summer, when cloud rests on the uplands for days, their backs become mottled.

Minute green growths establish themselves in the stained ends of the hairs. These are not the same plants that cover the valleys. They live briefly in the damp coat, drawing on salts from the skin. The animals comb them out with their lower teeth, but never remove them all.

A well-fed adult in this season may look like a piece of pale turf lifted onto legs.

As the weather sharpens, the stained hairs loosen. They collect in sheltered grooves and along the margins of pores. Among them are eggs of tiny coat-dwellers, spores, seeds carried from feeding hollows. Some germinate. Most are absorbed into the closing layers.

The animals become white again.

They cross farther now, from one firm place to the next, visiting the last open water. At a reservoir they do not stand on the bank to drink. They hook their hindfeet into the fibers above it and lower themselves headfirst, keeping their weight off the polished edge.

Their whiskers touch the suspended ice.

A tongue passes through a hole too small for the muzzle. The animal drinks until it feels the bank begin to tighten, then draws itself up. A few drops caught in its chin fur freeze before it reaches shelter.

From the ridge, you might see that brief white movement and nothing more.

Beneath you, the animal enters a worn slit in the bark. There is another waiting inside. They touch faces in the dark, and the one that has been waiting moves aside.

Far below their chamber, a valve closes. Both animals stop.

The pressure passes into a neighboring run. Their feet loosen. One begins to groom the ice from the other’s jaw.
