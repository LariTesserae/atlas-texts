The woman moving the chair is called Eren. She has lived in the tower for eleven days and is already irritated by it.

The letting clerk told her that the lower rooms had finished expanding. This was true of the floors. It was not true of the wall beside her table, which has developed a shallow inward bulge and pushed the chair into the route between the kitchen and the door. Each evening she moves it. Each morning, coming through with her breakfast, she strikes her shin.

She has tied a red cord around the offending chair leg. There are more effective precautions, but this one expresses her feelings.

Eren inspects bridge joints for a living. She can distinguish a dangerously stressed membrane from a healthy one by the change in pitch when she taps it, and she is qualified to stop freight on three major causeways. At home she has covered the wall’s inspection strip with a calendar.

Her son keeps uncovering it.

***

The inhabitants wear several thin layers rather than one thick garment. Their clothes have smooth inner faces and loose outer cuffs that can be detached and shaken out. Dust lodges in hems, under watch straps, in the folds behind the knees. A person arriving for dinner first stands in the entrance recess while a small fan pulls the scales off them.

Children dislike standing there. Visitors are embarrassed by the quantity collected beneath their feet. People who work in the growth yards can leave a pale outline of themselves on the filter.

Most wear their hair close at the temples, where mask straps pass. Long hair is braided or wrapped. An exposed hand is usually glossy at the knuckles with protective balm; every public counter has a little dispenser, and the cheapest varieties smell strongly of the same resin as a cut stem. There are fashionable balms with the smell removed. There are expensive ones with a more agreeable resin put back in.

Their faces are expressive above the masks. A brief narrowing of the eyes can mean amusement, acknowledgment, or *yes, I know the tram is late too*. On pollen days, lovers meeting at the exchange touch foreheads before they find somewhere indoors to kiss.

They are not particularly quiet people, although the city makes them seem so. Living walls swallow arguments. A dropped pan may be audible in the room below while an entire party disappears into the thickness of a partition.

This gives rise to a common domestic mistake: assuming that because nobody can hear you, nobody knows.

***

Eren’s son is nine. His name is Tavi, and he owns a ceramic marble that belonged to his grandmother.

It is blue, with a small white flaw beneath the glaze. Three generations have rubbed their thumbs over the flaw without altering it. Tavi finds this more remarkable than anything growing in the city.

He rolls it along the floor to discover changes in slope. He rolls it into the entrance recess to inconvenience the cleaning fan. Once he puts it in a collection slot, hoping to follow its progress through the inspection panels.

Retrieving it costs Eren half a day’s wages.

She tells him this at length. Then she tells the story at work, where she laughs so hard that she has to take off her mask.

Children are taught the city’s warning signs early. They can read a warm seam with the backs of their fingers before they can read a freight notice. They know that a wall which suddenly becomes smooth may be less safe than one covered in reassuring wrinkles. They learn to recognize the sweet smell that precedes certain kinds of uncontrolled budding.

This does not make them obedient.

They test how far a grazer will travel with a thread tied around its middle. They insert scraps into immature walls to see whether the scraps will be pushed out or enclosed. They race along freight lanes because the deep grain makes them wonderfully fast, and because the adults who forbid it generally did the same thing.

The most admired child in Tavi’s class has discovered a place where a disused staircase still grows underneath its replacement. He will take you there for two lengths of copper wire.

***

Adults vary greatly in their ability to read the vegetation.

A good nursery worker can walk into a room and notice that the ceiling is thirsty for a compound whose name most residents have forgotten. Other people repeatedly kill domestic cuttings despite living inside several million tons of healthy plant.

There are enthusiasts who train elaborate shelves out of their walls. There are tenants who keep everything in free-standing metal cupboards and refuse to let so much as a coat hook take root.

In certain prosperous districts, interiors are held almost motionless. Specialist crews visit at night, suppressing buds, redirecting pressure, adjusting the feed. A dining room can retain the same dimensions for twenty years. The service is costly, and its customers speak of it as simplicity.

Elsewhere, families measure rooms before buying furniture.

Eren grew up in a flat with a table that could be shortened by removing sections. At large gatherings, its unused pieces were brought out from under the beds. Her mother still refers to a period of family prosperity as “the years we kept all six leaves in.”

The poor are not people without things that grow. Growth is easy to obtain. They lack things that can be relied upon to stop.

***

Eren’s mother, Sove, lives in the oldest district.

Her address has remained the same through two withdrawals and one complete regrowth. She has occupied an upstairs room, a temporary room near the freight station, and a ground-floor room approximately where her original stairs began. Her official tenancy is continuous.

She keeps the keys to all three doors.

Only the most recent key works. The others are plain pieces of metal worn smooth in ways the neighborhood no longer records. She carries them on separate rings so they will not scratch one another.

Sove dislikes being asked whether the changes trouble her. Some did. Some were improvements. The second building had better ventilation and an appalling kitchen. Her first bedroom overlooked a place where freight handlers played a throwing game after work, and she misses the game more than the view.

She does not believe the city remembers her.

When Tavi asks, she shows him a wall that has grown over the outline of an old service hatch.

“It remembers that,” she says. “It doesn’t remember who repaired it.”

The repairer was Tavi’s grandfather. He had been dead for four years when the hatch disappeared.

There is a photograph of him in Sove’s cupboard, held between two thin sheets of glass. In it he is younger than Eren is now, seated on an unfinished bridge joint with his lunch balanced on one knee. Behind him, a supervisor is approaching to tell him to get off.

Sove likes the supervisor’s inclusion. Without her, she says, everyone would claim he had always been sensible.

***

Pets are usually animals that can survive being temporarily forgotten.

The commonest is a little six-legged scavenger with blunt mouthparts and a glossy shell the color of scorched bread. It eats shed scales, sleeps in warm sleeves, and makes a patient scraping noise when it wants a container opened. Wild ones live in the service channels. Domestic varieties have broader backs and are less inclined to disappear into walls.

Tavi’s is called Cutter, although it cannot cut anything tougher than a dry cuff lining.

It recognizes the warmth of his hands. Whether it recognizes him is a subject on which he and his grandmother disagree.

At night it settles beneath the red cord on the chair leg. Eren, who said it would never be allowed loose in the flat, checks for it before moving the chair.

There are larger animals in the deeper working districts: broad, low-bodied haulers used where rails cannot yet be laid. They have soft gripping feet, small eyes, and sensitive fringes around their mouths. They are fed dense cakes from the harvest lots and trained by touching different places along their shoulders.

Their handlers insist that each has a distinct temperament. One refuses to pass under newly grown arches. Another steals insulating sleeves. A third works willingly all day but will not move until its handler has pretended to inspect its feet.

Machines are replacing them in the established districts. At the frontier they remain useful, because they can become frightened before an instrument has decided anything is wrong.

***

The people who work at the northern edge are recognizable by their boots. The soles are broad and replaceable, with blunt projections that spread under load. Walking in them on an ordinary street is awkward. Frontier workers sit down on the first tram platform and change into soft shoes, carrying the boots home in stiff bags.

Their work attracts both the patient and the reckless.

A surveyor may spend an entire shift observing a displacement too small to feel. A harvesting crew may have minutes to clear a pressure lot before its folds harden around their equipment. Both kinds of worker complain that the other is paid too much.

The frontier has its own small trades. People sell hot food from carts that must be moved every few hours. Instrument repairers rent benches in temporary shelters. Someone always offers to buy mineral inclusions from the root beds, although removing them is forbidden.

Some inclusions are merely grit. Others are pieces of the world above: a dark seam of metal, a crystal flattened by pressure, once the delicate impression of a creature that lived before the black plain existed.

These things pass from hand to hand. The city can use them, but people sometimes prefer to keep them.

Eren owns a thumb-sized piece of black stone. She bought it from a man whose account of its discovery became less plausible with every sentence.

It sits on her calendar to keep the lower edge from curling.

***

There are citizens who never visit the frontier. There are others who go every week.

Some attend the public explanations and ask precise questions about expansion rates. Some lean on the railing and talk about marriages, prices, a pain in the hip. Couples go because there are long stretches with nobody else nearby. Bereaved people sometimes go because watching new ground rise is easier than returning to a room that has already begun to erase the marks of someone’s furniture.

Nothing requires them to find it hopeful.

On rest days, Eren brings Tavi there. He likes the suspended measuring lines and the survey posts tilted at improbable angles. She likes the absence of domestic decisions. They buy something hot, sit on a bench whose legs rest on broad ceramic pads, and watch the crews.

Tavi asks where the next district will be.

A notice shows the proposed avenues, a transit stop, two schools, and a generous scattering of empty growth lots. Eren points them out. He asks which school he would attend if they moved there.

She tells him the district will not be ready while he is still a child.

He considers this with offense, as though the builders have neglected an obvious deadline.

Behind the railing, one of the pale folds gives a small tremor. A worker looks up from an instrument. Farther along, another raises a hand, and the people nearest the temporary walkway step back.

The interruption lasts less than a minute. Conversation resumes.

When they get home, the chair has shifted again. Eren kneels to check the inspection strip while Tavi puts Cutter on the floor. The little animal walks straight to the wall, feels along its base with its mouthparts, and settles against the new bulge.

Eren rests her palm above it.

The surface is warmer than it was yesterday. She leaves the strip uncovered and calls maintenance. While she waits for an answer, Tavi gets out his marble to measure how far they will have to move the table.
