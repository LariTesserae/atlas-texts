The first part of it you are likely to see is a foot.

It comes up between the floor roots without breaking them. The roots ease apart, one after another, and a pale sole presses into the hollow. It is broad enough to cover both your hands. Along its edge are six short toes, each bearing a translucent nail with a green line running through it. The sole is finely ridged, like the roof of an animal’s mouth.

It rests there for a long time.

A snail may cross it. Seeds attach their threads to the skin. Eventually the toes curl, with considerable care, and the foot withdraws. The roots close over the place, leaving a little syrup on the surface.

There is no leg beneath it that you could uncover by digging.

The leg exists farther in.

This is one of the arrangements the hollow has learned to sustain.

***

Its inhabitant is being born very slowly, and not in the usual order.

The feet are the oldest parts. They have been moving beneath the floor for eleven winters. At first they were no larger than the furred animals sleeping in the walls. They gripped roots and remained gripped for months, drawing nourishment through the soft nail beds. Now they bear weight. When one settles, leaves throughout the hollow tilt slightly toward it.

The weight belongs to a body which has not yet wholly arrived.

There are portions of that body in several places: a length of flank behind the eastern wall; a shoulder in the dense vegetation overhead; a throat, still sealed at both ends, lying beside the channel through which the largest pool empties. Living tissue connects them, but the connections take routes a knife could not follow. Sometimes blood leaves a vessel in the shoulder and returns to the same place a little older, having passed through an organ that will not form until another winter.

The cold makes this possible to survive.

Its blood moves slowly. Its cells wait well. Where a connection is unfinished, the vessel closes into a soft loop, and the blood goes around again.

There have been losses. Beneath a mat of flattened leaves lies a small, perfect hand, frozen hard and covered in seedling roots. It formed too far toward the boundary. For a while its fingers continued opening and closing when the feet moved. Now the hollow is growing another, closer to the pool.

The inhabitant has no knowledge of the first hand. But when the new fingers flex, they curl tightly inward, protecting the place where a palm will be.

***

The bud beneath the water contains its head.

For now the head is smooth, chin tucked against what will become its chest. The green covering is not skin. It consists of narrow leaves fitted so closely together that their edges appear to be seams in one surface. Beneath them, a pale face is slowly acquiring depth.

The mouth formed early.

It opens against the membrane and drinks. A tremor passes through the pool; the transparent leaves bend downward; several of the jointed swimmers lose their hold and spread their legs. Water enters the membrane through a collar of fine pores and disappears between the lips.

What the head takes from the water is more complicated than sugar.

The animals leave substances behind them: shed skins, salts, the traces of fright and courtship, minute fatty particles from their eggs. These pass across the developing mouth and into a fringe of tissue behind it. The inhabitant is learning the hollow by swallowing what has lived there.

It knows the swimmers first.

Long before it has eyes, it can distinguish the one missing two limbs from the smaller one that always crosses beneath the lowest leaf. It knows which cluster of eggs is about to hatch. When a swimmer dies, the mouth remains open for a little longer than usual, taking in the water around the body.

Later it will learn to drink less deeply.

For now, everything reaches it.

***

It does sleep, though most of its life could be mistaken for sleep.

During true sleep the feet turn onto their sides. The throat beside the drainage channel contracts. A faint vibration passes through the water, too low to hear clearly, and the animals in the walls stop chewing.

Then small movements begin in parts of the body that otherwise remain still.

The unfinished hand grips. Skin along the buried flank puckers into little ridges. High above, the shoulder presses against its leaf cradle.

These movements do not quite agree with one another. The feet seem to be climbing while the hand feels for something level. Sometimes a tremor travels from one part to the next and returns changed, as though the body has encountered a difficulty in its sleep and is trying again.

Afterward, the passages between its scattered parts are a little more reliable.

It can bend a toe and feel the bend immediately. It can swallow without bringing a knee up beneath the floor.

Such improvements take whole winters.

***

There is already something particular about it.

Other things might have grown here. The leaves have room for other arrangements; some of their unused folds enclose narrow chambers, lined with soft hairs, in which nothing has ever developed. But this inhabitant is becoming patient in certain ways and impatient in others.

It dislikes water dripping repeatedly onto the same patch of skin. The exposed shoulder will endure pressure, frost, or the white grazers feeding nearby, but a regular drip makes it shift until the entire ceiling gives a low report. Then the drop falls elsewhere.

It is interested in hard seeds.

Whenever a foot comes through the floor, the toes feel for them. A seed is rolled along the ridges of the sole, held between two nails, released, found again. This can continue for days. The seeds frequently germinate during the process. Once their roots take hold, the foot grows still until the seedlings can be transferred to the floor without tearing.

Near the pool is a little crescent of plants that grow nowhere else in the hollow. They are all the same height. Each was held for approximately the same length of time.

The inhabitant has also begun to answer the drumming.

Its replies are clumsy. A foot presses upward twice. The throat tightens. Far above, a thick leaf knocks against another.

Usually the smaller animals continue at their own pace. Occasionally the hollow becomes quiet for several breaths, and in that interval the foot moves once more.

It is learning how much force an answer needs.

***

When a stranger enters, it becomes attentive from the extremities inward.

The feet stop playing. The skin behind the wall tightens. Water ceases moving through the sealed throat.

It cannot yet recognize the shape of someone standing in the hollow. It receives instead a scattered account: warmth moving among the roots, unfamiliar salts in the pool, the pressure of a heel, air disturbed by breathing. These sensations arrive at different times. A visitor who stays only a moment may occupy its attention for weeks.

If you remain still, a foot may emerge near yours.

The toes spread against the floor. One advances until the nail touches your boot.

It does not seize you. It maintains the contact, adjusting whenever your weight shifts. Through leather, ice, and wool, it can feel the small movements you make to remain upright.

This is new information.

Nothing else in the hollow balances so much weight on so little of itself.

After you leave, the foot sometimes stands on its toes beneath the roots. The attempt lifts a low ridge across the floor. Pools spill from their leaf cups. Sleeping animals complain without waking.

Eventually it settles again.

***

As the body grows, its parts will draw closer.

The flank will emerge from the wall. The shoulder will descend. The feet will find themselves attached to legs that occupy the intervening space continuously, and for a time their old habits will be dangerous. A step once taken beneath the entrance may strike the edge of the pool. A hand reaching along a familiar route may meet its own ribs.

The hollow is preparing for this as carefully as it prepares for the opening of the bud. Roots thicken where weight will fall. Leaf margins withdraw from the places where joints must bend.

There will be a difficult winter in which it can no longer fit beneath the floor and cannot yet stand.

The furred animals will nest against its sides. Water will collect in the hollows above its collarbones. It will have to learn which sensations belong to a swimmer moving through the pool and which belong to an insect walking directly over its skin.

Only then will the leaves around the head begin to part.

The face inside has two closed eyes set far apart, a broad upper lip, and a delicate fringe beneath the jaw. The fringe is folded now. In moving air it will spread and taste what the water once carried to the mouth.

Its eyes may open at different times.

The hollow has been keeping light for them in the paler interiors of the ceiling leaves.

***

It is not certain that it will ever go up into the meadow.

The seam between the roots is much too narrow. Its eventual height will exceed the hollow’s present height, and the outer roots have shown no inclination to widen their boundary. Beyond them, the body’s oldest connections would fail. Blood would have to travel the full length of every vessel.

Perhaps, by then, it will be able to.

For the moment it has other work.

Under the layered pool, the mouth drinks. A new crease deepens across the palm. The feet settle into places that can bear them, and the heavy body between those places becomes a little more continuous.

One foot has found a seed too large to hold between its toes.

For several days it has been turning the seed, trying different grips. Now, very slowly, the second foot is coming toward it.
