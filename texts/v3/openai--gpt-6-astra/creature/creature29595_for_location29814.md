At first, the only sign of them is that certain handrails are polished underneath.

The upper surfaces carry mineral crust, and the sides have grown slick with the beginnings of leaves. But beneath each rail runs a strip of bare metal, warm and smooth, with five shallow hollows at intervals. The hollows do not match the spacing of a human hand. Two are close together; the others are spread far enough apart that whatever made them must have held the railing along much of its forearm.

There are similar marks on the undersides of stairs, inside the rims of intake mouths, and along cables that have never risen above the mud.

Something uses these routes. It travels below the surfaces on which a person would put their feet.

You are most likely to see one when a change of depth passes through a clearing. The leaves turn edgewise. Silt draws into thin vertical lines. Then a pale length of something appears beneath a catwalk, already holding on.

It may have been there all along.

Its body is narrow when seen from above, hardly broader than a ladder. From the side it opens into a deep, loose arrangement of ribs, membranes, and long folded limbs. Water passes through it. Pieces of foliage pass through it. A searchlight can shine between its ribs and illuminate a patch of brickwork beyond, although a moment later the same beam encounters a solid flank.

This is partly a matter of posture. It can draw itself together with extraordinary completeness. What looked like a membrane stretched between separate bones becomes the smooth side of a heavy animal. The limbs lie in grooves. The outer skin tightens, darkening from cloudy white to the brown of wet insulation.

But posture does not account for all of it.

When the bottom changes beneath one, portions of its body remain at the old depth while others go with the new. You may see its gripping forelimbs close beside you and, far below, the slow movement of its hindquarters. The middle is not stretched between them. It is simply difficult to find.

The animal waits until the water settles before bringing itself together.

This seems to require care. It releases its holds one at a time. Along each flank, little muscular shutters open and close, exposing pale folded tissue. A trembling passes from the rear of the body toward the front. Only when that trembling has reached its head does it move on.

They have heads, though these are seldom the first part you notice.

The head is small, blunt, and tucked beneath a collar of overlapping plates. There are no visible eyes. Around the front runs a soft seam which parts in several places when the animal presses its face against a surface. Behind it are rows of short, densely packed feelers, so fine that they resemble a wet brush.

It uses these on everything it intends to trust.

Before transferring its weight to a newly risen shelf, it brushes the stone. Before crossing a cable, it tests the cable at two points. Sometimes it holds its face against a pump housing for several minutes while the rest of its body remains loosely spread in the current.

The machinery can be deafening there, but it appears to be listening for something much smaller than the machinery.

Its hands are easier to understand.

Each forelimb ends in five fingers, two short and three very long, with broad, blunt tips. The fingers bend in either direction. They can close around a rail without the wrist turning, or spread into cracks too narrow to see. The skin on their inner surfaces is almost transparent. Under it, dark cords tighten separately as each finger takes a load.

An adult can hang by one hand through a surge that strips leaves from a whole grove.

It will nevertheless test a rung before using it.

There are injuries among them: missing fingers, scars that gather the skin into white knots, patches of flank where the shutters no longer close evenly. One near the eastern ridge carries a length of cable through an old wound. The wound has healed around the insulation. When the animal folds itself, the cable bends with it.

They do not seem immune to anything here. They are only practiced.

***

They do not eat the vegetation.

Nothing in their passage suggests appetite for it. A broad leaf caught between the ribs is worked free with a hind foot. Roots that have entered a resting place are loosened and laid aside. Even the clear gel of decomposition passes untouched through the openings in their bodies.

They gather instead where waters of different depths meet.

Several may hang along the edge of a new shallow, forelimbs fixed to the older bottom, their bodies extended into the disagreement. The membranes between their ribs swell. Fine channels within them turn blue, then almost black. For a time the animals become heavier-looking, more definite. Their skin fills out around the joints.

On the downstream side, the water is less disturbed.

It is not still. A stone dropped there may yet fall much farther than it should. But the violent little reversals cease, and loose mud begins to settle.

Whatever nourishment they take arrives with the strain.

A feeding animal keeps adjusting its grip, letting one part of its body drift deeper while holding another against the shelf. Too little difference and it closes its membranes. Too much and it pulls itself clear, abruptly, with all its limbs.

The youngest sometimes wait too long.

An adult will reach into the disturbance after one, arm disappearing to the shoulder, fingers searching at a distance that the eye cannot judge. When it finds the small body, it does not simply haul it out. It draws the youngster against its own underside and holds it there while both of them slowly refold.

Afterward, the young one may remain attached for hours.

***

Their resting places are not dens in the usual sense.

They favor structures that have remained recognizable through many changes: the underside of a stair landing, the space between two concrete legs, a transformer casing held against its platform by one surviving bolt. Around these they weave loose cradles from stripped cable, old stems, and lengths of soft metal.

The cradles look poorly secured. Some are fastened at only one end.

They need to be able to move.

Inside, an animal folds until it becomes a compact, ridged mass. The hands remain outside, gripping different parts of the structure. Even asleep, it changes those holds. One finger lifts; another takes its place. Along the flank, a shutter opens briefly and shuts.

Several often rest together, but not in a heap. Each leaves room for the others to open.

Their cradles contain objects.

A yellow wheel with no valve beneath it. Three ceramic insulators nested together. A section of railing, severed at both ends, whose polished underside has been worn almost flat.

Some of these things are useful. Others are carried from one resting place to the next despite their weight. An animal displaced by rising stone may leave its woven cradle behind and spend most of an evening working a particular bolt out of the concrete.

It keeps the bolt pressed beneath its collar while it travels.

At the next resting place, it fits the bolt into the weave and touches it repeatedly with the soft seam of its face.

***

They do work on the machinery, although they are not its operators.

They ignore many defects a human would consider urgent. Water can pour through a split housing without attracting them. A bearing may shriek for weeks.

But a change too small to hear can bring three of them across the basin.

They arrive separately, following the submerged rails. One fastens itself above the affected joint; another takes a hold below. The third works its long fingers into the gap. Their bodies open and become pale. For a few seconds the cables around the installation lift out of the mud.

Then there is a single knock.

It travels through the basin with unusual clarity.

The animals remain where they are until the first new growth appears around the joint. Then they withdraw their fingers, carefully, so that none of the pale shoots are torn.

Sometimes this intervention leaves a pump running more smoothly. Sometimes it stops the pump altogether.

They have no general preference for silence.

What matters seems to be whether the next change will leave somewhere they can hold on.

There are abandoned installations around which the vegetation has become impenetrable. No polished metal can be found there. The animals approach these places only to turn back, and their young are pulled away from the outer stems.

***

Their young first appear in the cradles as cloudy, folded things smaller than a hand.

For several days they have no firm outline. Their limbs cling to the weave while the rest of them shifts with each vibration of the supporting structure. An adult lies beside them with one flank opened, sheltering them from the strongest pressure changes.

They develop their grip before they develop the ability to let go.

You can watch a parent spend a long time freeing a youngster from a cable. It loosens one small finger, holds it clear, then loosens the next. By the time it reaches the fifth, the first has fastened again.

The adults are very patient with this.

Later, the young are taken to the duplicated stairways south of the pump house.

An adult holds the middle railing. The youngster is placed on one of the others. They face each other through the stems, close enough to touch by ordinary distance, and wait for the bubbles to issue from the bolt holes.

At that instant the adult taps the rail.

The youngster feels the tap beneath its hands.

This is repeated many times, on different steps, at different depths. Eventually the small animal reaches around the railing and takes hold of something that is not visibly beside it.

The adult’s fingers close around its own.

***

When evening brings the heavier load, they leave their cradles.

All through the groves, long hands move along the bottoms of rails. Pale flanks open briefly in the lamps. They pause at junctions, testing the metal, and separate toward different installations.

One remains beneath the nearest pump house with two young pressed against its underside.

As the banks begin to move apart, it descends to the lowest step. Below that step there has been only mud for months. Now there is a widening dark interval, and far down in it the corner of a new platform catches the light.

The animal lowers one arm.

It waits through a valve stroke, a shudder of the foundations, the heavy passage of a blue bead rising beside its face.

Then its fingers find the new railing.

It holds both structures for a while. The young loosen their grips and turn beneath its body, brushing the unfamiliar metal with their faces.

Only after each has taken hold does it release the old stair.
