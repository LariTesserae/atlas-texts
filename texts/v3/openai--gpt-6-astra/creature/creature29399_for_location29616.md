The people who live there have broad thumbs and sore eyes.

The thumbs come from work. Even a child can hold a wet cord against the side of one finger and tie a knot without releasing it. Old people have a smooth patch on each palm where the skin no longer takes a print. Their nails are kept short, except sometimes the nail of the smallest finger, which is useful for lifting the edge of a mineral crust without disturbing what lies beneath.

Their eyes water in the streets. They wear strips of dark cloth across their brows, with fringes that hang almost to the cheekbones. From a distance, a person carrying a basket seems to have no face. Indoors the fringe is pushed up. There are usually little white deposits at the outer corners of the eyes; a mother wipes these away with her thumb while her child turns impatiently toward something else.

Visitors mistake this for tenderness peculiar to the town. It is tenderness, but it is also necessary. Left alone, the deposits crack the skin.

There are visitors, though not many. They arrive bent under timber, felt, grain, lamp oil, and bundles of soil sewn into old clothes. On the way down they carry salt, certain dried coatings from the collection plates, and the thin shells used elsewhere to face the insides of lamps. They also carry stories about a town where nobody speaks.

The inhabitants find this story funny. Some of them talk almost continuously.

They talk with their heads close together over work, in the dark recesses behind the felt, or while lying three abreast on a sleeping platform. A long argument may have to move from room to room as its participants finish their tasks. When someone is left behind, the others wait until that person catches up before continuing to be offended.

People who can shout are admired, especially by children. It is a gift of little practical use.

***

In the house above the oldest spillway lives a woman who repairs sleeves.

She repairs other things, but sleeves are what people bring her most often. Cuffs rot, stiffen, split along the folds. Elbows wear through from leaning into channels. She keeps a row of little bags against her belly, each holding a different kind of needle. The needles must be kept warm and dry, and she does not trust a cupboard.

Her own left sleeve has been replaced so many times that only a narrow crescent at the shoulder belongs to the original coat. This crescent is red. She will show it to anyone who asks, though she dislikes being asked what the coat looked like when it was new. She was nine then. She remembers wanting a different one.

There are four other people in her household, sometimes six, and a large pool animal whose back is visible through the floor hatch.

The animal is there more reliably than the people.

It has a blunt head, no neck worth mentioning, and two eyes set so low that they are often hidden by the folds above its mouth. Along each flank are small puckered openings. Its limbs end in soft, divided pads which can close over a stone edge with extraordinary strength. When it sleeps, the pads continue to grip and release, one after another, in a slow sequence.

The sleeve-mender calls it Button. Everyone else calls it the thing under her floor.

Button eats the growth from the underside of the house. In doing so it keeps a narrow outlet clear. Twice a year, approximately, it leaves, and someone has to lie on the boards with an arm down the hatch and rake the outlet by hand. The woman resents these absences. She has explained the arrangement to the animal several times.

It responds to a touch on its back by drawing its limbs close. To move it, she presses two fingers into a particular crease behind its head. Nobody taught her this. One winter she tried many places.

***

The young of these animals are almost transparent.

For a few days after birth their limbs are visible inside them as pale, folded strokes. They cling beneath their parents until the pads harden, then spread into the narrow channels. A household may discover twenty of them in a week and none the following year.

Children hold them against the dark felt to count their legs. There is seldom agreement. Some limbs divide close to the body; others are tucked so tightly under the belly that they look like creases. A child who has announced a number will often refuse to put the animal down until everyone accepts it.

The adults distinguish the larger ones by scars, missing pads, or peculiarities of movement. One always enters a channel backward. Another rolls onto its side before passing under a bridge that is easily high enough to admit it upright.

They are neither livestock nor quite wild. Some are killed and eaten, particularly after a bad trading season, but an animal that has spent years keeping a house’s drains clear is generally left alone. This is a household decision. It has produced feuds.

Their flesh is dense and needs prolonged heating. Their fat burns with a blue edge. In the coldest rooms, a lamp may briefly give off the smell of the pool.

***

The people know the water first through their feet.

Children are carried until they can be trusted not to sit down wherever they become tired. Then they are given soft shoes with replaceable soles and taught to stand still. A moving child feels only cold; a still one can distinguish a thread of water passing under the paving from water running across it.

Their first useful errand is usually to stand somewhere and return when the sensation changes.

This work bores them. They invent changes. They forget the errand and follow a shrimp. They come back earnestly describing a warmth that nobody else can find. Adults must decide whether to shift a cupboard on the evidence of someone who still eats loose lamp fat.

Eventually the children learn. Or most do. There are grown people who have never been good at it, and who make themselves useful by carrying, cooking, bargaining, mending. Nobody entrusts them with a sleeping room on a doubtful floor.

Good water-readers are not always solemn. One of the best is a young man who makes obscene little figures from softened grey ice. He leaves them on window ledges, where they gradually slump into innocent shapes. When he stops in a lane and puts down his basket, people nearby also stop what they are doing.

He has been wrong twice that anyone remembers. He remembers more.

***

Most households keep a cloth account.

It hangs inside the door: a length of felt with cords, splinters, rings of shell, and knots sewn to it. These record things lent, duties owed, and arrangements that cannot safely depend on one person’s memory. A straight beam may belong to someone in another house. The right to lift that beam during a flood may belong to a third household. Two families may share a collection channel on alternate mornings, except when the water tastes metallic, when neither is permitted to use it.

The accounts become complicated. Children learn them by being sent to ask questions.

“Why does their grandmother own our lintel?”

“Because ours burned her stairs.”

“When?”

“Before your grandfather had teeth.”

“Which grandfather?”

That requires a visit.

These visits are how children learn the town’s history: standing beside a warm lamp while someone searches for the right grievance.

There are written records too, kept in the driest upper rooms. They say which channels must never be blocked, even if they have been dry for a hundred years. They contain measurements of openings that no longer exist above water. Some pages have been copied so often that the original names are gone, replaced by descriptions of later occupants.

The people who copy them leave those descriptions alone. They are not certain which details matter.

***

The birds belong to no one, though particular roofs are defended on their behalf.

They nest in shallow heaps of shed feathers, shell fragments, and stolen thread. Their eggs are grey rather than white and surprisingly warm. When a bird rises from its nest, it spreads its feet for a moment before folding them underneath again. Each foot has a dark central pad surrounded by narrow toes, like a small hand wearing a mitten with the fingers worn through.

They eat winged insects that emerge from the water so briefly that many people have never noticed them alive. Afterward the insects’ empty skins collect in sheltered corners, fine as scraped horn.

Sometimes the birds also eat the transparent shrimp. They stand at a channel’s edge and lower their whole faces into the flow. A bird lifting its dripping head looks, for an instant, old and furious.

The inhabitants watch their departures more closely than their arrivals. Yet a roof empty of birds is not necessarily unsafe. There may be a cat beneath the eaves.

There are only three cats in the town at present. All are much discussed. One belongs to the sleeve-mender’s granddaughter and has learned to sleep in a suspended basket. It objects to being lowered, so the girl must dress beneath it, pulling on her salt-stiff trousers while a paw reaches down to strike her hair.

***

Few people here are alone for very long.

Privacy is made with lowered fringes, turned shoulders, and the agreement not to look through an open hatch. A person can sit among six others and be understood to have withdrawn. A child takes time to learn this. So does someone newly in love.

Grief is harder. The ordinary work keeps arriving at the bereaved person’s feet: a basket needing lifted, a cold hand, a leak. Neighbors quietly move some of it elsewhere. Eventually they begin bringing it back.

The sleeve-mender lost her younger son in a lower passage. For a season afterward nobody brought her small sleeves.

At last a man arrived with his daughter’s coat, apologized for its condition, and asked whether anything could be done. The child had burned one cuff and picked apart the other.

The woman examined it. She said the whole garment had been badly made. She asked who had done the previous repair, though both of them knew it was her.

While they spoke, something nudged the hatch from below.

She put her foot on it without looking down, and the man moved his chair out of the way.
