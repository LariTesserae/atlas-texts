Sera Venn lives on the ground floor, although for six months her address said first.

The discrepancy came from a correction to the foundations and a disagreement between the housing register and the people who maintained the stairs. Deliveries accumulated outside the wrong door. For a while she put a note on both landings. Now she tells visitors to get out of the lift wherever they can smell onions.

She is broad across the shoulders, with a narrow face and a white streak in one eyebrow where a tension cable struck her years ago. In warm rooms she wears her sleeves pushed up. Outside, she covers every part of herself methodically, including the damaged eyebrow, which aches before snow. Her coat has a stiff patch across the stomach from repeatedly leaning against wet railings. Its pockets contain a pencil, a packet of dried pear, two different keys for the same service cabinet, and a smooth black stone her son gave her when he was small.

He is twenty-six now. He denies having given her the stone. She remembers him finding it, holding it out with both hands, although it is scarcely larger than a plum.

Sera maintains the connections between buildings.

Not the foundations themselves. Those belong to another department, whose staff arrive in clean vehicles and spend much of their working day below floor level. She is responsible for the things that have to continue fitting while the foundations move: enclosed bridges, service sleeves, accessible ramps, the short covered passage through which patients are wheeled from the clinic to the imaging rooms.

She can hear a bridge that needs attention. Walking through one, she sometimes stops in the middle of a sentence and sets the heel of her hand against the frame. Her apprentices find this theatrical until she makes them take their gloves off and feel it too.

A badly seated bearing has a dry little shiver. Ice in the wrong joint feels different. Sometimes the noise is only a loose panel, and sometimes she has to close the passage and make everyone go around.

She dislikes doing that. She dislikes it especially at the clinic, where going around means dressing frightened people for the weather.

At home she is less attentive. A cupboard door has scraped the kitchen floor for three years.

Her son mentions it whenever he visits.

“It isn’t going anywhere,” she says.

***

There are people in the city who rarely leave the connected interiors.

Sera’s upstairs neighbor takes the tram to a records office, eats lunch under a glass roof, buys vegetables in the station market, and returns through the same heated passage. He knows the open country principally as something that delays services. He has excellent eyesight and cannot reliably distinguish a fox from a resting bird at any distance.

Other residents go out almost every day. They have strong opinions about socks, know which culverts can be crossed without wetting a knee, and leave messages telling one another that the small fish are back in the western basin.

Sera belongs to neither group. She goes where the work is. On her days off she often stays inside.

She likes loud music, fried food, and competitions in which people cook elaborate meals under unreasonable time constraints. She has a standing argument with her sister about whether the contestants are allowed to practice beforehand. Their calls sometimes last two hours. Outside the kitchen window, whole migrations pass unnoticed.

But she knows the animals along her maintenance route.

At the hospital heat exchangers there is an old doe with a healed split in one ear. One of her forelegs no longer straightens completely. She stands with that foot tipped forward, scarcely taking weight, while vapor dampens her chest.

Sera first thought the doe would not survive the winter. That was four winters ago.

The animal has learned the service schedule. When Sera opens the outer gate, the doe moves aside before the latch sounds. She retreats only as far as necessary, then waits with her head lowered while the filters are changed. Once, when a replacement crew left the gate open, she entered the court and had to be persuaded out from between two banks of hot pipe.

Now there is a second latch. The doe tests it occasionally with her mouth.

Sera does not feed her. She does, however, work from the far end of the enclosure on very cold days, leaving the warmest section undisturbed until last.

***

Under the guideway lives a small predator that most residents never see whole.

They see its hindquarters disappearing beneath a cover plate, or a pale jaw briefly raised above the sedges. It is longer-legged than it first appears. When it hunts, it compresses itself close to the ground, moving with its belly almost against the wet stone; when it runs, the body lifts abruptly, as though another animal has unfolded underneath.

There are several along Sera’s route. One has a dark forefoot and occupies the embankment beside the old freight stop.

It eats nestlings when it can get them, crustaceans when it cannot, and the small blunt-headed fish stranded by falling water. In autumn it stores food in dry pipe sleeves. Maintenance workers find the caches and leave them alone unless the sleeve is needed.

Its territory does not have a stable shape. A warm seep can draw it two hundred meters from its usual shelter. A pool freezes, and a profitable stretch becomes useless overnight. During the spring thaw it follows the water upslope, hunting in narrow sheets of runoff that did not exist the previous afternoon.

Sera once watched it carry an egg across a pedestrian bridge.

It had trouble with the expansion joint. The egg was too large to hold comfortably, and the animal could not see the gap immediately beneath its muzzle. It put the egg down, stepped across, reached back for it, and continued.

For several weeks afterward she looked for it whenever she crossed that bridge. She had seen cleverer things, but something about the practical difficulty stayed with her.

***

Her mother lives two districts away, in a court with seven trees.

The trees arrived before the apartments were occupied. There are photographs of them being lowered through the unfinished roof, their root balls wrapped in pale fabric. In those pictures the branches look like packing material. Now their crowns meet over the benches.

Her mother has the photographs because she was on the original planting committee. She retains copies of most of its correspondence, including a bitter exchange about soil depth. She can still become angry over it.

For years she helped remove the fallen leaves to keep the court tidy. Later the gardeners asked the residents to leave most of them where they fell. This reversal offended her more than the soil dispute.

“We were told,” she says whenever it comes up.

She is not particularly interested in trees. She likes the court because it is sheltered, because her husband used to sit there, and because from the third bench she can see who is entering the building without being conspicuous.

Lately she has begun putting leaf litter back around the nearest trunk with the tip of her walking stick. She does it while talking, without looking down.

Sera visits on the evening after the clinic bridge inspection. She brings groceries and adjusts the pressure on a window seal. Her mother complains that the room has become too quiet since the adjustment.

They eat by the window. A bird lands on the outer sill, leaving two dark marks in the frost. Neither woman knows its name.

***

The children have names for places that do not appear on the maps.

The mouth. The three islands. The dead station, which is functioning normally but has a disused platform on one side. A particular pool is called Deep Water although an adult could cross it without wetting the tops of ordinary boots.

Sera’s son used to go there with two other children and bring home transparent creatures in jars. They were forbidden to release anything into a different pool, so returning the contents became an elaborate duty, frequently remembered at bedtime.

Deep Water is gone now. Its basin tipped enough to drain through a seam in the southern edge. For a season it was an oval of dark, slippery stone. Then dust collected in the hollows, and this year there is a little grass.

Her son passes it on the way to see her. He works nights in a market bakery and arrives smelling of flour and the sharp soap used on the mixing equipment. Sometimes he brings a loaf that split badly in the oven.

He wants to leave the plateau.

Not urgently. He is saving money. He wants to live somewhere with streets crowded enough to stay busy after midnight, somewhere he can walk out without checking the weather in three directions. Sera has offered to help with the fare and has not mentioned it again.

When he visits, he eats at the kitchen counter. He opens the scraping cupboard to get a plate and makes the usual remark.

This time, while she cuts the bread, he kneels and examines the hinge.

“Have you got a screwdriver?”

She tells him which drawer.

Outside, fog has reached the lower windows. Something moves through it near the handrail, too low to be a person. A few grains of loosened frost strike the sill.

Her son folds a scrap of cardboard under the cupboard door to hold it level. Sera stands beside him with the bread knife still in her hand, watching until he asks her to move out of the light.
