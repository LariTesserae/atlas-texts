It lives just above the waterline, under the crooked shelf, where a flap of liverwort has come away from the clay but has not yet fallen. In the dimness it looks like the wet underside of the flap. There is the same dull green, the same thinning toward the edge. Only when the pool rises does the distinction become clear: the liverwort lifts with the water, and the thing beneath it tightens against the bank.

It is about as long as the pale stone, though it seldom gathers itself into a shape that could be measured. Most of its body lies spread against the clay. A narrower part goes into a crack. Another turns under the shelf, following a root until the root becomes too fine to bear it. Where these portions meet there is a small, thickened saddle, brown enough to be mistaken for the remains of a leaf.

This is the part that contracts first.

The contraction travels outward slowly. Its edges lose their scallops. Water squeezes from beneath them, making a bright line, and the whole animal becomes a little darker and much harder to lift. A shrimp can catch a loose margin with its legs and trouble it for several seconds without getting anything free.

When undisturbed, the margins soften again. They are fringed with filaments so short that silt settles across them without quite hiding their movement. Each filament bends separately. Along one stretch they may all lean toward the entrance; around the corner of the shelf they turn the other way, answering the small return current there.

It feeds along this fringe. Softened matter catches, is tested, and passes underneath. A fragment too large to take in travels some distance around the perimeter before being let go. Sometimes a whole midge rests against it for an hour. By the time the body drifts away, the delicate joints have come apart.

There is no obvious head. There is, however, a place that usually goes first.

In clear water this can be seen making its way down the wall: a blunt, translucent lip extended over the clay, held still, then followed by the rest. Two dark grains sit inside it. They move when it moves and remain when the silt is washed away. Between them the flesh is almost colourless.

It advances most readily along a snail’s track. The track gives it something to eat and a route across clay that might otherwise crumble under its grip. It takes a long time to travel the length of a root. During that passage it seems less like an animal going somewhere than a damp patch changing its boundary.

Yet it can hurry.

When a flake comes loose above it, the first small disturbance passes down the roots. The animal draws its leading lip beneath the saddle and pulls its spread portions inward. This takes less time than the fall. The flake may strike beside it, covering half its body with slurry. For a while nothing shows. Then a narrow edge works out, folds over the fresh clay, and begins clearing a passage.

It does not always escape whole. One side bears a notch that has not filled in. Within the notch the new tissue is pale and smooth, without filaments.

The machinery keeps it drawn in more often than the falling bank does.

It feels the heavy note through its whole underside. When another turbine starts and the uneven beat develops, contractions pass across it in shallow waves. A margin releases, grips again, releases. Sometimes the waves meet in the saddle and make it lift slightly from the wall. During busy afternoons it can spend hours doing this, with food drifting past its closed fringe.

The rise of water is easier for it to answer. Water has direction and pressure. It can turn its thin side toward a current, settle into a root groove, or move above the reach of the next pulse. The sound arrives everywhere.

Its present place beneath the liverwort is a compromise. The clay there carries the vibration strongly, but the shelf takes the force out of incoming water. A release that sends leaves spinning across the pool merely thickens the dark fringe of silt along its back.

The hollow is younger than the animal. Before the bank broke, it lived farther down, among the compressed leaves held against a drowned willow root. It came into the new space on a piece of that leaf pack. For a day the leaves moved in and out through the slit without finding a resting place. Eventually they lodged against the pale stone. The animal left them during the quiet interval that followed, crossed the floor, and climbed until its front edge reached air.

It can remain out of water as long as its underside stays wet. In air its filaments collapse together, giving the margin a smooth, unfinished look. Small openings become visible along the saddle. They close when a drop strikes them.

At night it sometimes reaches nearly to the fern stems. There the clay holds a little warmth from the meadow, and fallen spores and pollen have collected in the roughness. It feeds beneath them, leaving a clean, wandering band that is soon dusted over again.

The wren has found it once. A quick blow lifted a corner from the wall. The animal folded around the beak, slippery and unexpectedly tough, and was shaken loose. It fell into the pool and stayed contracted beside the stone until well after the bird had gone. The notch dates from that morning.

Under the saddle, protected even when the rest is spread thin, it carries a row of small amber swellings. Through the skin of the largest, something occasionally flexes. When these open, the young will have only a few hours in the shelter of the parent’s folds. Then a current will take them, or they will crawl onto the bank. There is room for several beneath the shelf. There may not be a shelf by then.

Now the pale stone has disappeared. The animal has already moved upward, leaving a faint gleam where its body lay. Its leading edge reaches the snail that is turning on the wall.

For some time they remain in contact. The snail’s withdrawn head presses against the translucent lip; one feeler comes out, touches it, and shortens again. The animal makes a shallow hollow in its own margin. Slowly, with its shell tilted toward the pool, the snail passes through.

Below them, water fills the abandoned track. Above them, a drop gathers on a root. The animal spreads into the damp clay left clear by the snail, and the small amber bodies beneath its saddle settle with it against the wall.
