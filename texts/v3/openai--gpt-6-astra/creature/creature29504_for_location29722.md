On the sheltered side of the pump house, just above the concrete footing, there is a patch of growth that does not quite match the growth around it.

It is brown rather than green, with a faint copper edge. Water stands on it in short, broken lines. For most of the afternoon it remains tucked beneath a fern, resembling an old frond that has collapsed lengthwise and begun to soften.

Then one end lifts.

The movement is slow enough to seem at first like swelling. A crease opens beneath the lifted end, revealing a smooth, pale surface. Two short feelers emerge, followed by a second pair so small that they are visible only when their tips interrupt a reflection. The whole thing draws forward. Where it lay, there is a clean oval in the algae on the wall.

It is an animal about the length of a hand, though its length changes considerably as it moves. At rest it is broad and gathered, its back covered in overlapping wrinkles. Extended, it becomes narrow, almost rootlike, with a blunt head and a heavy rear portion that follows reluctantly over the stone.

There is a shell, but not where you expect one. A small, flattened plate lies under the skin behind the head. When the animal bends sharply, the edge presses up like a coin beneath wet cloth. It would be no use against a beak. Perhaps it serves some purpose in the distribution of its weight, or perhaps the animal merely continues to make it.

Its back is the color of the garden’s older fern stems. Along the sides, beneath the folds, it is a translucent gray. Against the wired-glass window, with the pump-house light behind it, you can see a few dark structures suspended inside.

It does not often climb the window. The glass offers little to eat.

Most evenings it leaves the footing and works along the seam where concrete meets rock. Its mouth is underneath, invisible unless it passes over a gap. It grazes the soft film there with a motion that leaves closely spaced pale crescents. By morning, these have begun to darken again.

It eats other things when it finds them: the softened edge of a leaf, a fly held in the surface of a pool, the white growth beneath a piece of timber. A dead fern stem may occupy it for two nights. It has no reason to cross the garden quickly. Much of what it needs grows back on the places it has already visited.

Nevertheless, its routes are not arbitrary.

Below the pipe’s third joint, it always pauses. The stone there is warm, but a fine thread of hotter water periodically emerges from under the insulation. Before crossing, the animal extends its head and touches the wet surface with the shorter feelers. Sometimes it proceeds. Sometimes it folds sideways and takes a detour along the underside of the pipe bracket.

The detour is longer by perhaps half a body length.

That is sufficient.

It can distinguish changes too slight for a hand to notice at first touch. Its front edge keeps making these small enquiries: into a current, beneath a leaf, across the boundary between sheltered stone and stone exposed to the notch. When cold air enters the cup, the feelers shorten. The head thickens as it withdraws. A fold of skin comes forward over the place where the eyes would be, if those two dark points are eyes.

Near the lip, it never becomes fully extended.

There is food there, a rich dark glaze where the water spreads thinly before descending. The animal feeds with its rear half anchored beneath a stone, reaching out and returning, reaching a little farther, returning again. It can spend an hour doing this without advancing more than an inch.

Beyond that inch is the mountain.

The running pump enters its body through every surface it touches. On concrete, the pulse is sharp. On moss, it is dispersed. The loose panel’s rattle makes the animal flatten, not because the sound is loud but because the bracket on which it rests shivers suddenly beneath its foot.

It cannot know that there are two pumps.

It knows that one part of the shelter trembles and another does not; that warm condensation gathers beneath a particular flange; that the dark recess behind the dismantled casing remains wet even when the outer stones begin to dry. Beneath the stiff sack is a place where its whole body fits without pressing against anything cold.

There are others.

They are difficult to count because the same individual can look like a lump at noon and a long, thin scrap at dusk. A smaller one lives among the pipe collars. Another, much darker, is usually beneath the slab nearest the lowest pool. On warm evenings two may feed on opposite sides of the same stone, their paths eventually meeting. The encounter consists of a long contact between their heads, then one rising partly over the other. Neither appears to hurry the result.

The largest has a notch in its left flank. Healed skin draws the surrounding folds into a little star. When it contracts, the mark disappears.

During a flood, they climb.

The first rising water does not disturb them. They continue feeding beneath it, their backs taking on a silvery appearance as air remains trapped among the wrinkles. Small bubbles escape when they turn. Only after prolonged submersion do they begin moving upward, following the stems and brackets they ordinarily use to cross from one feeding place to another.

Eventually they gather above the water on the back wall of the pump house.

Here their bodies become compact. The feelers disappear. A clear secretion thickens around the edge of the foot, fastening each animal to the wall inside a narrow, shining border. They wait with the copper margins drawn tightly in.

The maximum-water line on the cabinet is several inches below them.

Once, someone opening the cabinet found three attached to its underside. One came away on a glove. In the cold air it contracted into a dense, slippery weight, much heavier than its appearance suggested. The worker set it on the roof of the pump house and went on changing a fuse.

By the time the cabinet was shut, the animal had reached the sheltered edge of the roof. It descended headfirst behind a conduit, leaving a broad track that narrowed where the metal was cold.

There is no particular affection between it and the people who come up the ladder. Boots crush it if it is beneath them. A tool left on wet stone may provide a useful shelter. Grease is something it touches once and avoids.

Its young are laid deep in the moss, in soft packets no larger than a thumbnail. They emerge nearly transparent, each with a bright fleck beneath the skin where the little shell is forming. For a while they remain within the same handful of growth. There are entire journeys available there: down a stem, through the hollow beneath it, across a grain of volcanic stone to another stem.

Some never leave that handful.

A hot seep changes course. A pocket dries. Water lifts the moss from its shallow hold and carries it toward the lip. The adults persist through disturbances that erase the young without any visible trace.

Yet on the footing, beneath the older grazing marks, there are fine new ones.

After sunset the largest animal comes out from behind the pump. Condensation has joined into a continuous film along the concrete, and it follows this toward the door. Under the lamp its back loses the resemblance to dead vegetation. The folds lift and settle. Its pale leading edge widens against the wall, adhering, releasing, adhering again.

Behind it, the pump turns at its fixed speed.

Ahead, a bead of water hangs beneath a bolt. The animal reaches up until its head touches the bead. For a moment the water holds its shape around the feelers. Then it breaks, runs over the animal’s face, and darkens the wall below.

The animal stays extended. Its mouth begins to work along the newly wetted stone.
