It lives mostly beneath the overhanging lips of the terraces, where the stone is wet on both sides.

From above, there is almost nothing to distinguish it from the deposit. A slight thickening at one notch. A cloudy place in an otherwise clear thread of water. If you put your face close enough, you might see a fringe along the underside of a shelf, finer than the hairs on a wrist. The fringe leans downstream. Occasionally a few strands lean the other way, although the water has not changed direction.

There is more of it underneath.

Its body is a loose, translucent fold pressed into the space between yesterday’s stone and the water passing over it. At its thickest it has the faint yellow color of a fingernail held against light. Elsewhere it is difficult to say where tissue ends and the wet surface begins. It adheres along many small points, leaving passages between them. Water moves through these passages, carrying whatever the creature can take from it.

There is no head that stays a head. Where the current brings something usable, the fold swells and develops a short, soft opening. Several such openings may appear along one edge. By the next afternoon most have closed. One has become a narrow tube reaching upstream beneath a mineral shelf.

It feeds so slowly that the difference between feeding and being stained would require a long observation.

The little brown traces in the water interest it. So do certain invisible things arriving during the hours when the pump’s rougher note changes. Then its fringe extends. The filaments become longer than the depth of the stream and trail over the next basin’s edge. Their ends adhere there. Over several hours the creature draws a portion of itself across, leaving a clear cord stretched through the falling water.

It occupies four basins in this way, though the number varies.

The newest basin contains only a spreading edge. In the oldest, there is a thick, folded portion tucked under a shelf that has begun to close around it. Between them run connections so thin that a drop falling from the bracket can break one. The separated parts do not search for each other. They continue opening and closing. Sometimes their edges meet again later.

Whether this is one animal is a question that would become harder, rather than easier, with watching.

Its exposed surface collects mineral. The first grains give it a pale dustiness. Later they form a crust, and the crust stiffens until the creature can no longer draw that part inward. It withdraws through small openings, patiently reducing itself to what can pass. The abandoned covering remains attached to the terrace.

Some of the delicate shelves have been shaped around these coverings. Their undersides retain shallow channels and tiny chambers, including chambers from which nothing managed to emerge.

The creature has no general means of preventing this. It keeps a few passages clear by working their edges with a ring of nearly invisible folds. Around its active mouths, the new stone remains soft a little longer. That is enough, usually. Growth proceeds at the front while the back becomes a place it used to live.

In the main pool, beside the screw, one portion has grown unusually thick. The screw shelters it from disturbances at the surface, and the threads provide deep grooves in which it can fasten itself. A pale lobe rests against the brown stain. When sunlight reaches the bottom, this lobe contracts until the grooves appear empty. It takes most of the sunny hour to do so. After shade returns, it comes out again.

It is not asleep during that interval. Within the sheltered grooves its small openings continue their work.

The pump’s vibration reaches every part of it. Across the broad, thin portions, minute contractions pass in sequences too regular to be quite independent of the machinery. Where two frequencies produce the shudder you feel in your teeth, its fringe gathers into little bundles. When the beat passes, the strands separate.

It has grown under this pressure for so long that stillness would be a substantial event.

Once, during a brief interruption of the pump, the folds beneath all four basins loosened together. Several attachment points released. A portion hanging under the curb sagged into the falling water and stayed there, visibly thicker than anything you would normally find in that place. Before the flow diminished, the machinery started again. Most of the body tightened back against the stone. One dangling piece was carried into the pool below.

It remained there for days, caught on the edge of a submerged leaf.

Rain is more difficult. Cold water crosses boundaries the creature has maintained by growing only along the warm routes. Its openings close. The transparent margins turn faintly opaque and curl underneath themselves. Parts that have stretched between basins draw into short, knotted lengths.

During heavy rain, a few come loose.

They collect against the curb or travel toward the entrance, where the water thins over rough concrete. Most cease to be distinguishable before the surface dries. One has survived beneath the iron bracket, in the round hollow receiving condensation. It is smaller than a grain of cooked rice and spends much of its time tightly folded. Warm overflow reaches it irregularly. Whenever that happens, it opens a delicate, uneven rim.

Nothing of its kind lives among the three shoots. The cooler green patch marks a boundary it has approached repeatedly. Along the warm side are several small white curls: coverings left by extensions that withdrew or died. New growth still reaches that far.

In ordinary shade, with the pump running steadily, the largest part rests beneath the waterfall at the unfinished curb. There the water arrives reliably, and the apron below gives its filaments somewhere to attach. A dozen soft mouths face into the falling sheet. You could look directly at them and see only the slightly ragged bottom edge of the concrete.

By the next morning that edge will be a little farther down. Behind it, enclosed in new stone, will be the fine empty passages through which the creature fed today.
