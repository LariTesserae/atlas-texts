The first one you meet is trying to get something out from under a stone.

You have been watching the stone because dust keeps approaching it and stopping. A few grains rise, hang in a short curved line, and fall. Then the stone shifts sideways. Beneath its edge appears a narrow, wet-looking limb, which folds twice in places you were not expecting it to fold.

The inhabitant withdraws the limb and waits.

It is about as wide as your outstretched arms, though little of that width belongs to a solid body. Most is an overlapping skirt of thin plates resting on the ground. The plates are gray, with buff edges, and have the same subdued polish as the hard inclusions scattered across the plain. A central portion rises perhaps twenty centimeters. From where you stand, it looks like another low swelling of the rock.

After a while it puts the limb back under the stone.

Whatever it is doing requires delicacy. The narrow tip opens into several smaller tips. One braces against the underside; another makes a scraping movement. You hear nothing.

At last it draws out a translucent, many-footed thing with dark grains in its body. The animal curls around the limb. For a moment neither moves. Then the inhabitant carries it across its own back, lowering it into a recess between two plates.

Only when it begins to leave do you understand how much of what you were looking at was alive.

The skirt lifts in sections. Under it, rows of short limbs take hold of the stone, each ending in a pale pad that spreads on contact. The body advances without bobbing. Plates slide over one another, exposing fine black edges, then settle into an arrangement indistinguishable from the previous one.

Its passage leaves a broad oval in the dust. That vanishes almost immediately.

***

They are easiest to see just before dawn.

During the cold hours, they raise the outer edges of their bodies. Membranes unfold beneath the plates: thin, dark surfaces, held clear of the ground on branching supports. Moisture gathers there in quantities too small to form drops. Along the margins, salts change color.

Several may be taking in water around the same shallow depression. From a distance they resemble stones undergoing some slight, common deformation.

They remain separate. Even where there is room for only a few, their membranes do not touch.

As the first light reaches them, each folds itself away. This takes longer than their ordinary movements. The delicate surfaces must be pleated into channels beneath the armor without catching a grain between them. Small limbs pass repeatedly along the folds. An individual with a damaged membrane may still be occupied when the others have lowered themselves and gone.

Sometimes one of the others comes back.

It approaches the damaged side and inserts two limbs beneath the raised plates. The work can last most of the morning. When it is finished, the two rest edge to edge, their bodies making a single low shape against which the dust accumulates.

They do not appear to sleep then. Their exposed margins continue to respond to passing charge, lifting in little sequences.

***

There is soft flesh under the mineral covering.

You see it first inside a shelter, where an inhabitant has loosened its plates. The central body has risen from the depression in the floor. What seemed a shallow, armored animal outside now has depth: a folded underside, muscular columns, pockets that expand slowly in the dense air.

Its flesh is a muted plum color. Near the joints it becomes almost transparent.

The plates are not simply a shell. Some grow from the body, while others have been fitted over them. Old pieces are held by living lips of tissue. A large individual may carry a patch of white mineral taken from a seam many days away, a greenish strip polished thin at both ends, several little tiles of an unfamiliar black substance.

Replacement pieces lie beneath the bench.

They select them by touch. An inhabitant may hold a fragment against itself for hours, shifting it through small angles, before putting it aside. Two pieces that look identical to you provoke quite different responses.

Their outer coverings carry charge around the vulnerable organs. In open country, a charged front passes over them in a brief lifting of dust. Occasionally a grain shines at the tip of a plate. The narrow gaps close before the front arrives, and for several seconds the inhabitant ceases moving altogether.

Those seconds matter. You find a dead one with one side of its body still extended, the soft parts beneath it dried into a dark, shallow web. Its plates have remained in place. Dust flows across them with no visible disturbance.

Another inhabitant visits it repeatedly.

On the fourth visit, it takes away a small green piece from near the center.

***

They have no face you can easily address.

There are light-sensitive organs beneath several plates, allowing them to watch the horizon through a ring of low openings. Finer eyes sit at the ends of limbs and are extended for close work. If you kneel beside one, an eye may rise on a delicate stalk to examine the stitching on your boot.

The eye is cloudy white, crossed by a dark bar. It rotates inside its own transparent covering.

Your face receives less attention than your hands.

When you offer an empty palm, the inhabitant rests three limb tips against it. The pressure is light but complicated. Each tip alternately spreads and narrows. A sensation like a weak electrical current moves as far as your wrist.

Then it touches the ground, the bench, and your palm again.

For several days, this is the nearest you come to conversation.

Outside, they communicate through contact with the stone. The pads beneath their bodies can both feel and produce very small changes in its electrical state. You begin to recognize an exchange by the timing of their movements: one stops, another alters course far away, the first resumes.

Distance does not seem to make an exchange less private. Two close neighbors may remain entirely unresponsive while an individual at the edge of sight answers.

The buildings add something.

Inside, the inhabitants make sounds.

At first you mistake them for bodily noises: a soft knock, a short vibration, a series of low notes produced while the breathing pockets fill. But the sounds recur in arrangements. Others answer. Fine membranes, kept tightly folded outdoors, open beneath the raised central body and tremble in the thick air.

Their voices are quiet and unexpectedly resonant. Some have a rough overtone. One individual produces a faint whistle before every sustained note.

In a crowded room the floor carries one conversation while the air carries another.

***

They eat so little at a time that you can watch them for weeks without noticing a meal.

The translucent grazers are part of it. So are substances scraped from beneath rosette leaves and dark films growing in sheltered cracks. An inhabitant transfers these to a soft opening on its underside, using limbs that otherwise serve for cleaning and repair.

But they do not simply range across the plateau consuming what they find.

Near inhabited shelters, the stone cups have been altered. Their rims are lowered on one side, deepened on another. Some hold a few grains of a pale salt not found in the surrounding rock. Others contain flakes placed at precise angles, shading a portion of the colony during the brightest hours.

An inhabitant working among the cups may move a single grain, then go away.

You cannot always tell which colonies are thriving. The largest may be receiving no attention at all. A cup containing one small rosette, black and worn nearly flat, may be visited every day.

The many-footed animals move between these tended hollows. Their translucent bodies become dark with what they gather. The inhabitants carry some, release others, and sometimes retrieve them from places into which they should not have gone.

The one you first saw beneath the stone was returned to a cup later that afternoon.

Its rescuer remained beside it until it had gone under a leaf.

***

Their young are much easier to distinguish from the ground.

They are amber-colored and have too few plates. Their limbs extend beyond the protection of their skirts, and they move with an untidy sideways eagerness, often lifting one side of the body higher than the other.

They stay indoors.

In one shelter, three young occupy the depression in the floor. An adult lies across part of it, providing a roof beneath its own body. Small limbs continually appear from under the adult’s edges. One reaches for a loose grain. Another takes hold of it. Neither lets go.

The adult continues making its low, even sounds.

Later, when it rises, the young separate and begin crossing the room. Near the doorway, each slows. The outer air touches the exposed membranes, and the little body draws itself tight.

One ventures as far as the threshold.

A dust sheet passes outside. The young inhabitant flattens so abruptly that its plates make a faint clatter. After the dust has gone, it remains motionless for a long time.

From the depression, another calls.

The one at the threshold answers, then advances another body length.

***

They maintain things you cannot see.

An inhabitant may spend half a day resting across a straight seam with its central plates slightly parted. Its limbs are inserted into holes that were invisible before it found them. Nothing else happens.

At sunset, it withdraws. The holes close, or become impossible to locate.

Elsewhere, several assemble along a natural fracture. They bring mineral pieces tucked beneath their armor. Some are no larger than seeds. A white fragment passes from one body to the next and is returned twice before anyone sets it down.

The work is slow, but their response to failure is not.

Once, a pale flash runs along a seam in full daylight. Every visible inhabitant moves. Two cross the plain at a speed you have never seen them use, skirts streaming just above the rock, their undersides working in a continuous ripple. Another retreats into a shelter. A fourth extends its membranes in the sun.

By the time you reach the seam, they are still again.

There is a new crack beside it, thin enough to look like a hair.

You never discover whether they made it or prevented it.

Their relationship to the structures is difficult to judge. They handle some parts with quick familiarity. At others they hesitate, try different fragments, wait for responses that do not come. A shelter near the rim remains empty despite repeated visits. They have removed everything loose from it, including the bench.

On the floor is an oval discoloration where an inhabitant once rested.

They leave that untouched.

***

Travelers arrive carrying dust unlike the local dust.

It gathers deep in the joints of their armor: rust red, chalk white, sometimes a dull violet. They stop outside a shelter and clean themselves before entering. The work is painstaking. Other inhabitants come out to help, passing slender limbs through the places the traveler cannot reach.

Useful fragments are sorted from the debris.

There are also objects for which you can find no use.

One traveler carries a thin, curved piece of translucent stone. Indoors, it sets this on edge near the opening toward the rim. The low sun shines through it, laying a bent patch of honey-colored light across the floor.

An inhabitant moves into the patch.

Another touches the stone and changes its angle.

For the remainder of the evening they keep adjusting it as the light declines. The young repeatedly pass their limbs through the illuminated space. Once the sun is gone, the traveler puts the piece away.

The next evening, they ask for it again. By then you recognize enough of the floor-borne exchange to be certain.

***

They do not all keep the same hours or tend the same places.

One leaves its shelter late and returns early, bringing fragments that it arranges beneath the bench. Another often remains outdoors through the night weather. In the morning its plates are so clean that its body reflects a blurred streak of sunrise.

An old individual occupies a room with a narrow view of the suspended slabs beyond the rim. Several of its plates no longer fit closely. A small membrane has been repaired so many times that the seams outweigh the original tissue.

Visitors clean the dust from its joints. It usually allows this for a while, then lowers itself, obliging them to withdraw their limbs.

Sometimes it goes out alone.

You watch it travel to a low knob a few hundred meters away. The crossing takes hours. On the sheltered side is a colony of six rosettes in a deep, smoothly worn cup.

The old inhabitant examines each plant. It clears a little powder from the rim and probes beneath the leaves. Then it settles beside the cup.

Long after sunset it is still there.

From your place in the doorway you can barely distinguish it from the knob. Dust lifts along its windward side. Far below the continent, lightning fills a bank of cloud, and for an instant the gaps beyond the rim shine blue.

Something touches the floor beneath your boots.

The inhabitant sharing your shelter repeats the signal. When you turn, it has shifted away from the depression, leaving space beside it. Its breathing membranes are open. A few grains drift down through the dense, warm pocket of air under its body.

You sit.

After a while, the old one comes home.
