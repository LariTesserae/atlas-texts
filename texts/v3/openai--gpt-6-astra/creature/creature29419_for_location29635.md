The woman lowering the basket has tied its rope around her ankle. She sits beside the opening in her floor, splitting a reed with her thumbnail. Each time the rope tightens she lets another loop slip from beneath her heel. She does not look down until the basket touches something.

Her name is Eda. She has a broad face, a missing lower tooth, and a pale groove above each hip where her stone belt rests. Out of the water she moves stiffly. One knee will not straighten all the way, and on the mountain paths she has to plant that foot sideways. Below her house, where she can pull herself along a rope and let her legs follow, she is still quick.

She makes the best door bindings in the town. A good binding holds a door closed without preventing it from lifting when a root thickens underneath. Eda’s will accommodate several years of growth. She is less skillful with baskets, which lean toward her working hand and have to be weighted on one side.

The basket at the end of her rope contains a meal for her brother, who is working beneath her. He will eat it when he comes up. Lowering it is her way of telling him he has been down long enough.

He knows this and resents it. He is forty-six.

***

The people of the roofs have no single look that distinguishes them from the people farther down the lake. Children are born with their parents’ brown or black hair, their grandmothers’ prominent ears, the occasional light eyes that send a family searching through its remembered marriages. Visitors expect something stranger. Sometimes they ask whether the children learn to swim before they can walk.

Some do. Some do not. There are children who will launch themselves from a raft before they can speak clearly, and children who must be coaxed down three rungs of a ladder for an entire summer.

The adults watch both kinds.

Small children wear loose harnesses of soft plaited bark. On a busy roof, their lines run to a ring that slides along a crossbeam, allowing them to follow someone from the hearth to the sleeping mats without reaching an edge. The arrangement requires constant attention. Two children can twist their lines together; a third can crawl through the resulting knot. Older siblings spend much of their time lifting lines over heads.

By seven or eight, most children know how to reach a neighboring raft from underneath. They learn where the ladders descend, which roofs have a handhold at the waterline, and how to turn onto their backs if they come up between homes and find no one looking.

The first underwater errand is usually to fetch a stone from a shallow ledge. An adult puts it there, then goes down beside the child to retrieve it. No one pretends the stone is needed.

Later there are real errands. Later still, there is the first time a child returns without completing one and has to say so.

This is praised, though not always convincingly. Adults have their disappointments, and a lost iron knife is a considerable thing.

***

At the surface, the town is less spacious than it looks from below.

There are drying racks, covered fires, bundles of sedge, fish stretched open on splints, children’s harnesses, visiting boats, pots of soil in which onions grow badly. A root raft that has thickened into a good broad platform is seldom left broad for long. Someone builds a storage shelter on it. Someone’s married daughter returns with a husband. A neighbor asks to lay out reeds for a few days and leaves them there until autumn.

People quarrel over sunlight.

They also quarrel over what grows beneath them. A willow feeding one household’s raft may shade another household’s ladder. Its roots may hold together a wall belonging to a third. Cutting it requires discussion, and discussion may continue until the branch in question has become much larger.

There are families who own rooms they cannot enter except through someone else’s rooms. There are inherited doorways whose doors disappeared generations ago. A woman at the eastern end of the town insists that the fish beneath her grandmother’s stairs belong to her. Most people disagree, but they do not fish there when she is watching.

The drowned buildings remain useful enough to keep such claims alive. They shelter breeding fish. Their shaded walls grow food. Their beams provide anchorages that no floating construction could replace. In a few upper rooms, baskets of clay are kept cool beneath weighted lids; in others, bundles of split wood soak until they can be bent.

A house with sound roof timbers and a reliable air chamber is wealth, even if its owner sleeps above it beneath a patched reed mat.

***

There are perhaps a dozen people everyone trusts to inspect a failing root.

Eda is one. Another is a young man called Senn, whose hands are so large that he cannot reach into many of the joints he is asked to examine. He works with a girl who has narrow wrists. She puts her arm into the darkness and tells him, afterward, what she felt.

“Smooth on the left. Soft behind. Something moved.”

“What moved?”

“I took my hand out.”

They go down again.

Senn is patient with plants and impatient with people, a distinction he considers reasonable. He can persuade a root around a corner over two seasons, but cannot wait for someone to finish explaining why they have not cut the sedge he requested.

He has begun keeping short lengths of broken root beneath his sleeping platform. He dries them, cuts them open, and studies the changes inside. His wife dislikes the collection because small things live in it. He says that is part of what he is studying.

The town needs people like him, but it does not always enjoy having them.

It also needs those who cannot dive. Eda’s mother, who has not gone below the surface in eleven years, braids the guide ropes. She works by touch while talking to whoever is passing. Her fingers find a poorly twisted strand before her eyes do. She cuts it out without interrupting the conversation.

One of the strongest swimmers in the town is forbidden to work alone because he repeatedly stays down too long. One of the poorest swimmers remembers every repair made in the western street for thirty years. When those two descend together, each believes he is looking after the other.

***

They eat more than the walls can provide.

The snails are boiled, picked from their shells with thorns, and cooked again with crushed grain. Fish are trapped in narrow entrances fitted between roots. In spring, people take boats to the wet margins of the lake for shoots and eggs; in summer, they cultivate small patches on the mountain slopes. Most households have somewhere above the water where a few beans, turnips, or bitter greens can be grown.

Those patches are reached by paths that leave the knees aching. Eda sends her brother.

Grain comes by boat from settlements beyond the cleft. So do salt, iron, gossip, and spouses who initially sleep badly because the floor shifts beneath them. In exchange, the town sends woven mats, dried fish, fine basket willow, and lengths of root cord that remain supple when wet.

A visiting trader once called them a people without fields.

They remember him because he attempted to trade a cracked pot as sound, and because a child dropped his hat through a floor opening. The remark about fields has mostly been forgotten.

***

Underwater, their faces become less readable.

Hair lifts away from the head. Loose skin at the jaw smooths. A familiar scowl may be only the effort of keeping water out of the nose. People who seem hesitant above can move with startling certainty below; loud people must wait to be touched on the arm.

They speak with their hands, but sparingly. Come here. Hold this. Go up. Trouble.

More elaborate arrangements are settled beforehand. If a branch cannot be freed, leave it. If the wall shifts, move toward the square. If one person drops a tool, the other watches the person, not the tool.

These instructions are repeated even by pairs who have worked together for decades. Sometimes they are repeated angrily.

In the air chambers, speech comes back too close. Two wet heads rise almost cheek to cheek beneath a beam, and a sentence intended as a practical observation sounds like a confidence.

“There’s rot behind it.”

“I know.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“I wanted you to feel it first.”

They stay only long enough to recover. The little darkness warms around their mouths. A hand seeks the toggled rope, and one head disappears, then the other.

***

The white stone belongs to no household.

Children touch it because adults do, then invent explanations among themselves. It keeps the tree upright. It is an egg. It marks the place where the first person drowned. If taken out of its niche, it will become impossibly heavy.

Older people do not agree either. Some remember being told it came from the mountain before the flooding. Others say it was put there much later, by a man whose daughter survived an illness. Eda’s mother says there used to be two stones.

There is no ceremony attached to it that everyone observes. A diver may brush it with two fingers, lay a whole palm against it, or pass without stopping because the cold has entered their hands.

Once, during a repair, Senn removed it to clear the niche. He placed it on a stair and forgot to put it back.

Before evening, three people had come to his roof.

None accused him of anything. Each asked whether he had finished his work.

***

Not everyone stays.

There are young people who go down the lake and discover that they like a floor that does not move, a fire large enough to stand beside, a night without water knocking under the bed. Some return for visits with boots they must remove at every threshold. Some send goods. A few cease to send anything.

Those who remain are not necessarily the ones who love the town most. A person may stay because a parent needs lifting from a sleeping mat, because their marriage is here, because they know the profitable places to set traps, or because leaving has always seemed like something to do next year.

Eda once intended to leave. She had packed an iron knife, two good awls, and a change of clothes. Then her father injured his back. By the time he recovered, the person she meant to travel with had married elsewhere.

She is not secretly waiting. She likes her house. She dislikes several of her neighbors. There are mornings when the smell of the wet reeds makes her happy before she has fully woken.

Her brother comes up through the floor opening, pushing the basket ahead of him.

He has brought back the food untouched and a length of rotten binding. He sets both on the mat, climbs out, and sits with his feet still in the water. For a while his hands shake too much to undo his belt.

Eda puts down the reed she is splitting and loosens it for him.

Neither mentions the basket. He tells her that a dark fish has taken up residence behind the old cupboard, larger than the one that lived there last year. She says it is the same fish. He says she should come and look.

Below them, in the room neither has ever seen dry, the fish returns to the cupboard. It settles with its belly just above the wood. The disturbed snails have begun to emerge again, and a newly secured root rests against the wall, held for now by a knot small enough to fit inside a closed hand.
