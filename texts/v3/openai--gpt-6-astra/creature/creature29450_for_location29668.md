For a long time, the only evidence of the animal is that one opening beneath the canopy does not collect white mineral around its edge.

Crystals fall there as elsewhere. They lodge among the ribbon leaves and sift into the dark. But by the next morning the lip of the opening is clean, and the fine, colorless feeders have gathered a little farther away.

There is sometimes a faint ridge in the soil beside it. Sometimes the ridge is on the other side.

If the foliage is lifted, there appears to be nothing underneath but the usual felt of dead leaves and branching growths. A few pale plates lie embedded in it, fitted closely together. They have the dull surface of buried stone. Pressing one produces no immediate response.

Later, after the foliage has been left alone, the plates separate by the width of a hair.

Something underneath draws in air.

***

It lives at the boundary between the garden and the rock.

Most of its body is spread too thinly to make a recognizable shape. Broad lobes pass beneath roots, narrow at constrictions, and widen again wherever the soil is deep enough. In a mature garden, one animal may extend through several thickets without occupying much more space than a layer of fallen leaves. A person standing above it would be supported mostly by roots and stone.

The pale plates cover only its exposed portions. Beneath the canopy they are small and loosely joined; near a bare patch they become thicker, overlapping like the stone scales left by weathering. Their surfaces incorporate grit. Old plates can carry mineral stains, leaf impressions, even tiny rooted cushions.

There is no permanent front.

Along the animal’s margins are soft, folded structures that serve in different combinations as mouths, feet, and organs of touch. A fold pressed against stone grips it. The same fold, opened beneath a dead leaf, encloses the leaf and slowly removes its organic layers. Against another part of its own body it seals, making a continuous surface.

Its movements are consequently difficult to notice even when it is moving. One margin advances beneath the soil while another withdraws. A passage that took years to occupy may be abandoned in an afternoon. The animal does not have to pull its whole bulk through a narrow space; it transfers itself through, thinning on one side and accumulating on the other.

There are limits to this. Its oldest tissues lie in several dense knots, joined by tough cords buried among the roots. These cannot flatten indefinitely. When a knot must pass a constriction, the animal may spend days loosening the soil, moving grains individually, taking away a little of a dead root.

The narrow passages under the canopy are not all made by grazers.

***

It eats what the garden has finished with.

Fresh leaves usually repel it. Their sealing fluids stiffen against its feeding surfaces, and it draws away before the contact becomes costly. Fallen leaves are another matter. So are the abandoned skins of grazers, failed seeds, spent flowers, and the fine branching organisms that grow through old soil.

It does not consume these indiscriminately. Some pale filaments remain intact inside its body folds, cultivated in chambers that open and close as it moves. They soften the resistant layers of leaves. In return, the animal brings them food and keeps their surroundings more stable than the open soil.

A scrap of foliage may pass through several chambers over many days. What remains is a delicate mineral framework, still shaped like part of a leaf. This is carried outward and deposited beneath the garden’s edge.

Around a very old resident, these remains form a buried apron. Roots grow through it. The garden advances a little more easily there.

Its tissues contain the same general family of mobile fluids that the plants draw from the cooling rock, but it cannot simply drink from the seams. Much of what it needs has to pass through other living things first. A basin can be rich in mineral seepage and still be useless to it. It needs accumulated litter, mature roots, a sheltered underside.

It arrives late in the history of a garden.

There are gardens that never acquire one.

***

The animal knows the plateau chiefly as pressure, temperature, and the arrival of substances.

Light reaches it through scattered sensitive patches, enough to distinguish a disturbed canopy from ordinary shadow. It has little use for a picture of the sky. More important is the moment a root begins to contract in evening cold, or the change in tension along a stem when something heavier than crystals has settled on it.

Touch travels well through the closely packed ground.

A grazer feeding several body-lengths away makes a repeated pattern: grip, scrape, stillness; grip, scrape, stillness. The animal gradually withdraws its soft margins from the grazer’s route. A root penetrating the soil produces almost no movement at all, but produces it persistently. The animal yields around it.

The thin predators among the leaves are harder to distinguish. Their stillness resembles the stillness of everything else. Sometimes one strikes a feeding fold near the surface. The fold closes, sheds its outer tissue, and leaves the predator holding something that thickens rapidly in the air.

This defense is expensive. A resident repeatedly injured in one part of a garden will stop feeding there. Over years, the avoided area may become dense with undecomposed leaves.

Thus even a motionless predator can alter the soil beneath an old plant-ring.

The animal is most active when mineral weather has recently passed. Its upper openings admit the cold, crystal-bearing air, and combs of fine tissue sort the fall. Some grains are carried into storage pockets. Others are expelled through separate slits.

This is why its openings remain clean.

It begins sorting before the plants finish folding their leaves. Beneath a whitened canopy, dozens of little apertures work in darkness, each producing a minute, intermittent current. A loose ribbon of dead foliage may tremble over one of them although the garden above is still.

***

In a stable basin, it can remain for a very long time.

New soil accumulates over its back. Roots thicken, die, and leave tunnels around which its body has already adapted. Its plates are replaced unevenly. Some stay buried for decades; others are shed each season near an opening where temperature changes are severe.

It retains small habits.

One individual carries its waste to three particular margins, although a fourth would be nearer. Another opens its air slits early and repeatedly suffers damage from the last heat of the day. One tolerates grazers passing over it; another closes their tunnels with compacted leaf remains, so that each season they must find a different route.

Such differences are not obvious during a visit. They become apparent only through long observation of the same few square yards.

There is no convenient moment at which its youth ends. It grows where growth is possible, loses portions where conditions deteriorate, and renews the cords between its dense internal knots. A large individual may become smaller for many years, then spread again when a new stand of foliage shades the ground.

Sometimes two parts lose contact.

If each has a knot and enough stored material, both can survive. Their severed margins seal. They continue using the same passages, and at first behave much as the single animal did. Later, one may retreat while the other prospers. They may meet again without joining.

But this is not how most young begin.

***

After certain weather fronts, when the shaded flowers have opened together, the animal raises narrow folds into the lower foliage.

They are easily mistaken for new shoots. Their tips bear no leaves, only a film that holds scent close to its surface. Small flower-feeding animals brush against them, picking up pale reproductive grains and carrying them through the thicket.

The folds remain extended for a few hours. By daylight they have collapsed.

Young develop in shallow chambers beneath the adult’s plates. At first they are compact, each with one hard center and a translucent skirt tightly tucked underneath. They feed on prepared scraps. The adult shifts them away from cold margins and toward portions of the garden where litter is accumulating.

Most remain nearby after release. They occupy spaces too narrow or too poor to interest the parent. Many disappear there.

A few grow a different arrangement of plates: thicker on the upper surface, fewer at the margins, capable of closing over the entire soft body.

These are the ones that may leave.

***

On open stone, the traveler looks almost nothing like the animal beneath the garden.

It is a low, closed object, perhaps as broad as a hand, perhaps much larger in a well-fed individual. The plates meet along irregular seams. Only underneath do the folded margins emerge, working against the rock in a slow sequence of grips.

It carries no visible foliage. A little dark soil is sealed inside its upper chambers, together with living filaments and unfinished food.

It leaves during mineral weather.

The first crossing may be only from one sheltered stone to another. It stops before the bright air clears, draws every soft surface inside, and anchors itself. Its underside fits the small irregularities of its resting place. From above, it is a rounded block in a hollow.

It may remain there through several fronts.

Travel depends on reserves, shade, the temperature of the ground, and traces borne on the descending air. The bitter compounds of a distant garden can sometimes be detected, but the currents are unreliable. A traveler may follow a promising direction for months and reach a bare basin beyond which the scent has already dispersed.

It cannot see the green on the opposite escarpment.

Many die with their plates still closed. Wind polishes them. Their chambers empty through minute cracks, and the plates eventually settle into a shallow scatter that resembles a patch of unusually thin shale.

Others reach vegetation too young to sustain them. These wait at its edge, spending themselves slowly while the plants put down roots.

The rare successful arrival enters without ceremony. It opens beneath a cushion, consumes a dead leaf, and begins to loosen the soil. For a while it remains able to close itself again. Then new growth extends beyond the protection of its traveling plates.

After that, departure becomes much harder.

***

At evening, the resident spreads toward the cooling surface.

Soft margins rise into the spaces beneath fallen leaves. The upper plates relax. Air moves through the cleaned openings, carrying the fading scents of flowers and the first cold from the pale flats.

When the stone begins to click, the animal pauses.

Each contraction arrives sharply through its underside. Nearby folds close; a feeding chamber seals around its scrap of leaf. Then, if no further disturbance follows, they open again.

Across a large individual, these responses pass unevenly. One part is already feeding while another still holds itself tight against the ground. Farther beneath the roots, young are being shifted into a warmer chamber.

Above them, the foliage rustles. The next white bank climbs the escarpment, and the first scattered crystals find their way down.
