The people who live here have not yet acquired a common way of walking.

Some still turn sideways too early when they meet someone in a lane. Others carry themselves as if there were room beside them for a companion, and knock their knuckles against the pipes. The children are already better at the place. They know which stair treads permit a full foot and which require the foot turned lengthwise. They can carry a dish around the painted hump without looking down. Their elders watch this competence with relief and, sometimes, an obscure resentment.

Most arrived with more possessions than their rooms could hold. There are cupboards in which the doors have been removed to recover the space those doors would need to open. A long dining table was cut into three short tables; its carved apron continues from one household into another. Things that could not be sold or altered went upstairs again, carried out by the same people who had carried them in.

A few things remain absurdly intact. In the thin building, on the second landing, a woman keeps a hatbox large enough to obstruct her only chair. The hat inside has a brim of faded artificial fruit. She has worn it once since coming here. On that occasion she put it on at the top of the stairs.

Her name is Sella. She repairs the laundry’s sheets and makes alterations to work clothes. At first she took in the work at her own door, but people waiting on the landing prevented her neighbor from entering his bedroom, so now she receives it beside the shoe-drying cabinet.

She knows the neighborhood through its clothes. She knows who has lost weight, who sleeps in a shirt worn all day, whose work requires kneeling, whose cloth at the neck has been washed until it no longer holds much water. The heat has spoiled several of her old methods. A lining that would once have made a jacket hang properly makes it unwearable here. She has learned to leave gaps under the arms, to move a seam away from the place a tray rests against the hip.

She has not become an austere person. She adds unnecessary pockets when asked, argues about the merits of different cuffs, and keeps a length of red braid in a paper sleeve. A young man who sorts ceramic rejects paid her to sew some of it inside his collar, where hardly anyone could see it. She charged him very little, then regretted doing so because he could perfectly well have paid the full price.

Her room is directly above one of the conduit’s elbows. At night she folds her mattress against the wall and sleeps on the floorboards near the door. She leaves the hatbox on the chair.

Across the lane lives a man called Iven, who attends the pumps for part of each day. He was employed to do something else when he came—installing the meters, perhaps; accounts differ, including his own—but the pumps became his work because he kept returning to look at them.

He is not especially strong. His forearms are narrow and he has a fleshy, troubled face. He dislikes being watched while he makes an adjustment. When someone stands over him, he stops and explains the adjustment in such exhaustive detail that the person usually leaves.

He listens poorly with one ear. Consequently he is less impressed than other people by the quietness of the machinery. He lays two fingers on a housing, watches the float through the glass, rests the end of a screwdriver against a pipe. At his request, a little strip of tin has been fixed beside each pump handle. The running pump makes its strip tremble.

These strips were not in the original plans. Neither were the marks showing where the float stood on certain mornings, nor the hook for his clean rag, nor the board placed over a cracked paving slab that pinched the heel of his shoe. His presence has accumulated around the machinery in small amendments.

He has a daughter who visits from above on the seventh day. She brings oranges, although they are expensive and he has repeatedly told her not to. They eat them with their elbows on their knees. He saves the peel until it goes stiff, liking the smell in his room. His daughter thinks the room is unbearable. Each visit, she suggests that he come up and stay with her; each visit, he hears a slightly different reason why she believes he cannot manage.

He walks her to the eastern stair and stops there. Afterward, for a while, he is rude to anyone who asks him about the water.

There are children in six of the households. During working hours they collect in whatever space is least useful to adults that day.

For a week this was the stair leading into solid earth. They sat on its lower treads, their backs against the clay, until someone noticed the sealant rubbing off on their shirts. Now it is the landing outside the room where insulating sheets are stacked. They must move whenever a load comes through. Their games have developed short rounds.

They play at changeover, passing some small object among themselves without letting a watching child see whose hand receives it. They play at the upper city, though several remember it well enough to dispute the others’ inventions. In that game there are enormous rooms, needless journeys, and water used once and thrown away. One child always insists on having a horse.

The oldest girl, Tem, has a book with pictures of household pests. She brought it down because she liked the plates, especially a blue-black beetle shown much larger than life. The book tells her where to look for things she has never found here. She has lifted drain covers and inspected the undersides of sacks. She once left a saucer of sweetened meal behind the laundry for three nights.

Nothing came to it.

An adult told her this was a blessing. Another told her the excavation was too new. Tem has kept both explanations without finding them satisfactory. She draws the beetle on scraps from the paper works, adding joints until it could fold itself into the narrowest spaces. She does not show these drawings to the younger children, who make its legs too thick when they copy it.

Her little brother is responsible for wetting the three pots beneath the main. He regards the plants as unreliable animals. Sometimes they seem to drink; sometimes water passes straight through them. A translucent leaf appears without warning and becomes, over several days, like all the others. He prefers duties with a visible conclusion. Nevertheless, he remembers the pots when the adults forget.

The flat brown growth belongs to a woman who works at the press. She brought it wrapped around a damp piece of wood and would not allow it to be packed with the furniture.

No one is certain what she gets from it. She says it improves the air. Someone else says a shaving of it can be put in broth. She has never been seen to take a shaving. Each morning she lifts it with both hands, checks the board, and sets it down again with the same slight rotation. Its underside is pale and finely ridged.

She is otherwise careless with her possessions. Her cups have broken handles; the hem of her coat hangs loose. This exact tenderness toward the brown thing embarrasses her when it is noticed. She waits for the court to empty before tending it, but the court almost never empties, and eventually she must do it in front of whoever is there.

Not everybody here is good at sharing.

One household has fitted a private catchment beneath a roof channel that formerly fed a common trough. The owner says the water comes off his own roof. His neighbors point out that the roof was paid for as part of the lane works. For several evenings people gathered beneath the disputed spout, explaining the same measurements to one another.

The matter is still unresolved. The owner’s wife continues to bring trays from the laundry past the people who argued with him. They make room for her. Sometimes they make rather more room than necessary.

There is also a man who washes too much, a woman who will not wash enough, and someone who keeps hanging a bag from the forbidden brackets. The bag is removed and left on the stairs. It reappears. Nobody has yet caught its owner in the act.

The enamel signs are obeyed most faithfully by a former loading clerk with painful knees. He does not work for the municipality, but he owns a notebook and has begun recording obstructions. He has been useful on two occasions and intolerable on many more. When his knee swelled so badly that he could not descend from his room, people carried meals up to him. He noted that one of the trays had been left against an expansion wall.

They told him exactly what they thought of that. The meals continued.

The neighborhood contains people who expect to leave as soon as they have saved enough, and people who have already stopped saying so. It contains a couple who speak affectionately only when separated by a wall. Their voices travel so well that half the lane knows the names they use for each other. Face to face, they discuss receipts.

A young kiln worker has begun courting Sella’s red-collared customer. They meet near the unfinished chamber, where there is nowhere to sit and little reason for anyone to linger. Their conversations concern the work at first, then people known to one of them, then increasingly small matters that take increasingly long to explain. They have had one quarrel about whether a remark was meant seriously. For two days afterward, the western platform seemed unusually empty.

Some residents sleep as soon as their shifts end. Others delay going to bed because their rooms are hotter than the lanes. Late at night there are figures sitting under bulbs with nothing in their hands. They are not necessarily lonely. A person may have come outside to escape someone they love.

At sunset, when the air begins to descend the stairs, the first people to feel it make adjustments without announcing them. A stool turns. A shirt is pulled away from a chest. Someone moves a tray off an empty seat.

Sella sometimes brings her mending to the eastern stair. Iven sits farther down, where he can still see the pump court. The children put their bare feet on the cooler edges of the steps until they are told to leave passage. For a few minutes conversation becomes easier. News from above is repeated; somebody describes a meal; someone else complains that the cool air used to reach farther, although no one can establish when this was.

Then the small current weakens. The people nearest the bottom remain seated a little longer, hoping for another.

One evening Tem brings out a paper beetle. She has cut and folded it so that the legs stand away from the body. Set on a tread, it is almost too light to stay where she puts it.

Sella shifts her foot to protect it from someone passing. Iven bends forward to inspect the joints. In the last of the descending air, one leg moves.

For the rest of the evening the beetle occupies that step, and everyone steps over it.
