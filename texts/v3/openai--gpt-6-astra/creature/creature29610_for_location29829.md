The creature beneath the doorway plant comes out again when you sit down.

At first you see only its front limbs: two pale, many-jointed instruments that test the threshold without putting weight on it. Each ends in a little fan of dark teeth. One fan presses against the stone. The other remains raised.

It waits through three changes in the sound.

Then the rest of it slides from beneath the leaves, considerably more animal than the small plant appeared capable of hiding. Its body has been folded into the gap between a root and the doorframe. Unfolded, it is about as long as your hand, low and broad, with six limbs and overlapping plates the color of dust on glass. The plates do not quite touch. In their seams something dark contracts and loosens.

It carries a flake of green material beneath its body.

At the edge of the shelf, it stops. Its forelimbs stretch upward, find purchase, and draw it onto the ledge. There it puts down the flake and begins scraping the pale growth from its rim.

So the shelf is a feeding place. Or has become one.

The creature eats with an opening you cannot see, somewhere behind its foremost pair of limbs. Little shivers pass backward beneath the plates. It pauses whenever you move your feet.

Only when it turns sideways do you notice that one of its rear limbs is shorter than the other. The end is blunt and polished. It has arranged its gait around the injury so completely that the missing length is apparent only at rest.

There are grains embedded in its back, mostly white, with one rust-red chip near the middle. They stay fixed while loose dust blows away.

You watch it until a much larger version reaches through the doorway and takes it off the shelf.

The movement is quick, but not violent. A long pale limb curves around the little animal, which closes all six legs over its meal. For an instant it resembles a clasped hand. Then both disappear around the doorframe.

Something outside begins scratching at the wall.

---

The larger inhabitant has been on the roof since before you arrived.

You can see it from the bridge if you lean out, though recognizing the whole of it takes effort. It lies in a scallop above the doorway, its limbs extending along separate ribs of stone. What you took for a pale border around the shelter is the edge of its back.

It is perhaps four feet across when spread out. Most of that width is limb.

The central body is shaped like a shallow dish turned upside down, covered in the same translucent plates as the small crawler. Toward the middle, the plates are thick and clouded; at the edges they diminish into flexible scales. Beneath them runs a fine branching darkness. Occasionally a branch brightens blue, then goes dark again.

Its six limbs are unequal in ways that seem permanent. The rear pair are short, immensely broad at the joints, and fastened deep under the roof lip. The middle pair can flatten into straps. The front pair are slender and end in those toothed fans, each tooth capable of moving separately.

There is no obvious head until it lifts one edge of its body.

Under that edge are two black hollows surrounded by fringes. The fringes open toward you.

You feel a faint tapping through the parapet.

It does not turn to follow your face. When you move your hand without touching anything, it makes no response. When your boot shifts, both hollows angle toward the bridge.

You have been conspicuous to it since you stepped onto the mountain.

The little crawler is attached beneath its raised front edge, still holding the green flake. There are two others there, smaller and almost clear. Their backs contain only a few embedded grains. They grip narrow ridges on the adult’s underside, tucked where the plates overlap.

The injured one tries to descend.

A forelimb intercepts it without interrupting whatever work the other forelimb is doing inside a crack.

---

The work consists of removing things and putting most of them back.

The adult draws a length of silver root from the stone, runs it through its teeth, and returns it to a slightly different position. It extracts three mineral chips, rejects two, and presses the third beneath a lifting scale on its own shoulder. A strand of dead foliage is rolled between the tips of its forelimbs until it splits into fine ribbons.

Some ribbons are eaten. Others go into the crack.

Over the next hour, the crack acquires a pale rim.

There are rims like it all around the shelter. Old ones are almost indistinguishable from the rock. A few have broken open, exposing layers as thin and regular as the inside of the dry leaves.

The animal is growing something there.

Not quickly. Often several minutes pass during which it holds one fragment perfectly still, waiting for a particular pressure in the stone. Then its limbs tighten. The fragment seats itself with a click you feel more clearly than hear.

Once, a root tip flashes blue against its teeth. The light runs up the forelimb and spreads briefly beneath its back.

It keeps hold.

The garden supplies its materials in inconvenient forms: dust moving too fast to catch, charge arriving in fierce little surges, leaves that may remain alive for years after breaking free. The inhabitant takes each when it can. There is food stored beneath the roof, but also sorted piles of unfinished pieces, some weighted with stones, some trapped behind taut rootlets.

One pile includes a splinter of dull ceramic.

Another includes the wing cases of the broad hunter you saw crossing the shaft.

The creature visits these stores in no fixed order. It seems to remember a need halfway through another task. At times it simply stops, flattens itself, and lets the great sound pass through its body.

During these pauses the young crawl over its underside. One finds a seam and works its mouth against it. The adult bends a middle limb around all three.

---

There are others.

Across the gap, a patch of pale cliff shifts down by the width of a finger. Far below, something opens six limbs around a ceramic plate and lifts its central body clear of the stone.

They are widely spaced. Each occupies a recess with a different arrangement of roots, polished ribs, and hanging growth. The larger recesses contain more than one adult. In a deep pocket below the bridge, you count seven, fitted together so closely that you cannot tell which limbs belong to which body.

Their movements sometimes travel along the wall in sequence.

One lifts its front edge. Another closes its fringes. A third strikes the stone twice with a middle limb. The action passes out of sight below a shelf, then returns several seconds later from the opposite side of the shaft.

Your shelter’s resident answers.

Its reply is not loud. Amid the hammering, you would never hear it. But your palm is against the wall, and for a moment there is a small, unmistakably separate pattern beneath your hand.

The young stop moving.

The adult repeats the pattern. The injured crawler answers with a scrape.

It tries again, more slowly.

This continues for some time.

---

Later, the wind falters.

It does not stop, but one of the sustained notes thins away, leaving the garden’s other sounds exposed and badly balanced. A bank of fans beneath the far ledge droops. The long green ribbon twists around itself.

The resident immediately leaves the roof.

Its body rises higher than you would have thought possible. The middle limbs shorten by folding along their many joints; the rear limbs release in turn. It runs sideways down the wall with the young clamped underneath.

For the first time, it looks heavy.

It reaches a ceramic patch half buried in root felt and braces across it. Both forelimbs vanish into the silver mass. Roots pull taut. One breaks, releasing a brief spray of blue sparks.

The animal removes something no larger than a seed.

Nothing changes.

It presses the toothed fans flat against the exposed ceramic. Its body begins to tremble. The movement is small at first, then so rapid that the outlines of its plates blur. The young squeeze farther inward.

Across the shaft, another adult has taken the same posture.

A crack sounds from the depths.

The drooping fans open halfway. A pressure strikes the underside of the bridge, and dust lifts from every gap between the tiles.

The resident holds its position until the missing note returns.

Then it releases the patch and climbs slowly back to the roof. Along one forelimb, several teeth have turned chalk-white.

It spends the rest of the afternoon breaking those teeth off, one at a time.

The young eat the fragments.

---

You might call it a keeper, watching that repair. Yet it neglects a split leaf that batters itself to pieces nearby, and makes no attempt to save a net-hunter torn from its hooks. When the little animal with the shortened leg catches a six-winged grazer, the adult takes it away and eats it.

Later it gives the youngster something from beneath its plates.

Its care has boundaries you have not learned.

So does its ownership. You discover this when you put your glove on the shelf.

The resident comes down into the doorway. It extends a forelimb and touches the glove at several points, delicately separating two fingers. Its fringes open almost against the fabric.

Then it pushes the glove onto the floor.

You retrieve it.

It climbs onto the shelf and settles where the glove was, compressing its limbs around its body. From beneath one edge, the injured crawler emerges carrying another green flake.

The adult rests while the little one eats.

Up close, the grains embedded in its back are not scattered at random. They occupy old cracks and worn places. Some are sunk so deeply that new translucent layers have grown across them. Others remain sharp. Near the center is a piece of black material with a curved, manufactured edge, much like the socket on the bridge.

Its back is a history of things that held.

One of the seats has shallow grooves across its upper surface. The resident fits two limbs into them while keeping the others on the shelf and wall. The posture leaves its underside sheltered and its sensing fringes directed toward the doorway.

The second seat has the same grooves.

You shift your weight carefully.

This time, before your boot settles, the animal taps the shelf. A brief pattern. A pause. The same pattern again.

You remain still, unsure what is being asked.

Beneath its body, the little crawler taps back.
