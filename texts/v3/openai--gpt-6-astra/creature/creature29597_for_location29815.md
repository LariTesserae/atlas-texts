At first you take it for a portion of the path that has failed to settle.

A strip of crosshatched surface remains raised after the surrounding ground has finished moving. It is about as long as your forearm, blunt at one end, divided at the other into several overlapping tongues. The tongues flatten one after another. A small crease travels forward beneath the strip, and the whole thing advances by the width of a finger.

Then it stops.

The ground ahead of it dimples. Something fine and dark passes from beneath its leading edge into the dimple. For a while neither moves. You hear the faint rasp of fibers being drawn through a narrow opening.

When the strip lifts again, there is no hole underneath. There is only a patch of fresh, close-grained tissue, already darkening in the heat.

You begin to find others.

They are easiest to see beside old seams, where their grain runs across the grain of the plateau. Most lie so flush that even the exhaust light does not catch an edge. A few have raised themselves into low arches. Beneath these, in the red light from a distant valve, you can see a body folded into many shallow pleats.

The polished upper surface is a covering. It consists of thin, unequal plates, each overlapping the next far enough to allow the animal to lengthen without exposing what lies between. The plates are almost black at their centers. Along the edges they are translucent brown, like resin spread thin over a blade.

An old one carries repairs. Pale staples cross a split in its back. One plate has been replaced by three smaller ones. A root has grown through another, and the animal has gradually worn a channel around it, leaving the severed loop embedded in its covering.

There is no consistent outline. In a broad passage an individual spreads into something like a flat, many-jointed leaf. At a junction it narrows, feeding its width forward through its own folds. In the smallest openings it travels edge-on. What looked like the thickness of its shell proves to be several layers of body waiting to unfold.

Its limits become apparent only when it turns. Then the trailing parts gather, the overlapping tongues separate, and you glimpse a soft red line deep inside.

It closes that line quickly.

***

They live where one moving part must allow for another.

A shaft cannot fill its bearing completely. A pressure chamber needs room for its wall to thicken. Two expanding trunks, however closely fitted, cannot exchange their loads without a small interval of unequal strain. These allowances are continuously created and continuously reclaimed.

The animals follow them.

Their undersides carry rows of short, hollow bristles. Each bristle can press independently against the surface below. Hundreds of these contacts let an animal read the ground: heat, current, the direction in which a sheet of fiber is about to pull. On a quiet stretch it may remain motionless for hours, with only a few bristles changing position. When a load transfers, it can move suddenly, flowing into the space released by the first fraction of the movement.

It is already inside before you see the seam open.

The animals have no room to be careless. A passage may close by growth rather than by the return of a movable wall. A promising cavity may turn out to be a compression bed preparing for its next stroke. Their flattened remains occur in the old layers, though it takes practice to distinguish them from the machinery: a small fan of plates, a row of bristle sockets, an outline interrupted by roots.

The living test constantly.

Those divided tongues at the rear are useful here. They remain in contact with the passage behind while the front explores ahead. An animal can feel a retreat becoming unsafe before it has committed the rest of its body. Sometimes you see one stretched between two seams, almost impossibly thin, its middle suspended over hot bark while both ends search.

It may hold that position through several strokes of the works.

***

The thread you saw entering the ground was its mouth.

There are several, folded beneath the forward plates. They can spread into fine brushes or bind together into a stiff probe. With these the animal feeds on the narrow margins of unfinished growth: wax before it hardens, cells scheduled for removal, the nutrient-rich lining of a socket that has not yet accepted its seed.

It also eats buds.

Not the fully connected ones, which the surrounding tissue guards and supplies, but buds stranded by a change in pressure or left pointing into a space that no longer exists. The animal rests over them and lowers its brushes. When it leaves, the buds have been emptied without being torn free.

This is why the ground sometimes grows smooth beneath a feeding animal. A little local crowding has been relieved. The remaining tissue takes up the difference.

Elsewhere its feeding causes trouble. A probe enters too deeply. A useful lining is scraped away. A socket receives its seed and fails to wake it.

The surrounding growth responds by tightening.

Along the front edge of the animal are tiny glands that release something with the smell of hot varnish. Applied sparingly, this secretion makes living bark hesitate before closing. It resembles one of the substances passed between neighboring machine organs when they need space to move.

An animal can request a little clearance.

It cannot hold that clearance indefinitely. The secretion is expensive, and old, heavily loaded tissue responds poorly. You sometimes find a dark outline on a seam where an animal has spent everything it had. Its covering remains outside. The rest has been taken into the joint.

For days afterward, smaller individuals gather around the outline and feed.

***

They do not seem to have eyes. Light falling on them produces no obvious response. A hand held above one does.

The plates nearest your palm lift slightly. The bristles beneath that part of the body withdraw. You are cooler than the ground, and you interrupt the flow of heat.

If you keep still, it may begin moving toward you.

The movement is slow enough to be mistaken for accommodation in the path. One edge reaches your boot. It tests the sole, finds no living reply, and works a thin tongue into the tread. Other parts follow. Soon it is spread over your toe, examining each recess.

Through the leather you feel a series of small, firm pressures.

It is searching for a seam that will yield.

You can dislodge it by lifting your foot, but not by shaking. When unsupported, it folds around whatever contacts remain. Its grip grows stronger as its body becomes smaller. You have to bring it close to the ground and wait until the forward bristles find a surface whose movements make sense.

Then it leaves you with surprising haste.

A faint glossy print remains on the boot. Fine dust adheres to it for the rest of the journey.

***

Their busiest time is the interval of complete quiet before a stroke.

As the machinery pauses, animals emerge along the load-bearing routes. Some come out of cracks too narrow to admit their visible coverings. Others detach from surfaces you have been looking at for minutes.

They spread themselves flat.

Across the plateau, thousands of little bodies establish contact with as much ground as they can reach. Their plates loosen. The spaces between them expose threads of dull red.

The first tension from below travels through their bristles.

Then they move.

For a few seconds the worn paths are alive with crossings. Large individuals slide down the flanks of rising ridges. Smaller ones run in the temporary furrows beside them. Animals that have waited on opposite sides of a sealed junction pass through it together when it opens. Several may overlap, their plates slipping across one another with a dry clicking too faint to hear from standing height.

They have felt where the load is going. They travel through the allowances it leaves behind.

Most disappear before the vents have finished opening.

A few remain on the surface afterward. They lie unusually thick and still. Beneath their coverings, something continues to contract long after the ground has quieted.

These are carrying young.

***

The young develop between the inner folds, each in a separate shallow pocket. They begin as pale discs with a dark, hard rim. Before they can move, their bristles press through the pocket wall and rest against the underside of the parent.

They learn the strokes there.

A parent carrying a nearly mature brood spends a long time at junctions. It samples one surface, then another, sometimes returning through the same passage many times. Eventually it settles where several loads meet but do not arrive together.

When the right sequence passes through the ground, the parent unfolds.

The young leave one at a time, each following a different loosening seam. Newly exposed, they are almost transparent. You can see their mouths curled beneath them and the little reservoirs of oil that sustain their first movements.

Their plates darken within minutes.

The parent remains spread after the last has gone. Some recover their shape and leave at the next stroke. Others cannot bring all their folds together again. Growth enters beneath them, attaches to the worn inner surfaces, and slowly raises their coverings into the surrounding bark.

On the oldest paths there are patches of crosshatching finer than the rest.

***

After several days, you recognize one individual by a crescent missing from its rear edge.

You find it first beside a tower, then much later near the sealed loading mouths. Its route seems to resemble yours. It favors the broad, polished load lines. It avoids new growth. At difficult junctions it waits.

Once, when the path ahead closes, it leaves the ground and climbs a buttress. Halfway up, it stops beside an unmarked strip of bark. Its front edge works slowly beneath the surface.

A slit opens.

You cannot follow. The opening is barely wide enough for two fingers. But a little farther along the buttress, the pressure released by that slit makes a larger passage withdraw into its housings.

By the time you reach it, the animal is gone.

You see the crescent again near the plateau’s edge. The animal lies with its front half extended over the drop, bristles spread into the rising air. There is no surface within reach below it.

For a long time it tests the darkness.

Then a pulse arrives through the foundation columns. The edge inclines. Far underneath, something broad moves close enough to send a stronger current of heat up the face.

The animal draws its rear tongues together.

Its grip releases in order, from back to front, and it slips over the edge with the same small, deliberate motion it uses to enter a seam.

You wait for a sound.

Behind you, the place where it rested begins to close.
