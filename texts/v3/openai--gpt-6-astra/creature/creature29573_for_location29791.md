The larger animals are easiest to find by looking for leaves that face away from the sun.

There will be five or six in a grove, belonging to unrelated plants, their broad surfaces turned toward the same apparently empty place. Beneath them the ground is warmer by a small but unmistakable amount. Fallen scales lie with their bright edges inward.

At first the animal appears to be a pale stem seen through several layers of foliage. Then one notices that the same stem is visible through a gap farther to the right, bent at a different angle. Neither view is a reflection. Moving sideways does not bring them together.

The animal stands between them.

It is about the height of a person kneeling, although height is not its most reliable dimension. Its body is narrow and deep, the color of a peeled almond, with a greenish translucence along the lower edge. Four limbs are usually visible. There may be others behind the body, or the visible ones may join it more than once. They end in small, blunt pads held just clear of the stone.

Its back bears a low ridge of overlapping plates. These are not armor. Where one lifts, the underside is crowded with fine white folds, delicate as the gills of a mushroom. Light entering them does not emerge beneath the animal. Instead, a nearby leaf brightens along its veins.

The head is carried low.

There is something almost grazing in its posture, but it never lowers its mouth to a plant. Indeed, no mouth can be found. The front of the head divides into two soft, flattened lobes, each lined on its inner surface with black pinpoints. When these lobes spread, the leaves around it turn a little farther.

For a while nothing else happens.

Then a gap appears in one leaf: a narrow, clean crescent, without torn fibers or an exposed edge. The two sides of the crescent are still connected if traced with a fingertip. Seen from above, however, a piece is plainly missing.

A matching green curve becomes visible inside the animal’s chest.

This is as close as feeding comes to being recognizable.

It takes very little from any one plant. Afterward, the leaf slowly resumes its ordinary direction, and the animal remains among the stems with its head lowered, accommodating what it has taken. Its internal folds change their spacing. Sometimes a small clear note occupies the air beside its shoulder.

Other animals of its kind approach these notes.

They do not necessarily approach the body producing them.

Two may stand twenty yards apart, screened from one another by dense growth, while their heads incline toward adjacent points in an open patch of air. Between them, the overlapping scales of the ground cover lift slightly. Something passes there too slowly to be seen in transit. One animal becomes more opaque along its flanks; the other acquires a new white plate.

When they separate, the note remains for a time.

The little amber creatures gather at its edges. They stand on stems with all six feet suspended above the surface, their enclosed white points aligned toward the sound. Occasionally one disappears from its perch and is found inside the translucent flank of a larger animal.

It has not been swallowed. The stem on which it rests is also visible there, and beyond the stem a minute, sharply focused portion of the grove.

The larger animal carries this view until the amber creature leaves.

There are usually several such openings in an adult: a dark frond, a strip of pale ground, the underside of a leaf that may be growing a considerable distance away. Most are no larger than a thumbnail. One old-looking individual—though nothing about its tissues is worn—has an oval of blue darkness beneath its ribs. Through it can be seen one of the western shelves, from below.

The openings are not fixed. They close when the animal is alarmed.

Alarm itself is difficult to recognize. There is no startle, no gathering of limbs. The animal becomes abruptly easier to understand. Its outline resolves; all four feet occupy sensible positions beneath it; the head joins the neck at an ordinary angle. For a moment it is merely a strange pale quadruped standing in dry vegetation.

Then it is gone.

The plants around its former position remain turned toward it.

***

They shelter beneath the mineral shelves, though not necessarily beneath the nearest shelf.

In the narrow blue spaces are places where several lie together, their limbs folded close and their dorsal plates slightly raised. The distance between resting bodies is remarkably regular. A young one can fit into an interval that appears far too small; an adult leaves a space beside itself that looks occupied even when the stone is plainly visible through it.

They rest with their heads facing outward toward the undersides of distant leaves.

Here their color changes. The cream of the body deepens into pink, and fine lines appear across the skin, joining plate to plate. A sleeping animal resembles a piece of the ground that has been persuaded to hold itself upright.

But they are not made from the stone, at least not in any simple sense. A mineral seam passing beneath a sleeper continues, slightly displaced, across its flank. When the animal departs, the line in the ground is sometimes straighter than before.

Along these resting places are small white structures resembling tightly folded hands.

They are the young.

Each has four blunt lobes gathered around a green interior. No head is evident. The outer surface is soft enough to retain the impression of a falling scale, yet the mineral beneath it is often split into fresh, hair-fine divisions.

Some remain like this wherever they are found. Others can also be encountered standing among the plants, scarcely larger than a hare, with their first dorsal plates open to the light.

The folded young does not vanish when the walking young appears.

A dark speck in one plate, or a peculiar fork in the green interior, makes it possible to recognize the same individual in both places. Later, the mark can sometimes be found on an adult. There is no visible strain between these bodies, no cord or passage. The adult may travel miles from the shelf.

Yet it is careful near certain cracks.

It will step over an ordinary seam without hesitation, but stop before another that seems identical, lowering its divided head until the black points nearly touch the stone. Beneath that crack, perhaps, its smallest body is still folded.

It waits there with a patience unlike its usual stillness.

The leaves immediately around it cease turning toward the light.

***

The white winged animals hunt them.

What hangs above the fronds is only the most conspicuous part of the hunter. The open wings are thin and faintly ribbed, without feathers. Between them is a short body tapering to a downward-pointing tip. There is no visible head.

Directly beneath it, the spaces between plants become deeper.

A fallen leaf may be seen well below the surface on which it lies. A low rosette appears at the bottom of a narrow green shaft. The mineral remains level, but reaching toward it takes longer than the movement should require.

A pale grazer caught beneath one of these hunters begins to lose its remote views.

The little windows in its flanks go blank. Its plates flatten. The green crescent in its chest becomes briefly vivid, as though held up to sunlight, and then disappears.

Sometimes the grazer escapes by becoming ordinary and leaving.

Sometimes it remains.

There is no struggle at the end. The four feet touch the stone. This is so slight a change that it may be missed, but afterward the animal never moves again.

Its body stays beautiful. Light still passes through the thin edges of its ears—or the lobes that serve where ears might have been. A faint rose color persists beneath the plates. The white hunter lowers by an inch or two.

Elsewhere, under a shelf, a folded young loses its green interior.

Nothing decays. In time the dead body becomes difficult to distinguish from the mineral backs dividing the fields. Plants establish themselves along its joins. Their roots enter without breaking it.

The other grazers do not rest there.

***

Among the living there are differences that seem to be preferences.

One returns to black-stemmed groves, taking its small crescents from the pistachio leaves. Another feeds almost entirely among the low scales and carries a crowded silver-green texture beneath its skin. Some tolerate amber passengers. Others close every opening when the little creatures approach.

A few are curious.

They follow a visitor at an oblique distance, appearing in gaps ahead without ever overtaking. When the visitor stops, they stop too, although a pale limb may remain visible in the place they have just left.

Eventually one may come close enough to examine an outstretched hand.

The divided head opens. Its inner black points are not eyes, but under their attention every crease in the palm becomes sharply apparent. The animal smells faintly of crushed green stems. No breath touches the skin.

It seems particularly interested in the pulse.

As the beat rises beneath the wrist, a white fold opens along its back. At the next beat, another opens. The animal holds very still, attempting something.

For a moment there are two faint impressions of the hand, one where it is and one just beside it.

Then the second impression fails.

The plates close in an uneven sequence. The animal withdraws its head, not frightened, but with the careful motion of a creature that has touched an unfamiliar edge.

It may follow for the rest of the crossing.

Behind it, the leaves turn back one by one. Ahead, under a distant shelf, its folded young rests in a crack in the immaculate stone, holding within itself a small green darkness that has never yet been opened.

When the adult pauses, the darkness stirs.
