At first it is one of the places where the sediment settles differently.

A patch of bottom remains smooth while the ground around it gathers little ripples. Fine shell grains arrive there and stop. When the current strengthens, they move again, but not quite in the direction the rest of the sand is moving. They travel sideways toward a crease too shallow to cast a shadow.

The crease closes over them.

Most of the animal lies underneath. What shows is a broad, soft back, gray as the suspended clay, with a few darker patches that could be exposed gravel. It is about the length of a seal, though there is nothing seal-like in its outline. Its margins spread thinly into the mud. Silt covers some of them; others fold beneath the body. Even in clear water it would be difficult to say exactly where it ended.

It has no upper shell. A hard covering would give the current an edge to lift. Instead, small plates lie buried in its skin, overlapping loosely, allowing the back to bend without wrinkling enough to catch the flow. Between them are pores that close when touched by sand. The plates are most closely packed around an old injury: a pale, puckered area where part of the margin is missing.

There is a front, but it is usually hidden.

Under one end of the body, a short muscular lobe works through the loose surface layer. Its underside bears paired rows of soft knobs, each surrounded by a fringe of finer tissue. It tests the mud a little ahead, then to either side. Sometimes it pauses over a place that looks no different from any other. Sometimes it draws back sharply, and the whole animal contracts by a fraction.

There are worms beneath this plain. Small crustaceans move between grains. Eggs adhere to fragments of shell; dead tissue arrives mixed with the blackened weed and the mineral dust. The lobe finds these things. It can separate a soft body from the material around it with movements small enough to leave the upper surface of the mud intact.

Larger pieces are passed into the crease beneath its front edge. Much of the mud goes in with them. Later, along the sides, little ropes of cleaned sediment emerge and slump apart.

It feeds almost continuously when the water is gentle.

In stronger flow it draws in the lobe and settles. Water escapes from beneath its body through grooves that remain open after the rest of the underside has pressed against the bed. A fringe of blunt hooks catches in the firmer mud below. They are not long enough to reach rock, and in the loosest deposits they hold nothing. Then the animal goes with the sediment.

This happens often enough that its body is shaped to survive it.

Lifted clear, it folds its margins inward. The broad patch of ground becomes a thick, uneven bundle, turning slowly in water full of grit. It does not swim. Small contractions keep the feeding lobe protected while sand scours across its back. When it touches bottom, one margin opens. If there is purchase, the others follow. If not, it closes again.

It may land several hundred yards from where it fed. It may come down within reach of the shallow impression it has just left.

For a while after landing, it does nothing visible.

Underneath, the soft knobs press downward in sequence. The hooks extend and withdraw. It is finding out whether the bed will bear it, whether water is moving through the mud, whether something larger is working below. Only after this does the front edge loosen and the feeding lobe emerge.

There are no eyes to uncover. Along its margins, beneath the protective overhang of the back, run several rows of translucent beads. Each contains a few dark grains suspended in fluid. As the animal tilts, the grains shift. Other organs register pressure, salt, the faint chemical differences between water that has crossed bare clay and water that has passed through a colony of burrowing animals.

A nearby brittle star lifting an arm produces a disturbance it can detect. So does a bank beginning to slide.

The first response to danger is usually a tighter grip. This serves it well against fish and badly against collapsing ground. Old individuals bear torn margins, embedded pebbles, sections of back where the plates have grown together crookedly. One has a broken sponge spicule lodged so deeply in its skin that the tissue has sealed around it, leaving a rigid white point. Another has lost most of one side and moves with an almost imperceptible turn.

They are not numerous. A long passage across the basin might cross several without revealing any.

Occasionally two meet beneath the loose silt. Their margins touch and remain touching. The feeding lobes continue to work, but more slowly, exploring the narrow strip of ground between them. After a time one shifts, and the other follows far enough for their undersides to overlap.

What remains afterward is a small cluster of dull capsules pressed into firm clay. Each is coated with grains selected from the surrounding sediment. They look like the rounded droppings of a burrowing worm.

Most are scoured away.

The young that survive emerge already broad and thin, small enough to shelter beneath a valve of broken shell. For a long time they feed there, reaching only as far as the sheltered mud. Their backs are almost transparent. A dark speck entering the mouth can be followed inward until it joins the other dark specks in the gut.

Eventually the shell shifts, or the animal grows beyond it.

Near the center of the basin, where the ground seems level, an adult is moving. It advances by fastening part of its underside and drawing the rest forward. The movement is slow enough for silt to settle on it as it goes. Behind it, a shallow track fills almost immediately.

A current clears the water for a few seconds.

The pale ground appears. Across it lies a slightly darker patch, with shell grains gathered along one edge. Beneath that edge the feeding lobe extends, touches something, and carefully turns it over.
