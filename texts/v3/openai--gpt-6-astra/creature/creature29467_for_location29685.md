The oldest grazer has no fur along the middle of its back.

There is a pale ridge there, polished by years of passing beneath the same low projection. At either end the fur remains thick. Water gathers in it, and the small flat animals come to drink, working their heads between the hairs. The grazer allows this until their weight becomes troublesome. Then it lowers its body against the wall and waits for them to step off.

It is not much larger than the others. Its age shows chiefly in the care with which it places its feet. Before transferring its weight, it presses each hoof against the stone and makes a small twisting movement. Sometimes it tests a foothold several times, although nothing about the foothold has changed.

Its hooves are divided into four narrow leaves. Between them is a soft, dark pad that never quite touches the rock. The pad senses the vibration carried through the hard outer parts. In sleep, the animal folds three legs beneath itself and keeps the fourth extended, its hoof resting against a vein of crystal.

Most of the grazers sleep this way.

Their young sleep with a foot against a parent.

There are two young in the split at present. Both belong to the animal whose ledge is nearest the warm pool, though other adults feed them. A young grazer approaches from either end, searching with its bristles until it finds the row of small openings along an adult’s flank. It feeds for a short time. The adult’s vessels darken visibly beneath the blue skin.

Afterward, both animals remain still.

Feeding a young one costs more than movement. An adult that has nursed may spend the rest of the day with its mouth against a damp seam, taking in whatever passes there. The youngest flat animals gather around its lips. Some are swallowed. Others crawl into the folds beside the mouth and remain there, their translucent backs rising and falling.

The grazers do not appear to distinguish these from the film they eat. Yet when one of the larger flat creatures crosses a feeding place, the mouth closes until it has passed.

The flat creatures are not all the same.

Those on the upper walls have broad, cloudy plates and a little pigment around their eyes. Those farther down are almost clear. In the deepest chamber there are individuals whose bodies can be found only by the displacement of water beneath them. Their violet channels are fine as the cracks in old glaze.

Some have six limbs. Some have six limbs and, beneath the rear plate, the beginnings of another pair.

These larger ones seldom leave the newly opened fractures.

They hold their heads inside the stone for so long that smaller animals settle on their backs and begin to feed there. Their front plates gradually separate. A soft collar becomes visible between head and body. It swells, rests, and swells again.

Eventually one of these animals goes into a crack that seems much too narrow for it.

The plates remain outside.

They lie in order, still joined along one edge, like something carefully taken off. For a while the discarded back retains its shape. Water collects beneath it. Then feet break it down, or a grazer removes it with a single sideways movement of its mouth.

What enters the mountain is longer than the animal that stood outside.

It has been folded inside the plates.

There are traces of these long bodies in the crystal bridges. A cloudy filament may run through a clear strand, branching where the strand branches. Viewed from another angle, it disappears. Sometimes a dark bead travels along it so slowly that its movement becomes apparent only after several hours.

The crystal around such a filament is usually unbroken.

Even at a snapped end, the cloudy line does not necessarily end with it. A wet thread may extend a little way into the air, withdraw, and extend again. Flat animals gather beneath these places. They raise their blunt heads. Occasionally the thread touches one, leaving a gray spot that the animal carries away.

The film on the walls begins in such spots.

It grows best where there is moisture, but moisture alone does not account for it. There are equally wet surfaces where nothing grows, and dry fractures from which a faint gray bloom continues to emerge. The grazers can tell these places apart before the bloom is visible.

The oldest grazer tends one at the base of the sounding wall.

It clears the small animals from the opening with its bristles, gently enough that they retreat without tightening. Then it presses the soft side of its mouth against the stone. A little fluid passes from its lips into the crack.

On these days it does not nurse the young.

Something within the opening draws the fluid inward. There is no sucking sound. A bright meniscus simply recedes beyond sight.

The grazer waits after it has finished. Its bristles remain spread against the wall, each touching a different point.

When the low sound resumes, it leaves.

***

The hanging animals are easier to recognize as separate bodies when one dies.

The colony loosens around it. Small hands release their holds in succession, and the body passes downward through the cluster. It may take most of a day to reach the bottom. The last animal holds it by one limb for a long time.

Then it falls.

Alone on the floor, it looks unfinished. The fur conceals a narrow chest and an abdomen almost entirely occupied by thin, folded sacs. Its limbs are long enough to encircle two or three neighbors. There are broad gripping surfaces on its wrists as well as its hands.

Its eyes are small and functional. They are usually buried in someone else’s fur.

A solitary animal cannot make the great breathing sound. Its chest is too slight. The pressure that opens its sacs comes partly from the bodies around it: each contraction squeezes a neighbor, and each expansion gives another room. The breath moves through the colony before it moves into the chamber.

An animal separated alive will crawl toward any warm, yielding surface. Against a grazer’s belly it can breathe for a while.

The grazers tolerate this too.

They carry strays to the places where the lowest clusters can be reached. There the little animal extends its arms. Several hands reach down, although no eyes have turned toward it.

The dead ones are eaten slowly, beginning at the wrists.

Their fur remains in the hollows. It is unusually resistant to decay. Deep in the oldest resting place, beneath the loose material that smells of mushrooms, the hairs have been pressed into a hard, dark felt. Hooves polish its surface. When wet, it resembles smooth stone.

Some hollows have this lining; others are worn directly into the rock. The worn ones occur on small blocks that no longer match the walls around them. They have been carried from ledge to ledge.

A grazer can move a surprisingly heavy block if it has several days.

***

There are inhabitants that have never entered the chambers.

A fresh fracture occasionally exposes one.

At first it appears as a narrow, wet band within the stone, much thinner than a root. There is no cavity around it. Its surface follows the grains of the mineral so closely that it seems to have grown with them. On exposure to air, it contracts.

The contraction is transmitted inward. A darkening runs away through the translucent crystals, branching into the mountain.

Nearby animals become still.

If water reaches the exposed band soon enough, a membrane forms over it. The flat creatures press themselves around its edges. Within hours there may be several layers of them, their overlapping backs making a low blister on the wall. Their violet fluid pulses rapidly.

Beneath them, the membrane thickens.

If the band dries first, a section of the wall falls silent. Drops still strike it, but the returning murmur is absent.

The grazers stop bringing stones to that section.

No one could readily say how large an animal has been injured. The exposed band may belong to something no longer than a finger, or to something whose farthest parts lie below the cloud-covered ridges. Branches separate and join within the rock. There are places where two pulses meet and continue as one.

The mountain has opened through this life, leaving portions of it on either wall.

Some connections still span the gap.

***

The bare places are visited rarely.

Near the warm pool, a grazer will sometimes approach the empty shelf and lower its head until the bristles touch the accumulated dust. It does not lick or feed. After a while it backs away.

The young do this less carefully. One steps onto the shelf, withdraws its foot, and stands with the leg folded high against its body. There is nothing visible on the hoof.

For several breaths the hanging colonies remain quiet.

Then the young grazer puts its foot down elsewhere.

Far within the wall, a pulse passes through the wet, branching bands. At the broken ends of crystals, threads extend into the air. Most reach nothing. A few touch the opposite stone and adhere.

The new connections are soft at first. Droplets run along them without making a note.

Animals gather underneath. They feed, rest, and lay their small pale young against the places where the threads enter the wall. Mineral slowly clouds the living strands.

Much later, when a hoof shifts on a distant ledge, one of them sounds.
