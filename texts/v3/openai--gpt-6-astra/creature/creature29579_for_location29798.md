The people of the Lower Mill wear their pockets on the inside.

An outside pocket fills with chaff. It also offers shelter, and a worker who leaves a coat hanging through two shifts may have to choose between reclaiming it and waiting for something to hatch. Their clothes fasten closely at wrist and ankle. Most are the color of the floor, not by preference but because the mill’s fine dust survives washing. At the throat, where the cloth turns inward, you sometimes see blue.

There are nineteen permanent residents at present. This number includes a woman who spends most nights in the orchard and excludes a boy from above who has been sleeping beside the sorting chamber for almost a year. The disagreement is about his apprenticeship, not his welcome. He has a bowl and a place for his shoes.

They are ordinary people, though a visitor may take some time to establish this. They look past faces toward moving hands. They stand close without speaking. When listening to a machine, they lean against its housing with the fleshy part of the forearm, keeping their fingers clear. Several have a thickened patch of skin below the elbow.

Their hands are not uniformly delicate or beautiful. The woman who tends the press has a thumb that will no longer straighten. The youngest sorter bites his nails until they bleed and has to work with the fingertips of his gloves tied shut. An elderly man who can feel a damaged bearing through two walls cannot reliably thread a needle. He leaves his mending outside his daughter’s curtain and pretends afterward that he has forgotten whose work it is.

They sleep through the heavy knocking. What wakes them is a clean, unaccompanied click.

***

The animals most often mistaken for part of the inhabitants’ clothing are called hems.

A hem is a low, six-legged animal about as long as a hand, with overlapping brown plates on its back and a soft underside that grips without sticking. Its head can withdraw so far beneath the first plate that it appears to have no head at all. Two pale feelers extend through notches at the front. The feelers move separately, testing air and surface.

Hems occupy the boundary between a thing and whatever rests upon it. There are hems under baskets, under gate weights when the gates are raised, under the rims of bowls. They follow a lifted crate to its new position. A person sitting on the floor will acquire one or two beneath the fold of a trouser leg.

They eat the white shoots.

At night, or during the portion of the work cycle that the residents call night, hems spread across the square. They do not crop a shoot at the base. They cup it in the mouth and draw it out slowly, taking the tender growing point without opening a wound large enough to bleed sap. A feeding hem rocks slightly backward, braced on its four hind legs.

This is the small movement you see all along the freight lanes once you know to look.

The mill families protect them without quite considering them domestic. A broom used on the open floor has a gap cut through the middle of its bristles so a hem can pass beneath. Freight gates descend slowly for their last finger’s breadth. Children learn to lift a resting hem by sliding a thin piece of bark under it; pulling one off by its shell can leave its underside attached to the floor.

They are not affectionate. They will settle against a warm foot, but they settle just as readily against a recently stopped motor. The residents nevertheless recognize individuals. One large female, identifiable by a white split in her rear plate, has occupied the press-house threshold for eleven years. People step over her even when she is elsewhere.

Her young spend their first weeks beneath her plates. At a certain size they make the edges of her body look badly fitted. She stops entering narrow spaces then, and becomes irritable when anything touches her back.

The children call her the Landlady. The woman who actually assigns the lofts dislikes this.

***

Below the hems are the seam animals.

Their bodies are pale, flexible, and difficult to count. A mouth may belong to a creature no larger than a grain of oats or to a length of flesh following a root joint for several feet. What appear to be antennae are sometimes legs held up into the air. The residents distinguish them by the marks they leave around a drop of water: small radiating tracks, a clean central pit, a damp crescent with one dry place inside it.

They have names for these marks more precise than their names for the animals.

Most seam animals feed on fungi, loose cells, and each other. A few work their mouths against living root tissue. These are tolerated until their feeding begins to enlarge a joint. Then someone paints a narrow line of sour paste across the floor, and for two or three days that section of the square looks unusually solid.

The creature that follows the translucent centipede is different. It lives beneath the upper layer of roots and presses itself upward to trap prey against the floor.

Usually only a traveling rise is visible. Sometimes, where a joint opens, you can glimpse the surface of its back: dull ivory, finely wrinkled, with short black bristles lying flat in opposed rows. It moves equally well in either direction. No one below has seen an entire adult.

The millwright keeps a drawing of one in a ledger. The drawing has been extended across three pages and corrected in several inks. One end remains speculative.

It is useful, until it takes a hem.

Then the millwright lays a length of warm tubing across the floor and waits with a hooked knife. He does not kill the creature. He makes the floor unpleasant to hunt beneath, and it withdraws toward the unused rooms.

For several shifts afterward, small animals gather in astonishing numbers along the square’s edges.

***

The beetle keepers occupy the loft above the sorting chamber. Their work begins before the rollers turn.

They inspect a sample of each sorting strain in shallow dishes. A beetle’s judgment depends on its jaws, and a colony can misgrade an entire morning’s fiber if too many adults are nearing their molt. The keepers offer measured strips, watch where they are carried, and exchange trays between chambers until the work can begin.

An excellent beetle is not rewarded. It would have no use for a reward beyond the conditions that allow it to remain an excellent beetle. The keepers attend closely to warmth, crowding, and the age of the food fungus. They speak of a colony as being unsettled, overfull, or ready, and can argue for hours about which it is.

The small lizards belong to no keeper.

They have flattened heads and toes that spread around the tray rims. Their throats flash yellow during disputes, a surprisingly vivid color in the green light. They know the resin bands and stop short of them, except for newly hatched lizards, which have to learn. A shallow box beneath each bearing catches the ones that fall.

One keeper empties these boxes into a nursery recess. Another considers this a waste of time. The recess is always occupied.

During ventilation the lizards retreat behind the trays, gripping with their tails wound around their hind feet. Afterward they come out dusty and begin licking their eyes.

***

The human children spend much of their time at the edges of adult work.

There are few places below where a child can be left entirely unattended, but many where they can sit within reach of someone’s heel. Small seats have been grown into workshop walls. Their hollows are polished, and some have been raised or cut away as machinery changed. One now faces directly into a cupboard.

Before they can read, children can recognize the gate signal and distinguish a note tube from a drain. They are taught not to put a finger into anything that seems to be waiting.

They collect objects that the mill cannot digest or incorporate easily: glass marbles, glazed fragments, a steel nut brought down in a freight basket. These stay themselves for years. A child may bury a marble beside a sleeping place and find it again after the recess has shifted sideways and its old entrance has grown shut.

The few toys made below are ingenious and short-lived. A jointed animal carved from fresh cork may begin to tighten at the knees. Little carts warp. Dolls develop a soft patch beneath their clothes. There is a particular kind of beetle that eats the paste used to attach hair.

The adults check the toys during the weekly stoppage, when they are checking everything else.

***

The oldest resident has a loft beside the stair.

She no longer works a full shift, but people bring her things: a piece of discolored bearing tissue, a moth caught inside a sealed conduit, a curl shaved from a door that has swollen when the other doors have not. She lays these on a square of dark cloth and examines them near a lamp brought from above.

Sometimes she can identify the trouble. Sometimes she sends the object back with a note saying to leave it alone.

She has lived through three failures of the main ventilation and remembers the removal of a tree whose roots had entered the press crookedly. She also remembers arrangements no one else bothers to record: where a family used to keep its shoes, which wall was once dry enough to store flour against, the sound of the old bell before the doorway sealed.

Asked what answers from inside, she says she has never opened it.

Her own room contains a bed, a stool, several stoppered jars, and a picture of a harbor. The picture has been taken out of its frame because something nested behind it. She has never seen the sea and does not particularly wish to. She likes the bare stone in the foreground, and the great distance between the boats.

When ventilation begins, she fastens the picture flat beneath a board.

Then she sits with her feet lifted while the hems come under her stool. The air freshens. Behind the curtain, loose possessions stir in their places. She waits for the four stems to finish closing, feeling the last uneven gust against her ankles.

If one closes late, she is already reaching for her shoes.
