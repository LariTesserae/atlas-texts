At first, the thing beside the doorway appears to be another repair.

It is pale, blunt at one end, and pressed so closely against the wall that mineral glaze seems to continue over it. A wedge driven under a failing lintel might look like this. Then its lower edge loosens. Four small fingers uncurl from a groove, and the wedge draws itself through the door.

There are others. Once you have seen the first, some of the irregularities along the terraces become difficult to account for as masonry.

They are smaller than their houses suggest. The low doors admit them readily, although an inhabitant carrying something must usually go sideways. Their bodies are broad through the shoulders and narrow below, with a flattened back covered by overlapping plates. These are not quite shells. At the edges they bend; where they have rubbed against stone for years, they become opaque and thick. A young one is almost as translucent as the animal under the bridge. An old one has the color of a tooth long buried in clay.

They have four limbs that can serve as either hands or feet. The forward pair are finer. Each ends in three short gripping fingers and one longer finger, soft along its underside, which is generally kept folded out of harm’s way.

Their faces are on the undersides of their heads.

This makes them appear, when resting, to have no faces at all. To eat, or examine a small object closely, they lift the front of the body. There is a narrow mouth, a pair of dark points set far apart, and between them a pale field of skin that contracts at a touch. They recognize light, but do not see much by it. An inhabitant crossing a bridge in darkness is scarcely inconvenienced. One crossing a bridge whose handrail has recently been repaired proceeds with great caution.

The soft finger goes first.

It follows the rail, enters a notch, comes back, and tries again. The other hands remain planted. Only when the unfamiliar surface has been thoroughly felt does the body follow.

They are not invariably slow. A loose flake falling from a roof will send them under cover so quickly that the lane seems to have emptied before the flake lands. But most of their movements are economical and interrupted by long rests. In this cold, a meal lasts them a considerable time. So does an injury.

A missing finger is a serious thing here.

Several of the oldest inhabitants have fingers sheathed in little fitted caps, made from the same dark stone used in the houses. The caps protect what remains, but cannot feel. Their wearers greet one another with the opposite hand.

***

The polished recesses are places to wait for someone.

An inhabitant settles beside one with a hand resting in the hollow. Anyone passing touches the exposed fingers. Most exchanges take only a moment: recognition, a question, a refusal or assent. Longer conversations require the participants to fit their hands together.

The motions are minute. A finger presses twice against a joint. A thumb is enclosed and released. One speaker draws a line across the other’s palm, then returns to a particular point on it.

Their language distinguishes many kinds of pressure.

There is the pressure of asking, which leaves room for the other hand to withdraw, and the pressure of reminding, which does not. There are things said with the backs of the fingers because saying them against the palm would be too familiar. An impatient inhabitant will sometimes answer before the question has been fully shaped, and the two must unclasp, settle themselves, and begin again.

They can quarrel almost without moving.

From across a lane, an argument may look like two neighbors sharing a comfortable rest. Only the tightly braced hind feet betray it.

The arrangements beside the doors are less expressive. They concern absences, possessions, work to be done. Three dark grains on a sill mean that the household has food to spare, but not enough to send someone around with it. The pale chip in its fitted groove means that someone inside wishes not to be disturbed.

There are qualifications too small to notice from the crack above. Whether a grain rests on its flat or rounded side matters. So does the end of the chip that points toward the door.

Sometimes a child reverses one.

This is considered funny at an age when very little else is.

***

There are thirty-one inhabitants, though not all would agree on the count.

Two very young ones still spend most of their time fastened beneath the plates of an adult’s back. One old inhabitant has not left an upper room for so long that several children know the room as a place rather than a household. Food disappears from a shallow dish inside its entrance. The dish is returned clean.

The occupied houses are scattered through the town. Empty rooms do not particularly trouble them. A room can remain empty for several lifetimes and still belong to someone’s family. There are doorways no one uses but no one would block, and a ladder maintained because it leads to a shelf on which something important was once kept.

They do not remember what.

They remember that the ladder is to be maintained.

Elsewhere they are quite practical. One abandoned house has been dismantled from within until only its street wall remains. Behind the respectable doorway is a store of graded grit.

Most food arrives in the water.

They stretch short lengths of fine filament where a current narrows, then comb the collected matter into shell dishes. What looks, at this scale, like clear water carries fragments from a world they cannot enter: softened pollen, the broken substance of leaves, minute bodies, particles whose origins have been lost in their passage through the seam.

The catch is sorted by touch and mouth. Some is eaten immediately. Some is pressed into thin cakes and kept on the highest shelves.

A good cake is dull grey and bends before breaking. A poor one shines with too much mineral grit.

They have preferences. One household takes nearly everything bitter. Another is notorious for offering visitors food so thoroughly washed that there is hardly anything left to eat.

The animal beneath the bridge gathers from the same current, but it is not livestock. It cannot be made to stay in a chosen place, and attempts to handle it generally cost a limb, either its own or its handler’s.

They leave it alone.

When it sheds its casing, however, someone retrieves the casing before the water carries it away. The thin joints can be separated and used as scoops. Its finer hairs are useful for cleaning the grooves in message stones.

There are old scoops in houses whose owners have never seen another animal under another bridge.

***

Their backs collect the town.

Minerals lodge between the plates. Fine grit becomes embedded along the shoulders. After many years, an inhabitant begins to resemble the particular wall against which it prefers to rest.

This is inconvenient rather than mystical. The deposits have to be removed.

Households groom one another with shell scrapers, working carefully around the flexible seams. It is intimate work and often tedious. Children wriggle. Adults put it off. An inhabitant who lives alone may visit a neighbor ostensibly to discuss a damaged roof and eventually turn its back.

There are people who are sought out because they are gentle, and people who are sought out because they are quick.

One of the thirty-one is neither, but tells very good stories through the hand it is not using to scrape.

Their stories contain more routes than faces. A person is remembered by where they went, what they carried, which hand they offered at a meeting. To follow an old story properly, listeners sometimes shift their grips so that a turn can be made in the right direction.

The world above occurs chiefly as weight.

Footsteps pass through the buried stone with a force quite unlike the water’s continuous noise. The inhabitants feel them in the whole body. They stop working, not necessarily from fear. There is no useful action to take.

Some footsteps recur often enough to become familiar.

No one knows what makes them.

***

The little roof under the falling drop shelters the place where their young are first put down.

They are born soft, folded tightly, with the plates of the back still joined by a clear skin. For a while they are carried. Then the plates must separate, and the limbs must open fully.

Wet paving makes this difficult. The new skin adheres to it. A limb pulled free too hastily can remain crooked.

So there is the dry patch.

It is not warm. It has never been warm. But the roof keeps the falling water off it, and a shallow groove around its edge intercepts the damp creeping inward. The stone beneath the shelter is slightly raised, though centuries of use have made the rise almost impossible to see.

When a young one is ready, several adults come down together.

One cleans the paving. Another waits at the nearest causeway to stop anyone carrying a wet load through the passage. The adult bearing the young crouches under the cupped roof and loosens it from its back.

There is room for the young one and very little else.

Above them, the drop strikes. The roof shudders on its four legs. Water spreads over its rounded edges and descends on every side.

The adults cannot clasp hands beneath it. They wait without speaking.

Slowly, on the dry stone, the folded creature extends a limb. Then another. Its back separates into plates that have no grit in them, no glaze, no worn places. It rests between efforts.

Eventually the long, soft finger opens.

An adult puts one hand beneath the edge of the roof. The finger encounters it, withdraws, and reaches again.

They remain like that for some time. There is no hurry to carry it home.
