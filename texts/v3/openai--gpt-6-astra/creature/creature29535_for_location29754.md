The keeper is under the crown that opens first.

At this hour it is difficult to distinguish them from the growth. They have drawn a broad leaf over their shoulders and hooked its edge beneath one knee. Their back rests against a trunk; their feet are braced on opposite sides of a seam. Only their hands are exposed. One holds a cord. The other is inside a split in the bark, feeling something that moves there.

They are waiting for it to move again.

Orange powder gathers along their ankles. A few grains climb the cord.

The keeper withdraws their hand, bringing out a small, blunt-headed animal clenched around one finger. They turn it over, examine the pale joints beneath its body, and put it into a neighboring fork. It immediately begins climbing back. Before it reaches the split, the keeper closes the bark with a loop of cord and pushes a wedge under the knot.

Then they let go of the leaf.

The updraft takes it so abruptly that, for an instant, the keeper seems to unfold an enormous golden wing. But the leaf belongs to the tree. It rises above them, and they crouch underneath, narrow and dark and plainly separate.

They have four limbs, though their proportions are unfamiliar. The thighs are short, the lower legs long, the feet almost as long as the forearms. Each foot ends in three broad gripping digits and a smaller one turned inward. On flat ground they walk on the outer edges of their soles. In the thickets they use the whole foot, wrapping it around branches or laying it open against the undersides of leaves.

Their hands are finer. Each has two opposed pairs of fingers, with a short hard spur at the wrist. The left hand is missing the end of one finger. Wax has accumulated in the old break, darkened by years of work.

They are not large. Standing upright, they could pass beneath most of the broad leaves without stooping. They seldom stand that way outside the shelter. Their habitual posture is low, knees apart, one hand available for the ground.

A garment of overlapping leaf skins covers their trunk and hips. It is held close by cords rather than allowed to hang. The seams face backward. Several of the patches are lighter than the rest, and one has been fitted poorly: its corner flutters whenever the keeper turns left. They catch it absently and tuck it down. Later it comes loose again.

Beneath the garment their body is covered with small, close plates. These are not armor in any substantial sense. A stone edge can damage them. They keep the fine material inside from escaping.

Along the throat and beneath the arms, the plates diminish into folds as soft as the partitions within a leaf. Warm dust passes through those folds. When the keeper strains, faint yellow lines appear under the skin and slowly fade. Their elbows make a dry sound when bent too far.

Their face has a shallow central ridge, a mouth below it, and two deep-set eyes protected by clear inner lids. Above and beside the eyes are rows of delicate pits. Each pit holds a short filament. Some stand straight out; others bend toward the skin. Even when the keeper’s face is still, these filaments are busy.

The mouth is broad but not expressive in a familiar way. Its edges lock together. Behind them are grinding surfaces that have worn unevenly, so the keeper chews on the right.

They eat the yellow meal stored in the shelter, mixed with wax and the soft interior of certain pods. They take it in small pinches. After eating, they may sit for a long time with their palms against the warm bench, distributing what they have swallowed through slow contractions of the body. A little dust escapes from vents behind the jaw. They rub these clean with a strip of leaf skin.

There is no thirst in them. There are other discomforts: meal too coarse to pass easily through the narrow tissues; wax that has hardened in the cold of an exposed limb; the unpleasant lightness that comes after a wound has exhaled too much.

The keeper has known all three. A dense scar runs from beneath one arm to the hip. The plates there are small and irregular, grown around the outline of an old binding.

They favor that side when the mountain exhales.

***

There is a second inhabitant in the shelter.

It occupies a basket under the warmer bench and is small enough to be mistaken for one of the animals carried inside the seed cases. Its body is folded close, its gripping feet disproportionately large. It has the same paired fingers as the keeper, though it has not yet learned to oppose them reliably. When it wants something, all four close at once.

The basket contains husk fibers, wax crumbs, three smooth stones, and a wooden ring that must once have belonged to a tool. The small one rearranges these constantly. Sometimes it builds a low wall against the basket’s mouth. Sometimes it pushes everything out and calls until the keeper puts it back.

Its call is a thin, sustained vibration. In the garden’s noise it travels hardly farther than the doorway. Through the stone bench it can be felt outside.

The keeper does not answer every time.

They found the small one in a chamber exposed when an old root lifted out of its seam. It was gripping the chamber wall beside a dead kernel, still surrounded by the fibers in which it had descended. There was no record of where the seed case had come from. It might have circled the hollow for half a day or arrived over the rim from somewhere below the clouds.

The keeper brought it inside, warmed it against their abdomen, and fed it a little wax softened in the mouth.

That was some time ago. It now climbs onto the bench without help. It has also learned to wedge itself behind the meal pots, a place from which the keeper cannot extract it without moving everything on the shelf.

Its throat still has the swelling used by the small seed-borne animals to force themselves out of their cases. In some creatures this becomes a calling sac. In others it shrinks. In this one it is slowly dividing into the folded passages visible along the keeper’s neck.

There are chambers in the oldest trunks large enough for a body of the keeper’s size. Most have been empty for longer than the present cords have existed. One contains a broken wrist spur. Another has finger marks worn into the wood around its entrance.

The keeper sometimes cleans that entrance with the end of a hooked pole. They do not use it for storage.

***

Much of their work consists of arriving before a small event becomes a large one.

A creeper turns into a wind tunnel. The keeper draws it aside while it is still supple. A colony of bark feeders hollows a limb close to its attachment. The keeper listens through the wrist spur, then cuts away a portion that would otherwise fall onto the inflated plants below.

They leave most damaged things alone.

The split branch braced with bone carries two nests on its sheltered side. It also rubs a neighboring trunk in the evening. The keeper has tried three different wedges in the gap. Each changed the note but failed to stop it. The current wedge lies on the ground nearby, abandoned. The booming continues.

They dislike the chalk-colored seed eaters.

This does not prevent them from lifting one out of a fissure when it gets stuck. They do so with the hooked pole, keeping well clear of its forelimbs. Once freed, the animal strikes the hook hard enough to chip it. The keeper jerks the pole away and knocks its handle twice against the ground. For several days afterward they drive that particular animal away from the shelter, although it looks much like all the others.

They have preferences that serve no obvious purpose. They save unusually dark pieces of rootwood. They favor the shrill callers with striped throats over the plain ones, tolerating a striped colony directly above the shelter slot while removing nests elsewhere. They keep a pale, curved scrap on the ledge behind the tools. It came over the rim. Its edges have been polished by handling.

Sometimes they carry it up the steps.

At the notch, they lie flat and extend their face beyond the stone. Their filaments stream backward. Grit clicks against the clear lids of their eyes. They remain there through several changes of wind, holding the scrap in a closed hand.

Then they return to the hollow.

***

Tonight the small one comes out during the flowering.

It has heard the baskets stirring beneath the bench and followed a rolling husk as far as the doorway. Now it stands with its feet spread on the sill. Its garment is a single piece of leaf skin with holes for the limbs. The keeper has tied a cord through its back, but the other end lies loose inside the shelter.

The crown above the keeper is fully open. Its pale branches strain upward. Animals run along them, casting brief, intricate shadows through the settling pollen.

Something is wrong with the nearest flower. One of its long divisions remains folded, caught beneath the cord the keeper has just tightened.

They climb to it. Dust streams past their face. The climbing animal they removed from the bark has reached their shoulder and is trying to get under the garment.

Below, the small one steps off the sill.

The keeper’s head turns before there is any sound.

For a moment they hold their position, one foot around the trunk, the damaged hand gripping a leaf joint. Their free hand reaches toward the trapped flower. The small one lowers itself onto all fours. Orange grains tremble beneath its belly.

The keeper drops.

They land badly on the scarred side, slide across the rock, and catch the small one by a foot as the current rises. Its limbs open. The leaf garment swells around it. The keeper pulls it against their chest and lies over it while the loose cord lashes the ground.

Above them, the trapped division tears free without their help. It goes upward among the pollen, turning end over end.

They stay down until the heavier debris begins to fall.

Then the keeper carries the small one inside. There is a new split in the skin at their elbow. They press wax into it, working with their teeth because the small one will not release their other hand.

For a while they sit together on the bench.

The little body is vibrating. The keeper lays two fingers against its throat, lightly, until the vibration slows. Outside, seed shells knock along the stone. Something large comes down in the southern thicket with a prolonged crash, followed by a tumult of offended calls.

The keeper looks toward the doorway.

They wait a little longer before going to see.
