A child is first taught to put a hand on the floor.

The lesson takes place in a room with a drain running through its center. Someone holds the child against a warm chest while another person walks away, stops, and comes back. The child feels the steps through the heel of the hand. Later there are two people walking, then someone carrying water, then someone who drags one foot. The child learns to distinguish the weight of a person from the weight they are carrying.

Only after this comes the guide cable.

The people of the plateau have hands broad enough to cover their own faces. Each finger ends in a blunt nail that grows around the tip, protecting it without covering the sensitive pad beneath. Between the fingers, the skin folds deeply. Spread flat, a hand can feel the faint pressure wave of an animal moving through water a short distance away.

Their feet are less sensitive. They are working surfaces, thickened by grit and the constant softening of rain. In old people the soles separate into hard islands divided by deep seams, and these seams must be cleaned. A neglected cut can shelter a colony that digests living skin almost as slowly as it digests stone. Someone may walk on an infection for years before it reaches a tendon.

Grooming takes much of an evening.

In a station household, people sit with their feet in one another’s laps. They use hooked scrapers, warm brine, and a resin whose smell clings to the rooms. Small children pass from lap to lap. Arguments continue through the work. To be angry enough to refuse someone’s foot is a serious condition, usually noticed by everyone before it is acknowledged by either party.

Their heads incline forward from powerful necks. Along the jaw are shallow pits covered by a membrane finer than the skin around them. These register changes in air pressure, including those made by a hand passing close to the face. In still rooms a person can feel another person turn away.

Outdoors, the pits close.

The rain is too much information. So are crowded wings, the exhaust from an air tool, and the gust that comes ahead of a herd entering a narrow hollow. People traveling in bad weather bind a strip of porous cloth beneath the jaw. Without it, a long journey leaves them nauseated and uncertain of distances.

They retain eyes. These lie under a continuous fold of skin above the nose, and sometimes become infected. Most people know eyes chiefly as a place where an otherwise healthy relative once had to be cut.

Their voices are quiet in company. Much of their speech takes place close enough for a listener to feel breath against the jaw. Across a workroom they use a narrower language of knocks, whistles, and short calls chosen to survive particular kinds of machinery. The same instruction sounds different in a compressor chamber and beside a lathe.

A visitor can understand every word and still be a danger.

At the larger stations, newcomers wear loose ceramic pieces on one ankle. Their colleagues can hear where they are without having to ask. Removing these too early is a common form of youthful vanity.

***

A settlement occupies less space than its sounds suggest.

The houses cluster along galleries cut into the high side of a divide. There may be forty households above a pump chamber large enough to contain them all. Kitchens, nurseries, tool shops, and food vats occupy the warmer walls. Storage lies farther out, where the stone is cool. Waste channels run beneath the walking surfaces, accessible through covers that must never be mistaken for flooring.

The covers have coarse ribs. Doorposts bear household marks at the height of a resting hand. Every stair announces its first and last step with a different texture.

Wear is a public concern. A threshold polished smooth can be more dangerous than a cracked beam. There are elderly people whose work consists almost entirely of finding places where familiar surfaces have ceased to say what they should.

Inside a house, belongings have precise locations but need not be arranged neatly. A coil of tubing may remain sprawled across a bench for weeks if everyone knows its shape. Moving it a hand’s breadth to make room for a bowl can cause an argument. When guests help with household work, their first question is where things are to be put back.

Compressed air arrives through pipes thick enough to embrace. It drives workshop tools and ventilates the lower rooms. Some households have a small reed fitted to a branch line, sounding at the lowest pressure the family considers safe. People grow used to its wavering note. After moving away, they may have difficulty sleeping in a house whose reed is tuned differently.

Food grows in the return-water galleries.

There are trays of folded tissue that feed on dissolved minerals, vats of sour microbial paste, and hanging ropes colonized by soft-bodied grazers. These last are harvested by drawing the rope through a fitted ring. The animals fall into a basin with a sound like thick rain.

Fresh food comes from the surface. Root-mat wedges. Herd milk, which must be strained twice. The swollen joints of marsh stems. Small flying animals caught in acoustic snares that imitate an unoccupied passage.

The snares work only for a season or two before the local fliers learn them. Trap makers listen for unfamiliar calls along the migration valleys and are secretive about their best imitations.

Very little food is eaten crisp. Crispness generally indicates grit, shell, or a failure of soaking. A prized meal yields gradually between the tongue and palate, releasing pockets of fat or brine. People chew slowly, both for pleasure and to find what should not be swallowed.

***

They keep animals, although ownership becomes approximate beyond the house drains.

The most familiar is a low, many-jointed scavenger with a wedge-shaped head and a fringe of feelers around its mouth. It lives under benches and behind warm pipes, eating scraps, parasites, and the growth that accumulates around leaking seals. When well fed it makes a soft scraping call by rubbing plates inside its throat.

Households recognize their own by this sound.

A good one can be sent through a blocked drain wearing a cord. A bad one eats the cord. Most learn to distinguish the harness used for work from the loop used to restrain them for cleaning, and become elusive when either is lifted from its hook.

They are affectionate in a burdensome way. They press their full length against sleeping people and shed during humid weather, which is almost all weather. Their plates are found in bedding, soup strainers, and the housings of delicate instruments.

Outside, the relationship with the broad-backed herds is more complicated.

People clear routes between feeding basins and build low resting banks where calves can climb out of deep water. In return, they take milk and occasionally an animal. Some herds tolerate this. Others remember a killing for generations and attack anyone carrying the scent of station resin.

A herd keeper spends long periods almost motionless in water, one hand resting on the throat of an adult animal. Through the throat come the movements of the rest of the herd: unease, feeding, a calf being pushed from one body to another. Experienced keepers can tell when the animal beneath their hand has noticed something they themselves cannot hear.

They leave at once when that happens.

The trunk-clinging predators can imitate people as well as calves. They are particularly good at a wet cough.

For this reason, anyone returning alone to a surface camp must put a hand around the wrist of the person admitting them. The grip is answered in a sequence agreed before departure. People occasionally forget the sequence and spend an uncomfortable interval being questioned from behind a shut door.

The predators have learned to wait near doors.

***

Most inhabitants never go far from the vibration of their own station.

This does not make their country small to them. A station’s territory includes water arriving from places no living worker has visited, pipes laid by dead crews, and obligations to households several weeks away. A pressure loss at home may begin with a landslip beyond the range of any familiar call.

Records are kept on fired strips with patterns of holes and ridges. Technical records are usually read with both hands: one following the main line, the other testing dimensions or annotations alongside it. Long histories hang in bundles whose arrangement is part of the text.

Children are allowed to handle copies. Originals can lose a date to the oil and abrasion of too many affectionate readings.

The maintenance crews carry shorter records in cases against the ribs. Their clothing is close-fitting at the joints and loose across the chest, where trapped air helps keep the skin from chafing. Each carries a length of personal cable in addition to the tools. It has a distinctive lay, recognizable through gloves.

If a worker falls through a root raft, anyone who finds the cable can identify whose weight is at the other end.

There are jokes about each station’s habits. One is said to measure a flood only after it enters the kitchen. Another sends so many requests for replacement bearings that people claim it feeds them to the children. A distant northern station is known for crews who continue talking while they use listening cups. No one agrees whether they are exceptionally skilled or merely careless.

Marriages often follow repair journeys. So do infections, new recipes, and changes in pronunciation.

A person who returns after many years may still know every turn of a gallery and find that nobody there knocks on a door correctly.

***

When the plateau begins to lift, the oldest people sometimes notice first.

They sit with a palm resting on a wall or floor even when there is no reason to listen. A familiar tremor changes. Several sources of vibration, ordinarily distinct, begin to interfere with one another.

Then the working calls pass through the station.

Tools are secured. The largest shafts are uncoupled. People bring children up from the lower galleries, carrying them against the chest so that both hands remain free. The domestic scavengers flatten themselves in doorways and must be pulled out by their harnesses.

There is always someone reluctant to abandon a task. There is usually time to finish it. This is how the accidents happen.

On the ridge above the settlement, people wait among the herds. Warm flanks press against their shoulders. Calves search indiscriminately for milk. The guide cable slackens and settles into the mud.

When the waterfalls stop, nobody speaks loudly.

The low calls from the basins can be felt through the membranes of the jaw even while they are closed. There are several kinds. Some begin beneath the range of hearing and rise until the ribs ache. Others break into irregular pulses, separated by pauses long enough for a listener to think they have ended.

People have lowered instruments into the basins. They have found warm currents, immense quantities of shed tissue, and depressions in the bottom that were absent on earlier surveys. Once, a sounding weight came back enclosed in a soft sleeve that continued contracting for three days.

There are arguments about what this means.

The basin keepers, a small and poorly paid profession, are certain only that individual calls persist. One basin has made the same faltering double note through six generations of records. Another ceased calling after an upstream station diverted its warm discharge. The diversion was eventually reversed. The call did not return.

During the quiet, parents place their children’s hands against the ground, just as they do at home.

There is much to distinguish. The herd shifting around them. Water rising through roots. Someone farther along the ridge trying to free a trapped boot. The basin voice traveling through the bones of the hand.

Then a drain below begins to run.

The people wait for the first rush to pass before descending. They test each stretch of cable. At the house doors they feel the familiar marks, check the thresholds, and reach inside to learn what has moved.
