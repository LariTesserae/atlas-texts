The first one you notice is standing behind a thin sheet in an enclosure you have already entered.

Light passes through the sheet and through parts of the body beyond it. This makes the figure difficult to separate from the branching veins. You see a shoulder, then lose it when you move your head. What you took for a thickening in the upright beside the entrance is a hand laid against it, fingers pointing down.

It does not withdraw the hand.

There is room for you on the shelf. There would be room for both of you, if it came out.

Its height is close to yours, though the proportions are less familiar. The trunk is long, the hips narrow, the legs bent a little even when it stands. Its arms hang with the elbows turned outward. In the sheltered heat it wears nothing, but its surface does not quite resemble bare skin. A fine, pale layer lies over it in overlapping sections, close enough that you see the edges only at the shoulder and along the outside of the thigh. Where an elbow bends, these layers draw apart to expose a moist green crease.

The face is the most opaque part. It has a broad brow and a small mouth, held neither open nor firmly shut. Two dark eyes look at you from beneath clear, swollen lids. There are no whites to the eyes. Within each, several points catch the light at different depths.

It looks at your chest while you breathe.

After a while, its own chest rises. The movement is shallow and does not seem to end in an exhalation. Instead, something shifts lower in the body, beneath the ribs, and a ridge along its side slowly subsides.

You become aware of a sweeter smell inside the enclosure. It is not strong. It resembles the pale flesh immediately beneath a fruit’s skin.

When you move toward the entrance, the sweetness diminishes.

***

They are not plentiful, but there are more of them than the first hours suggest.

One sits in the fork of a buttress with its feet resting on different levels of the floor. Another stands in a narrow interval between two fins, facing neither way along the passage. From a high ridge you can sometimes distinguish them by their heads: smooth, slightly darker shapes among all the pale tips and curls. Once you know what to look for, a clearing that appeared empty may contain three.

They do move.

The beginning of a movement is hard to catch. A hand has been resting on a knee; later it rests beside the knee. You look away toward a pool, look back, and a figure is upright. Yet if you watch long enough, you may see one walk across a court.

It first presses its toes into the floor. The short blades beneath them lean aside, exposing small patches of firmer tissue. Its weight passes forward. The heel lifts with a slow peeling motion, and for an instant clear threads extend between the sole and the ground. These thin, detach, and draw back into the foot.

The next step is easier. After three or four, it walks almost as you do, though it places each foot fully before lifting the other.

The ground does not retain its footprints. The blades return to their previous angles.

Under yours, some stay bent.

A figure following at a distance may stop beside one of these places. It lowers itself, touches the bent blades with the underside of a wrist, and remains there. You need not imagine distress in this attention. Its face is unchanged. Sometimes it leaves before the blades have straightened.

Sometimes it is still there when you return.

Their hands have four long fingers and a shorter opposing one. The fingertips are flattened, with translucent pads divided by fine lines. They touch things with these pads, but also with the wrist, the inner elbow, the soft area below the jaw. A greeting, if that is what you witness between two of them, consists of one laying the side of its face against the other’s forearm.

They hold this position for several minutes.

When they separate, a faint patch of amber remains on the paler one. It fades so gradually that you cannot be certain when it is gone.

***

Inside the enclosures, the shelves bear shallow variations in their smoothness. One has a hollow that receives a hip, another a raised lip beneath a resting forearm. These are difficult to see until occupied.

A seated inhabitant fits without appearing confined. Its back settles against the wall, its feet reach two slight rises in the floor, and its head comes to rest beneath an inward curve of the upright. Along several inches of contact, the distinction between body and shelter grows less obvious. Not because either changes greatly. Their surfaces are already alike.

After a long rest, a person can leave a warm place on a chair. Here, when the inhabitant stands, the shelf retains something more particular: a darkening, a fine network just beneath the surface, the smell you noticed when you first entered.

The body carries an answering mark.

There are enclosures with no inhabitants you ever see. There are inhabitants that pass the same empty enclosure repeatedly and never enter. The smaller rear chambers are not always for smaller bodies. In one, a very tall figure stands with its knees folded beside its chest and its head inclined. Its arms extend through two narrow openings into the front room.

Another rests its hands in those hands.

Neither turns toward you.

They make no audible speech. At close range, however, the skin of the throat sometimes tightens in successive bands. A corresponding movement may appear in the figure beside them, or in the upright against which a palm is laid. You can feel it if you put your own hand there: three slight increases in pressure, a pause, then a fourth so slight it may be your pulse.

From farther away, these exchanges look like complete stillness.

***

The smallest you find is no longer than your forearm.

It lies along the edge of a basin, above the water, in a recess that might have been made for a folded leaf. Its head is large and turned sideways. The eyes are covered by an unbroken clear membrane. Both hands rest near its mouth.

There is no cord, no visible connection to the ground. Nevertheless, you cannot see where the skin of its back ends. The recess rises around it too closely.

An adult sits within reach. One arm lies along the basin rim, the fingertips in the water. At intervals a dark bead appears beneath the small one’s throat, travels downward under its surface, and disappears into its chest.

You come back much later. The adult is gone. Another is sitting on the opposite side, its foot immersed in the basin.

The small one has opened one hand.

That may be all it does in the time you know the hollow.

Larger individuals do not seem to belong to a simple sequence of ages. A short one may have heavy, opaque skin and eyes sunk deep beneath its brow. A tall one may be almost transparent through the ribs. Some have folds down the back that remain sealed. Others have those folds parted, showing narrow surfaces within, pale and finely veined. You never see them spread.

A few carry another shape close against the body: a slight swelling along the flank, with the suggestion of a brow or knuckles beneath it. These inhabitants make room for that side when they sit. Their companions touch it as readily as they touch a hand.

Nothing in their conduct tells you whether this is illness, kinship, or a part of the body that has not yet become distinct.

***

They take things in, though you rarely see them eat.

An inhabitant may lower its mouth to the clear margin of a blade and remain there without biting. Afterwards, the margin is duller. At a pool, they lay the undersides of their wrists on the water. Small dimples form around the skin. The surface becomes level again when the hands are raised.

There is no visible waste. Beneath a resting body, the floor sometimes grows briefly translucent.

They attend to the scarce animals. You once see a seedpod creature cross an inhabitant’s open palm. Each of its six feet finds a depression there, as exact as those beneath the shelves. It settles against the base of a finger. The hand stays open.

Hours later, both are in the same position, except that the finger has curved slightly over it.

You do not learn what frightens them. Sudden sounds make their clear lids thicken, hiding the dark structures within. But they do not flee. A dropped flask brings several faces toward you and a deep, almost inaudible creak from the floor.

The attention lasts until you pick it up.

They are gentler with your movements than with your belongings. They step aside before you reach a narrow place. But a bag left on an unoccupied shelf may be eased toward its edge by the slow rising of the tissue underneath. One of them will catch it before it falls and hold it until you return.

It will not examine the contents.

***

On the high fold near the middle of the hollow, one eventually sits beside you.

Its arrival takes most of an afternoon. At first it stands below the fold, with one hand laid against the ascent. Later it is level with your feet. When it finally sits, it leaves between you a space just wider than a hand.

Up close, there are irregularities you have not noticed before: a clouded patch beneath one eye, a seam across the shoulder, tiny brown points embedded in the clear edge of an ear. Its warmth reaches you without contact.

You look out together.

A long time passes before it leans forward. You think it is about to stand. Instead, it reaches beside your foot, toward the leaf half released from its sheath.

It does not pull the leaf free.

With two fingers it lifts the overlapping edge of an older blade, making a little space beneath it. The transparent tip shifts into that space. The movement is scarcely greater than the trembling of a droplet.

Then the inhabitant takes its hand away.

It sits beside you until the pressure of the shelf begins to hurt your legs. When you get up, its face turns toward the place your body has left. One hand moves across the warm depression, stopping where your palm rested.

You descend the fold. Looking back from below, you can still see it there.

It has not moved into your place.
