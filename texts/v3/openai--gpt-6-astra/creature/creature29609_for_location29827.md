What lives here has never needed to see the whole of itself.

There is nowhere in the hollow it could stand exposed, no level surface on which to uncurl. Its body has grown among obstructions. One shoulder is higher than the other. The ribs on its downhill side are close-set and short; on the other they spread around a permanent bend. Resting, it occupies several spaces that do not appear to connect: a pocket behind the boulder, a channel beneath the bamboo, the narrow dry interval between a root and the rock against which the root has thickened.

A person putting a hand into one of these places might touch something warm enough to be mistaken for sheltered soil.

It is not very warm. It could not afford that.

Most of its covering is a dense, dull underfur, almost black near the skin and grey at the ends. Along its back the hairs are longer, flattened, and stiff. Litter lodges in them. A few support the same small liverwort that grows on the shaded stems of the rhododendrons. Where the creature habitually rubs against stone, the covering has worn away, revealing skin the colour of a wet mushroom, creased into little squares.

Its head is small enough to pass through a gap that will not admit its chest. Having put its head through, it can wait a long time before deciding whether to continue.

The eyes are open tonight, although almost nothing reaches them. They sit far apart beneath folds that keep out falling grit. More useful are the bare patches beside its mouth and along the undersides of its limbs. These rest against roots, stems, soil. Through them it feels the different arrivals of the wind: first in the high crowns, then in the older wood, finally as a pressure against the places where it is lying.

It knows the drop by the absence of such contact.

It has never gone down to discover how far the mountain continues.

There are six limbs, though only the front pair look much like limbs while it is resting. The others fold close, their long digits laid back along the belly. Each digit ends in a soft divided pad, with a small hooked nail recessed between the halves. It can grip bamboo without making it knock against the next cane. It can hold a fern stem without crushing the passage through which the fern lifts water.

When the hollow moves, it moves with it. That is why nothing tonight has appeared to move independently of the weather.

During a gust it advances one hand. As the branches return, it lets its weight pass after it. Where three shrubs are pressed together, it crosses between them. In stillness it waits, even if the stillness catches it stretched across an uncomfortable opening. Much of its strength consists of being able to remain in an unfinished movement.

It eats what collects here.

Under the litter are pale, slow larvae. There are beetles tucked into rotten wood, fungal growths softened by damp, seeds caught where a leaf has curled around a twig. Its narrow mouth can open farther than its head suggests, but usually it works delicately. It turns a fragment with the tip of its tongue, takes what is beneath, and replaces the fragment.

At this temperature it may feed only twice between one darkness and the next.

It also brings things home. A tuft of shed wool. A strip of bark made pliable by decay. Whole leaves, selected less for size than for the way their stalks can be threaded through other leaves. There are stores of these in places the recent slip has opened to the air. Seen in daylight, they would look like the ordinary accumulations of the hollow, except that some of the stalks pass through the same loop twice.

The slip took away its oldest sleeping place.

For years, perhaps longer than the present bamboo has stood, it slept with its cheek against a stone buried in the uphill wall. The stone was smooth on that one side. Its body had made the surrounding chamber larger by degrees, pressing the soil outward, incorporating roots as they grew across it. In that chamber it could release every grip at once.

Now the stone is somewhere below.

For three nights it returned to the raw face and put its head into the air where the entrance had been. It did not search the rubble. The route still existed in its movements: left hand under the crooked trunk, hind foot against the fork, head turned sideways. Only at the end did the route become impossible.

Tonight it has stopped going there.

There are two young in the displaced turf.

They were carried down with it, enclosed in a shallow nest beneath the ferns. Neither was crushed. One has begun to acquire the stiff hairs along its spine. The other is still covered in fine fur that parts when it breathes, exposing a narrow line of skin.

They lie clasped around each other. Their feet close on anything that touches them, including each other’s ears.

The adult has been trying to move them.

This is slow work. A young one cannot simply be carried in the mouth; it grips whatever it is leaving. The adult must ease one little hand free, offer its own fur, wait for the new hold to become secure, and proceed to the next. It has managed to bring the larger one onto its belly twice. Twice a gust through the new opening has made it retreat beneath the turf, where the young one has fastened itself to the nest again.

The machinery below troubles it chiefly because the vibration enters through every contact at once. For a few seconds a root no longer tells it clearly what is happening to that root. It flattens its pads, waits for the heavy impacts to pass, and resumes.

Now its forelimb lies beneath the rhododendron that hangs over the edge.

It is not holding the shrub up. The shrub is far too heavy. But the splitting root crosses the way from the displaced nest to the pocket behind the boulder, and until a moment ago that root was something it could trust.

The adult tests it once more.

Fibres separate against its palm.

It withdraws the hand and reaches higher, into the torn mat. Its nails find living wood beneath the soil. With its other hand it gathers the smaller young one against its chest, slowly releasing the tiny feet from the lining of the nest.

For a while there is no gust. It remains extended, the young one attached to it, its hind limbs still braced under the slab.

Then the leaves rise.

It goes with them, drawing its belly clear of the turf. A handful of soil falls past. The larger young one, left beneath the ferns, reaches into the newly empty space and closes both hands on a leaf.

Behind the boulder, the adult settles the smaller one into the dry crease it has prepared.

Before it can return, it must wait for the hollow to move again.
