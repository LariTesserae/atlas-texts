The person beside you has been there long enough for water to gather along the lower edge of their sleeve.

You took them for a repair.

They are crouched over a seam in the causeway, one arm inserted to the shoulder between two overlapping sheets of bark. Their other hand rests palm-up on the path. On it stand three little animals, translucent except for a dark hook inside each head. From time to time the person lifts one, touches it to the seam, and puts it back.

They are listening to something the animals do.

When they finally withdraw their arm, it is streaked with yellow moisture. There is an extra bend in it, or what looks like one: a length of forearm that can flex sufficiently to follow a drainage channel around a corner. The fingers are ordinary in number but disproportionately long. At the base of each is a soft pad, folded now, which spreads when the hand takes weight.

The person sets the animals in a wooden cup and covers it with a pierced lid.

Then they look at your feet.

“You’ve been standing on the cool part,” they say. “Come a little closer.”

***

They are not small people, though they can make themselves occupy very little space.

An adult stretched upright might look you in the eye. Usually you meet them folded: sitting with knees beside the shoulders, moving on hands and feet beneath a sloping wall, resting lengthwise in a shallow hollow with the face turned against the grain. Their ribs overlap enough to let the chest flatten. A sleeping household can resemble a row of coats laid out to dry.

Their skin is matte brown, gray-green, or the subdued red of a newly opened stem. The palms and soles are paler. So is a narrow strip along either side of the neck, where the skin is thin and densely supplied with blood.

When the air grows poor, these strips darken.

They have lungs, and can speak at length, and do when there is reason. But exertion makes them lower their heads toward the living surface. Their nostrils lie well back beneath the cheekbones. A person resting face-down on a root can breathe without lifting their mouth out of the warmth.

Infants are carried against the parent’s lower ribs, wrapped with a strip of fresh leaf between their bodies. On a long crossing the leaf must be changed. You will see a family stop at an otherwise unremarkable tuft of growth, thank whoever planted it, and cut one small, breathing square.

Their hair is sparse and stiff, thicker along the spine than on the head. Droplets collect on it. A person coming in from the mist combs these droplets into a drinking cup with a little flat implement that is also used for cleaning grafts.

They are careful to wash it between uses. They are less careful when tired.

They wear short, close-fitting garments and many ties. Loose cloth is dangerous where a change of wind can lift it beyond reach. Nothing important hangs from a single fastening. The working clothes of an old tender may carry forty loops, most empty, each made for a tool that has at some point fallen.

The tools themselves are plain. Their handles fit particular hands.

A child inherits a blade before it fits them, and spends years hoping the old owner will stop borrowing it.

***

Their homes are made in the thickness of things.

Not deep inside: beyond the breathing skin, the air in a cut chamber can become heavy and sour. They live where two layers have been encouraged to part, or where a broad fold makes a sheltered interval. The outer wall is still a leaf or a strip of root, alive on both faces. A room has to be kept open by use.

Every morning someone passes a scraper around the doorway.

This is a task for children once they are old enough not to remove the healing margin. Before that they are allowed to clear the sleeping shelves, which thicken very slowly and are difficult to harm. The scrapings are cooked. They taste faintly sweet, with a bitterness that grows stronger during dry weather.

There is no universal household shape. Some families occupy long narrow rooms in which everyone must turn over together. Others live around the rim of a water pocket, their doorways opening onto a common ledge. In one old settlement, several hundred homes are arranged along a hanging seam, and the morning journey to the shared kitchens is made head downward.

Visitors complain about this.

The residents say the visitors hold their cups incorrectly.

A household usually includes people born there, people married in, and someone who arrived to repair a defect and stayed. There may also be a child from a distant span whose journey home has become seasonally impossible. Such children are not considered abandoned, although they sometimes grow old before the route becomes convenient again.

People ask after their families through travelers. They exchange little lengths of cord carrying knots, scent, and tooth impressions.

Writing exists, but a flat inscription cannot always preserve what happened to the distance between two places. A cord can be stretched, doubled, threaded through itself. A well-made account of a journey is read with both hands.

***

Their children learn to fall.

First into a sling. Then off a low rail, with an adult below. Then sideways, from one face of a root to the other, catching the warm underside and holding still until their breathing settles.

The important lesson is not to reach for the largest thing.

A broad leaf may turn. A heavy branch may be much farther away than it appears. The child must find something living that is under tension and likely to remain under tension for the next few breaths.

They learn this by touch before they can explain it.

They are sometimes reckless afterward. Young people cross gaps on slack fibers, ride the first lowering of a new platform, or venture out along a membrane that has not yet thickened enough to hold a peg. They dare one another to lift both hands and both feet clear of the world.

For an instant they belong entirely to the air.

Adults speak very harshly about this game. Some have scars from playing it.

There are songs, too, but many are felt better than heard. A singer braces the jaw against a hollow rail and hums. Farther along, listeners place their elbows on the wood and cup their hands around their ears. A familiar song can travel through a neighborhood without disturbing the animals asleep between its rooms.

Lovers sometimes choose a stem that serves only two houses.

Children find these stems and tap on them.

***

They keep animals, though ownership becomes difficult when both keeper and animal must follow the changing routes of rain.

The little transparent creatures in the tender’s cup are seam-feeders. Their hooked mouths take loose tissue without cutting healthy fiber. A sound graft makes them restless. Hidden rot quiets them; they settle and eat.

A good tender knows each animal’s appetite. One that has recently fed may give a dangerously reassuring answer.

There are pale, six-footed domestic beasts with warm bellies and a habit of sleeping across doorways. These lick the sweat from cool wood and store it in paired sacs under the jaw. They yield water when stroked firmly from throat to breast. They also bite, steal fermenting food, and develop attachments to people who do not officially own them.

Households argue about them with great seriousness.

The large moss-backed browsers are not domestic. Their routes are negotiated with planted food, bitter washes, and the opening of gates so enormous that an entire settlement may spend a season loosening one.

A herd can bring water to a district. It can also strip away thirty years of careful surface growth.

Herd tenders walk ahead carrying baskets of dung, testing it for seeds. They know which arches will bear the animals, which feeding grounds are exhausted, and where a mother with young will refuse to go. Some travel for decades beside the same broad, patient individual.

When that animal dies, the others may stand around it until the span begins to bend.

Then the tenders have a terrible job.

***

These people are not uniformly patient.

A person can spend a lifetime training the beginning of a stair and still become furious when dinner is late. They quarrel over sleeping places, borrowed scrapers, and whose turn it was to empty a clogged gutter. Someone is always proposing a faster way to widen a road. Someone else has kept a sample of the last road widened that way.

Nor are they equally willing to work for descendants.

There are neglected bindings. There are grafts concealed under cosmetic bark. There are districts whose comfortable floors are supported by work owed to poorer districts below, work that has been deferred for so long that no living person remembers the original agreement.

The cords remember enough.

Occasionally a failing branch must be cut open to retrieve the knot that establishes responsibility. The hearing then takes place beside a wound which worsens while the parties speak.

Their oldest penalties are practical. A person who has stolen light may be required to maintain the shaded surface until it recovers. This can consume the rest of their working life.

People flee such sentences.

Elsewhere they arrive as strangers with useful skills, and are given a room.

***

The tenders of unfinished structures have a reputation for distraction.

They spend part of each day attending to things that cannot yet bear weight. A row of shoots needs very little actual labor, but a great deal of comparison: the angle of a leaf, the tightness of a hoop, the position in damp air of a column that will not be solid for centuries.

Some are excellent company. Others interrupt a meal to look past your shoulder at an arcade no one else can see.

Their work is checked by pairs. One looks at the growing thing; the other looks at the space it is intended to occupy. They trade places often. There are documented cases of two people agreeing for years about a structure that was never going to exist.

The signs of such mistakes are subtle. The future arcade acquires too many entrances. Its shadows remain when the air dries. A distant figure appears to be tending the mature work, but never moves to another task.

When this happens, the hoops are cut.

The shoots straighten with a force that can break a wrist.

It is considered cruel to ask the tenders what they saw.

***

The dead are returned to the shallow world where things can use them.

There are sheltered ledges for this, screened from roads. Small animals do most of the work. The bones are cleaned and apportioned according to instructions left by their owner.

Not everyone wishes to become a tool. Some ask to be enclosed in a graft, or placed beneath the leaf where they slept as infants. A few want to fall.

That request troubles families. There is no certainty that falling ends.

People comply anyway, sometimes.

The useful bones are taken by relatives and apprentices. A needle may have been a grandmother’s finger; a smoothing tool, part of someone’s shoulder. Nobody regards this as making the object infallible. A badly shaped ancestral tool is altered until it works.

There is affection in the wear.

***

The person who invited you onto the warmer part of the root is called something that means, approximately, *the one who came back with the cup*. It refers to an incident from childhood. They have other names, but this is the one used at home.

They have two children, a painful hip, and responsibility for seventeen bindings. Three were begun before their birth. One was begun by a person they disliked, whose work they have since come to admire.

They are worried about the seam.

Not enough to close the road. Enough to remain beside it through the turning of the leaves, one palm spread on either side, feeling how the tension passes.

While they wait, they mend the lid of the animal cup. They ask where you are going. When you point, they turn your hand a little to the left.

“From here,” they explain, “you start that way.”

Later, a child arrives with food in a folded leaf. The tender eats, complains that it has been over-scraped, and saves the softest piece for the seam-feeders.

The child settles nearby. After a while, without being told, they put one hand on the root.

The older person moves it a finger’s width.

Both wait for the next leaf to turn.
