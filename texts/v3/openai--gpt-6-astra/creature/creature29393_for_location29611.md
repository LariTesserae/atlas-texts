The people who tend the stations can usually be recognized by their hands.

Even asleep, they keep the fingers slightly apart. A person waking in a bunk will move one hand before opening their eyes, making the little outward turn that means *here*, or *awake*, or, if someone has been sitting beside them for a long time, *you can go now*. Children learn this before they learn to write. It is not one of the official maintenance signals.

There are several kinds of language in the stations. The displays carry instructions, measurements, inventories, messages from elsewhere. The slates are used for things that need a drawing. Hands carry most of what passes between people, though much can be said by where a person stands, whether they have secured a tool before turning around, whether they wait inside a doorway or outside it.

Speech is possible. The people are not mute, and not all of them are deaf. But the protection worn over the ears cannot keep the mountain out of the bones, and uncovering an ear to listen is a poor habit. New arrivals shout for a few days. Then they begin to watch.

Some retain the habit of moving their lips as they sign. Some find this irritating. There are couples who have been disagreeing about it for thirty years.

Their work clothes are close-fitting and heavily patched at the knees. Anything that can flap will eventually abrade itself against something. Buttons are uncommon. Pockets close across their whole width. A dropped screw may lie on the floor for a moment, trembling in place, then begin a deliberate journey toward a grating.

Most people have a small crescent of scar tissue somewhere on the back of a hand, where they once reached between two surfaces that appeared to be moving together.

They are not a separate kind of human. Their differences have accumulated within ordinary lives: damage, skill, preference, the posture learned in a narrow service passage. An old technician can distinguish a loose mounting from a worn bearing through the sole of one boot. On another station, with different floors, she has to learn again.

Her name is Mara. In conversation she is indicated by two fingers laid across the wrist, a gesture inherited from a broken watch she wore long after it stopped working.

She came to the province as an adult. There are photographs of her standing on soil.

She keeps them in a locker, not because they trouble her, but because there is little wall space that does not already have a purpose.

***

Outside Mara’s station lives an animal that the inventories call a branching obstruction.

This describes what it does to an intake, not what it is.

At first it resembles a length of translucent packing material caught around a bolt. It has a thickened edge, faintly yellow, and a middle so clear that the rock beneath it seems merely wet in a different way. One end is fastened to the metal. The other lifts, pauses, and fastens somewhere else.

Over a month, it may move the length of a hand.

Young ones occur beneath ledges, in the shelters occupied by pale crabs. There they are compact, folded into rosettes, with all their openings turned toward the narrow space between stone and body. A crab walking over one presses it flat. Afterward it slowly fills again.

An undisturbed adult is less easy to recognize as a single animal.

It extends narrow sleeves through cracks and around the backs of stones. The sleeves divide, join, and divide again. Where two branches meet, their walls dissolve into a common passage. Food moves through that passage in cloudy pulses. So do small opaque bodies that might be organs, except that they do not remain in any particular place.

One animal can occupy both sides of a ledge without ever crossing its exposed face.

There is no central mouth. The feeding surfaces are carried in little cups along the sheltered edges. Each cup turns out a fringe fine enough to be visible only when particles collect on it. When disturbed, it draws the fringe inward and closes.

The disturbance travels through the animal. Nearby cups shut first. Farther ones continue feeding until a slow whitening of the tissue reaches them.

It takes minutes for a large individual to become fully alarmed.

By then, whatever touched it has usually gone.

***

The branching animals were here before the turbines.

Deep in the faults, where water moves through porous bands in the rock, they spread across the boundaries between fast and slow flow. Their tissues contain tough fibers laid down under tension. A sleeve stretched between two projections can hold its feeding edge in moving water without exposing the whole body.

Around the stations, this way of growing has found new possibilities.

A gantry offers more attachment points than a ledge. A cable provides a route across open water. An intake supplies food continuously, though an animal that grows too near its mouth is pulled apart.

Most do not survive their first approach.

The survivors spread backward into the protective framework, thickening where the metal trembles least. Their growth records the station’s vibration. On one brace they form short, crowded loops; on another, long unsupported membranes. Two identical pieces of ironwork may acquire entirely different inhabitants.

Mara can recognize her station’s animals in photographs.

There is one beneath the repair cradle that she has been trimming for eleven years.

Its oldest visible tissue is the color of weak tea. The newer growth is clear. Under the floodlights, its passages throw thin, doubled shadows across the steel.

It has no apparent interest in light.

Small fish do.

They shelter inside the loose outer branches, where a larger swimmer cannot turn easily. Their movements stir food against the feeding fringes. Sometimes a fish dies there, and the animal closes around it so slowly that other fish continue using the same shelter. A white spine remains for several weeks, passing from one part of the network to another as the sleeves contract.

The animal neither hunts the fish nor protects them. Nevertheless, the fish return.

***

Mara’s station houses nine people when everyone is home.

There are twelve bunks. One of the empty ones is used for drying clothes. Another has been occupied for years by equipment that officially belongs somewhere else. The third is kept clear, with its straps folded and its lamp working.

The youngest resident repairs inspection crawlers. He was born in another station, far enough away that he has visited it only twice since leaving. He likes recorded voices. During meals he sometimes watches a person speaking on a small screen, with the sound rendered as words below the face.

He is particularly interested in hesitation: the intake of breath, the unfinished word, the mouth held open while a thought changes.

Mara finds these recordings slow.

She prefers diagrams, except when they are badly drawn.

They have worked together for four years and know very little about what the other does before sleeping.

The station’s cook is also responsible for the pressure seals. He takes this combination of duties seriously and resents jokes about it. When he is angry, his signs become unusually complete. No pronoun is left implicit. No instruction depends on context.

He grows a white fungus in trays behind a warm exchanger. The trays smell of bread dough and old cloth. Nothing else in the station smells alive in quite that way.

Once each week, he opens the cabinet while the others are eating.

People pause with their utensils raised. Then someone remembers to thank him.

***

The residents go outside more often than their work strictly requires.

Not far. A journey of several kilometers needs planning, equipment, someone awake at a tracking board. But the cradle and the nearest ridge are accessible by tether.

They go to look at things whose dimensions have not been decided by an engineer.

There is a fracture above the station that fits a gloved hand at one end and could admit the whole station at the other. There is a fallen slab balanced on three points. Beneath it, pale crabs have worn faint tracks through the bacterial stain, though their feet have made no impression on the stone itself.

Mara visits the branching animal on her way back.

In the water, she cannot speak to it even by habit. She checks her tether, braces her boots, and takes the cutting tool from its clip.

The part that must be removed is always new growth. It advances toward the cradle’s sliding joint, where an eventual movement would crush it into the mechanism.

She cuts well away from the joint, leaving a broad margin.

The severed tissue contracts into a pale knot. She catches it rather than letting it enter the intake. For a while, the cut edges release cloudy threads. Then the sleeves close, one after another, and the surviving branches tighten against the frame.

Several fish leave.

Several remain.

Once, after a long absence, she found that another technician had cleaned the entire brace. The work was correct. The metal was bare.

She stood beside it longer than she needed to.

By the following year, clear cups had appeared beneath the lowest bolt.

***

Nobody knows how long the animals live.

A branch can be older than the attachment that supports it. The outer tissues die; inner passages find another route. A section carried away in a tool basket may establish itself beneath a different station, provided it has not spent too long in dry air.

Whether that constitutes another animal is a question the residents have discussed more than once.

Their arguments are not especially solemn. They take place over meals, interrupted by requests for salt, or by someone leaning across the table to draw a better diagram.

Mara thinks separation matters.

The crawler technician thinks it does not.

Outside, beneath the cradle, the feeding cups open again. The whitening retreats from the cut. Fluid begins moving through a new connection, no wider than a thread, that formed between two sleeves while Mara was away.

A fish turns within the branches and brushes a fringe shut.

Only that cup closes. The others go on feeding.
