The creature in the new stairwell has lined its nest with shavings from the underside of the landing.

It is about the size of a closed hand, though the nest is much smaller. Most of the animal lies folded into itself. Two thin limbs are hooked over the rim; between them, a moist, blunt face turns slowly toward the warmth of your fingers. Its eyes are sealed beneath transparent skin. Something moves behind each seal, following you a little after you have moved.

It has been eating the stairs.

The damage is slight: a row of scalloped bites where the landing joins the wall. Beneath each bite the exposed tissue has hardened to a deep amber. The landing no longer sags at that end.

When the animal yawns, you see that its teeth are crowded along one side of its mouth. The other side has a thick, ridged pad. It grips a shaving, pulls, and settles deeper into its little supply of heat.

There are others in the older buildings. You find their scallops around doorframes and beneath windowsills, always where two directions of growth have met badly. Sometimes a nest is empty except for a pellet of dense wood and a tuft of colorless hair. Sometimes the occupant has become too large to leave by the opening through which it entered. Then the wall grows a second opening, usually at floor level.

For several days afterward, the animal can be seen sitting between the two holes. It appears reluctant to choose.

***

The inhabitants who use the doors are harder to approach.

They come out when the pumps pause, in the interval before the hurried fourth turn. A little movement passes along the row of dwellings: a thorn stopcock twists, a scale on a roof lifts, and someone steps into the street carrying a shallow tray.

At first you may mistake the tray for part of the bearer. Both have the same dull, fibrous surface. The distinction becomes clear when the tray is set down and the hands remain bent around its absent weight.

These inhabitants stand no higher than your knee. Their bodies are narrow at the shoulders and broad near the ground, with a loose outer layer that hangs in overlapping panels. The panels look like clothing until one catches on a railing. A drop of clear fluid gathers at the tear. The owner stops, presses the edges together, and waits.

Underneath is a darker, finely pebbled skin.

They have four principal limbs. Usually two are used for walking, but a person carrying something fragile will lower a third, transferring the burden to a short pair of hands concealed beneath the chest. Their feet open on contact with the paving. Several pale pads spread from a central stalk, each testing a slightly different patch of ground.

On an old street the pads barely unfold.

On a new one they spread so widely that the walker must proceed sideways.

Their faces occupy the undersides of their heads. To look at you, they lean backward. You see a small mouth, two shallow hollows filled with black moisture, and a fringe of stiff filaments that tremble in the air from your breathing.

They do not like to stand directly beneath you. If you stoop, they move away until your head no longer overhangs theirs.

One will occasionally make an exception. It comes close, braces itself on three limbs, and holds a filament near the seam of your boot. After a long examination it plucks something from the leather.

A seed, perhaps. A grain of road salt.

It carries the object home in both hands.

***

Their work leaves little distinction between a tool and a meal.

At the stair workshop, an attendant sits beside the unfinished eighth tread. Every so often it pushes a finger into the soft material and withdraws a thread. The thread goes into its mouth. It chews slowly, holding the tread with its other hand.

Eventually it gets up and walks to a tank behind the workshop. It opens a slit beneath its lowest body panel and releases a small quantity of milky liquid into the tank.

The tread advances.

Several attendants may spend a whole dim cycle doing this. They take turns at the slot, each tasting from a different place. One prefers the edges. One reaches deep into the center and invariably draws out more than it can swallow, leaving a damp cord hanging from its mouth while it considers the next step.

There is no evident supervisor. There is, however, an individual who arrives carrying nothing, touches the rack, and causes everyone to stop.

This one has exceptionally worn fingertips.

It tests each finished tread by placing a hand above it, without quite making contact. Once, after a lengthy inspection, it lifts the third tread off the rack and lays it on the ground.

The others give it room.

By the next cycle the rejected tread has softened. Small wall animals are feeding along its length. The inspector returns, takes a mouthful itself, and goes away.

Beside the strips of distance, the attendants behave differently. They keep their limbs close to their bodies. They never reach across a strip to retrieve a dropped implement. A tool left on the wrong side may remain there until the whole rack has emptied.

One attendant has a hand much farther from its wrist than the other.

The intervening space is difficult to look at. There seems to be an ordinary forearm, then enough room for another forearm, then the hand. When the owner rests it on the bench, the bench appears longer.

The others pass it objects without hesitation.

***

In the dwellings, they sleep standing in recesses.

Each recess fits its occupant loosely at first. During sleep, little folds in the wall rise around the feet and support the body. The outer panels lift. Beneath them are rows of fine pores, and against these pores the wall extends soft, brushlike structures.

A sleeping inhabitant gives off a faint smell of boiled grain.

There may be several recesses in a room, occupied by people of markedly different sizes. There are also loose objects: bowls, lengths of cord, a smooth stone, a dead beetle held in a loop of fiber. Such things are kept on shelves grown beyond the reach of the wall’s feeding animals.

The smooth stones receive considerable attention. They are turned over, rubbed, carried from one shelf to another. Sometimes two neighbors meet outside and exchange them. Each examines the new stone for a long time before entering its dwelling.

You cannot tell what makes one desirable. Some are no more than grit. One household has a piece of blue glass, so small that it is almost lost between the bearer’s fingertips.

During the brief rain, that household brings the glass outside.

Four inhabitants stand around it as it lies on the paving. They wait until a droplet strikes it. Then the smallest picks it up and carries it indoors.

***

The young do not fit their recesses.

They sleep in shallow communal pockets near the warm junctions of the main, tangled together with their feet closed. They have too many little pads and very short body panels, so that the soft skin beneath shows whenever they bend.

While awake, they follow animals.

A child may spend hours walking behind a bolt beetle that has left its hole, imitating its gait with painful concentration. Others lie flat beside the foundation grazers and attempt to eat what they eat. An older inhabitant eventually comes to fetch them, sliding a broad hand beneath their bodies and lifting them without interrupting their chewing.

They are allowed near most of the machinery.

They are pulled away from the cistern.

This is done gently but at once. Even when the cistern is dry, an adult will place itself between the rim and an approaching child, allowing all its foot pads to spread.

The children remain interested. They bring things to the edge: pieces of shed skin, a thorn, once a living gutter animal held very carefully by its folded wings.

The offerings are removed when the children have gone.

Nothing is thrown into the water.

***

The workers below the reflection resemble the people above, but their outer panels sit differently. Their bodies look fuller. They walk without testing the floor.

Occasionally an inhabitant of the upper town kneels at the cistern and waits for someone beneath it to notice.

When this happens, the reflected worker stops what it is doing. It may set down its tray or turn away from the rollers. The two hold their hands near the surface from opposite sides.

They do not touch.

The water between their palms becomes minutely corrugated. At the rim, petals tighten against one another.

Sometimes the visitor rises almost immediately. Sometimes it remains until the last illuminated room has flattened into the dark film, its hands still held out.

Afterward it may walk home by a very long route, although its dwelling is beside the square.

An individual with a damaged body panel comes repeatedly. Below, someone is always waiting at a table. There is a third chair, too small for either of them.

You never see anyone sit in it.

***

They share their rooms with the animals, but not indiscriminately.

The translucent wall feeders are tolerated until their moving bodies cloud a panel completely. Then an inhabitant taps the wall with the edge of a bowl. The little creatures gather toward the sound, and the bowl is held against a slit until it fills.

The contents are carried to a building still under construction.

The gutter sleepers are left alone. When one falls, anyone nearby stops. A fallen animal cannot open its wings against the paving. It lies on its back with its tiny feet closing on nothing.

The inhabitants lift it with a forked implement kept inside certain doorways.

They will interrupt a heavy delivery to do this.

Yet they eat other animals alive, quietly, holding them under the lower edge of the face. At the pump house you may hear a crisp series of sounds and turn to see an attendant wiping its fingers on the inside of a body panel.

The rootlike predator is given the entire street.

Even the wall feeders become still when it passes. Its loose extremities feel ahead through the seams, and sometimes one extends beneath a dwelling door.

From inside comes a single hard knock.

The extremity withdraws.

***

There are very old inhabitants whose feet no longer close.

They move on many spread pads, slowly enough that the paving sometimes levels itself beneath them before they complete a step. Their outer panels have thickened into rigid shelves. Small animals nest under these shelves, emerging to feed when the host rests.

The oldest remain beside the municipal hall.

They are easy to mistake for fittings: a low rail, a bracket at the base of a column. But when the pressure changes, a hand lifts. When someone approaches carrying a new length of space, the filaments beneath an old head begin to stir.

The carrier waits beside them.

For a while there is only the sound of the strip flexing in its tray.

Then one of the old inhabitants shifts a foot. A seam opens in the paving, exposing pale tissue below. The carrier kneels and begins easing the new interval into place.

The work takes a long time. Someone brings a bowl for the old one. Someone else supports its lifted limb.

By the time the seam closes, the carrier and the bowl-bearer stand several paces apart.

Between them is a patch of empty street, clean and cold, wide enough for a small gathering. The old inhabitant lowers its foot at the edge. Its pads spread, finding the knots.

Before the others leave, one of them bends to remove an animal from beneath it.
