The woman who tends the arrival basin has one foot larger than the other. She wears sandals made to fit them, but visitors sometimes assume that one sandal has swollen in the damp.

“It’s the foot,” she tells them, if they ask.

She is called Seli. In the mornings she sits beneath the fiber arch with a bowl of sour fruit and a length of cord, adding knots for the people who pass through. She uses a different knot for someone arriving than for someone leaving. A person who intends to return before evening gets a loose loop, which she can pull straight with her thumbnail.

This is not the official record. The official record is kept somewhere inside the arch. Once a season it produces a thin, leathery sheet covered in raised marks, which nobody presently living in the town can read with confidence. Seli dries it, rolls it, and puts it with the others.

Her cord tells her whether to keep someone’s supper.

She has been doing this long enough that she often recognizes an arrival before the basin opens. Not the person themselves, but the sort of difficulty they are bringing. A smooth lowering of the water generally means someone traveling alone with little luggage. Water that bunches against one side of the basin suggests a load poorly secured at the other end. If the reflection breaks into little squares, she gets up and fetches the gardener.

Sometimes the basin admits a smell before it admits a traveler: hot dust, coal smoke, a sharp resin that makes everyone nearby sneeze. Once it gave off the smell of fresh bread for three days without opening. People were irritable about that.

Seli herself came through as a child. She remembers a high window and a blue bowl standing on a sill. She does not remember whether these were at the place she left or somewhere she stopped along the way. For many years she asked arrivals about the bowl. Now she asks whether they have eaten.

Her house has a low room at the back where strangers sleep. The room was requested for a niece who never came. It is cooler than the rest of the house and grows a crop of small white mushrooms along one wall, despite repeated discouragement. Seli gives these to her neighbor, who says they are edible. She has never asked whether he actually eats them.

***

There are forty-three people who can be counted on to be in the town through most of the year, and others whose residence is harder to settle.

One man sleeps here but works beyond the basin. He leaves before dawn wearing a stiff gray coat, and returns with his cuffs gritty and his hands smelling of oranges. His children have never seen his workplace. He has offered to take them, but the youngest will not use the basin, and the oldest dislikes oranges.

Two sisters occupy rooms on opposite sides of a canal. They have been quarreling for nine years and share a kitchen. When the canal narrows, they complain separately to the gardener. When it widens, they complain together.

A person known as Uncle arrived with a troupe of performers and stayed behind to recover from an illness. The troupe is expected back. Uncle now has grandchildren in the town, none related to him.

Some inhabitants leave for years and return to find that their houses still admit them, though the arrangement of rooms has become unsuitable. Others leave for an afternoon and find a wall where their door was. This is less a question of affection than of what the town has needed to do in the meantime.

People mark their homes with things that do not grow there: a glazed dish set above an entrance, a brass ring, a square of red cloth. The objects look curiously definite against the living surfaces. After rain, when the walls are dark and wet, you can pick them out across the square.

Most household possessions are small enough to lift. The larger ones have handles.

***

The gardener is a broad young man with a sparse beard and very clean fingernails. His name is Oren. Visitors frequently mistake his mother for the gardener because she is old, speaks little, and spends her afternoons looking dissatisfied beside a patch of yellowing pavement.

She used to be a cook. She thinks the patch would make a good place for a second bathhouse.

Oren carries a shallow basket containing reed wedges, little bags of salts, a hooked probe, and several pieces of cloth that must not be allowed to dry. He also carries his lunch. His work sometimes requires him to spend half a day lying on his stomach with one arm in a drain, and he has learned to put the lunch where he can reach it with the other hand.

He knows the town chiefly by touch.

A healthy transport channel has a faint uneven pulse. A wall preparing to move becomes warm well before it swells. Certain root infections can be detected by pressing two fingers to a surface and drawing them apart; the tissue between them stays pale instead of darkening again.

He cannot ask the town what is wrong. He can make one part of it less comfortable than another, feed it something it lacks, or hold a passage open until traffic establishes a new habit. Much of his work consists of returning the next day to see whether his advice was taken.

People sometimes bring him disputes that are only partly botanical.

A bedroom has become damp since a lodger moved in. A house has started closing its windows during visits from a particular cousin. A kitchen produces drinking water that tastes bitter whenever the household gathers for a meal.

Oren checks drainage, light, salt balance, the condition of the roots. Occasionally he finds a cause. Occasionally he asks who has been pouring what into the floor.

He is patient with the town and rather less patient with claims made on its behalf.

“It doesn’t know your cousin,” he says.

This is not entirely certain, but it is useful.

***

Children here learn to swim before anyone trusts them to carry a full cup.

They are lean or round, bold or reluctant, much like children elsewhere. Most have small pale scars below the knees. They run in short bursts, stop without being told, and look down more often than up. The younger ones hold hands when crossing a court, not because they have been instructed to, but because a companion is useful when the ground starts to tilt.

They know which canal mouths pull hardest after rain. They know the shallow pocket under the bathhouse where warm water collects. They know that a fish lying on newly risen pavement can propel itself a surprising distance if approached from the wrong side.

Their games require frequent renegotiation. A boundary moves. A hiding place seals. The surface chosen for a race develops a soft patch that favors the lightest runner. Arguments over whether the town is helping someone can occupy an entire afternoon.

During flowering week they become temporary officials. They sit beside the greenish blossoms with their brushes and take payment in food, coin, or credit at the storeroom. The conscientious ones learn to distinguish insects by their approach. The lazy ones wave at everything.

There is always one child who becomes attached to a beetle.

This year it is Seli’s youngest neighbor, a solemn boy who keeps his beetle in a pierced seed case and brings it to work. He has been told repeatedly that it cannot sit with him beside the machinery. He puts the case several paces away and spends his shift watching it instead of the flowers.

***

The scratching in the walls belongs mostly to small, jointed animals with flattened bodies and a covering like dull brown enamel.

They are longer than a finger when fully grown. Each has six walking legs, two short gripping limbs, and a soft underside that folds around whatever it is eating. They graze on dead root hairs and the thin fungal films that form inside air chambers. When disturbed, they brace themselves so firmly that pulling one out is likely to damage the wall.

People call them seam-crabs, though they do not live in the canals and drown if submerged for long.

Every house has them. At night they come out around the window rims and clean the pores. Their eyes reflect lamplight as two green points, then disappear when they turn sideways.

One of the sisters feeds hers scraps of boiled egg. Her sister says this is why that half of the kitchen smells.

Larger creatures occupy the wet spaces below.

There are blunt-headed fish with lips tough enough to rasp the hanging roots, and eels that learn the timing of the household drains. Pale snails spend their whole lives on the undersides of rooms, moving through a forest whose ceiling rises and falls with the people above. When a house shifts, the snails close themselves tightly against it. Some of the oldest carry several smaller generations on their shells.

Oren occasionally brings one up by accident. He holds it in a basin while he works, then returns it to approximately the same place.

He dislikes the idea of putting it beneath the wrong house.

***

There is also something beneath the old market square that nobody has seen entire.

Its presence is established by practical inconveniences. Repairs made there are undone from below. A weighted line lowered through a maintenance opening will sometimes be drawn sideways, very gently, until all the slack has gone. Once, during a long dry interval, a broad gray surface rose into the opening and remained there for two days.

It was warm and finely ridged. Water gathered in the ridges.

People came to look. One child touched it. Oren’s mother set a bowl on it while she adjusted her sandal, then picked the bowl up again.

The surface withdrew on the second night, leaving an odor like crushed pond weed.

There are several opinions about what it belongs to. Seli thinks it is part of the town. Oren thinks it is an animal using the town for shelter. His mother remembers the same thing happening when she was young, but remembers the surface as white.

Whatever it is, it favors the square. When the old market was moved to a neighboring island, the gardeners of the time tried to encourage that section of the mat to contract. The surrounding courts narrowed. Two empty buildings subsided into low ridges. The square remained.

Now people cross it on their way to the bathhouse, dry laundry along its sunny edge, and occasionally sit in its middle when they want to be away from a wall.

On quiet evenings, lying flat with an ear to the pavement, they can hear a slow rubbing sound beneath the usual movement of water.

Not everyone enjoys this.

***

After the rain, Seli goes out to retrieve whatever has lodged against her doorstep. She keeps a shallow basket for the purpose. Seed husks go into a canal. Usable things are washed and set on the wall where their owners may see them.

Today there is a sandal, a spoon, and a small twist of blue cloth tied around something hard.

She unties it. Inside are three teeth.

For a while she stands holding them in her wet palm. Then she wraps them again and puts the bundle beside the spoon.

Across the court, Oren is kneeling at a seam that opened during the storm. The boy with the beetle crouches beside him, asking questions. From one of the houses comes the smell of frying batter. Someone calls for a child; two children answer, neither the one wanted.

The basin behind Seli darkens.

She turns, still holding the basket. In its surface, beyond the reflection of her shoulder, snow is blowing sideways across a flight of steps. A figure waits at the bottom with a bag held against their chest.

Seli sets down the basket and moves the recovered sandal out of the way.

The steps begin to open.
