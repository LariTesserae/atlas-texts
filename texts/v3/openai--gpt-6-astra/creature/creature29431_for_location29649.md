The white animal has a little clay caught beneath its fourth pair of legs. It cleans the other legs frequently, drawing each one through its mouthparts, but leaves this pair alone. The clay is blue when the light reaches it. In darkness it pales with the rest of the body.

Seen from the entrance, the animal is an ordinary water louse, though unusually small. Its plates overlap neatly. Its antennae test the fibers ahead of it. A darker fleck moves along the amber line in its back after it has fed.

Seen from farther inside, it is difficult to count its plates. There is always another where the body bends.

It cannot be watched from both places at once. A stem intervenes, or the bark turns out to have a second curl.

It feeds on the softened undersides of leaves. The marks it leaves are minute, shallow, and close together, a roughened patch rather than a hole. It will spend several days working along a single vein. Sometimes it stops with its mouthparts still against the surface, and the leaf continues to thin beneath it. Sometimes the mouthparts move without touching anything.

There are things it avoids eating. The pale branching growths are one. Another is a brown film that appears occasionally on the freshest clay. When it finds this film, it presses the front of its body flat and backs away, keeping both antennae extended toward the spot. It does not return until a clear filament has grown across it.

Whether the film tastes bad or is something it recognizes cannot be determined.

Beneath its bark, it rests with the tips of its antennae inserted into two small pits. These are scarcely deeper than pores. They remain in the clay when the bark lifts, and are a little farther apart afterward.

During these rests, a second arrangement of limbs can sometimes be made out.

They are not beneath the visible legs. They occur farther back in the hollow, among the leaf veins: short, transparent joints, each ending in a soft pad. One braces against the underside of a stone. Another rests on the bark facing of the highest terrace. There is no visible body connecting them. A filament passes through the place where such a body would have to be.

The pads tighten when a knock comes through the lake bed. Near the entrance, the white louse tightens its grip at the same moment.

These farther limbs are slow. A joint may take all the remaining life of a patch of light to straighten. But they are not weak. When one presses against a leaf, the leaf yields, the root behind the leaf yields, and a clean seam appears in the clay beyond the root. The limb holds its position until green has entered the seam. Then the pad loosens.

For a while there is a small oval impression on the new wall.

The animal does not seem to visit the terraces in its white form. It approaches the lowest one, turns, and returns to the entrance. Yet something wears the undersides of the bark flakes smooth. Their exposed faces remain rough.

A grain of sand placed on the upper ledge will sometimes move inward until it rests beneath the splinter. There it may stay for weeks. Fine pale structures gather around it, too regular to be root hairs, too soft to be jaws. They turn it by fractions.

Eventually the grain is taken somewhere the light does not reach.

Others are rejected. These appear at the mouth of the hollow, balanced on the still end of the algae thread. They are usually sharp grains, or grains with a dark inclusion. A round piece of quartz may be brought back and rejected several times before disappearing inside.

Deep in one fold there is a row of accepted grains, each held separately in a transparent pocket. No two touch. Beside the smallest lies an amber speck.

When the water louse is feeding, the speck darkens.

When the hollow is empty, it lengthens.

The emptiness is quite specific. The leaves have not gone, nor the bark, nor the plainly visible animal. But the little depressions beneath its feet lose their depth. Its outline can still be followed around the head and down either side, yet there is no particular point at which the water ends and its body begins. An antenna rests across a root without concealing it.

Farther in, all the separate pads release their holds.

Nothing collapses. The growths draw back by less than the width of a root hair, and remain there. A leaf whose edge has been pressed against stone for months is briefly free on both sides.

This is when the hollow is most vulnerable. A disturbance at the mouth sends a tremor through every clear filament. The white shape contracts but does not withdraw. The clay beneath it looks wet in a way that the submerged clay ordinarily does not.

Then one pair of legs becomes distinct. The amber line gathers toward the head. Somewhere behind the bark, a pad takes hold of a surface that was not there before.

The plants ease forward again.

It is tempting to call the farther structures another animal, one that shares the shelter with the louse. There are differences between them. The transparent limbs have no hard plates. Their pads seem capable of absorbing silt. In one surviving patch of light, something resembling a mouth can be seen among them: a narrow fold with a pale fringe that closes successively from one end to the other.

But when the water louse loses the tip of an antenna, the fringe develops a gap.

The missing part is not replaced quickly. For a season the louse tests every narrow opening with its uninjured side first. The gap in the fringe gradually fills with short, colorless threads. When the new antenna tip appears, those threads are gone.

There are no eggs in the hollow. There are empty skins.

They are difficult to distinguish from leaves. A shed plate becomes clear almost immediately, and its margins soften into fibers. The older ones have green branching lines through them. Some are rooted to the clay by what were once the sockets of legs.

The animal browses these skins as readily as any other surface. It is especially thorough around the joints. There may be nourishment there, or something that must be removed before the green can spread.

One old skin lies beneath the lowest terrace. It is larger than the animal now living near the entrance. Not much larger: only enough that the present body could turn around inside it.

The louse sometimes goes there and remains out of sight.

On its return it carries no food. It moves slowly, with all its legs distinct, and settles beneath the bark before beginning to clean itself. It attends to the antennae first, then the front legs, then the small overlapping plates at its tail. The fourth pair it leaves untouched.

Behind it, on the lowest terrace, a pale joint bends beneath the bark.

Another takes hold beside it.

For a long time there is no further movement. Then the white animal shifts a little nearer the entrance, making room under its curl of bark, although nothing visible has come to join it.
