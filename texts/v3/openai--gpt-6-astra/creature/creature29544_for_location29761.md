The broad swimmers come close to the walls to sleep.

In the open water they seem almost circular, a breadth of dark flesh traveling without effort between one column and the next. Near a ledge, their shape becomes less simple. The front edge folds inward. Two short limbs emerge from beneath it and find holds in the rootwork. The rest of the body settles against the wall, leaving a narrow space through which water continues to pass.

They sleep with their weight on these limbs and their faces under the ledge.

One has rested here often enough to make two hollows. Its left gripping foot has fewer digits than its right, and the hollows differ accordingly. The animal has no knowledge of the shape of its own feet. Nevertheless, in the last small movement before it settles, it shifts sideways until each foot is in the proper place.

It is longer than a person, though much of that length is fin. A muscular body occupies the center, blunt at the head and tapering behind into a short, heavy tail. The fins extend from its sides in loose folds. In motion their edges carry a traveling wrinkle, so slight that the animal appears to be moved by the water it is actually pushing against.

Its back is the color of the oldest root skin. Its underside is pale, with irregular dark patches where blood vessels lie close to the surface. These patches change shape slowly as the animal rests.

There are small eyes, useful chiefly for knowing whether something has passed overhead. Most of its face is given to other senses.

Below the mouth hang six thick feelers. Each divides near the end into soft, fingerlike branches. During a crossing they are bundled backward against the throat. At a wall they spread.

The animal touches the sill, the ceiling, a seam between fused stems. Its feelers are pale enough to be mistaken for new roots. Only their decisions distinguish them: one tip lingers, another withdraws, two follow the same groove in opposite directions.

Under the skin of its face are cavities that receive vibrations through the jaw. The whole country sounds against its bones.

***

It feeds on the inside of the architecture.

Not on the load-bearing roots, which are tough and poor in nourishment, but on the soft growth in the channels between them. Films thicken there. Silt catches. Small creatures live and die in folds that the current cannot scour.

The swimmer puts its mouth over a channel opening and seals its broad lips against the rim. Then the floor of its throat lowers.

For several moments nothing visible happens.

Deep inside the wall, material begins to move against the usual flow. A clot loosens. Threads of pale film stretch and part. Water clouds beneath the animal’s cheeks, and the loose skin of its throat fills until it hangs like a pouch.

It closes its mouth, presses the water out through fringed slits behind its head, and swallows what remains.

The sound is low and abrupt. You feel it in a hand laid against the wall.

Little builders come out after the swimmer moves on. They work around the cleaned opening, fastening exposed root tips and grazing the damaged tissue. Some of the smallest are swallowed during a feeding. Others are dislodged and settle a few body lengths away. The larger ones retreat at the first reversal of the current, gripping deep inside their nests until the pull has passed.

A swimmer visits many openings but empties only a few. At most it touches the rim, waits, and leaves.

Its feelers detect the taste of crowded life. Its jaw detects how water is moving within the wall. A channel nearly stopped with food sounds different from a channel already clear. So does a chamber full of eggs.

The animal does not always distinguish them correctly.

There are seams whose inhabitants withdraw whenever its shadow arrives.

***

Each adult keeps a route through several basins.

The route is not a boundary. Other swimmers cross it, sleep along it, and feed at its openings. But its owner knows the intervals: how long to drift before a column begins to sound close, where a descending current will take the body beneath a terrace, which wall has a warm taste of decay and which tastes only of growing wood.

There are places where it turns before its feelers reach anything.

A young swimmer traveling the same passage stays near the floor. The old one crosses the middle of the opening with water all around it, correcting its course by minute movements of its fin edges. It knows this volume without needing to touch its limits.

A substantial part of its brain is occupied by such distances.

It retains them longer than it retains particular injuries, longer perhaps than it retains the animals with which it has mated. A scar across its back no longer troubles it. A passage that narrowed many years ago still causes it to draw in its fins.

This is how the vegetation grows clear of their paths. Where a fin repeatedly brushes a tender shoot, the shoot dies or thickens away from the contact. Where a swimmer rests, its feet inhibit growth in two small patches. Where it feeds, an opening remains open.

The animal becomes familiar with a route that its familiarity helps preserve.

Occasionally two adults approach the same narrow passage from opposite ends. Neither hurries. They stop with their faces several body lengths apart and remain there, suspended in the current.

One releases a pulse of water from its throat.

The other answers, or does not. After a while one turns partly on its side, folding a fin against its body, and both pass through.

An old animal with an injured fin may refuse to fold it. Such an encounter can take a very long time.

***

Their young live in the root fur.

They begin as narrow, almost transparent things, with gripping limbs disproportionately large and fins no broader than loose flaps of skin. They eat the smaller animals that remove the burrowers’ pellets. When disturbed, they flatten against a root and curl their feelers beneath their mouths.

For a long time they are small enough to enter the builders’ passages.

The builders tolerate them until they begin to feed on eggs. Then a nest may close around a juvenile’s face: roots drawn together, soft bodies crowding behind them, blunt jaws taking hold of feelers. A young swimmer can lose the branching ends of all six in one incautious visit.

They regrow, unevenly.

The young gradually move from holes to cracks, from cracks to the undersides of leaves. There is a difficult interval when they are too broad to hide well and too weak to cross a basin. Long fish wait beneath the terrace lips for them.

Those that survive spend much of their early growth pressed against the ceiling of some shallow recess, learning the sound of the water outside.

Eventually one lets go.

Its first crossing is usually downward. It leaves a high lip and descends with its fins stretched rigidly, expending almost no strength, hoping in whatever bodily way it hopes to encounter another surface before anything encounters it.

Some miss the shelf.

Below the outer lobes, the columns continue into darkness. A small swimmer may find one and cling to it. There is food on the column, but less of it, and the sounds reaching through the wood are difficult to separate. The animal begins to climb.

A few return beneath the terraces much later, thin-bodied, their gripping feet worn raw. They rest at the first thick seam they find.

Others continue downward.

***

Adults sometimes travel together.

They do not remain side by side. One follows several lengths behind the other, entering the disturbances left by the leading animal’s fins. In that water it can read fatigue, hesitation, the slight asymmetry of an old wound.

At feeding places they separate. At difficult passages the leader waits.

Such pairings can persist through many circuits. The animals rest in neighboring hollows, their feelers occasionally meeting along the wall. Contact is brief but repeated. A feeler laid across another’s mouth is gently pushed away. Later it returns.

When they mate, they hang beneath a terrace with their pale undersides turned together and their gripping feet fastened to the ceiling. Their fins surround them in slack folds. Small grazers continue across the backs of both animals.

Afterward they may travel together again. Sometimes they leave in different directions.

The eggs are laid singly, far apart, in pockets where a clean current passes through the rootwork. Each is enclosed in a tough, porous skin. Builders fasten stems around them as they would around any persistent object in a useful hollow.

The parent touches each egg after placing it, then touches the surrounding wall.

On later circuits it may stop at that opening without feeding.

Whether it recognizes the egg, its own secretion, or simply a recent change in a familiar place would be difficult to determine. When the young animal has gone, the adult often continues to stop there.

***

The swimmer with the damaged left foot has lived along one route for a very long time.

At the far end of its circuit is a passage beneath a low ridge. It used to enter without slowing. Now it rests its face against the sill and tests the opening with all six feelers.

Something inside has changed.

A bundle of stems has thickened where another channel was lost. The water still passes, but the swimmer’s body no longer can. It feeds at the entrance. It scrapes the inner surface with the hard edge of its lower jaw. The builders gather behind its mouth and begin fastening what it loosens.

For many circuits the animal returns to this work.

Then, on one visit, it touches the sill and turns aside.

The alternate way is longer. It leads around the end of the ridge and through a basin the animal previously crossed only when following a mate. At first it travels close to the ground. Its feelers drag lightly over the root skin.

Later it begins to rise.

There is a place beside a column where the current lifts its heavier left side. It pauses there, held level without effort. Fine material moves past its mouth. Somewhere within the column, an obstructed channel makes a sound it has not investigated before.

The swimmer spreads its feelers.

Behind it, beyond the ridge, the old passage continues to narrow. Ahead, its jaw rests against the wood, listening.
