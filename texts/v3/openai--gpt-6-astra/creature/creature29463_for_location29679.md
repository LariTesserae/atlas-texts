In one of the narrow basins, the reflection of a partition has a pale interruption. It remains when you move your head.

At first it seems to be a piece of glaze lifted from the bottom: a broad, translucent skin, faintly yellow at its thickest edge. Water passes over it. Beneath it, the scratches left by the maker’s blade are still visible, though slightly enlarged.

Then a notch opens in its margin and travels slowly around it.

The creature is wider than your spread hands and almost without thickness. Most of it lies against the ceramic. Around its perimeter is a milky band containing small, repeated structures like the seeds in a cut fruit. These contract in sequence, drawing the edge forward. The rest follows, not quite sliding, not quite flowing. Where it crosses a repaired crack, its middle gathers into three shallow pleats.

There are others in the basin. Once you have seen the first, the apparently clean bottom becomes difficult to look at. Here is a smaller one beneath the outlet. Here are two overlapping so closely that only a doubled edge distinguishes them. Along the wall, a large specimen has climbed above the waterline. It wears the water as a film.

Its exposed surface dulls in the air. Immediately, little furrows open across it, bringing moisture up from below.

This is why you have seen no tracks.

They pass along the wetted walls, beneath ledges, through the shallow streams on the clay tongues. On a broad slope one spreads almost to invisibility. At a constriction it gathers itself into a thick ribbon. It takes several minutes to negotiate a hole narrower than a finger, and during that time the structures in its margin crowd together into a cloudy knot.

It does not release one surface until it has secured another.

A drop falling clear of a spout would not necessarily kill it. But a creature carried in that drop would land folded upon itself, its delicate underside pressed against the wrong surface, its margins stuck together. In deep water it might open again. On a dry ledge it would remain a small, dull lump until someone found it.

The sloping tongues are built generously. Even the smallest has room for two to pass.

You may watch them for a long while before understanding that these are the makers.

One comes to a place where water has begun to escape through a seam. It settles over the leak. For some time nothing happens except that the dark patch below the seam stops spreading.

Then the creature draws its outer parts inward.

Its body thickens. The broad film becomes a low mound, clear at the edges and cloudy within. Several of the seedlike structures rise through it and gather in two opposed groups. Between them, a pocket opens to the air.

Two short limbs take shape.

They are not especially graceful. Each ends in a pad and three unequal projections, with the innermost set somewhat apart. Their surfaces acquire fine wrinkles as they lose water. One grips the wall while the other reaches beneath a little roof and takes down a spatula.

The thumbprint at the spout was a thumbprint.

It works slowly, keeping most of its body spread in the pool. A narrow wet tether joins that body to the raised part. When the tether begins to thin, it puts the tool down and subsides. After a rest, it gathers itself again.

A second creature arrives with a bead of dark paste held in a fold.

Their edges meet. The newcomer waits while the worker removes the old material from the joint. Then it brings the paste forward, and for a moment the two are joined by so many fine, transparent bridges that it is impossible to say which one is holding it.

They separate without haste.

The repair is slightly untidy. The worker scrapes it back, examines it with the underside of one pad, and scrapes again.

They have preferences in workmanship. Some always leave a rounded junction; others cut it square. A particular maker along the eastern terraces finishes every patch with three light impressions, even where only a single impression would smooth it adequately. Another has made several dozen outlets with an unnecessary little turn at the tip. The water follows that turn. So does everyone using the outlet.

Not all of them make things.

In the deeper courts are large inhabitants that rarely gather themselves into limbs. They lie folded around the bases of the porous slabs, their margins extending into several adjoining pools. Smaller creatures move over them. Now and then one of these great, nearly stationary bodies contracts, and a pulse travels through the water, too slight to disturb the surface but strong enough to lift the fringes of its neighbours.

The valley is not silent to them.

They feel the drag of water through a seam. They feel a basin begin to empty three walls away. A mallet striking a fitting sends a blunt disturbance through the ceramic, and nearby bodies flatten until the work is done.

Their own signals are made against surfaces. An edge grips and releases; a thickened pad presses twice, then remains still. In shallow water these movements carry farther than the bodies that make them.

At a crowded junction you can see a request pass from one creature to another. Several withdraw into the side basins. A broad, heavily folded individual comes through, carrying something opaque within itself.

It is a broken butterfly repair, each half wrapped separately so the sharp edges cannot cut.

There are disputes. Two bodies can remain opposed at a notch for hours, alternately covering and uncovering it. Neither strikes the other. Downstream, the water rises and falls by the thickness of a nail. Eventually a third inhabitant arrives, presses along both their margins, and stays between them.

Sometimes this helps.

The young live high on the ridges.

They are easier to see than the adults because their bodies contain a little chalk-white mass. Each occupies one of the spoonful pools, turning around its pale centre with patient, uneven contractions. The smallest cannot yet climb a vertical wall. Their basins have low inward-curving rims.

An adult visiting them spreads over several cups at once. Its edges dip into each, leaving a trace of cloudy substance that disperses slowly. Afterwards it rests beneath the perforated screens.

Some cups remain empty for a season. They are kept wet all the same.

As the young grow, the white mass diminishes. A margin forms. Eventually one reaches over its rim, finds the damp slope beyond, and begins the long descent through the galleries. It may reverse direction many times. Adults passing it leave room.

The oldest inhabitants are not necessarily the largest. Some have narrow opaque scars that will no longer spread thinly. These must choose wider passages. Others have lost the ability to raise a working hand, though they can still brace a wall with their bodies while a neighbour sets a patch.

There is one near the lower end of the valley with a small fired-clay bar permanently enclosed in its flesh. The bar supports an old injury. When it moves through a shallow pool, the ends show as two pale elevations. Someone has polished them smooth.

You never find where the clay is fired. Occasionally warmth comes from a deep opening, carrying a dry smell into the wet air. Creatures emerge from such places gathered thickly around fresh pieces of ceramic. They lower the pieces into cooling pools and wait beside them. The routes beneath the paving are busy, but too narrow for you to follow.

Above, there is little wear because almost no one bears weight on the paths. The broad roughened walls are working places, used when something must be lifted clear of the water. What looked like a valley awaiting inhabitants has scarcely a basin unoccupied.

Late in the afternoon, beneath the newly overflowing trough, several bodies gather on the waiting tongue.

The first lengthens into the advancing water. It stops where the wet clay ends. Another comes alongside it, then a third, their clear margins pressed together.

As the darkened line moves down the slope, they move with it.

Farther below is a court that has not held water before. Its fittings are complete. In the dry recesses, small tools have been laid ready.

The inhabitants enter along the walls.
