At first, the thing beneath the footbridge looks like another load of roots.

It is caught against the downstream pier, where the current turns back on itself. Brown fibers stream from it. Now and then it rolls far enough to show a pale patch, then settles into the brown water again. Only when the worker comes onto the bridge with the rake does it lift its head.

There are two small ears, folded almost shut, and a broad, blunt muzzle carrying a fringe of stiff white whiskers. Its eyes sit high enough to remain above water while the rest of the face is submerged. One eye is clouded. The other follows the rake.

The worker stamps twice on the bridge.

The animal releases its hold and sinks. A moment later it surfaces beyond the shed, pushing a bundle of sedge stems before it.

The workers call them bank bears, though they are not much like bears out of the water. Their bodies are long and low, their hind feet disproportionately broad, their tails short, flattened, and almost hidden by the coarse hair of the rump. A large female is about the length of the rake. Most of what makes her look substantial is fur: dark underwool beneath long, pale-tipped guard hairs, with a greenish stain along the flanks where algae grow on the oldest strands.

On firm ground she walks with an awkward rolling motion. On a peat raft she spreads her feet and becomes unexpectedly light. She can pass over a patch that gives way beneath a much smaller, narrow-footed animal, placing each paw among the stems and pausing until the surface steadies.

Her forefeet are not fully webbed. They have thick, mobile toes and curved nails worn flat at the ends. She uses them to hold food against her chest, pull apart root masses, and feel along the underside of a bank. Underwater, with her eyes closed, she can find the opening of her own passage among a hundred trailing roots.

The brown fibers seen beneath the bridge were partly vegetation and partly her coat.

She has been collecting material from the railing.

***

Bank bears live in the shallow country, but they do not sleep in the water.

Their homes are inside the thicker peat islands, where a cap of moss rests on years of dead sedge roots. From above, an occupied island looks only a little fuller than its neighbors. The sedges may lean outward from a low mound. There may be a patch of unusually green growth where the animals bring up mud, or three clipped stems standing together beside a hole.

Beneath that cover is a chamber lined with dry grass.

Keeping it dry occupies much of their lives.

They enlarge it upward as the water rises, biting roots loose from the ceiling and pressing them into the floor. They carry in fresh stems, strip off the wet outer leaves, and tuck the pale inner portions beneath their bodies. They open new entrances at higher levels and plug old ones with material dragged from below. In prolonged rain, a family may move its sleeping place several times without ever leaving the island.

Eventually there is nowhere higher to go.

Then they leave.

The old female near the pump shed has three resting places across the adjoining basins. One is in a rooted island, one beneath a willow whose branches have grown into a loose cage, and one inside the maintenance house, behind the boarded lower windows. She reaches the house through a broken drain.

The workers know about that one. There are tooth marks on the bottom of the storeroom door, and the spare sacks have gradually disappeared into the wall cavity.

They have moved the electrical cable to a higher shelf.

***

They eat the tender white bases of sedges, pondweed stems, freshwater mussels, and the fat larvae found inside drowned turf. In the flooded meadows they turn small mats of vegetation over with both forepaws, working along their edges until worms and beetles tumble free. Fish interest them chiefly when trapped in a pocket of retreating water. A healthy fish in an open channel is usually too much effort.

Their teeth leave neat, slanting cuts. Along a feeding bank, the clipped vegetation can look as though someone has been tidying with shears.

They also cut stems they do not intend to eat.

These they carry crosswise in their mouths, often in bundles so large that only their eyes show above them. At the sleeping island they push the stems into weak places, thread them through living roots, or wedge them beneath a shelf that has begun to tear. They are persistent rather than skillful. Much of what they place is washed away. Enough remains to slow the water and catch finer debris.

Older islands may have generations of this work inside them.

Pull apart a fallen section and there are layers: black peat, pale sedge, willow twigs, mud, another black layer. Occasionally there is something brought from the road—a frayed length of rope, insulation stripped from a wire, a glove stiffened into the fabric of the bank.

The animals’ work makes sheltered water for insects and fry. Ducks nest on the firmer mounds. Small birds follow a feeding bank bear, landing behind it to search the turf it has exposed.

None of this prevents a flood from taking the whole structure away.

***

The young are born well furred, with their eyes open but their feet still soft and folded.

For several weeks they remain in the upper chamber. An adult coming home announces itself by rubbing its teeth together: a faint, dry ticking that carries through the packed roots. The young answer with high squeaks, almost lost outside beneath the sound of rain.

Several females may share an island. They sleep pressed together, and a returning animal will climb over the others, nosing into the warm spaces until it finds its own young. The smallest disappear completely beneath the adults’ long flank fur.

When a chamber floods, the adults carry them out one at a time, gripping the loose skin above the shoulders.

This is when the waterbirds gather.

A bank bear can swim while carrying a youngster, but cannot dive far without letting it go. The large scavengers from the escarpment learn to watch an island that is riding too low. They circle without haste. Below them, the adults make repeated journeys between the failing nest and whatever shelter remains.

The workers sometimes see these crossings from the house.

There is a wooden fish box beneath the kitchen table, lined with an old coat. It has been used twice. Its corners are thoroughly chewed.

***

The old female avoids the plateau’s outer channels.

There is food there, and fewer competing families, but the water behaves differently near the rim. A gust can hide the pull of an outlet. A broad, apparently shallow passage may narrow beneath a peat shelf and carry everything under it toward the falling water.

She approaches unfamiliar channels with her whiskers spread and her forepaws resting on the bottom. Before committing herself, she lets her body drift backward a little. If the pull is too strong, she scrambles out.

Young animals are less cautious.

After some storms, calls travel between the islands long after daylight: short, husky notes, repeated with long pauses for an answer. Bank bears ordinarily keep in touch at close quarters, through roots and water. Calling over an open basin exposes them. An adult searching for a missing youngster will climb onto a stone and call anyway.

Their voices are surprisingly small.

***

The running pump troubles them.

Its vibration reaches the sleeping islands through the submerged ground. When it first starts, heads appear in entrances all around the basin. Some animals slap the water with their forefeet. Others move their young.

After several days, most become accustomed to it. The old female has found that the discharge stirs food from the channel bed, and she feeds below it between visits to the bridge.

Her repairs and the workers’ maintenance are often in conflict. She stuffs sedges into a gap; they rake them out. She fastens a torn root mat to the upstream rail; they spend an hour cutting it loose. Once she dragged nearly the whole coil of cable from beneath the eaves before its weight stopped her.

The stamping is an arrangement of sorts. She has learned that it precedes the rake.

At dusk she comes back to the downstream pier. The worker is inside the shed, leaning close to the engine to listen for a fault. Water moves along the old wheel tracks. Beyond the bridge, one of the rooted islands lifts slightly, settles, and lifts again.

The female puts her nose beneath its torn edge.

For a while only her hind feet show, sculling gently. Then she backs out with a mouthful of black fibers and swims toward the maintenance house.

Inside the wall, above the rising water, something answers her ticking.
