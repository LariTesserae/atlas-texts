The one holding the thorn has a damaged foot.

It is the left hind foot, and it does not spread completely. When the worker braces against the wall, five points of contact hold and the sixth slips until it finds a fiber. The other worker waits for this without looking back. They have carried things together before.

Up close, the citizens are not quite the pale color they seem in the entrance light. Their backs are smoky and translucent, with a yellow thickening where the limbs join the body. A meal shows darkly through the underside. Older individuals have cloudy plates behind the head, scratched by passages they could once fit through easily. Their feet open into several soft-edged gripping lobes. On wet bark these spread flat; on cord they close around one another.

They usually walk on four limbs and keep the front pair free. In a difficult passage they use all six, carrying tools beneath the mouth.

The mouth is small and complicated. It can scrape a film from a wall, cut a strand held taut between the forefeet, or clean the hinge of another citizen’s limb. During delicate work, two pale mouthparts extend beyond the flattened head and hold an object so lightly that a tightening fiber can draw it away.

The thorn worker tests the fresh opening with these.

There is sweetness in the split, but also something sharp that makes the mouthparts fold back. The worker retreats half a body length. The other lays down the cord.

For a while neither touches the opening.

They are not waiting for an instruction. Freshly exposed tissue changes as the root closes around its wound. Some splits become safe to work after the wetness has dulled. Others continue to sting for days.

The worker with the cord goes to fetch a dry scraping from an older wall. They will press it against the exposed edge and test again later.

This is much of what they know as building.

***

An adult citizen could lie across the broad end of a sedge seed and leave room beside itself. The young are smaller chiefly in the abdomen; their feet and heads look too large. They catch themselves well when they fall, but cannot yet judge which surface will bear them.

A youngster will reach confidently for a reflection on the pool.

For this reason, the nursery rooms have low ceilings, and every floor fiber is fastened at both ends. The very smallest are kept among curls of dry pith. They chew these, sleep against them, and are moved to a fresh curl when the old one grows slick.

Their eggs are laid separately, not in a mass. Each is fixed by a short stalk beneath a roof flake, where an adult can pass a foot around it and feel its entire surface. There are eggs in ordinary dwellings as well as in the nursery. Some are attended by two citizens, some by several, and some are carried to another house before they hatch.

The little animals that move over the eggs are not all driven away. One kind eats the sour growth that gathers at the stalk. Another punctures the egg.

They feel almost identical.

Nursery attendants learn the difference by holding one gently against the soft joint beneath a forelimb. The harmless kind keeps moving. The other settles and begins to work its mouth.

An attendant who has done this for many seasons has a scatter of tiny dark marks along the joint.

The newly hatched citizens learn such things long before they learn the layout of the town. A youngster is allowed to touch a roof grazer, then the empty skin of a ceiling hunter, then a living grazer again. An adult holds its antennae lightly apart so that it does not confuse one impression with the next.

There are mistakes, impatience, youngsters who turn away, and adults who teach too quickly.

In one nursery, a particular piece of harmless curled fiber has frightened three successive groups of hatchlings. The attendants keep meaning to remove it. It is useful for hanging a drinking bead.

***

Their antennae are not simply feelers.

Near the base they sense movement in air and changes in dampness. Farther out are joints that can be held stiff or allowed to trail. The tips are fine enough to find the notch left by a thorn point.

When two citizens meet, they usually touch antennae on one side only. A brief crossing is enough between familiar individuals. A stranger is examined more slowly: mouth, harness if there is one, the plates behind the head. Odors linger in these places. A citizen arriving from another root brings a whole recent route on its body—stale water, crushed sedge, the dry powder of a winter seed case.

Much of their conversation happens with the forefeet against a shared surface.

On a taut line, a small sequence of pulls can travel beyond reach. On a wall, they make close, rapid pressures that another citizen feels through the feet or belly. Detailed exchanges require contact. Two workers discussing an anchorage may remain head to head for a long time, touching a knot between them, then one another, then the knot again.

Names are used at this distance.

The thorn worker’s name includes a double pressure and a brief outward turn. It cannot be given accurately through a loose bridge. At a distance, others identify the worker by a simpler signal associated with the tool.

The damaged foot is not part of either name.

It was injured in an earlier chamber, when a doorway closed around a load being brought through. The worker could have released the load sooner. Several people know this. One still brings it up when they disagree about how tightly to pack a carrying basket.

***

Their food comes from many places and must be kept apart.

Sweet wall scrapings ferment if stored wet. Grazer eggs keep well in the coolest baskets, but only after the stalk ends have been sealed with a particular sap. Seed dust is valuable because it can be eaten dry. A soft-bodied swimmer taken from the pool is held against a sloping surface until its water has run off.

They tend patches of edible film beneath loose bark flakes. A patch may be fed with chewed pith and washed with water brought from one drinking place but not another. Different households favor different films. The smells mingle beneath the storehouse: sour, green, faintly oily.

There are citizens who complain that everything tastes of the new root.

They remember a chamber farther inland where the walls released less bitterness. They also remember hunger there, though not always in the same conversation.

Waste is carried below the last drinking place. Molted skins are sorted. Clean ones become packing; damaged ones go to a damp recess where smaller animals break them down. Before a molt, a citizen grows slow and irritable, and the gripping edges of its feet begin to lift. Someone checks its room for hunters. Someone else leaves a portion of soft food within reach.

For a little while afterward, its body is almost clear.

The others touch it cautiously. Even an old acquaintance feels unfamiliar before the new plates harden.

***

The town has people who are good at particular work, but few who do only one thing.

Bridge tenders must know knots, current, and the behavior of the armored grazer. Nursery attendants know which wall films can be eaten without preparation. Anyone living near the entrance is expected to help lift the lower drinking trough when the flow reverses.

The six-room storehouse has a keeper who remembers quantities by touch. Along the inside of one support are short cords, each tied in a different arrangement. These can be removed and carried to a household when its contribution is in dispute.

Disputes are common.

Dry fiber takes effort to gather. The safest sleeping rooms are limited. A household may guide a rootlet toward its own wall and leave another household with a narrowing passage. Someone always wants to keep a doorway open after the surrounding tissue has begun to strain.

When a disagreement concerns a structure, the people involved assemble on it.

They feel what the others are claiming. Sometimes this settles the matter. Sometimes it only establishes that everyone has understood the problem and still wants different things.

The empty spaces are especially difficult to defend.

A clear terrace looks available. A basket placed there for one night becomes three baskets. Then a rain cover is tied above them. By the time the floor begins to swell, somebody has a convincing account of how long that household has used the site.

Several citizens spend a considerable part of their lives persuading others to move things a short distance.

***

Beyond the roofed streets, the ceiling hunters remain.

A hunter may be motionless for so long that a new worker mistakes it for a thickened fiber. When it extends, the change is abrupt but nearly silent. Its front end finds the vibration before the rest of its body has followed.

The citizens do not hunt them routinely. A wounded hunter becomes difficult to locate, and the smaller grazers multiply quickly where a ceiling territory is left empty. Instead, the citizens watch where each one feeds and move the covered crossings when necessary.

Occasionally a hunter comes down.

Then the nearest citizens flatten against the bark. Those farther away pull hard on the alarm lines. Work crews arrive with forked thorns and a loose mesh that can be dropped without putting a body beneath it.

Sometimes they drive the animal back upward.

Sometimes afterward there is a tool to retrieve and nobody to return it to.

A recovered body is carried outside when the water is low. This takes more citizens than carrying it through the town would require. The sedge bases have open ground between them, and on open ground there is nowhere to press oneself flat enough.

The returning group is checked carefully for clinging animals.

For several nights, the dead citizen’s usual sleeping companions close their room too tightly. Its absence leaves more air than they are accustomed to warming.

***

They have lived in many chambers, though no one alive has seen more than a few.

What passes between towns is portable: thorn tools, dry eggs, knots tied around short sticks, preparations for food, warnings about particular smells. Some families carry a tiny roof flake from a former dwelling. It has no structural use. It is brought out for the young to examine, then wrapped again before its edges can crumble.

The polished thorn at the new opening has been shortened twice.

Its oldest notch no longer serves any purpose the worker understands. During sharpening, that part is left untouched.

Inside the swelling root, the worker returns to the split. The dry scraping has darkened, but the exposed tissue no longer stings. The fork is inserted. Cord is passed around it and fastened to a support far enough away to allow for growth.

The second worker puts its head against the floor.

Beyond the opening, something moves over a yielding surface. Many small feet, perhaps. Then a pause, and a faint dragging.

They remain still until it has passed.

The thorn worker turns the fork by a little less than the width of one gripping lobe. Wet air touches the underside of its head. There is room beyond, but no certainty yet about its shape or what feeds there.

A youngster arrives carrying a short length of cord. It is too short for the work and badly stripped, with damp shreds still attached.

The second worker takes it anyway and makes a small loop beside the safe edge of the opening. The youngster grips this with both forefeet.

Held there, it can lean forward without falling. Its antennae enter the new darkness, withdraw, and enter again.
