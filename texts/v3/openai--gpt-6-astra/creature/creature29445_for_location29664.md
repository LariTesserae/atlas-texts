On the underside of the tipped slab, something has cleared the sediment from a patch of stone.

The clean area is no larger than a hand. It has an irregular border, with narrow extensions following the shallow grooves in the rock. At first it appears to be another exposed surface. Then one of the extensions shortens. A little sediment falls away, and the clean patch lifts enough to show that it has an underside of its own.

The animal is pressed so closely to the slab that its back has taken the shape of the stone.

It is young. Light passing through its body reveals a few cloudy strands, a darker knot near one end, and a system of fine canals that divide and join again without quite repeating themselves. There is no shell. Along its edge are eight short limbs, although only five are visible; the others are flattened beneath it, holding it against the rock.

One limb releases. Its tip opens into a small, ridged disc.

For a while, nothing else happens. Then the disc folds shut, reaches past the animal’s border, and settles on a place where a root has begun to enter the gap.

The root bends under the touch. The animal withdraws the limb and chooses bare stone instead.

It will take most of the afternoon to bring the rest of its body forward.

There are larger ones in the forest.

Their backs no longer show everything inside them. They become cloudy with age, the color of pale stone viewed through several yards of water, with patches of brown where small growths have established themselves. An adult can cover the end of a broken trunk. The oldest are wider than the spaces between some of the trees, but width is not a fixed property of them. They can gather themselves into a thick, narrow shape, passing through a gap one fold at a time.

They have more structure than this suggests. Beneath the soft back is a cage of slender, flexible rods, joined near the darker end of the body. The rods spread when the animal rests and draw together when it moves. Between them lie sheets of muscle so thin that sunlight can pass through an adult’s raised edge.

The limbs are longer than they first appear. Each is usually stored in a deep groove beneath the body. Extended, it may reach farther than the animal is wide.

An undisturbed adult keeps three or four attached to the slope, another against a trunk, and one free in the water. The free limb turns slowly. Its open disc receives movements too slight to stir a leaf.

This is how it notices the fish.

Before the fish emerges from the trees, the disc closes. Two limbs loosen from the stone. A fold travels across the animal’s back, drawing in its margins.

The fish passes overhead.

The animal remains contracted long after the leaves have stopped moving. Then its free limb opens again, and the body gradually resumes its previous shape.

It is capable of moving quickly. If touched beneath its lifted edge, it can pull all its limbs inward and throw itself several body lengths through the water. For that brief interval it looks quite different: compact, blunt at one end, trailing a loose skirt at the other. It steers by opening the skirt unevenly.

The landing is less controlled. It catches whatever is within reach and may spend an hour disentangling itself without breaking anything.

Most of its journeys are made along the bottom.

Under the darker end is a mouth: a narrow opening surrounded by fine, closely packed folds. It does not crop the leaves. It feeds where warm water passes through new stone, drawing the seepage beneath its body and holding it there.

The folds inside its mouth are rust-colored.

In a young animal they are almost white. Their color deepens after it has occupied a fissure for some time. Tiny living grains settle among them, multiplying in the warmth and in the faint mineral taste of water from below. The animal tends this growth with movements too small to see without lifting it from its place. It lets water through, closes some passages, opens others. Periodically the mouth folds inward, and part of the rust-colored lining is digested.

A good fissure can sustain an adult for years.

This leaves much of the forest untouched. A broad leaf may rest across an animal’s back for a season without losing its edge. Snails cross it. Ribbon roots sometimes fasten to the thin coating of sediment that gathers in its folds.

When the animal eventually moves, those roots restrain it.

It does not pull free at once. It shifts a little, waits, shifts again. Some roots release. Others stretch. An old individual may travel with several feet of ribbon plant following it, until the plant finds another hold.

Their bodies carry traces of these occupations. There are grooves left by roots, pale areas that have spent years against stone, small hard inclusions around which the flesh has closed. An animal resting upside down beneath a ledge may retain, across its back, the shallow pattern of a place it left long ago.

These marks fade, but slowly.

At the outer margin, some of the larger depressions in the water are theirs.

They come there infrequently, leaving the warm fissures and crossing the forest toward the unsupported shore. No single route serves them all. One follows a descending fracture and then climbs the rim from below. Another moves through the crowns, distributing its weight among many branches. The leaves turn aside beneath it and return imperfectly, leaving a pale opening that closes over several days.

At the last stone, the animal stops.

A limb extends into the clear water beyond the cliff. Its disc touches the boundary and spreads.

From beneath the lake, the point of contact looks silver. From the air, it would be a shallow bulge, with the small ridges of the disc visible through it.

The animal tests several places before going farther.

Then it releases the stone.

It hangs entirely against the boundary, its limbs set wide, its body suspended above the clouds. The water bears its weight as reluctantly as it yields to the stems along the rim. A hollow forms around each disc. Between them the animal settles into a larger hollow, until its back and the outside air are separated by only a thin thickness of water.

Here its underside receives light from unfamiliar directions. Clouds brighten beneath it. A dark mountain passes in and out of view without moving. On clear days there are rivers far below, narrow and white, which its sensing limbs cannot feel.

It remains there through the afternoon.

As the surrounding water cools, the animal spreads itself thinner. The cloudy tissues separate into translucent bands. Small chambers become visible along the inner bases of its limbs, each containing a folded shape.

Not every animal that visits the margin has these chambers. Some come empty and leave empty. Others rest beside an occupied hollow, close enough for their limbs to touch, though no crowd gathers and there is no particular season when the rim is busy.

The young are released after sunset.

A chamber opens beneath the body, and a small creature unfolds into the water. It has the same eight limbs, disproportionately long, and a back so clear that only its movement distinguishes it from the lake. For a moment it holds the adult with all eight discs.

Then it lets go.

It does not descend immediately. It hangs in the slight exchange of warm and cool water along the rim, opening and closing its limbs. Several may drift in the same place without touching.

The adult catches those that move too near the boundary. A limb curls beneath each one and returns it to the water on the inward side. This continues through the night.

By morning, most have gone among the dark-leaved stems.

Some settle there. Others are carried farther into the basin by currents that barely exist at the scale of an adult body. They lodge beneath leaves, in the folded bases of ribbons, or against the rough faces of recently broken stone.

Very few find a warm fissure while they are still small.

The one beneath the eastern slab has found only a thread of seepage. It emerges from a crack narrower than a root hair, crosses the stone, and loses itself in the lake.

The young animal has been following it.

By evening it reaches the opening. Its limbs gather close. The ridged discs take hold on either side, and its back spreads across the crack until the clean patch seems once again to be part of the slab.

Above it, the three shoots continue growing. Sediment slips into the lower corner. The root that was touched in the afternoon advances a little farther into the gap.

Beneath the animal, where the warm water is held against its mouth, the first few folds are beginning to darken.
