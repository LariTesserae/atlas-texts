The larger animal at the basin is only large by the hollow’s measure. A person could lift it in both hands, though it would be difficult to keep there.

It drinks with its forefeet braced on the mineral rim and its hindquarters still beneath a leaf. Its body is long, low, and covered in brown fur that parts into little ridges along the spine. The underside is lighter. There is a pale place beneath the jaw that shows when it raises its head, but not while it drinks. Then almost all that can be seen is the working of its throat and the occasional widening of its ears.

One ear has a split in it. The edges have healed separately, leaving two small points that turn together toward a sound.

It takes several mouthfuls, stops, and waits. A bead gathers above it. When the drop falls, the animal blinks but does not retreat.

The smaller creature on the root shifts a foot.

Both remain where they are.

Eventually the drinker backs away, placing a hind foot on a stone it cannot see. Its tail follows across the basin’s edge: nearly bare, dark above, with a slight kink near the tip. The descending animal waits until the tail has gone before coming lower.

By then the larger one has passed beneath the fern.

It does not have a single shelter. It has places for different conditions.

During the hottest part of the day it lies behind the lower terrace, in a space between the fitted stones and the earth they once held back. The entrance is scarcely wider than its head. Beyond it, a root has lifted one stone enough to leave a shallow chamber. There are dry leaf strips inside, some chewed soft, some still stiff enough to scrape against its flank when it turns.

In cooler weather it sleeps farther up, in a hollow branch caught across two rocks. The branch receives a little warmth through one of the roof openings. It also carries more smells from outside. The animal sometimes wakes there without opening its eyes, drawing air through its nose in short, separate breaths.

After rain it may use neither place.

Water has entered the terrace shelter before. The lowest leaf strips are pressed flat and stained red. The animal does not return while that smell is fresh, even when the entrance looks dry.

It knows the hollow in passages of bodily effort: where it must flatten its ears, where the stone gives purchase to its claws, where a gap requires a jump rather than a reach. On one route it turns its head sideways before its shoulder has reached the narrowing. On another it steps over a root that rotted through some time ago. Its foot still rises unnecessarily, though less high than it once did.

Across the bare stone by the southern wall, it usually goes quickly.

Tonight it stops there to work at a seed.

It holds the seed between its forefeet, which look unexpectedly delicate when they are occupied this way. The toes spread and close around the husk. Each time the seed slips, it catches it against its lower teeth and turns it to a new angle. Fine dust adheres to the damp fur beneath its mouth.

The shell opens abruptly. One half falls between its feet. It eats the kernel, searches the empty halves, then noses along a joint in the stone where older husks have collected.

There is food it passes without interest. There is food it cannot reach. The snails under the basin rim have been investigated many times; occasionally a paw goes beneath the lip and comes back wet. A crab of sufficient size makes it withdraw, although it will sit a short distance away and watch the burrow after the crab has vanished.

It eats softer things readily. Fruit that falls through the roof. A beetle turned over before its legs can catch. The white base of a shoot, pulled up and held close to the mouth while the green end trembles with each bite.

For several nights it has been carrying food away instead of finishing it where it is found.

There are three young behind the terrace.

They are old enough to have fur and to quarrel over a piece of leaf, but not old enough to leave quietly. When the female enters, they press toward her with their eyes half shut, climbing over one another until she rolls onto her side. Her belly is sparsely furred. The young knead it with feet that already have the shape of hers, though their claws are still pale.

She rests her chin near the entrance.

A root lies across the outside of the opening. Beyond it is a narrow strip of floor, then the underside of a broad leaf. This is all she can see from where she lies.

After a while, one of the young loses its place and begins to make a thin, repetitive sound. She brings a hind foot forward and scratches beneath her split ear. The sound continues. She shifts her body, and the young animal finds her again.

Much later she comes out carrying a soiled scrap of bedding.

She takes it farther than she takes the seed husks. Around the first heap of stone, through the low passage beside the buried log, past the mushrooms growing along its hidden length. At a crevice she stops and pushes the scrap in with her nose.

On the return journey, something moves above her.

She becomes very low. Her whiskers cease moving. Only her ears turn.

A gecko crosses the wall, pauses with one foot raised, and goes on.

The female remains still a little longer. Then she climbs the terrace by the narrow step, belly close to the stone. At the entrance she stops to smell the root, the earth beside it, and the edge of the opening.

Inside, the young have pulled apart the place where she was lying. One has worked its head beneath a curled leaf and fallen asleep there.

She grips the leaf, draws it free, and settles around them.
