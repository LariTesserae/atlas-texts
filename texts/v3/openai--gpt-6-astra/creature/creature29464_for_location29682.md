The person who lives there is not small. This is one of the first things you learn about them.

In the passage, you might imagine otherwise. They move ahead of you without striking stone, and when they stop there is room for you to stop also. But on the platform, when their knee touches yours, you discover the length of the thigh, the substantial body folded above it. They have broad shoulders. Their hands can close around the thick cup almost entirely.

They fit because they know where to bend.

One shoulder goes forward while the opposite hip lowers. A hand rests against a swelling, not to push it away but to establish where the body is. They wait for you beyond each turn. They do not pull you through by the wrist. If you need help, they put your hand on their elbow and let you feel the movement before attempting it yourself.

Their skin is warm, though no warmer than anything else here. What distinguishes the elbow from the mineral is the small adjustment it makes beneath your fingers.

They speak before you touch their face.

“Here,” they say, although you already know where they are.

Their voice is ordinary. It catches sometimes. They clear their throat. They have learned to speak quietly without whispering, since a whisper loses its shape among the columns. From the platform, their words reach you plainly if you sit on the step. Move a little to either side and the consonants disappear.

They know this, and turn their head when you move.

At first you understand their body only in pieces: a knee beside yours, a palm beneath the cup, the brief contact of a forehead when you both lean toward the same sound. Later, if you meet them outside, it is surprising to see these assembled into someone who can stand at a distance.

They are older than their movements first suggested. Their hair is cut close, unevenly at the back. There are grey hairs above the ears and a pale scar beside the mouth. In daylight they narrow their eyes, then shade them with a hand. They do not recoil from the sky. They look at the clouds with the practical attention of someone who has a journey to make.

They go down the mountain.

Not often enough to keep a path open, but often enough that people below recognize them. They bring things that can be carried uphill without spoiling: meal wrapped in hide, dried roots, a little salt. Sometimes they return with a length of fibre wound around their waist beneath their clothes. Once they carried up a new cup and broke it against their knee in the entrance passage. The sound was so unlike anything farther inside that they sat where they were for a while, holding the pieces.

The old cup remained.

Nothing edible grows in the hollow in sufficient quantity to support them. They know this without disappointment. When the food is low, they leave. When their clothes need drying, they spread them on rock outside and sit nearby, wrapped in the spare cloth kept in a covered niche beyond the entrance. They have been caught by weather there. They have spent long hours watching a cloud wet something the wind had almost dried.

Their life includes these inconveniences.

Inside, they own very little, but the little requires attention. A lashing beneath the platform loosens. A branch begins to give under the hip. They test each support with their weight before lying down. Replacement wood comes up the passage one piece at a time, its ends wrapped so that it will not scrape them as they turn.

They do not mistake the yielding stone for something that cannot be hurt. Nor do they know whether hurt is possible here. They simply dislike driving a hard thing against a surface when there is no need.

The foreign slab was their work. It took most of a day to bring it in. They still occasionally reach underneath to check its position, and the fractured edge remains startling even to them.

They have cut themselves on it twice.

When you ask about the strip of knotted bark, they let you examine it. Some knots are tight and small; others enclose a little loop. The bark has softened, and one knot has drawn so close to another that a fingernail is needed to distinguish them.

They begin to explain, then stop to feel the sequence.

“That one was before the step,” they say.

They cannot give you a year. There are things they remember precisely that would not help you place it: which shoulder hurt during the climb, the taste of a particular sack of meal, a person who came as far as the first bend and decided not to enter.

They remember that person without contempt. It was cold outside. They went out again and sat together below the stone lip.

The small animal beneath the platform is familiar to them, though they do not claim it is always the same one. When it crosses their hand, they hold still. Its feet are perceptible chiefly when they release their hold, a succession of almost weightless detachments.

Once, while repairing a lashing, they found several together in the joint between wood and stone. They put the branch back loosely and repaired the other end first.

They have no name for the growth that smells of cucumber skin. They sometimes touch it for the smell.

There are pleasures here that they would find difficult to explain without making them sound grander than they are. Lowering a tired foot into a basin. Finding the cup on the first reach. Lying with one arm above their head in a space that accommodates the elbow exactly. Hearing someone at the second turn and knowing, from the pause before the next movement, who has come.

They sleep on their side. The platform is just long enough if their legs are bent. Sometimes they wake with a numb hand and have to lift it with the other one. Sometimes they cannot sleep at all.

On those occasions they sit up.

There is no view to consult, no change in the room by which to judge how much of the night remains. They drink. They touch the knots. They listen to a basin release their foot and then become quiet again.

They have lived elsewhere and may do so again. The hollow has made no promise to keep them, and they have made none to stay.

But when you rise to leave, their hand finds the cup before your sleeve can catch it. They hold it while you lower yourself onto the step. Then they put it back in its place and tell you where to reach for the cord.

By the time you reach the first bend, they have settled their weight across the branches.

You hear one small creak behind you.

It is the sound of a person making themselves comfortable.
