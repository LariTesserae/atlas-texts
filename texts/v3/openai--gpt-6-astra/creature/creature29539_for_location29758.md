The first one is easy to mistake for material left over.

It lies beneath the overhang of a raised seam, where the heat has not quite driven the damp out of the clay. Most of it is concealed. What shows is a pale, folded edge, like a strip of hide put down wet and forgotten. There is grit embedded in it. One end has been tucked into a crack.

When the seam begins to lengthen, the strip draws tight.

For a while that is all it does. The ground moves; the pale material resists. A little clay gathers against it. Then a second fold comes out of the darkness and presses the gathering clay flat, working from the middle toward the edges so that no air is trapped beneath.

The movement is careful, practiced, and too small to belong to whatever is moving the plain.

Eventually it pulls itself free.

It is about the length of a person lying down, although it does not retain that length while crossing the open ground. Its underside travels in a succession of broad contacts. A portion settles, takes the weight, and releases as the next portion reaches forward. There are no feet. At each release, several fine strands of slip stretch between its body and the earth.

The upper surface is dull and finely creased. Where it bends sharply, the creases open to reveal a warmer color inside—not red, but the deep brown of clay that has never been exposed to air. Its outer covering is tougher than the ground. Loose grains roll off it. A splinter of fired earth caught beneath its weight leaves a white scratch rather than a cut.

It stops at a shallow dish of water.

One edge lowers into the dish. The water diminishes. Gradually the folds along its back loosen, and a depression left in its side by the crack begins to fill out.

Only when it turns do you see the hard pieces inside it.

There are perhaps a dozen: a pale wedge, several thick flakes, a curved fragment with a dark glaze on one face. They do not form a skeleton. They move independently through the soft body, gathering beneath whichever part needs to bear weight. Before it raises its front edge to cross a ridge, three fragments slide together there. They make a faint sound, no louder than teeth touching.

The glazed fragment has part of a painted line on it.

Something was broken before this creature existed. It has found a use for a piece.

There are others, but they are seldom in view long enough to be counted. A bank that seemed empty may release two of them as it warms. A low, broad one can remain wrapped around the underside of a cross-wall for days, its exposed surface taking on the color and paddle marks of the wall itself. Small ones shelter inside the hollow clay blisters. The apertures at the summits let them test the air without uncovering their bodies.

They do not appear to breathe in any continuous fashion. They gather vapor into their folds and hold it there. When they have to compress themselves, it escapes.

This accounts for some of the irregular steaming of the domes.

Not all of it.

Their most sensitive parts are on the underside, where the covering becomes translucent and densely ridged. They lay these surfaces against the ground before committing their weight. On dependable clay the contact is brief. At a fresh join they may remain spread out for an hour, shifting a fraction at a time.

A seam can look sound and still be impossible for them to cross.

The difficulty is not its width. A creature that can flatten itself beneath a tile may hesitate before a groove scarcely wider than a finger. It reaches across, makes contact, then withdraws the reaching part with an abruptness that throws the rest of its body backward.

The ground on the other side may not agree with the arrangement of its interior.

When this happens, it tries again with its hard fragments gathered elsewhere. Sometimes that is enough. Sometimes it must go a long way around.

An injured one can be recognized by the persistence of a shape it no longer needs. An edge stays curled after leaving a rim. A long hollow remains through its middle even when it is full of water. Such injuries are particularly common after storms, when the land changes around bodies too slow to release their grip.

There are places along the seams where several wait together in the heat, each holding an injured portion of another.

They use wet clay between the surfaces. They apply pressure gradually. Every so often they loosen their hold, allowing the damaged body to attempt its own shape again.

Some wait through many alterations of the plain.

Their usual activity is less conspicuous.

They brace leaking joins. They lift the sagging lips of basins. They put their bodies beneath thin places and remain there while the ground above them stiffens. In a long trough, one may occupy a faulty cross-wall with only a narrow loop of itself visible through the two finger holes. Water runs over the loop. The creature adjusts its thickness, keeping the passage open as the surrounding clay swells.

They seem to know which irregularities matter.

A bank may be left crooked. An ugly ridge of squeezed-out material may remain untouched. Yet a tiny crease at the foot of that ridge will bring one hurrying from cover, carrying a pinch of fine clay folded into its side.

It kneads the clay before using it. The hard fragments inside its body provide surfaces to knead against.

They are not making the world.

They work only where they can reach. A single bad stretch of channel can occupy a group of them until the next storm rearranges it completely. They have no evident control over the tools that move through empty air. When the great pressure comes down, they flee it, or flatten beneath it and are sometimes lost.

Nor do they gather around the abandoned implements in the old districts. An ordinary wooden paddle may lie untouched for as long as anyone watches. They pass beneath its split end for shade.

Whatever affinity they have with the workmanship, it does not amount to ownership.

They need the repairs.

A basin that empties unexpectedly can leave a whole district too dry for them to cross. A badly fitted depth can take away a body lowered only slightly into the water. If heat begins to fall from an opening instead of rising, the creatures beneath the neighboring banks must move before it pools around them.

Their maintenance keeps small portions of the plain inhabitable. The portions do not always connect.

In the soaking fields, where almost nothing holds, they travel in groups.

They join edge to edge, inserting a fold of one body into a fold of the next. There is no particular front. The one nearest suitable ground reaches it first; the others follow through the tension of the joined bodies. Across an interval whose width is changing, the group may stretch until each individual is scarcely thicker than a hide drawn out for drying.

The last to cross is often brought over with no hard fragments left inside it.

The others have taken those ahead.

Once the group reaches firm ground, they return them. Pale lumps pass slowly from fold to fold. Occasionally there is a delay over a particular shard. Two creatures hold it between them, applying small, contrary pressures, until one yields.

Most possess one piece they are reluctant to give up.

It need not be useful. A large, sturdy creature may carry a bead-sized chip through every redistribution of its weight. Another keeps a curved fragment with a hole in it, too brittle to serve as a brace. It places a thin fold through the hole while resting.

In the oldest districts, there are shallow depressions under sheltered ledges where such pieces have been collected.

The deposits are not sorted by size or strength. Some contain almost nothing but poor material: overfired splinters, soft crumbs, a flake of glaze detached from its body. The creatures visit them when there is no repair to be done. They take pieces out, hold them internally for a time, and put them back.

The clay beneath these deposits is exceptionally well maintained.

Their young begin as a pocket between two bodies.

For several days, sometimes longer, the pair remain pressed together along one edge. They go on moving and working, awkwardly. Water is admitted to the pocket. Fine material is gathered there. One of the adults usually contributes a small hard fragment, although not always at once.

At first the pocket moves only when the bodies around it move.

Later it tightens by itself.

The adults begin opening it to the air for short intervals. What is inside looks less like a small version of either than like an absence that has acquired a lining: a damp hollow, puckered at the edges, closing rapidly when exposed to too much heat.

It must learn to hold water before it can separate.

The first attempts are poor. Water escapes through creases that should have sealed. The young body follows the spill, collapsing outward, and an adult has to gather it back together.

Eventually it can retain a few drops while moving.

Then the adults unfold from one another and leave it between them on the ground.

For a long time it stays there, holding its little quantity of water. If it loses too much, one of the adults returns. If it retains enough, they wait nearby while it discovers that one part of its edge can be lifted without releasing the rest.

There is no moment at which it is finished.

Even the largest remain capable of acquiring new folds, surrendering old supports, or dividing a broad contact surface into several narrower ones. Age shows chiefly in the covering. An old body carries pale lines where former arrangements have sealed over, and areas of deep polish from long service under moving banks.

Some become very thick. Some grow so thin and extensive that a visitor could rest beneath one, taking it for the underside of a clay shelf.

They can die there without falling.

As the moisture leaves, their surfaces harden. The interior settles around the fragments. The next adjustment may break them into useful pieces, or cover them with fresh ground.

Others find the pieces.

Not every piece is taken.

During the first movements of a storm, the creatures emerge in unusual numbers. They leave repairs incomplete. Those carrying young move toward the old districts; others go to the edges of basins and lay their sensitive undersides against the trembling lips.

They are feeling for a place that will remain a place.

One finds it at the corner of a trough: a patch hardly larger than its own body, with a steady heat and no movement between its layers. It spreads itself there. Another joins it. A third arrives carrying a small one so loosely formed that its water shines through.

Around them, the trough lengthens. Its opposite bank moves away into the vapor. A groove passes through the neighboring plain, scored by an unseen point.

The creatures press closer.

Under the three bodies, the little patch stays level. They cannot prevent it from traveling, and soon it is far from the trough, riding the edge of a broad new sheet of ground. But its surface remains unbroken.

When the pressure eases, they separate carefully.

The smallest has lost its hard fragment. For a while it gropes through the water beneath itself, opening and closing an edge.

One of the others puts a chip beside it.

It is not the same chip. The young one passes over it twice before stopping. Then it draws the chip inside, gathers its folds around the unfamiliar shape, and begins the slow work of getting up.
