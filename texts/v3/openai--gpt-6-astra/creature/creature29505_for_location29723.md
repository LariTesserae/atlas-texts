The person who comes out of the amber doorway does not immediately step into the weather. She stands sideways in the recess, fastening something beneath her chin. One hand remains inside her collar for a while. Then she reaches back into the building and brings out a long pole.

She is shorter than the pole by almost half. In her outdoor clothes she has a blunt, continuous outline: hood, shoulders, heavy skirt of waterproof fabric divided around the legs. Nothing loose except a loop at one wrist. The wind takes that and holds it straight.

Her name is Edda. There are eleven people living in the valley, and two of them call her Ed, which she dislikes from one and accepts from the other.

She is not the oldest person here, though visitors tend to think she is. Her face has the uneven color of someone who spends long stretches cold and then comes close to a stove. The skin beneath her eyes is pale, the cheekbones red, the creases beside her nose almost white. She has a thick upper lip, a short gray braid, and one front tooth that overlaps its neighbor. When she is concentrating, she pushes at that tooth with her tongue.

She has lived elsewhere. In a dry city, for nineteen years, she worked on the suspension systems of high buildings. She still owns two pairs of shoes from that life. Their soles are smooth and astonishingly thin. Occasionally she takes them out because she likes them, not because they are useful or because she intends to leave again.

Tonight she has come outside to find a fault that the river instruments say is not there.

The diversion is holding. Its buried anchors are sound. The altered gradient across the channel is within the range appropriate to the tide, the sediment load, and the pressure of water coming down from the hills. Inside the basalt building, she has examined a precise, moving representation of all this.

Nevertheless, water is appearing beneath the wrong alder.

She noticed it through the doorway half an hour ago: a pale disturbance among dark roots, visible only when the branches lifted. It might be a new spring. It might be water backing through an abandoned animal burrow. Either would be ordinary. But if it is the beginning of a channel around the eastern bank instruments, ordinary will become expensive before morning.

She descends from the rock with the pole on the upstream side of her body. Each placement is deliberate without being slow. She does not test every patch of ground. She tests the patches whose appearance has changed.

There is a place where her left knee will not bend quite far enough. At high steps she turns that leg outward. The path recognizes her weight and offers a slightly different arrangement of footholds than it offers her nephew, who takes the same descent in four long strides. She has asked it not to compensate any further. An accommodating path can make the first unaccommodating patch of hillside a nasty surprise.

Under her hood, behind each ear, is a small implanted receiver. It can give her the status of the diversion, the whereabouts of anyone carrying a valley transmitter, and the tremor of a selected instrument translated into something she feels along her jaw. She uses this last function often. It leaves her hearing free.

At present she feels a regular pressure near her lower right molars. The machinery is working steadily.

The river sounds wrong anyway.

***

Her house is the middle one of the three on the ridges. It is not particularly tidy.

The entrance is tidy, because disorder there gets carried inward in wet armfuls. Poles stand in clips. Gloves hang fingers upward over a warm pipe. There are two benches, one for arriving and one for leaving; the arriving bench is always damp. A tray beneath it holds grit, spruce needles, and the occasional small creature that must be returned outside.

Beyond the entrance, things accumulate.

Books lie open facedown despite her objections when other people do this. She keeps little samples of stone on the windowsill, most unlabeled because she expected to remember where they came from. There are bowls she has repaired badly and one she has repaired so well that she turns it toward guests in the hope they will notice.

Her chief extravagance is a room kept warmer and brighter than the rest of the house. In it she grows a citrus tree.

The tree is seventeen years old, crooked, troubled by scale insects, and currently carries four fruits. It consumes a disproportionate share of the household’s attention. Edda washes its leaves individually. Her nephew says they could grow the fruit in a nutrient cabinet in a fraction of the space. She says he is welcome to eat cabinet fruit.

She likes the tree’s shadow on the wall. She likes going into the room before bed and finding the air still warm.

Her nephew lives with her for part of each year. He is thirty, makes acoustic surveys of the inlet, and leaves cups in places where cups cannot reasonably be expected: behind a hatch, on a stair, inside an empty boot. They have a long-running dispute about whether the house should anticipate this.

When he is away, she sometimes finds a cup and leaves it where it is for another day.

***

At the alder hollow, Edda lowers herself onto one knee.

Water moves among the roots with a smoothness that the surrounding flood does not have. She touches it with the pole. The tip goes down farther than it should.

She withdraws it carefully.

A mat of roots has lifted from the gravel beneath. Water is passing under it, not yet strongly enough to tear it loose. From above, the alder still looks firmly planted. Its twigs shake; its trunk holds. Beneath it there is room for her arm up to the shoulder.

She touches two fingers to the side of her hood.

In the house on the upper ridge, a man pauses while drying a plate. He feels the call against his wrist. Edda waits until he answers, then speaks with her mouth nearly enclosed by her glove.

They do not exchange greetings. She tells him which hollow, how deep, and which way the water is moving.

He asks whether she wants the eastern field extended.

“Not yet.”

Extending it would probably stop the undercutting. It could also drive more water into the old spruce stand, where soil has taken centuries to gather between the stones. There is no setting that preserves every part of the valley tonight. There rarely is.

She asks him to reduce the lift upstream for ninety seconds.

While she waits, she stands with one shoulder against the alder. She can feel small movements traveling through its wood. The tree is younger than she is, perhaps by forty years. When she returned to the valley, this hollow was mostly rushes. She remembers putting a basket down here and watching tiny black flies settle all over its handle.

The water slackens.

Something beneath the roots knocks once, then settles. She probes again. Gravel, loose but present. The channel is shallow enough to interrupt before it deepens.

She gives the man another setting, gets him to repeat it, and goes to fetch two rolls of coarse mesh from the building. They will hold brush and stones while leaving room for water to seep through. In spring, if the bank survives, willow roots will take over much of their work.

By then the river may have found something else to do.

***

People sometimes assume that someone living here must possess an unusual appetite for solitude. Edda does not. She prefers having another person in the room, provided they do not require continuous conversation. She likes gossip from places she has never visited. She likes hearing an argument develop over several evenings. When there are guests, she stays up too late and is irritable the next morning.

What she tolerates unusually well is interruption.

Meals are interrupted by weather. Sleep by a change in the sound beneath the floor. A conversation may stop while everyone listens, then resume without anyone needing to explain. Plans remain quite detailed right up to the moment they are abandoned.

This can make her difficult company elsewhere. She has left a concert because she thought she heard failing bearings in the ventilation system. She was correct, but her companion did not consider that sufficient justification.

She is neither fearless nor exceptionally strong. Fast water frightens her. So does the possibility of falling where nobody can see her, which is why she now carries the transmitter even on short walks. She began doing that after an argument in which she was plainly in the wrong and did not concede it for several days.

The valley has other residents she knows less well.

Something small stores shredded grass behind the warm wall of her entrance. She hears it sometimes while putting on her boots. She has sealed the inner panels and left the outer gap open. In hard weather a bird with a damaged foot sleeps beneath the handrail at the river station. An otter periodically leaves fish remains on the lowest step, and Edda periodically removes them. Neither appears to understand the arrangement as shared ownership.

The people maintain only a few dry, warm interiors. Around those interiors, life gathers at distances determined by appetite and caution.

Late tonight, after the mesh is placed and the river has held its course for an hour, Edda returns to her house. Her nephew has arrived while she was out. His wet coat hangs over both benches. There is soup on the stove.

She moves the coat, washes, and carries her bowl into the warm room.

Outside, sleet scratches against the wall. The citrus leaves are motionless. Her nephew sits on the floor beside the tree and tells her about a sound he recorded beneath the inlet, something repeated at irregular intervals beyond the seal grounds.

She asks him to play it.

They listen once, then again. Edda leans forward, holding the empty spoon.
