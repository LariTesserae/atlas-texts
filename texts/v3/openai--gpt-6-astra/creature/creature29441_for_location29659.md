The first part of it you are likely to see is a foot.

It comes out beneath the loading platform when the afternoon light has reached the back wall: a broad, pale foot with four blunt divisions, laid flat on the concrete. It stays there long enough to be mistaken for a discarded glove. Then the divisions spread, very slightly, and the whole thing draws back without a sound.

There are usually two or three animals beneath the platform. More shelter in the inspection trench, behind the ferns, though it is difficult to count them there. They lie pressed together, and the joins between one body and another resemble the folds within a single body. A worker putting his head down to look might report six. The next worker, looking from the other end, will say there are only two, but that one is very large.

They are called yardbacks. This is also the local name for a kind of low trolley, and an instruction to fetch the yardback sometimes needs clarification.

An adult is about as long as the handcart, though considerably lower. Its legs are short and emerge well out to the sides, allowing the belly to hang just clear of the ground. At rest it settles between them. The feet remain visible around the edge, disposed with an almost careless regularity, like wedges put under a piece of machinery.

Its back is grey. From a distance it looks dusty; close up, the surface is densely covered in tiny flat plates, each one overlapping the next by less than the width of a fingernail. Some are the grey of the ridge. Others are warmer, nearly brown. Along the shoulders, where the animal bends most often, the edges have been polished pale by rubbing together.

The resemblance to the broken ground is noticeable but not exact. A fragment from the ridge is hard all the way through. A loose plate from a yardback has a soft underside, faintly fibrous, and curls as it dries. The track man keeps these out of his pile of packing pieces. They compress under a load.

The head is less definite than the body leads you to expect.

It can be drawn so far beneath the forward plates that the animal seems to have no front at all. Extended, it is narrow and flat, with a soft upper lip that overhangs the lower jaw. The nostrils are two oblique slits. The eyes sit well back, dark and small, under rims that keep falling grit away from them. There are no visible ears.

When it searches the ground, the lip separates into three broad lobes. Each moves independently, turning a flake over, passing along the underside of a rail, testing the dark seam where concrete meets brick. The movements are delicate enough to lift a snail without dislodging the shell beside it.

It eats the snail whole. Afterwards there is a brief, contained grinding somewhere behind the head.

Snails are not its only food. Yardbacks take moths from the duct mouths, fallen leaves after they have softened, oily crumbs beneath the workshop doors. They spend a great deal of time scraping material from the interiors of ceramic outlets. The maintenance workers tolerate this because the outlets stay clear, although occasionally a small animal wedges itself into one and has to be persuaded out with a length of damp rope.

They do not respond well to pushing. Pressure makes them flatten and spread their feet. A yardback that has decided to remain somewhere can be surprisingly difficult to distinguish, mechanically, from the ground.

A wet rope works because it interests them.

They follow it with the divided lip, taking in its smell, and will sometimes emerge far enough to be lifted. Underneath, the animal is warm and almost unplated. Its skin has the colour and fine transverse creases of a mushroom stem. There is a shallow groove down the middle of the belly, usually packed with grey dust.

The workers lift them with both hands. Even the small ones are heavier than they look.

They are not particularly afraid of people. A person crossing the apron is an event of little consequence unless their route passes close to an outlet. Then a yardback may turn its head and wait to see what is being brought. It has no corresponding confidence in things that roll. The handcart sends adults under the platform and young ones into any crevice that will receive them. An empty spool, escaping across the concrete, can cause several minutes of quiet upheaval in the ferns.

Their longest journeys take place at dusk.

One leaves the trench and crosses the dry ground towards the western basin. After a while another follows. They travel separately, each with the same steady, low gait, their feet reaching forward beneath the edge of the back and spreading before they take the weight. In the generous light of the yard they cast very little shadow.

At the basin they arrange themselves around the rim. The head goes down; the soft lip flattens against the wet concrete. They can remain like this for an hour. Whether they are drinking, feeding on the film in the water, or merely keeping their mouths wet is uncertain. Their throats scarcely move.

The robin lands between them.

Young yardbacks are easier to see drinking. Their plates have not yet hardened, and water makes a visible dark passage beneath the skin of the throat. They are pale pink-grey, with disproportionately large feet and a narrow seam along each side where the back will eventually spread. They shelter beneath adults, but not necessarily the same adult from one day to the next.

No one has found a nest. Once, when a section of paving was lifted beside the machine shop, there were nine young animals in the shallow space beneath it. They stood in the impression left by the slab, blinking in the daylight. An adult appeared from the pipe trench and lay across them until the workers put a board over the hole.

By morning they had gone.

The fans matter to them in a way that is not immediately apparent.

During the ordinary running of the yard, a resting animal makes small movements beneath its plates. These are easiest to see along the rear edge: a slow tightening and release, not quite in time with the heavy beat overhead. When a fan changes speed, the movements alter. When all three are stopped, as happens once a year, the yardbacks come out.

They do not hurry towards anything. They emerge from under steps, from the warm foundations, from spaces thought too narrow to contain them, and stand in the open with their heads fully extended.

On these occasions there are more than anyone expected.

They touch the paving with the soft lobes of their mouths. Some lower the entire underside of the head to the ground. They remain there through the shouted instructions and the testing of belts, and withdraw only after the fans have been running steadily for some time.

The surveyor has tried excluding them from the area around his tripod. They do not disturb the instrument, but their backs make poor places to rest a measuring rod, and in stillness it is possible to make the mistake.

As for the rising ground, the yardbacks use it differently from the other animals.

They seldom bask on it. They do not dig at its foot. When a fresh crack opens, however, one will usually visit before the end of the day. It approaches along the ridge, settles beside the opening, and works the soft lip into it. Sometimes it remains until dark.

There may be salts there. There may be small creatures living farther down than anyone can see.

The yardback withdraws with grey powder around its mouth. It wipes this off slowly against the inside of a foreleg, then passes the foreleg through its mouth as well.

On cool evenings the newest fractures are good places to find them. Their broad backs fit among the plates of broken ground, but their warmth is perceptible if a hand is held close. Beneath the steady beating of the fans, they make a faint sound of their own: a dry sequence of clicks, irregularly spaced.

It might be the plates settling as their bodies cool.

Beside the latest crack, one has rested long enough for dust to gather against its downhill side. A pale root passes beneath its foot and disappears into the opening. The animal has placed the foot a little crookedly to accommodate it. Every so often the toes loosen, lift clear of the root, and settle again.
