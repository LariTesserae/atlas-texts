On one face of a mountain, the ribbons of vegetation move a little late.

A current passes. The ribbons on the neighboring faces lift, stream sideways, and settle. Here they remain pressed to the stone. Then, when the water elsewhere is quiet, they rise together.

The delay is slight. It could be caused by the shape of the cliff, except that the cliff changes and the delay remains.

There is also a place above the summit where falling mineral flakes stop. They do not strike anything. They slow, turn until their broad faces lie at the same angle, and begin to slide sideways through the blue. Some travel half a mile before falling again. Others dissolve on the way.

Between that summit and the next, eleven miles distant, something is holding a shape.

It is nearly all water.

Not a body filled with water, with a skin around the outside, but water divided by living partitions so thin and so widely spaced that a person could swim through much of it without knowing. A hand might encounter a faint resistance, like the beginning of a change in temperature. Drawn back, the hand would carry a clear thread between two fingers. The thread would part without pain. Both ends would withdraw.

Only in certain light would the injury become visible: a small, milky branching, already closing.

The partitions do not enclose a single volume. They make long chambers, blind passages, layers that overlap without touching. Some are narrower than a hair. Some could admit a mountain. Through most of them the sea passes freely. Others retain the same water for years.

There is no central cavity, no hidden organ waiting in the cleft between the peaks. What thickness it possesses is gathered against stone. Along a sheltered seam, a substance clearer than jelly enters the green mineral before it blackens. It draws something out. Behind it, the new rock hardens into fine parallel grooves.

The grooves are its only durable marks, and the mountain soon grows over them.

It feeds where stone is arriving.

It does not eat the olive films or the ribbons. Often they grow better near its attachments, in the small region where the water has been slowed and the violence taken out of changes in pressure. Its presence accounts for a few unexpectedly flourishing patches, though the shelter is unreliable. When it withdraws, the next current may strip a face bare.

At first, its extent seems simple: a transparent span between two summits.

Then a disturbance far to the north makes a line tighten through the water. A mineral flake turns where there should be nothing to turn it. Another mountain, almost lost in distance, sheds a faint cloud from one shoulder.

There are attachments there too.

And below, beyond the reach of direct light, there are older ones.

Its shape cannot be taken in from one position. More importantly, its shape is not constant enough to map. A chamber opens to the sea and ceases to belong to it. Two separate partitions touch, seal, and retain a new quantity of water. What was outside an hour ago is now passing slowly through a succession of living layers.

It can abandon miles of itself at very little cost. Elsewhere, the loss of something no larger than a fingernail will make it recoil from an entire ridge.

These small, costly places are not gathered together. They occur at junctions where many partitions meet: soft knots, faintly amber, each containing a complicated arrangement of salts and folded tissue. They are the nearest things it has to organs. Signals pass between them as brief changes in tension and chemistry.

It does not use the sea’s swift, pervasive instructions. Those belong to something older. Within this creature, news travels slowly.

One side may still be feeding while another has begun to flee.

Before a correction, its upper chambers flatten. Water is released through openings that have no fixed position. The long partitions turn edgewise toward the coming disturbance, and the attachments nearest exposed ridges loosen.

Sometimes there is enough warning for all of this to happen.

Sometimes the fold arrives while half the creature is still arranged for quiet water.

Then its extent becomes briefly visible.

Across the basin, lines of silver appear where the living layers crease. They intersect at impossible distances. A broad, shallow curve shines between mountains, vanishes, and shines again lower down. For a moment the empty water has the look of fabric being pulled from several sides.

It cannot oppose a fold. It survives by ceasing to hold together in selected places.

Whole chambers open. Attachments peel away. A mountain that had anchored one extremity is allowed to pass through it, tearing partition after partition as the direction of weight changes. The tears are clean and astonishingly long.

Afterward, most of the silver is gone.

What remains contracts toward sheltered stone. Lost tissue breaks down into substances too fine to cloud the water. For days, clear strands reach across the damaged region, finding one another, joining, testing the pull.

It is during this repair that its preferences become apparent.

It does not simply make the shortest connections. It restores certain curves and avoids certain passages, even when the new terrain would make them convenient. Around one black tower, it maintains an empty shaft much wider than the tower itself.

Nothing now happens there.

Long ago, perhaps, a fast-growing shelf split a junction before the creature could release it. The shelf has dissolved. The tower is new. Yet each fresh partition bends away from that volume of water.

Its memory is partly this: places its body will no longer enter.

Other memories are retained in closed chambers. Deep within a sheltered branch there is water warmer than the surrounding sea, with a faint yellow cast. Elsewhere, a narrow pocket holds a mineral mixture no longer emerging from any nearby seam. These reserves are moved, divided, and brought into contact with new growth.

When the same mixture is encountered again, the creature responds differently.

A feeding attachment may open at once. Or all the tissue on a mountain face may withdraw before the stone has shown any visible change.

It has no eyes. Light reaches almost every part of it, and each part alters its behavior a little. Darkness caused by a passing cloud is different from darkness caused by new stone. The first travels over it. The second remains, gathering pressure along an edge.

A summit grows into its awareness by displacing water, shading tissue, and offering chemistry.

For a long time, that is all a summit is.

Then an attachment takes, and the creature begins to learn its movements.

Some mountains are unsuitable despite their abundance of food. Their seams open too abruptly. Their upper faces grow outward faster than a junction can migrate. Others provide almost nothing to feed on but remain dependable through successive corrections. To these the creature gives thicker attachments. It returns to them after storms.

There is one narrow peak around which its oldest surviving tissue lies.

The peak has never approached the surface. It is poor in the substances the creature needs. Much of its upper stone is sealed and inactive. Yet when distant attachments are torn loose, the surviving partitions gather there, lying in many clear layers along the lee face.

For a while, the creature becomes almost small.

It can remain in that condition for months, feeding little, allowing the outer layers to fray. Then a thread extends into open water. It thickens imperceptibly. Farther out, it forks.

There are others, though it seldom meets them.

Their outer partitions sometimes intersect in the broad channels between ranges. Usually the layers slide past. Occasionally they adhere, and a slow exchange begins. Water from retained chambers passes between them. Soft knots form along the contact.

For several days, it may be impossible to say whether there are two creatures or one.

Eventually the currents pull them apart. Each departs with some tissue that was not its own.

Young ones also remain after a sufficiently complicated injury. An isolated portion closes its broken passages and establishes a few attachments on the same face of a mountain. Most cannot release and refill their chambers quickly enough to survive the next correction. A few spread around a ridge and out of sight.

The parent does not tend them. It may not distinguish them from its own distant, interrupted parts.

Yet when their partitions meet again, they join readily.

In the long quiet between disturbances, very little announces any of this. The sea stays clear. The mountains stand widely separated. A loose flake descends through blue water, slows for no visible reason, and slips sideways.

Far away, new thunder begins.

Along the sheltered face of the old peak, a clear attachment loosens. Another tightens. News of the pressure change is traveling through the creature, still miles from reaching its farthest parts.

Out there, beyond the last mountain it has touched, a thread is extending toward a newly risen point.

The thread encounters fresh stone. It rests against it, draws back, then rests again.

While the nearer chambers begin to open for the fold, this small extremity fastens itself and starts to feed.
