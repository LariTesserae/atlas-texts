The first inhabitant you distinguish is waiting at a crossing.

You have already watched it for some time, taking it for a flaw in the glass: a short, smoky inclusion with a bright grain near one end. Twice it has moved when you were looking elsewhere. Now it remains still while a second inclusion passes beneath it, descending through a depth the floor does not possess.

When the crossing clears, it goes on.

Its movement is accompanied by a minute change in the street. The two sides of the incision draw apart ahead of it and come together behind. The bright grain travels last. At a doorway, the smoke disappears first, then the grain; for an instant the doorway is deeper than it was.

That is how they enter a room.

Their bodies require a certain amount of distance to exist. A little of that distance is visible as length or breadth. Most is held in directions that the chamber makes available only under pressure. Released into ordinary space, one would be scarcely more than a fleck of warm mineral, a trace of water, and an intricate injury to the air.

Here, each has an inside.

Within it, thin membranes turn heat into differences of pressure. Water passes across them in quantities too small to form droplets. The membranes are anchored to a framework of dark needles, related to the ones coating the chamber walls, although longer and capable of folding. Between those needles, chambers open and close. Some store dissolved salts. Some receive impressions from the rock. A few must remain empty.

The bright grain is the oldest part. It forms before the rest of the body and grows by almost nothing over a lifetime. Every passage through the city leaves it fractionally changed.

You can sometimes see two such grains in one smoky length. For a while you might think this is a larger animal carrying a smaller one. But at a broad junction the length divides, reluctantly, and two inhabitants depart by separate streets. They had been traveling together with some of the space between their bodies removed.

They do this often in the colder districts.

They do not do it indiscriminately.

An individual approaching a group will stop well before reaching it. Several lights deepen. The newcomer turns, exposing a different arrangement of its internal chambers, and waits. Sometimes a narrow place opens among the others. Sometimes it continues alone.

There are inhabitants who have gone a long time without sharing an enclosure.

You cannot recognize their faces. With patience, you can recognize their ways of arriving.

One pauses just beyond every intersection, even an empty one. Another travels with an asymmetry that makes the silver in a street bend to one side. A third carries a tiny red inclusion separate from its grain, keeping it at the same distance through turns, thresholds, and abrupt changes of depth. At its destination it places the inclusion in a recess and remains near it. Later it takes it up again.

The red speck on the ledge belongs to this order of things. The city has no use for it in construction. Several inhabitants examine it before the ledge finally opens. What matters about it is not visible from above.

The burdens passing under the floor are easier to see than those carrying them. Some are packets of concentrated brine, wrapped in membranes that whiten under strain. Some are newborn frameworks: three or four needles holding apart a space so small that its collapse would make no sound at all. Others consist of heat confined within a traveling enclosure. These cast a soft darkness over the street lights as they pass.

The carriers shorten themselves around a burden, making their own internal rooms shallower. They proceed slowly. At intersections the unladen wait.

In the narrowest streets, a carrier may have to surrender a memory to get through.

It can be kept nearby, in a chamber prepared for that purpose, and collected on the return journey. Along one of the pale ledges are hundreds of these chambers. Their openings appear as faint stippling. An attendant moves among them, testing their pressure, allowing each a little warmth.

A carrier comes back, aligns itself with a particular opening, and grows still.

Afterward it may take a different route home.

Their memories have weight here, although not much. They also have requirements: a boundary, a stable gradient, occasionally a little clean water. A neglected memory becomes difficult to reenter. Its distances alter. What once happened nearby may recede until no living body can extend far enough to reach it.

The public building with the copper light contains some very old ones.

Its doors open infrequently. Whenever they do, the streets nearest it are cleared, and inhabitants arrive carrying water from several reservoirs. The building gives off a low note. Those waiting at its threshold answer by rearranging themselves.

Whatever is being recalled requires more room than any one of them contains.

Their young are raised close to the growing stone.

At first a young one is attached to a wall by all its needles. Its mineral grain hangs in a shallow pocket; its body opens around it whenever warmth rises from the fissure. Adults bring moisture and small deposits of selected dust. The young one learns to hold the resulting space.

There is a precarious period in which it can make itself roomy or keep itself closed, but cannot reliably do both. Nearby surfaces bear little white fans where water has been lost. An adult often rests partly around the young, maintaining a second boundary.

Eventually a single needle lets go of the wall.

Later, another.

The first journey is across the sheltered underside of a step. Several adults wait along the route, each holding open a space into which the young one could retreat. It may spend a long time at the midpoint, extended between two safe places, discovering how much of itself can be carried forward.

No part of the city has been worn smooth because their passage does not abrade it. Yet the old routes are different. Their edges have been adjusted so often to familiar bodies that they anticipate an approach. A street may open for a resident while remaining nearly impassable to a stranger of the same size.

This is one reason they are slow to abandon a district.

The failed courtyard still contains inhabitants.

You see this only when a drop strikes elsewhere and the whole basin briefly grows more luminous. Beneath the transparent flake, among the flattened blue doorways, are a few points of light that do not belong to the architecture.

They have compressed themselves into their oldest parts.

What remains around each grain is just enough body to preserve an inside. There is no room to move a membrane, scarcely room to distinguish one sensation from the next. Water reaches them through channels finer than the fractures in the glass.

At the amber boundary, others maintain those channels.

They arrive in shifts, although the shifts are unequal. One stays after its replacement has come. Another leaves, returns, and leaves again without doing anything you can detect. The one with the sideways bend in its light makes repeated journeys from a reservoir, carrying less each time as the route becomes narrower.

The amber marks indicate how much pressure the damaged district can bear.

When a mark withdraws, a little distance has been restored.

Restoration proceeds with extraordinary caution. A doorway opened too quickly could empty an inhabitant into a room before its body was able to unfold. The city must offer space in the same order in which the body can accept it.

For this, someone has to remember how that body was arranged.

One of the waiting inhabitants approaches the boundary. It stretches along the edge, then branches, making a succession of small enclosures. The enclosures differ subtly in proportion. Deep under the flake, a grain brightens.

The waiting body changes its arrangement.

The grain brightens again.

They continue for a long time.

Elsewhere the ordinary life of the chamber goes on. Water is apportioned. A newborn detaches its third needle. Two inhabitants meet beneath a platform and remain joined until the warmth begins to fail. In a little private recess, the red inclusion is turned so that another face points outward.

They perceive you chiefly as weather.

Your feet introduce two broad regions of pressure. Your warmth travels unevenly through the floor. When you lean closer, the city receives the salts and moisture of your breath as an abrupt change in the chamber’s season.

Several catchments establish themselves below your face.

An inhabitant near the entrance climbs into the uppermost surface of the glass. There it spreads itself thinly, placing its needles against the boundary. It cannot encompass your shape. Most of you occupies directions that, for it, lead nowhere.

It waits through several of your breaths.

On the next exhalation, a street lights beneath your hand.

Then another, a little farther away.

You may take this for an invitation. It may be an attempt to divert a dangerous source of water. The inhabitant holds its position while you draw back, and remains there after the catchments have emptied.

Above the ruined district, the hanging drop lengthens.

Those at the boundary have stopped moving. One is still extended in the unfamiliar arrangement required by the trapped body. Its own bright grain has dimmed. From neighboring streets, others reach toward it, lending enclosure without disturbing what it holds open.

The drop falls.

For once, the receiving hollow is already in place.

Water enters the damaged courtyard along a single illuminated line. Beneath the flake, one blue doorway acquires depth. It is very little depth. Less than you could measure with a fingernail.

Something inside takes it.

The waiting inhabitant contracts, then opens again, more slowly.

This time, from below, the movement is answered.
