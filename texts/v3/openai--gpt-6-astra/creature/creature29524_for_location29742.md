At midday, a pale surface moves behind one of the narrow openings.

It is easy to mistake it for a wall catching the sun. It has the same faint green translucence as the newest growth, the same shallow creases. But the wall beside it is unfolding upward, and this surface is moving steadily down. A bead of water remains attached to it. So does a small brown scar.

The scar passes out of sight.

Several streets away, it passes another opening.

What lives here has a continuous body, although there is no place from which that body can be seen whole. Parts of it occupy the chambers inside the stems. Other parts lie beneath the courts, in spaces the thickness of a leaf. Where its passages meet, the city makes those swollen collars of tissue. Where they fail to meet, it waits, sometimes for days, with one pale end pressed against a growing wall.

Its skin is smooth in the broad stretches and densely folded near the ends. There are no hairs. In strong light, threads can be seen inside it: dull red, branching, intermittently full. The threads empty in succession as a contraction travels through the flesh.

The contraction may enter a wall beside you and emerge through the floor behind you before it has finished passing the wall.

This is how the creature moves. It gathers itself into portions of the city that are near one another in some respect other than distance.

For much of the day, it is very slow.

***

There are several of them in each dense knot of towers.

Their territories overlap. A single column may contain a length of one creature in the morning and another at dusk. They differ in color, in the spacing of their internal threads, in old injuries. One has a row of opaque white knots along its underside. Another carries a deep crease that will not smooth out, even when its body is stretched almost transparent.

They do not possess a fixed number of limbs.

Where a body rests for a long time against a surface, it puts out short, flattened projections. These spread into the shallow depressions of the bark and hold without piercing it. A dozen may anchor a narrow length of flank; elsewhere, one broad pad carries the weight of a mass that fills an entire room. When the creature withdraws, the projections flatten into its skin.

The marks they leave are the soft indentations visible on new buttresses.

Some of those buttresses hardened around a resting animal. Others were held in shape by one while still too tender to support themselves.

The inhabitants spend a great deal of time holding things.

A roof beginning to unfurl is braced from beneath by a translucent shoulder. A passage under strain is lined for several hours by flesh that takes its shape precisely. Along a raised causeway, several creatures may attach themselves below the woven stems, each maintaining a slightly different tension.

When one lets go too soon, the consequences are audible across the plain.

The survivors remain attached through the collapse. Later, as the broken structures soften, pale lengths of body can be seen drawing free.

***

The closest thing to a head is a thickening that appears at whichever end is moving into unfamiliar space.

It develops gradually. The internal red threads gather into a dense fan. The skin wrinkles into concentric folds around a soft, central depression. At the bottom of the depression sits a clear, hard bead, smaller than a seed.

The bead turns.

Light entering it is carried some distance into the body. In a dark chamber, this produces a faint illumination along the red threads, revealing curves and junctions beyond the opening. Occasionally, for a few seconds, another bead shines back from deep inside.

The creature tests an opening by pressing the rim of its folded end around it.

It feels the passage grow.

A suitable passage receives a slow pulse of pressure, followed by another. The stem responds by widening. If the response comes unevenly, the creature loosens its grip and moves on. If the tissue is too dry, it may leave a wet print, then wait beside it until the moisture has sunk in.

They make very few direct wounds. The plain closes wounds rapidly, and the scar tissue is difficult for them to cross.

They can be trapped by a repair.

In tightly shut districts, where the surrounding plants have become small and leathery, one sometimes finds a clear bead sealed behind bark. The folds around it have shrunk into a dark ring. Farther along the stem there may still be living flesh, feeding weakly through connections too narrow to allow retreat.

The city grows around these obstructions. Eventually the bead is lifted high into a tower, or lowered beneath a court.

It remains clear.

***

Their food is produced where old growth is being taken back.

Before an avenue closes, its inner surfaces become coated with a thin, cloudy fluid. The inhabitants gather there. They spread their bodies against it, exposing fields of minute pores that are otherwise shut.

A feeding creature loses its smooth outline. Its skin develops thousands of tiny cups. Each cup holds a droplet until the droplet clears; then the cup closes.

Dry pith, pollen, softened bark, the dissolved contents of exhausted leaves—all pass through this film. The creature takes some substances and returns others. Around its feeding surfaces, fresh tissue grows dark and tough. The hottest parts of the city cannot sustain much new height without this material.

Water passes between inhabitant and habitation in smaller quantities, and with greater hesitation.

At dawn, a creature may spend an hour beneath a single condensation groove. It raises a narrow fold to the pore through which the water is descending. The fold swells. The wall contracts.

Then the wall draws some of the moisture back.

The exchange continues until both surfaces lie almost flat.

During rain, the creatures extend through the upper chambers. Their bodies become so thin that the light from the roof bowls shines through them. They stay there throughout the shower, pressed against the reservoirs, while the whole district holds still.

When growth resumes, they are already withdrawing ahead of it.

***

They sense one another through the tension of shared walls.

An occupied chamber has a particular give. An approaching animal pauses against its outer surface and sends a small contraction through the stem. The animal within answers by tightening or relaxing.

Usually one withdraws.

Sometimes both remain.

In sheltered chambers, two bodies lie together with their folds interleaved. Their internal threads fill and empty at different rates. Over several hours those rates draw closer, until a single pulse seems to travel through both animals.

Pairs return to such chambers even after the structures containing them have shifted far apart.

A familiar resting place may now be reached only through a high slit, down a shaft, and across the underside of a court. They find it. The bark inside is polished to a dark gloss. Old attachment marks overlap there, broad on one side, smaller and more numerous on the other.

If one animal fails to return, the other still makes room.

***

The great fruits contain young ones.

While a fruit is green, the developing animal is folded into its central cavity, scarcely distinguishable from the wet membranes around it. Its red threads are arranged in five loose fans. Each fan reaches toward one of the lines along which the rind will split.

As the fruit ripens, the young animal begins to pull.

The first chambers form around those pulls. Columns thicken where it attaches. Sheets of leaf settle against its sides. It shifts, and a narrow passage opens between two portions of its body.

By the time the fruit parts, the animal is holding an entire district in the arrangement in which it will descend.

It does not always manage this well.

A court may open inward. A stair may finish against its own underside. One chamber may remain sealed around a fold the young creature has not learned to withdraw.

For weeks afterward, it works at these mistakes.

Adults enter along the new district’s edges and feed on the remains of the rind. Sometimes an adult braces a wall while the young one loosens its hold. Sometimes it occupies a needed passage and must be pressed out, slowly, by a creature still pale enough for sunlight to reveal every thread within it.

The young learn the weight of roofs before they learn to conserve water.

***

At night, they come nearest to being visible.

The falling temperature brings moisture to the chambers. Feeding pores open. Bodies thicken, filling spaces that were empty throughout the day. Under the pressure, roofs rise and walls ease outward.

Through a slit that showed only sunlit rooms at noon, there is now a broad, faintly luminous flank.

It moves with the slow pulse of water being taken in.

Nearby, another surface answers. Farther away, a third. The pulses travel through the district at different speeds, crossing, delaying one another, occasionally settling for a while into the same rhythm.

A clear bead turns in the dark.

Around it, the folds loosen. The creature presses forward into a passage that has at last grown wide enough, carrying its old brown scar beneath the lifted roofs.
