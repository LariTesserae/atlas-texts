It is easiest to find just after a blackbird has been feeding.

Look beneath the flap of heather the bird has turned back. Among the pale, hurried animals there may be something that does not hurry: a folded piece of gray-brown flesh, no larger than the last joint of your thumb. Its upper surface is dry and finely cracked. Its underside is pleated, almost white. You could mistake it for a fungus until the edge draws inward and closes over a bead of moisture.

That is not the whole animal. Nor is it quite a part in the way a foot is part of a vole.

Follow it carefully, without pulling, and you find a flattened cord passing beneath a rooted stem. The cord divides. One branch ends a few inches away in another folded body; the other goes beyond the little area you have exposed. Here and there it narrows until it is scarcely distinguishable from the roots around it. Elsewhere it thickens into something muscular, capable of shortening enough to bring two neighboring folds together.

Most of it lies within an inch of the light.

It has no outline you could uncover without doing considerable damage to both it and the clearing. Some of its connections pass through old vole tunnels. Others have been enclosed by growing moss. A few are dead, though they still look continuous with the living portions. There are places where it has joined itself, and places where two pieces lie against one another without joining.

Perhaps several animals live here. Perhaps one has become several and has not yet finished separating.

The little folded bodies differ according to where they grow. Near the surface they are leathery and olive, with grit held in their creases. Lower down they are soft, translucent at the edges, and furnished with short bristles that keep wet leaves from lying directly against them. The smallest are simple cups. The larger ones can unfold a narrow fringe of blunt, mobile fingers.

These are used for feeding, but also for housekeeping. A cup repeatedly strokes the same moss stem until a loose particle drops away. Another lifts the edge of a dead leaf and holds it up for several minutes, allowing a passage underneath to remain open. Something caught in the fringe is brought slowly inward: a softened fragment of plant, fungal threads, occasionally a springtail that fails to jump.

There is no large mouth concealed below. Everything it eats must pass through one of these small openings.

It is eaten in return. The blackbird takes exposed cups along with the other things it finds. Beetles chew the pale connections. A vole sometimes cuts through a cord simply because it crosses a passage at an inconvenient height. The ends contract and darken. Over the following days, one may send out a blunt new growth that feels along the underside of a stem. The other may do nothing.

Its repairs are not exact. A connection restored after damage follows a slightly different route. A feeding fold grows where there used to be a length of cord. An isolated portion survives for a season in a damp depression, multiplying until it encounters another portion, or dying when the depression dries.

This makes its age difficult to discuss. The exposed cups are mostly young. Some of the thicker junctions have layers of old skin beneath them, like very small collars pushed down around a neck. There may be no living tissue here that was present when the oldest willow fell sideways. There may have been no interruption, either.

In the morning, it comes closer to the surface.

Not far enough to be seen from standing height. Its folds rise between moss heads, each lifting a few millimeters, and open into the damp air. For a little while the clearing contains thousands of minute, irregular rims. Dew catches on them. Some lean against grass blades, borrowing their support.

As the upper vegetation dries, they withdraw. This is not a simultaneous movement. One closes while its neighbor remains open. A fold shaded by a heather leaf may stay extended all afternoon.

It knows remarkably local things.

It knows the weight of a beetle crossing a stem attached to its skin. It knows that moisture has reached one side of a junction but not the other. Pressure applied to one of its thicker cords produces a contraction that travels several inches before fading. At a branching point the movement may continue down one arm and fail to enter the next.

There is nothing in the center of the hollow to which all this information must go.

Nevertheless, portions of it acquire habits. Along a frequently used vole run, the feeding fringes open after the animal passes rather than before. Beneath a particular willow gap, where starlings habitually land, the cups stay shut well into the morning. Remove the birds for a few days and they begin to open earlier.

It has had the mill above it for longer than any of its present feeding surfaces have existed.

The broad rush of the fans does not trouble it much. What reaches it most distinctly comes through the ground: a steady trembling, occasionally interrupted by a heavier beat. Its cords accommodate that motion. They rest against roots, tighten, loosen, find positions in which they are not continually rubbed.

On the rare occasions when the mill stops, the effect is visible if you are already crouching in the right place. A fringe retracts. Farther away, another remains extended but bends sharply downward. For several minutes the small bodies make adjustments to a stillness in which the footfall of a wren has become unusually distinct.

Then they resume feeding.

If you press your palm gently onto the moss, you may touch it without knowing. The first response is ordinary compression: leaves flatten, water rises, the woven surface yields. Keep your hand there and a pale rim beneath your smallest finger may fasten to the skin.

It cannot bite you. The attachment feels like a wet seed adhering.

For a while it works at a flake of loose skin, using the same patient fringe with which it cleans a stem. When you lift your hand, it stretches a little before letting go. Its cup remains open in the small patch of shade you leave behind, then folds inward as the sunlight reaches it.
