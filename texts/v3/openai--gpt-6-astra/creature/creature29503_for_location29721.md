At first it seems to be part of the low tree: a length of exposed root that has come out onto the stone and failed to find its way back into the ground.

It has the same damp, closely folded surface. Where a branch rests across it, there is a shallow indentation, and a little sediment has gathered there. One end disappears beneath the tree. The other lies beside the water, broadening slightly before it meets its reflection.

You could sit near it for a long time. You could put your hand on it.

The warmth would tell you nothing.

Eventually, somewhere along its length, a crease would open. The opening would travel slowly toward the water, without widening, and close again behind itself. It would be difficult to decide whether you had seen a breath or the settling of something saturated.

There is an animal here. Most of it is under the tree.

Its body is low and substantial, about as long as a person lying down, although it seldom extends itself enough to be measured. The skin gathers into folds where its limbs join the trunk. On the upper surfaces it is brownish green, finely mottled, with pale areas that resemble mineral deposits until they bend. Underneath, where little light reaches, it is the color of the inside of a thick stem.

It has four limbs. Their ends divide into short, broad digits, each with a soft pad and a narrow, hard edge set well back from the tip. These edges can grip stone without scraping it. At rest, the digits fold inward and the feet look unfinished, like roots that have begun to branch but stopped.

Its head is the part beside the water.

There is no neck that can readily be distinguished from the body. A broad slope leads forward to a mouth whose closed seam follows the stone so closely that water gathers along it. The eyes sit farther back than you would expect. They are small and amber, covered by clear lids over which a second, duller lid passes occasionally from below.

The animal does not need to lift its head to look up.

For much of the day, it watches the tree in the pool.

*

When cloud fills the hollow, it comes out.

First the digits unfold. Then the body rises just enough for the belly to clear the ground. A patch of stone appears beneath it, clean in some places and covered in others with a very short growth, green as the cut end of a stalk.

Its movement is remarkably ordinary once it has begun. A foot goes forward, settles, takes weight. The opposite side follows. Its joints bend with the small adjustments of an animal walking over an uneven surface. It stops when a pad encounters a loose flake and chooses another place to put that foot.

There is a scar on one forelimb. The scar is pale, puckered, and quite short.

In the water, that same limb sometimes has an additional digit.

It crosses the exposed stone between the islands without entering them. At the precise margin of the jointed stalks, it turns its shoulder and passes sideways. Several stems lean over its back. None breaks.

Beside the largest pool it lowers itself again, pressing the length of its underside against the warm rock. Water advances around its feet. For a while it remains there, with the tip of its face just submerged.

Then its mouth opens.

The movement barely troubles the surface. Inside the mouth are close, flexible ridges, pale at their bases and dark along their edges. They spread against the water and hold still.

Above them, a reflection continues to move.

The actual branch sways. The reflected branch sways. The extra leaf turns its torn edge toward the sky.

One of the ridges lifts.

The extra leaf bends in a direction the wind has not given it. Its stem draws out to a fine green thread. For a moment the reflected branch seems to have caught on something beneath the surface.

Then the thread parts.

The animal closes its mouth.

On the bottom of the pool, the fallen leaf remains exactly where it was. The little tear near its stem is still visible. Above it, branch and reflection now carry the same number of leaves.

A slow contraction passes backward through the animal’s throat.

It stays beside the water for the rest of the afternoon.

*

This is how it feeds, though there are days when it opens its mouth and takes nothing.

A newly fallen leaf is not always available to it. Neither is an old one. It can wait beneath a discrepancy for hours, shifting its mouth by fractions, while the retained image moves freely with whatever it was once attached to. Sometimes the animal gives up. Sometimes it returns after a cloud has passed, or after fresh water has entered through the upper rock.

The reflected tip of the weathered boulder does not interest it. It has rested its chin against that place often enough to leave a brown stain on the bank.

The vanished clump of stalks does.

Over a long interval, that clump has become less dense. Individual stalks remain clear and upright, but there are spaces among them now. Through those spaces the exact reflection of the present sky can be seen.

The animal gains very little from a meal that takes so long. Its body has the ample look of something living in plenty, yet beneath the soft folds the bones are close to the surface. When it stretches a hind leg, the joint shows sharply. Its belly contracts after feeding and then, over several days, grows loose again.

It drinks from the water that has not yet reached a pool. Along the upper face of the hollow it places its mouth against the broad beads before they join. The moisture disappears around its lips. Afterward it leaves a faint oval on the stone, briefly drier than everything surrounding it.

At noon it sleeps.

It fits beneath the low tree with an intimacy that has taken time. There is a space between two roots for its rear foot. Its back supports a branch that would otherwise hang lower. Where its flank touches the trunk, both surfaces are smooth.

The tree has grown around many positions of the sleeping body. The body has fewer positions left to choose from.

*

It does not keep the hollow tidy.

Fallen wood stays where it falls. Leaves collect against its limbs until they soften. Once, when it came out from beneath the tree, a small white growth remained attached to the crease behind its head. It carried that growth for several days before the damp cap collapsed.

Nor does it seem to tend the islands. It noses along their boundaries, especially where moss makes a raised lip, but has never been seen pulling up a shoot or pressing a seed into soil.

Still, the margins accommodate it. A curve in the edge of the moss receives the curve of its jaw. Between the stalks and a pale stone is just enough room for its body to turn. Some of these passages have become too narrow. It approaches them, pauses, and takes a longer route.

Others are much wider than it needs.

In one shallow sheet, on certain afternoons, its reflection fills such a passage almost from side to side.

The animal itself lies smaller within that outline, breathing.

*

It knows the six bowl-shaped leaves.

After rain it goes to them before it goes to the larger water. It touches the lowest rim with the underside of its chin. The fleshy stem yields a little, and the water trembles without spilling.

There is rarely anything to eat there. A bowl reflects a small, dark piece of sky and the underside of whichever leaf overhangs it. But the animal examines each one, raising itself awkwardly to reach the highest.

At close range, the pads beneath its feet are marked with tiny whorls. Soil lodges in them. Its mouth has a soft, uneven corner on one side, as though something healed there long ago.

It is particularly careful with that corner when it touches a leaf.

Late in the day, when the cloud has hidden the lower country, it sometimes remains beside the plant instead of returning to the tree. It settles in the abandoned groove with its forefeet folded beneath its chest. Six leaves spread above its head.

One of them slowly lowers.

The animal looks up. Its clear eyelids catch the broad white light. At the leaf’s edge, the swelling drop holds the stone, the water, the leaning rocks, and a small, distorted image of its face.

It watches until the drop falls.

Then it waits beneath the leaf while another gathers.
