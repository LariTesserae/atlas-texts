The resident is easiest to find by looking for a surface that has been cleaned where nothing ought to rub.

There is such a place under the public scales: an oval of brass, dull but free of salt. Another lies on the underside of a stair tread. A third is deep inside the collar of a pipe, beyond the reach of any straight tool. These are not its feeding places. They are places where it rests a particular part of its body.

At first, the body itself looks like something fitted to the machinery. A length of pale packing material has worked loose from a joint and folded down against the wall. There are two thickened ends, a narrow middle, and a ragged edge carrying the fine gray deposit. It might be leather. It might be a strip of the material once used to keep a door from rattling.

Then one end lets go.

The movement reveals a smooth underside crossed by muscular bands. They shorten one after another, not quite in sequence. The loosened end searches, touches the wall, withdraws, and touches again a little farther to the left. Only when it has found purchase does the rest of the animal follow.

Fully extended, it could reach from one side of a doorway to the other. It almost never extends fully. Most of it is folded, and much of what appears to be thickness is water held between folds. In a narrow passage it becomes astonishingly little: a tough central cord, a few compressed organs, a skin drawn after them like cloth through a ring.

It has no skeleton, but neither is it entirely soft. Small curved plates lie embedded along its middle. When it turns, they slide over one another. When it braces itself, they lock. Their edges show through the skin as a row of faint, unequal crescents.

The animal has two long limbs and several much shorter ones. The long limbs divide at their ends, though not into a regular number of fingers. One has three branches. The other has five, two of them joined by a membrane. The short limbs are mostly concealed beneath the body and serve as holds. They allow it to release both long limbs without drifting away.

Its mouth is a slit on the underside, well back from the end that usually goes first.

There are eyes, but they are difficult to recognize. They lie in the shelter of two folds and resemble grains of dark sand caught in mucus. In the upper water they can distinguish a lamp from its reflection, an open hatch from a sheet of metal. In the brine they become nearly useless. There the animal finds its way by pressing a thick, smooth pad beneath its forward end against the buildings.

This is the part that wears the brass clean.

Through it, the resident feels the pumps.

It can distinguish the three shafts, including the irregular drag in one that would be imperceptible to a hand on the wall. It knows when the tide has shifted by the changed load on the buried pipes. A valve closing is a brief, familiar shape passing through its flesh.

The district is not silent to it. Nor is it noisy. Most of what it feels belongs there and requires no attention.

A loose washer is different.

For several pump cycles, the animal remains under the scales while the washer knocks faintly inside a housing across the square. Then it begins to move.

Crossing the open paving is costly. There is nowhere to place its whole weight without entering the strongest brine, and the lower edge of its body contracts when exposed to that water. It takes the route along the buildings instead: behind a downpipe, under a balcony brace, through the broken corner of a cellar window. A passage that appears direct from the square may be inaccessible to it because of a sharp zinc edge. A route through four rooms may offer an unbroken succession of safe holds.

It has used some of these routes long enough to change them.

A nail has been bent flat. A curl of metal has been worked back into a hatch. Under one sill, where a body of its present size could no longer pass, the mortar has been scraped away in a shallow groove. The scraping took place over several winters. It began before the opening was too small.

The resident carries very little. In a pocket made by an inward fold of skin are a short brass pin, a flake of slate with one rounded edge, and a piece of stiff wire bent twice at the end. They remain there when it turns itself nearly inside out to pass a constriction. Sometimes it puts them down in separate holes and retrieves them afterward by touch.

At the loose housing, it does not attempt to stop the shaft. It wedges itself between the pipe and the wall and waits for the counterweight to descend. During the brief easing of pressure, it inserts the wire. On the next descent, it advances it. The work may take the remainder of a lamp’s lit interval.

The washer is not removed. It is pushed into a position where it no longer chatters.

This is sufficient.

The animal does not keep everything in repair. A clock mechanism can fall to pieces beside it. A stair can sag until a familiar route is lost, and it will find another. But some changes in the pumps bring it out of whatever recess it occupies, even when it has fed poorly and should remain still.

The warm patches around the bearings support much of what it eats.

It grazes with a row of small hard points just inside the mouth. The points do not bite deeply. They comb. After it has passed over a patch of growth, the fronds are shorter, the transparent tubes mostly intact. A whitish trail of disturbed matter settles behind it.

It also eats the soft films beneath rust scales, eggs left in screw holes, and small animals too injured to hold against the current. A dead shrimp can keep it in one place for a long time. It holds the shell beneath its body, opening one joint after another, leaving the feelers until last because there is almost nothing in them.

The living white shrimp in the washbasin is familiar to it.

When the resident enters that room, the shrimp raises its feelers. Sometimes it climbs onto the broad back and picks through the sediment trapped along the folds. The resident allows this, even opening a crease that it otherwise keeps closed. But it will not allow the shrimp near the mouth or the two dark eyes. A small limb rises to bar the way.

Neither seems to mistake the arrangement for safety. When the resident has gone too long without feeding, the shrimp stays on the far lip of the basin.

There is a room it returns to more often than any other.

The room is beneath an enclosed balcony, reached through a service hatch behind a second service hatch. A pipe warms one corner. Clearer water enters through a split in the ceiling, while the heavier water has an exit near the floor. The meeting of the two is unusually stable.

Here the resident hangs with its body spread across the boundary.

In this position it looks less like a strip of packing and more like an unfinished garment. Thin lobes open along its sides. Their inner surfaces are densely veined. Water passes between them, and minute pores open and close in groups too slowly to resemble breathing.

It cannot remain indefinitely in any one of the district’s waters. The upper layer draws salts from its tissues. The lower layer takes water out of them. Much of its daily life—if the long intervals between its activities can be called days—is spent arranging parts of itself at different heights.

When the fresh layer deepens, it shortens the holds on its upper side. When the brine rises, it gathers the lower lobes close. Its resting shape records the balance of the water.

Along the room’s wall, small objects have been pressed into an old strip of felt.

Most are practical: a second wire, a hollow rivet, three slivers of metal thin enough to enter a valve seat. There are also things it has brought back and never used.

One is the broken neck of a bottle, worn almost smooth. Another is a porcelain button whose remaining hole admits the tip of its smallest limb. There is a piece of black rubber that still yields when pressed.

It handles these while suspended in the room. Not always, and not in a fixed order. It will turn the button for a while, place it back, then remain motionless with a limb resting on the rubber.

The felt contains vacant depressions too. Some objects have been lost. One was taken out through the hatch and could not be brought back after a pipe shifted. The resident still sometimes feels along the place where it belonged.

Its body bears comparable absences.

One side has fewer small holding limbs than the other. A white seam runs across a feeding fold. Near the rear is a thick knot of tissue that contracts independently when the lamps dim.

The knot was once the beginning of another animal.

Under better conditions, it would have developed a mouth and its own set of holds, then separated along a thinning band. Conditions worsened. The pumps slowed for a season; the gardens shrank. The growing body stopped growing.

It has not died. The resident feeds it through vessels they still share. Occasionally it turns toward a warm bearing before the larger body does, pulling ineffectually at their common skin.

There may have been others once. Some of the cleaned resting places are smaller than the pad the resident now presses against them. Others are situated where its body cannot comfortably lie.

It visits these without lingering.

At the tide grille, it behaves differently.

It approaches from below, along the seam between iron and stone, and anchors every short limb before reaching toward the bars. Its eyes open as far as the folds allow. The other square hangs beyond: shifted upward, often dark, sometimes carrying a faint disturbance in water that is still on this side.

The resident has no difficulty using the passage for tools. It can feed the brass pin through one opening and recover it beneath the scales. It has learned which angle prevents the bent wire from snagging.

It uses its own body more cautiously.

One long limb goes between the bars. The end appears at the drain. It feels among the ceramic fragments there, retrieves whatever has lodged against the mouth, and withdraws. The motion is ordinary until it has to cross the square afterward and pass the place where its own limb has just been.

Then it stops.

It lowers the smooth pad to the paving. It touches the drain rim. Sometimes it waits until the lamps have changed.

Once, while its limb was still through the grille, something touched the smallest branch at the end. There was no grasp, only a pressure with the same yielding firmness as its own skin.

It withdrew before completing the work.

Since then, it has kept one branch folded back whenever it reaches through. What comes out beneath the scales has always had that branch folded back too.

For long periods the district appears uninhabited. The resident is resting between waters, or feeding in a pipe housing, or waiting with a tool poised for the load on a valve to ease. A lamp burns over an empty handrail. The fish remains beneath the cabinet. Gray matter settles over the routes along the walls.

Then a service hatch moves outward by the width of a finger.

The resident comes through carrying something beneath its body. It braces on the threshold, draws the last folded lobe clear, and closes the hatch behind it. The closure is unnecessary; water passes all around the frame. But the hatch has a loose edge, and left open it would tremble whenever the third pump turned.

It holds still until the familiar vibration returns.

Then it takes its meal home.
