The first sign of the larger inhabitants is a dry patch in one of the gardens.

It lies under an overhang, beyond the reach of direct light: a long oval where the moss has been pressed down but not crushed. Around its edge the fern tips curl inward. Something has been lying there often enough to change the way they grow.

There are other signs once you know to look. A channel cleared of ice where no sunlight reaches it. Six shallow depressions around a basin, each with a dark, polished center. At the entrance to a sunken room, a loose stone has been turned so that its hollow side faces up. It holds less than a mouthful of water. Beside it is another, and beyond that a third.

You may pass the inhabitant itself several times.

At rest it occupies the junction of floor and wall. Its outer surface is gray, but not precisely the gray of the stone: a warmer color, dusted with minute pale hairs that catch droplets without becoming visibly wet. What looks like a succession of rounded ledges is its body folded back upon itself. In the narrow spaces between the folds there is darkness, a little greenery, and sometimes the brief glint of an eye.

It does not open that eye because you approach. It opens it when your weight changes the sound of the floor.

For a while, nothing else happens.

Then a portion of the lowest ledge loosens. A limb comes out, very long and narrow at first, with its joints folded so closely together that they seem to keep appearing as it extends. The end divides into three blunt pads. These touch the ground separately, feeling before they take weight.

A second limb reaches past the first.

The creature rises without ever making a sudden movement. Its folded sides lift away from the wall. What had seemed to be several separate masses turns out to be one body, broad and shallow, carried on six legs. It is larger than the room appeared able to contain. Standing, it can put its small head above the rim of the garden while its feet remain among the ferns.

The head is the least conspicuous part of it. There is no muzzle thrust forward to announce its direction. A short, flexible neck descends from beneath the front of the body, and the face usually points toward the ground. Two dark eyes look obliquely outward. Between them, a seam opens into a mouth with close-set, pale scraping plates.

It eats a fern tip. Then another. It takes a considerable time choosing the third.

Along both sides of its body hang thick folds, overlapping from front to back. In shelter they resemble a heavy, badly fitted coat. Their edges are scalloped and slightly green. Small white animals sometimes sleep between the lowest folds, leaving their narrow heads exposed.

When the creature climbs into the street, it carries one of these passengers with it.

***

In dry weather, the inhabitants remain below.

They need less food than their size suggests. Much of what fills a resting chamber is folded skin, hair, and the light framework that supports it. The dense body lies along the middle, no thicker than a person’s torso, though several times as long. Its warmth is carefully enclosed. Putting a hand near a resting animal, you feel almost none escaping.

They graze the underground gardens in small sections, cropping vigorous plants and leaving the slow-growing cushions alone. Their mouths can lift an insect from a leaf without removing the leaf. They also eat the soft white growth at the bases of walls.

This feeding looks destructive until you see an untended chamber.

There, the living stone has advanced across two light wells. The garden beneath is pale and sparse. A drainage lip has thickened until water spills onto a sleeping shelf and freezes. In occupied rooms, such growth is scraped back each day. A creature may spend an hour working along a channel, moving its head only the width of a finger.

Its front feet do finer work. Each pad can curl around an object independently. With these it picks dead material out of the moss, shifts grit from a watercourse, or presses a broken piece of living wall into a wet seam.

Some pieces take. Others are removed much later.

The inhabitants do not appear to distinguish sharply between tending a plant and adjusting a doorway. Both require touch, moisture, and repeated attention. A newly opened passage may remain too small for its keeper throughout the keeper’s life.

Nevertheless, it is tended.

***

They come above ground when the clouds rise.

Usually one appears first and waits in a lane, all six feet planted well apart. Its head hangs down. The folds along its sides begin to separate.

Inside, they are neither gray nor hairy. They are a dark, muted red, crossed by fine branching ridges. Slender supports lift through them, pushing outward and upward. Each fold opens into a deep pleat; each pleat divides again. The transformation is slow enough that you can lose track of how wide the creature has become.

Fully opened, it spans most of a court.

The outer edges are pale and fringed. They tremble in air too slight to move your clothing. Closer to the body, the pleats remain nearly still, making a series of sheltered spaces through which the fog passes. Droplets gather on the fringe, travel inward along the ridges, and disappear into the thick skin at the roots.

The creature stands beneath its own spread surface like an animal sheltering under a low tree.

It cannot fly. A strong gust could overturn it. This is why it waits, and why it places its feet so carefully, and why the other inhabitants do not all emerge when it does.

Across the town, heads rise from garden openings. Feet touch thresholds. They are listening through the stone.

When the smallest court gives its clear note, several may begin to unfold together. When the note wavers, the fringes draw in. Sometimes an animal closes only one side, turning its exposed pleats away from a current moving between the walls.

The streets allow these adjustments. A body can stand in one lane while its collecting surfaces extend through two doorways into the next. At certain corners, the parapet breaks the wind at exactly the height of an opened fringe.

Whether the town grew around this behavior or the behavior developed within the town is difficult to tell. The oldest visible layers contain passages too narrow for the animals now using them.

They still lower their heads to some of those sealed openings.

***

A cloud-filled afternoon is their busiest time.

They move slowly through one another’s reach. Where two fringes meet, they are folded aside with extraordinary care. A foot is lifted over a trailing pleat and held suspended until there is space to place it. The youngest individuals, which have proportionally longer legs and only a few simple folds, pass beneath the adults.

There is touching, but little that resembles greeting. One animal presses its face against the underside of another and remains there. Two stand with the pads of their front feet together. Occasionally a small ripple travels down an opened surface, and a similar ripple answers from farther along the street.

They do make sounds. Close to a resting body, you can hear a series of soft internal knocks, too low and irregular to distinguish easily from the noises below ground. Some are produced with the mouth against the floor.

A knock given in one chamber can bring an animal to the doorway of another.

You cannot reliably summon one by imitation. It may approach, or withdraw deeper into shelter, or begin examining the nearest water channel.

***

The raised basins are used near the end of a cloud passage.

An adult braces around the rim and gathers its opened folds inward. The wet surfaces come together in a slow sequence, the outer pleats first, then the deeper ones. Water runs from beneath the body in several thin streams.

Some enters the basin. Some is taken directly by waiting young.

A young animal feeds by putting its whole face into a fold beneath the adult’s chest. There are no visible teats. The skin there becomes glossy, and the young presses and scrapes with a mouth still too soft for stone.

Afterward, the adult may stand over the basin for a long time, releasing only an occasional drop.

The small white creatures emerge then. They work along the damp fringes, licking deposits from places the larger animal cannot reach. Their green undersides come from sleeping against the old collecting surfaces, where a fine growth persists even through the dry season. They are tolerated, sometimes repositioned with a foot, but not cherished indiscriminately. An animal with tender new folds will keep picking them off.

***

The inhabitants live long enough to become recognizable parts of particular rooms.

An old one has a sleeping hollow polished to the exact shape of its folded body. Its collecting fringe is uneven. Some pleats no longer open; others have been repaired by thick, opaque tissue that sheds water poorly. It spends more time clearing channels than gathering fog.

Youngsters rest against it. They put their feet where its feet are working, interfering until it shifts them aside. When it follows a drainage route beneath the town, they follow too, stopping whenever it stops.

Eventually they begin stopping before it does.

There are chambers containing the remains of older animals. The supporting rods of their folds lie in pale fans beneath the moss. Living stone grows around them, preserving a little of their spread before the garden covers it. Other inhabitants continue to use these rooms. Sometimes they sleep within the shallow curve of a body that died there.

At dusk, the open surfaces fold away. The town’s occupants lower themselves into the green rooms, one careful foot at a time. A last adult stays above while the damp seams freeze, moving between channels and touching each with its mouth.

When it finally descends, it pauses with only its head and shoulders below the threshold.

Farther underground, something knocks.

It answers against the stone beneath its foot, waits, then draws the last of its pale fringe into shelter.
