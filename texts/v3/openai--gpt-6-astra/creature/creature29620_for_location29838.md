At first there seems to be too little of it to account for the district.

A translucent skin lines the underside of an inspection cover. It is almost the color of the water, visible only where it folds around a bolt and the doubled thickness catches the lamp. Along a submerged joint in the brickwork, the same substance has collected into a seam as fine as wet cotton. There is more inside the elbow of a warm pipe. A plumber opening that pipe would find a soft, pale lining, easily mistaken for a deposit until it began to withdraw from the cold edge of the cut.

It withdraws slowly. There would be time to wipe it away before noticing.

In sheltered places it grows thicker. Behind the submerged dial in the settling basin, it forms a loose collar around the instrument’s stem. Under the pumping-house floor, sheets of it hang between joists, their lower edges in the water. They are not quite membranes. Water passes through them, but reluctantly, collecting on one side before appearing on the other in rows of small, clear beads. Fine strands brace them against the current. Where a strand meets iron, it spreads into a flat attachment the size of a fingernail.

These attachments are older than the sheets. Some lie beneath several coats of paint.

Most of what lives here is inaccessible in this way: behind a flange, between courses of brick, inside the damp packing around a shaft. It occupies the difference between a fitting and a good fit. There are miles of such space in the hollow.

It has no central body waiting beneath the basin. The basin contains a great deal of it, but so does a wall above the lunchroom, where a disused flue stays warm through the winter. What joins those portions is sometimes a substantial cord inside a drain, sometimes only a film along mortar. In several places the connection passes through living roots. There, the pale substance becomes difficult to distinguish from the plant’s own tissues. Roots enter it brown and leave it white, branching more finely than they do in soil.

The plants are not disguises. They grow, shed leaves, and compete for light. A fern shaded by a larger neighbor will eventually die. Its roots remain useful for a while afterward.

But a plant rooted in this substance acquires another source of information.

Pressure from a vibrating engine foot travels through the wet foundations. Along that route, the lining tightens and changes what it releases into the root tips. Growth slows on one side and continues on the other. A damaged leaf alters the flow returning through its stem. Over many days, that alteration reaches the buds.

Nothing measures the distance to the belt. The next leaf simply grows into a different set of conditions.

This is how much of its conduct is accomplished. A local inconvenience becomes a chemical difference; the difference travels until something can respond. By the time a gutter root bends away from an electrical cabinet, the message that began the bending may already be several weeks old.

Pressure travels faster.

The instruments were installed for different purposes, and some have been disconnected from those purposes for decades. Yet the linings of their pipes now communicate through wet walls, root sheaths, and passages too narrow for a wire. The little gauge behind the lunchroom counter once belonged to a hot-water urn. Its pipe ends in a plastered partition. Within that partition, a soft sleeve closes around the open end.

It is still measuring pressure. The pressure now belongs to something larger than the urn.

The daily rise is a period of extension. Fluid enters the finest strands, stiffening them enough to push into pores that would otherwise hold them shut. Old repairs offer many such openings. Fresh brick meets old mortar imperfectly; a plugged doorway settles against its frame. For an hour or two, the organism reaches farther into these boundaries. Water displaced by its advance appears on the faces of the walls.

Later, it draws back. Some strands have secured themselves and remain. Others soften, empty, and are carried away.

Its growth can be read in the tide marks, although not as a steady ascent. In one season it gains a few inches inside a wall. In another it abandons an entire basement. A lamp burns out; a pipe cools; the balance of warmth and dissolved minerals changes. Years of occupation can retreat into a line no thicker than the original crack.

Heat alone does not feed it. Much of its living is made at the meeting of waters: oxygen-bearing seepage from above, mineral-rich water from below, the old oily drainage of the workshops. Across its thin surfaces, these meet at controlled rates. Other minute lives occupy those surfaces with it. Some make the orange bloom on the iron; others live where no air reaches. The district’s apparent emptiness contains an immense population, but almost none of it moves independently farther than the breadth of a pore.

The absence of small animals has accumulated gradually.

Water exposed in the streets is poor in the things larvae need. Its surface carries a film too slight to cloud a reflection, but enough to interfere with a breathing tube. Insects that settle on a leaf sometimes leave at once. Those that do not may find nothing suitable in its sap. There was no single clearing-out. Each generation found the hollow less hospitable, while the plants that tolerated it prospered.

The flowers still open. The organism has no use for the pollen on the sill, and no means of understanding its waste.

It is capable of waste in other ways. It continues to maintain a channel beneath a demolished shed because water still comes through it. It thickens around a pipe that has been cold for thirty years because the pipe warms briefly whenever a distant valve is tested. It has preserved entire arrangements that no longer benefit it enough to justify their upkeep.

For a long time, people helped with this.

They scraped linings from narrow pipes and left them in broad drains. They learned which sumps could be emptied and which had to remain wet. An instruction to leave one pump idling overnight survived several changes of management, long after the reason for it had ceased to appear in the maintenance book. Workers knew that a certain wall ran before the gauges reached the red, and put their tools elsewhere.

They did not need to agree about what the substance was. Different trades encountered different portions of it. To one crew it was troublesome growth; to another it was a sealing compound that appeared without being applied.

Their work shaped its body. In return, its habits became part of their working conditions.

Now a loose guard panel clatters because no one comes to tighten it. The organism registers the vibration and reinforces its attachments nearby. That is the extent of the repair it can make. It cannot turn a nut. It cannot replace a belt. Its accommodations are considerable, but narrow.

Someone entering the hollow would be perceptible to it first as a sequence of loads on the paving, then as warmth and a disturbance in the shallow water. Near the visitor’s feet, membranes would contract. A little fluid would move away through the seams. Elsewhere, a gauge might stir.

If the person stood still long enough, the local contraction would ease. Water would return around the soles.

It has encountered such interruptions before: boots, cart wheels, the temporary weight of stacked machinery. There is no special provision for their return, only responses still available in the tissues that remain.

At the locked gate, its outermost growth lies beneath the lowest step. A pale edge follows the mortar halfway up the riser, stopping where the brick dries. During the daily pressure rise it becomes faintly glossy. By evening it is dull again.

Above it are several dry treads and a little dust. Below it, through the thickness of the step, a strand runs back toward the warm foundations. It joins another beneath the passage, and another beside the canal, until the disturbance of a foot on the threshold has become a small redistribution of water under the pumping house.

The pumps continue turning. Along one submerged joint, a new attachment holds.
