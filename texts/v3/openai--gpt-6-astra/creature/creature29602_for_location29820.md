The one at the bench is not much taller standing than seated. For some time you may mistake this for a consequence of the low ceiling, or of a habit of stooping beneath the branches. But when they cross an open path, their shoulders remain at the height of your waist. Their arms hang almost to the floor.

They walk on broad, divided feet. Each foot settles in parts: first the outer edge, then two soft pads, then a small central piece that makes a dry click against the paving. On a terrace crowded with animals, the parts can settle separately, finding room between bodies without asking any of them to move.

Their covering resembles a work apron worn on all sides. It is pale near the shoulders and darkened at the knees, with smooth patches where the wrists rub against the hips. There is no clear division between clothing and skin. At one elbow a square repair has been sewn on with copper-colored thread. The thread enters the flesh, if it is flesh, and has acquired little branches.

You will not see their face immediately. The head hangs forward, and the upper back rises behind it in a shallow ridge. What looks from a distance like hair is a fringe of narrow, flexible strips, each dark at the tip. These sweep the work before the hands come down.

They do not use the fringe to see. Once, a falling chip catches in it, and they continue working until a small animal climbs up their chest and removes it.

The animal keeps the chip.

Their hands are the most finished part of them.

There are six fingers on one and five on the other, though this is difficult to establish because the shortest fingers fold across the palms rather than beside them. The fingertips are broad and softly ridged. At rest they cup inward, enclosing a space about the size of an egg. Their nails are small pieces of horn, set well back from the ends, where they can brace a tool without scoring whatever is being held.

They are warm enough that you can feel a hand approaching before it touches yours.

If you offer them a loose shaving, they accept it. If you offer a broken leaf, they turn it over. They may return either one, or tuck it into a fold at their side. There is no consistent preference for what is whole.

Sometimes they hold a fragment against the inside of a wrist for a long time, then put it back exactly where you found it.

They can be interrupted. This becomes clear when one of the pouch animals arrives at the bench and begins pressing itself against their ankle. They lower a hand without looking, and the animal pushes its narrow face between two fingers. The hand closes lightly around it. For several minutes the work stops.

When the animal leaves, a little powdered charcoal remains in the palm. They work it into the object they were making.

That object will have a dark patch along its underside. Much later, when it has begun to move, it will seek out hollows where that patch fits against the floor.

There are others.

You discover this not by seeing two together, but by noticing that the repaired elbow has changed sides. The next maker has a longer head and a shallow depression between the shoulders where three folded-wing creatures are sleeping. Another has no fringe at all. Fine grooves cross its crown, and dry dust gathers in them. It cleans these with a hooked twig while walking.

They share the bench without clearing it. A piece begun by one may spend several days in another’s hands. Tools are left in positions that seem precarious but are easy to reach from below. An awl balanced across two pegs is not being stored; its handle is warming in a place where a seam in the floor lets out a little more heat.

Some of the makers are small enough to stand beneath the bench. Their hands are not proportionately smaller. One has to carry its right hand against its chest when climbing stairs.

It would be easy to take these for children, except that the smallest has the most worn joints. Its ankles have been rebound many times. When it sits, it carefully arranges a loose piece at the back of one knee before letting its weight come down.

The larger makers wait for it to finish using a tool.

They do not seem to command the garden. A root lifting a paving stone will be given a wedge, or a new arch will be built over it. If a path becomes impassable, the makers first try walking another way. They are capable of cutting living growth, but they do this reluctantly and without ceremony, supporting the severed piece until it can support itself.

Their mistakes remain visible.

One of the walnut-sized animals has legs too close together to walk on a flat surface. It lives astride a narrow railing, moving backward and forward along the same length of wood. At each end, the maker has fitted a turning platform. The animal seldom uses either. Most days it sits above the entrance with its feet clasped beneath it.

A shrub near the alcove bears leaves too heavy for its branches. Every leaf rests on a separate little stand. New stands are made as the leaves enlarge. Once a week or so, a maker tries gently lifting a branch without its support, waits, and lowers it again.

Not everything begins moving.

On a high shelf are several small forms without dust on them. Some have legs; others are folded so tightly that their possible shapes cannot be guessed. They are taken down and held occasionally. One is passed from hand to hand among three makers, each pressing a thumb into the same shallow hollow.

Afterward it goes back on the shelf.

Nothing about their handling suggests that they have reached a conclusion.

They themselves need attention. At the end of a long period of work, the maker with the repaired elbow places both hands flat on the heated floor. Its shoulders sink. The flexible strips around its head spread across the clay.

Another kneels behind it and presses slowly along its back.

You hear a series of tiny settling sounds. A seam closes near the neck; another opens lower down. The kneeling maker pushes a pinch of pale powder into this opening and holds it shut. Neither moves for a considerable time.

When they separate, the first leaves a hand resting on the second’s foot.

Their resting places are scattered through the enclosure. One lies along the warm trough, partly covered in black flakes. Another fits into a recess beneath the central mound. Seen sleeping there, with its limbs drawn into the grooves prepared for them, it resembles a tool put away in its case.

But the recess is newer than the sleeper. Fresh marks show where it has been widened around one shoulder.

Beneath the paving, in the rooms too small for a finger, there may be more of them. Now and then the faint rubbing below stops when a maker overhead stops working. After a pause, both resume. If a loose tile is lifted, something farther down sometimes draws a smaller tile across the opening.

The makers never dismantle enough to expose it.

When they add a section to the wall, they spend longer touching the bricks than laying them. They lean their foreheads against the new surface. They stand with their palms spread across several courses. The smallest maker walks along the coping, lowering itself to sit every few steps.

Eventually an animal follows.

The first is usually one of the six-legged ones. It faces the new masonry and remains there through the gradual evening. Others arrive beside it. By the time the makers leave, there is an orderly row.

At the entrance, the folded green thing between your feet has begun to raise its uppermost edge.

You may sit there often enough to notice this. You may also notice that the maker passing with a tray of fragments no longer waits for you to draw in your feet. It knows the space you leave.

One day it stops beside the scored square. It lowers itself with some difficulty, settles one palm on the floor, and holds the other above the tiny growth without touching it.

There is room for another hand.

If you put yours there, the maker shifts a little closer. Its fingertips smell faintly of horn worked warm. For a while nothing happens. The new leaf remains folded, and a creature beneath the seat continues its patient feeding.

Then the maker adjusts your smallest finger, very slightly, so that its shadow does not lie across the roots.
