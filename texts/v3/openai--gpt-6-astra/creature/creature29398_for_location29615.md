The maintenance machine has a passenger.

For a long time there is no way to distinguish it from the machine’s other fittings. It lies along the rear housing, narrow and dark, with several limbs tucked into a groove. Dust has settled on its back. One extremity trails against the silver surface, moving whenever the machine moves, and seems to be a loose cable.

When the machine stops, the cable lifts.

It divides at the end into a cluster of fine, pale fingers. These hover over the new fissure, testing the air. Then the passenger raises the front of its body, unfolds two jointed supports, and steps onto the ground.

Standing, it is about as tall as a person sitting in a chair. Its body is long, carried nearly level between four walking limbs. Beneath the front end hang two shorter arms. What seemed a smooth black back is a covering of overlapping plates, each with a soft grey edge. They slide across one another as it turns.

It has no obvious face from this angle. The front of the body ends in a blunt wedge, with a narrow mouth underneath and two recessed patches that might be eyes. Along either side of its neck are three deep slits.

The slits open.

Inside them, something pale trembles.

It stands quite still while the ground grinds beneath it. A report from the south lifts the dust around its feet. Its neck contracts on one side, then the other, and a low vibration passes through the machine’s casing.

The machine moves back.

Only then does the passenger approach the crack.

The people of the city are easiest to find beside things that are changing.

They come to a doorway before it darkens. They wait beside a basin whose trees are about to flower. They gather around a building that has acquired an unfamiliar draught, touching its walls and the ground outside, sometimes remaining there for days.

Elsewhere they are difficult to notice. Their resting posture is extremely compact. Arms fold beneath the chest, walking limbs settle into recesses along the flanks, and the head draws back until its upper plates meet those of the shoulders. A sleeping adult resembles a length of weathered conduit or a dark coping stone.

There are many of them on the low roofs.

Others sleep under the flat branches at the basin rims, where the leaves touch their backs. Condensation gathers between their plates. Small browsers come to lick it away. The sleepers occasionally flex a shoulder to dislodge an animal that has become too persistent, but generally the arrangement is tolerated.

An old individual may carry a little vegetation in the grooves of its armour: fine mosses, a translucent liverwort, one or two rooted filaments. These are trimmed, not scrubbed away. The growth keeps the joints damp.

Their uncovered skin is a pale brown-grey, soft-looking and heavily folded. It shows at the throat, beneath the abdomen, and at the bases of the limbs. When one reaches upward, the folds open to reveal a faint copper sheen.

They can climb very well. In the basins, where there is something to hold, they move with their bodies almost vertical, using all six limbs. On the open plain they travel low, setting each broad foot carefully between the mineral edges.

The bare ground is hard on them. You sometimes see one stop, lift a foot into its hands, and pick a black splinter from the sole.

***

Their eyes resolve nearby things with great precision.

A person repairing a sign can inspect its smallest dark mark from a finger’s breadth away. A person preparing food will turn a single orange growth through several angles, checking the translucent skin for parasites. At a distance, however, sight serves them less well. They recognize motion and brightness long before shape.

Much of what they know about an approaching stranger comes through the ground.

The soles of their feet contain soft, ridged pads that can spread or withdraw. On uncertain ground, they pause with the pads fully open. The plates of the back lift a little. The neck slits widen.

To them, the plain’s roar has positions, textures, interruptions. A distant gate has a recognizable approach long before its frame changes colour. A tram’s empty compartments sound different from occupied ones. A fresh mineral sheet can be heard straining against the sheet above it.

They do not hear all this effortlessly. The work is tiring. Some districts are fashionable because the ground there is unusually legible. Others are cheap because several deep fractures cross beneath them and ordinary conversation requires repeated clarification.

They have quiet rooms.

These are small, suspended chambers with thick walls and floors that yield beneath the feet. Inside, they fold their neck slits shut and communicate by touching fingers to the soft skin below one another’s jaws.

There are public quiet rooms near the larger crossings. People emerge from them slowly, pausing in the entrance while the city returns.

***

Speech involves the throat, the feet, and the armour.

A short utterance may be a tremor delivered through one forefoot. Longer statements pass between several resonant cavities in the neck, accompanied by precise changes in stance. The back plates add dry clicks, used sparingly.

Two people talking across a plaza can look as though they are waiting for something.

Their conversations do not fill every available pause. They leave room for the city to speak through them: a gate opening, a hunter crossing, the long preliminary groan of ground about to rise. A sentence interrupted by such an event may resume much later, without repetition.

Children are less disciplined.

They drum on railings. They discover resonances in the undersides of trams. They set a whole row of waiting adults clicking involuntarily by producing a particular rasp against a curb.

The city contains shallow hollows where this behaviour is encouraged. Their rims are scarred with generations of striking and scraping. Adults take children there carrying little bundles of differently shaped mineral fragments.

On a busy evening, the noise from one of these hollows is appalling even by local standards.

***

Their young are carried beneath the body.

At first a child is pale, soft, and tightly curled, attached to the parent’s ventral folds by six tiny gripping limbs. It feeds there and sleeps through the movements of work. A parent attending a new fissure may occasionally reach underneath itself to adjust the child’s position.

Later the young climb onto the back.

Their first armour grows in separate islands, leaving broad strips of vulnerable skin between. They are carefully watched around sharp ground. Near family dwellings, the seams have been capped with the same yielding substance that edges the basins.

Children learn distance before they are trusted with a doorway.

A household keeps small objects for this purpose: cups, blocks, branches, a piece of fabric with a distinctive tear. The objects are placed on opposite sides of a room. One is moved. The child must discover what else has changed.

At the simplest level, this is a game about reflections and echoes. Later, adults introduce objects brought from adjoining districts, or place one item briefly through a frame.

A child who notices that the cup has become deeper is praised.

A child who reaches into it is stopped.

Even experienced adults sometimes misjudge. The city’s oldest residents tend to move cautiously around familiar furniture, especially after a major arrival.

***

Their homes are mostly below the visible buildings.

A folded roof may cover only a stairwell. The inhabited rooms descend beside a planted fissure, each opening onto leaves at a different depth. There are warm chambers near the mineral, cooler ones where water collects, and airy ledges used for preparing food.

They eat much of what grows in the hollows. The orange bodies beneath the runners are harvested when their surfaces begin to wrinkle. Certain insects are kept in latticed containers and fed prunings. Meat is eaten fresh or dried in the hot breath of selected seams.

Meals are held around a damp central stone.

Food is placed in shallow depressions, sorted by texture as much as flavour. Their fingers are quick and delicate. They split husks, extract soft interiors, and pass portions to those who cannot reach. The mouth underneath the head is small, edged with transparent cutting points like those found in the dust.

Old teeth are shed continually.

A guest who has learned this may begin to notice how carefully the eating stone is cleaned.

Households vary in size, but rooms are seldom crowded. Close kin often live in buildings separated by a considerable stretch of plain. They visit daily, carrying food or tools, and remain in touch through the ground between visits.

Illness presents difficulties. A sick person wants company but may be unable to tolerate its vibrations. Relatives sit at measured intervals outside the room, taking turns moving closer.

There are people whose occupation is simply to remain nearby without disturbing anything.

They are well paid.

***

The city’s hunters are not pets.

A large one may take a young person, particularly during the luminous alarms, when every smaller animal is crying at once and the ground is difficult to read. Families teach children to recognize the strange traveling quiet made by a heavy predator holding its body low.

Still, the crossings brighten for hunters. Routes through the basins are kept open. A dwelling built across one of those routes will be altered or abandoned.

This is a source of arguments.

So are the orphaned structures. Some want the rotating balcony removed before it falls. Others have harvested its scarlet fruit since childhood. Specialists have examined it repeatedly and disagree about where its weight actually rests.

Its present owner lives some distance away and maintains that the matter is settled.

Every few years the balcony rotates far enough to shade a different property.

Then the arguments begin again.

Beside the new fissure, the maintenance worker lowers its chest until the soft folds beneath its neck almost touch the ground.

It listens for several minutes.

From a compartment in the machine it takes a narrow strip of pale material, presses one end to the broken plaza, and waits. The strip contracts. A dark line travels along its centre.

The worker lifts it, examines the line, and puts it away.

Something moves under the worker’s abdomen.

A young child extends its head between the folded limbs, eyes bright in the mineral glow. One small arm reaches toward the crack. The adult catches the wrist without looking and holds it gently against its chest.

Together they wait for the next movement below.

When it comes, the adult places the child’s palm flat on an intact patch of ground.

The child startles. Its little back plates rise.

The adult keeps the hand there, supporting the wrist, while the vibration passes.

Then it lets go.

A moment later the child reaches down again.
