The animal beside the root has been entering the fissure for eleven days.

Most of it is still outside. Its hindquarters lie beneath a fan of leaves, indistinguishable from a length of discarded stem except where six small feet grip the stone. The feet are arranged unevenly. Two oppose one another near the head; the others emerge wherever its body has found purchase. They can be withdrawn and grown again, although this takes time and leaves a pale scar.

It is no longer than a finger. Along its back, overlapping plates hold a dusting of frost. Each plate is clear at the edge and opaque toward the middle, where grains of metal have accumulated in the tissue. Beneath them, something amber moves forward, stops, and moves back.

Its head has already flattened to fit the opening.

There are no eyes on the head. The light-sensitive organs are farther back, sheltered under the plates, and distinguish little beyond exposure and concealment. What faces into the crack is a cluster of soft wedges surrounding a hard, divided tooth. The wedges swell in succession. The tooth rests against the stone without scraping it.

For hours, nothing happens.

Then the rock contracts. The movement is smaller than a grain of frost, but the tooth follows it inward, and the wedges fill the space behind. A little more of the animal disappears.

It does not make its way through rock by force. It waits for the rock to make room.

At this age it is vulnerable to almost everything. A grazing mouth might lift it with the root felt. A transparent hunter could settle across its exposed back and digest it without disturbing the plates. A dry interval might arrive before the head reaches damp stone. Most of its siblings have died within sight of the hollow where they hatched, though enough of their bodies remain alive to be troublesome to scavengers. A detached foot can grip for a year.

This one has chosen well. The pale root beside it is alive and thickening. Moisture travels along the root’s surface, held between close-packed hairs. The animal presses a fold of its throat against them and drinks.

Eventually the root will die here. The animal will occupy the channel it leaves.

For now they advance together.

—

Under a nearby shelf, an older animal of the same kind has spread through a block of stone roughly the size of a house.

There is still something recognizable at its center: a short, armored body, much enlarged, lying in the chamber where it first settled. Its original tooth has divided into a rosette. Its feet have become thick attachments to the chamber walls. The plates of its back are buried under new tissue, but their outlines can be found, nested one inside another.

From this body, soft extensions follow every useful crack.

Some are only exploratory. They end in sensitive bulbs that taste the water films and withdraw from unsuitable minerals. Others have become permanent vessels, lined with overlapping rings that keep them open when the stone shifts. Where two extensions meet by different routes, they fuse. Fluid can then circulate rather than merely move out and back.

The first complete circuit changes the animal.

Until then, it lives on what it can absorb: traces of salts, dissolved organic matter, the leakage of roots. Afterward it can maintain differences between one part of its body and another. Warmth collects in a deeper chamber. Wastes move toward an exposed seam. A slow chemical current passes through a membrane that previously did little more than separate two fluids.

Its first rotating organ is very small.

It develops inside a muscular pouch, a lopsided wheel of stiff tissue with a hole through its center. For several seasons it merely rocks. The pouch contracts, the wheel shifts, and fluid slips past its edge. Deposits accumulate on one face and are dissolved from the other.

Eventually the wheel completes a turn.

The animal has no organ capable of registering this as an event. Nevertheless, feeding improves. Tissues that had been maintained at the edge of failure begin to thicken. The muscles around the pouch grow in the direction of successful movement. A bearing forms where there had been abrasion.

Years later, the wheel is turning continuously.

Small creatures live inside its chamber. They entered as eggs with the drinking water and have multiplied in the conductive bath. Their blunt mouths graze the bearing surfaces, removing deposits before these can obstruct the motion. They cling during each pressure change, flattening themselves into grooves worn by their ancestors.

The larger animal cannot survive long without them.

They cannot survive the cold outside it.

When the wheel stops for repair, they gather around its axle, where warmth lasts longest.

—

The basin-sized adults have no single outline.

One may occupy the northern half of a valley, descend beneath another, and send a narrow vessel back toward the surface several ridges away. Its neighbor may pass through the same rock at a different depth. Their tissues sometimes touch across a fault. Where they do, each grows a hard, smooth membrane. The membranes slide against one another as the continent settles.

A surface observer might assign six gray domes to six animals. All six may belong to one. A seventh dome, almost touching them, may be part of a body whose principal organs lie beyond the horizon.

Their boundaries are easiest to hear.

Each body has its own sequence of pressures. The sequence travels through its fluid systems, slows at narrow joints, and returns by several routes. The animal responds to these returning pressures. A blocked vessel, a broken attachment, a hungry chamber: each changes the pattern.

This is most of what it knows.

It knows the weight of the herd gathered over an upper organ, though not the separate animals. It knows when frost has sealed a discharge pore. It knows the pull of a growing fault and the peculiar lightness that precedes a collapse.

It can distinguish a neighboring body from an obstruction. When two expanding systems approach the same seam, their exploratory tissues pulse against the stone. Sometimes one withdraws. Sometimes both divert. Occasionally they persist until the seam is filled with crushed, inflamed tissue and neither can use it.

Such contests may continue for decades. Above them, a narrow strip of vegetation grows unusually thick on the leakage.

The deep blows heard across the plateau are not all calls. Most are simply work. But an animal can hold a piston short of its accustomed stroke and release it at a different point in the cycle. A neighbor detects the altered interval.

Before a large discharge, the animals nearest a slope often fall into sequence. Valves close. Conductive fluids are moved into broad, well-insulated chambers. The small inhabitants of those chambers retreat into recesses.

Then the blue light descends.

Afterward the ordinary rhythms resume, one by one.

—

Their young are made close to the surface.

A mature body diverts warm fluid into a shallow organ and grows a cluster of translucent capsules there. This is expensive. The vegetation above the organ loses its frost; grazers gather; predators follow. A reproductive animal can be located from far away by the dark patch over it, and by the crowd.

Inside each capsule, a young animal lies curled around a store of dense, bitter nourishment. It already has plates, a tooth, and the beginnings of its uneven feet. It has no rotating organ. There is not room for one, and no useful work for one to do.

The capsules are eaten.

Broad-jawed grazers scrape them from the warm stone along with the mineral meal. The capsule wall softens in the first part of a grazer’s gut, then hardens as it encounters the chemistry farther in. Most emerge intact, packed into small pellets with grit and undigested needles.

A grazer may take years to move between feeding sites. During that time the young wait inside it, scarcely developing. The gut is warmer than the air, and reliably wet.

After deposition, they must wait again.

They hatch in response to two things together: sustained moisture and the vibration of opening stone. Rain alone will not release them. Neither will a fracture in dry rock.

When conditions agree, the capsule splits along a prepared seam. The animal unfolds, consumes as much of the remaining lining as it can reach, and begins searching downward.

It may travel only a few inches in its free life.

Those inches determine the shape of everything that follows.

—

An adult can lose enormous portions of itself and continue.

A cliff fall may expose vessels wider than trees. Their ends close in sections, each ring contracting behind the last until the loss is sealed. The severed tissue remains warm for a while. Animals assemble along it so densely that its surface vanishes beneath their bodies.

Farther in, circulation takes other routes. Some organs are abandoned. Others reverse their flow. For several winters the sounds beneath that part of the plateau are irregular.

There are injuries from which recovery is impossible. A principal bearing breaks. A reservoir drains into an opening too large to close. The body spends its remaining reserves trying to establish a circuit that no longer exists.

Its death is uneven.

Upper chambers cool first. The creatures living there crowd into descending vessels, following faint warmth. Vegetation over the vents pales. Grazers linger, scraping the last deposits from pores that have begun to shrink.

Deep organs may continue turning long after the connections between them have failed.

In time, these also stop.

What remains is a fitted system of chambers and passages, lined against leakage, penetrating rock that no root could have opened alone. Water gathers in it. Small animals enter. Some of the bearing grazers survive in isolated pools, feeding on the last softened tissues of the wheel.

Young seam animals find their way inside.

A dead adult can shelter hundreds, each growing into a different portion of the emptied body. Their early circuits follow the old vessels. Later they diverge into fresh fractures, or meet across the chambers and lay down smooth boundaries against one another.

Much of the plateau has been inhabited more than once in this way.

Beside the pale root, the small animal pauses with its head just below the reach of daylight. One of its hind feet releases the stone. Slowly, the limb softens and draws back beneath a plate.

There is room now to bring that part of the body inside.

Deep below, a pressure cycle completes. The crack trembles against its throat.

The animal holds still until the tremor has passed. Then its tooth advances.
