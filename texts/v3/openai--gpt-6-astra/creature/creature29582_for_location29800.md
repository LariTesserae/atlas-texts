The paired depressions are deepest at their outer edges. Whatever made them put its weight down carefully, tested the stone, and then rolled each foot inward before taking another step.

Sometimes there is a third mark between them: a light drag, interrupted at regular intervals.

Follow these while they are fresh and you may find what appears to be a bundle of soaked hides caught against a sill. Water passes over it. One edge lifts and settles. Beneath the edge are several small, pale folds, packed together with the softness of something that has never been exposed to air.

Then the bundle raises itself on two legs.

It is about as high as your waist. Much of its body hangs between its knees, suspended in a deep, ribbed pouch. Above this is a narrow back, armored with overlapping patches of something finer than horn and less regular than scales. Silt fills the overlaps. Short bristles project from them, each tipped with a bead of clear fluid that the surrounding water does not readily take away.

There is a head, although it takes time to distinguish it. What seemed to be a loose corner of the hide turns toward you. A slit opens along its underside. Within it, two moist surfaces unfold and spread apart.

The animal holds them there.

You feel a faint alteration in the water around your ankles.

It folds the surfaces away again and steps backward into the enclosure.

***

Their feet are almost circular, divided underneath into muscular compartments. A foot can be soft enough to spread over slurry or drawn tight enough to grip a stone by its shallowest imperfections. The skin between the compartments is densely sensitive. Walking is a slow succession of inquiries.

They can move quickly. When a layer breaks beneath them, they collapse their legs, flatten the hanging body, and let the first rush carry them. Underwater they become broad, dark leaves with a thickened middle. The feet turn edge-on. Two arms, normally tucked into grooves along the belly, reach forward.

They steer toward differences in pressure too slight to ruffle the surface.

Their arms are longer than their legs. At the end of each is a cluster of blunt fingers, some opposed, some arranged around a soft central pad. They use these hands to prise food from crevices, groom the bristles on one another’s backs, and touch the thin membranes concealed under the head.

An adult alone keeps those membranes folded. Among familiar company, it lets them hang.

The membranes are easily injured.

An individual with one torn or scarred will often travel with a companion’s hand resting beneath its jaw. The companion changes the pressure of its fingers as they walk. This continues for miles without either turning toward the other.

***

They feed where the leatherlike plants clear the water.

One kneels beside a mat and presses the backs of its fingers along the growing edge. The tiny pale animals retreat into the pores. After waiting a little, it lays its mouth against the stone and draws water through the mat in the opposite direction.

The feeding makes a small, busy sound, audible only if your head is very close.

They also eat the clay pellets that appear on new islands, selecting some and rejecting others. Broken open, the chosen pellets contain concentric skins of living matter: dark, slick, tightly folded. The inhabitants swallow them whole. A meal may consist of six pellets gathered over an afternoon.

Their bellies contain gravel.

You can sometimes hear it shifting when an individual climbs a threshold. Some of the stones are swallowed for grinding, but others are retained long after they have become smooth. These are pale, porous pieces that slowly diminish. The creatures search for them in particular flows, occasionally abandoning a rich feeding place to stand for hours with their hands in a thread of apparently empty water.

Young ones receive their first stones from an adult’s mouth.

This is done with considerable attention. Several adults may examine a stone before it is passed to the waiting young. A rejected piece is carried away from the nursery rather than dropped nearby.

***

The light-sensitive organs are small and set far apart, sheltered beneath fleshy rims. They can detect a moving silhouette, the brightness of exposed mineral, the sudden white of a dry appearance. Fine detail seems to matter little.

Their world reaches them chiefly through the feet, the bristles, and the folded tissues of the throat.

The immense noise does not resolve for them into silence. It remains immense. They tire of it.

After prolonged travel, they become clumsy. Their feet stay spread after the weight has left them. They turn toward disturbances that have already passed. They may stop feeding and crouch with their hands pressed against the sides of the head.

This is when they seek the stone enclosures.

The chambers are built over hollows whose shapes continue beneath the floor. Water passes through narrow channels in the foundations, divides, returns upon itself, and enters the interior with some of its movements canceled by others. A person standing inside would still hear a great deal. To the inhabitants, particular burdens have been lifted.

They rest with their feet against the inner walls.

There are places on those walls polished by knees and head rims, as well as by hands. Some hollows are too small for a living adult. These remain in use by the young.

The faint dry smell comes from the lining they leave behind: a thin secretion rubbed into the stone during rest. Freshly deposited, it is translucent. With age it becomes chalky and gives off an odor like warm dust. They scrape away the thickest accumulations and renew the bare patches.

A chamber left untended gradually loses its usefulness. Fine grit enters the channels. One of the canceled pressures returns.

An arriving traveler may spend the whole of its intended rest clearing a passage with a flexible strip of root.

***

They do not live permanently at the enclosures. Food seldom permits it, and the water that approaches a doorway comfortably in one season may approach it with lethal force in another.

Their households travel.

A household is usually five or six adults, several young, and sometimes an old individual whose feet no longer close properly. They spread over a wide area to feed but keep contact through the bottom. At intervals one plants both feet, stiffens its legs, and draws the hanging belly upward.

A pulse goes into the stone.

Far away, another answers.

The signals carry poorly through deep silt and very well along certain buried beds. A household can be separated by a few yards and lose one another completely. Elsewhere, they remain together across a distance you could not see through clear air.

Much of their knowledge concerns where a voice can travel.

They learn submerged ridges as sequences of changes underfoot. They learn which layers carry a warning faithfully and which lengthen it into something reassuring. They know stretches where an old signal returns after the sender has moved away.

The young sometimes answer these returning calls.

An adult then presses them flat against the bottom and holds them there until they have felt the difference.

***

At the enclosures with cups, a traveler first clears away loose sediment. It puts a fingertip into each cup, one after another, then waits.

The cups have different depths, and narrow mineral veins run beneath them. Each responds to a different range of pressures arriving through the stone.

The traveler compares them with its own body. A finger in one cup; a foot on the pavement; the throat membranes open just above the water. Occasionally it shifts a small pebble from one cup to another.

Other travelers inspect these pebbles before entering.

There may be only three or four stones, but their positions can warn of a foundation channel beginning to clog, a dangerous flow approaching through a lower layer, or a nursery sheltering inside. Some arrangements persist long after the circumstance that prompted them has passed. The inhabitants disagree over these. They touch the cups, rearrange a stone, leave, return, and put it back.

The oldest individuals spend a great deal of time at such rows.

They are not always the ones whose arrangement is allowed to remain.

***

The young are carried in the ribbed pouch beneath the body. The pale folds visible there are their feet.

For a long time they have no usable legs. They cling to the lining and feed from glandular ridges while their broad soles lie pressed against the carrier’s abdomen. Through that contact they receive the movements of walking, the responses of stone, the answering pulses of other adults.

Before they ever touch the basin floor, they have traveled it.

When one is ready to emerge, the household finds a shallow place over continuous rock. The carrier lowers itself until the pouch rests on the bottom. The young unfolds, laboriously, and stands beneath the adult’s body.

Its first independent movement is usually to lift both feet.

It falls.

All the nearby adults put their hands into the water. They do not necessarily catch it. Their fingers surround it, providing small disturbances it can push against and follow. Eventually it finds the bottom again.

For several days afterward, it walks with one hand holding the rim of the pouch.

That makes the third mark between the tracks.

***

The dry appearances frighten them more than deep water does.

Before one forms, they often gather around the site with their throat tissues fully extended. Something in the approaching boundary draws them. They taste the water, touch the bottom, retreat, and come close again.

When the hard white light arrives, their membranes snap shut.

A creature caught inside may survive a small appearance. Its pouch contracts tightly. Clear fluid runs from the beads on its back, spreading beneath the scales. It folds its feet under itself and waits.

The household waits outside.

They bring no water to the edge. Water cannot be made to enter. They do, however, lay their hands against the boundary wherever the trapped individual’s feet appear to touch it.

Sometimes the dry patch contains bristled vegetation of a kind that grows nowhere in the surrounding basin. Sometimes it contains stone standing on edge. Once, an individual was seen reaching toward the shadow within a patch, extending its arm farther and farther while the others pulled at its body.

When the water returned, it held a small object.

It carried this into an enclosure and remained there through several changes of current. Others came to touch it. Nothing visible passed between them.

Eventually the object was placed in one of the wall hollows too small for an adult.

They still clear the silt from that hollow.

***

They have games.

In the quietest chambers, two or three will sit with their feet touching and try to send a pulse through one another without letting it reach the floor. Failure causes all of them to open their throat folds and make a rapid fluttering movement.

The young cheat by lifting a foot.

Adults sometimes permit this for a while, then send a sudden pulse through the wall instead. The young tumble against one another. Their gravel rattles.

They decorate the undersides of their arms with pale clay, drawing lines that are visible chiefly when they reach for someone. In the moving water the lines soon blur. A carefully marked individual may spend much of a journey with its arms held against its belly, preserving the work until it arrives.

There are quarrels over sleeping hollows, food stones, the pace of travel. There are individuals who habitually go ahead, and others who will not leave until every member of the household has crossed a difficult seam.

One may stop in the middle of feeding to rest its hand beneath another’s folded throat.

The other leans its weight into the hand.

Around them the stone grinds, buried crusts fracture, and whole fields of bubbles force their way upward. Neither moves for a little while.

Then the hand is withdrawn. They test the bottom, take their weight onto their feet, and continue toward a shelter whose entrance one of them remembers.
