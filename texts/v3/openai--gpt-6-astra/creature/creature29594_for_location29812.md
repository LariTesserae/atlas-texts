The animals in the yard are not strays. Each lies in a place that fits it, although the fit is harder to see than it is behind the grilles.

The one beside the pumping-house door has six legs. You discover this when it lifts its body a little, exposing three blunt feet on the nearer side. The feet are pale, soft-looking, divided at their ends into short pads. They have none of the metallic character of its back. Under the shell there is a small, vulnerable animal, closely folded, with joints that would be easy to injure.

It lowers itself before you can see its head.

There is a crescent of clean paving beside it, just wider than the sweep of a limb. Beyond that crescent the dust begins. Its shell has a shallow depression near one end, filled with the same dust, except where two fine appendages keep passing across it. These may be antennae. They emerge from underneath, wipe the depression, and disappear. Their tips never quite reach the middle.

The creature under the freight scale is of a different kind. What looked like a rounded back is three backs overlapping. They belong to one animal, or to three animals whose attachment is concealed by the scale. A little fringe of flesh runs along the lower edge of each plate. The fringes quiver independently. Now and then all three lift together, and the resulting dark slit has the attentive quality of an eye.

Neither creature is asleep. Their stillness is full of small accommodations to the forces passing through the ground. A foot tightens; a seam opens; something tender retreats behind a rim. They receive the district through their undersides.

The white animal in the basin receives it another way.

Its legs extend radially from a narrow body, but not at equal intervals. Some are paired closely; others have broad gaps between them. Each ends in a flat disc pressed against the porcelain. One leg rests on the basin’s old overflow hole. Another is bent upward, its terminal disc held clear of any surface. This raised foot trembles so rapidly that its edges look furry.

There is a head, very small and turned sideways. It has mouthparts like folded paper. From time to time these unfold into a delicate square and close again without taking anything in.

Its whiteness is not the whiteness of something that has never seen daylight. It is dense and opaque, like the inside of a tooth.

Behind the ventilation grilles, there are animals with scarcely any back at all.

Their protected surfaces have become thin, and the hard parts are concentrated where another body bears against them. A creature may have a polished knob halfway down one side, a groove running beneath its throat, or a plate shaped to accept the blunt end of a neighbor’s limb. In the deeper ranks, you glimpse bodies with long unsupported middles stretched between such points of contact. They breathe in narrow folds.

The eyes do not all look outward. Some face the belly of the creature above them. Some are turned into joints. One black eye, visible through a rectangular opening, rests almost against a translucent membrane on which a succession of little shadows passes. Its pupil follows each shadow across the same short distance.

There are eyelids here. There are lashes so fine they show only when they catch the light. One animal has a moist lower lid gathered into three wrinkles; another has a hard white ring around its eye and cannot close it. These differences persist among bodies so tightly fitted that you might expect all unnecessary detail to have been rubbed away.

A small face lies sideways against the wired glass of a shed window. Its mouth is nowhere visible. Above its eye, a short row of bristles has been bent flat by the pressure of another animal. One bristle points the wrong way.

You find yourself looking back for that face.

Feeding takes place in the walls.

At a pipe coupling, a soft collar protrudes from the masonry. The collar is alive. Along its exposed edge are dozens of mouths, each no larger than a pinprick, with bright triangular pieces set into the rims. The mouths close in succession around something you cannot see. Farther back, the same action travels through a row of throats.

There is no feeding frenzy, no struggle for a place at the supply. The hungry surfaces are already where the food passes. Some animals swallow; others hold channels open through their own bodies. A few have mouths turned outward, away from the pipe, and these work too, opening against the cool air.

It is difficult to tell what any individual receives.

The little animals that pass loads between the gears have broad shoulders and nearly transparent abdomens. Each takes the pressure on a pair of forelimbs, bends, and delivers it behind itself. Through the abdomen you can see a pale loop contract. The loop fills again as the animal straightens. All along the row, the effort appears briefly in the same exposed place.

They are exerting themselves. The work has not become effortless merely because it never ends.

There are creatures that seem to do very little. A broad, furred patch beneath an oil cup rises and falls. A worm with a dark head keeps the first third of its body extended across a screw slot. In the angle between two housings, something almost spherical holds a droplet in a cup formed by its lips. The droplet shivers. The lips adjust.

You cannot dismiss these as passengers. You cannot establish their duties either.

At the fountain the white animals are more separate. Each has room enough for its whole outline to be seen: four folded limbs, a swollen throat, two minute lateral eyes. Their open mouths are dark red inside. The whistle is made by a small lip of tissue stretched across the back of the mouth, and on each side of that lip a vessel beats visibly.

Some have a single gray spot on the crown. Some have none. One, beside the rusted left-hand spout, has a foreleg folded beneath itself at an awkward angle. It does not correct the position.

They face the running water without reaching it.

Perhaps this is a form of drinking. Perhaps the stream holds open the particular interval of sound in which they can breathe. Standing among them, you hear no separate whistles, only a brightness within the noise. Yet each little throat is doing its own work. Close enough to see them, you can no longer hear what any one contributes.

Nothing here offers the relief of being merely decorative, but there is beauty that appears to have no use: a violet sheen inside a joint, the scalloped edge of a breathing slit, a tuft of amber hairs sheltered under a flange. These are visible only from certain angles. The animals do not present them.

Nor do they hide.

When your shadow crosses the pumping-house door, the gray creature lifts itself once more. This time a head comes out: narrow, softly wrinkled, bearing two small eyes well apart. It turns slightly, bringing one eye toward your shoe.

The look lasts.

Under its shell the pale feet keep their hold on the pavement. Its fine appendages continue their incomplete passage through the dust. You wait for it to approach or withdraw, but there is only the looking, sustained alongside everything else it must do.
