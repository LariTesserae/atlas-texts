The builder has tucked one foot through the stair rail so that she can rest without drifting. Her other foot hangs loose, its toes spread slightly in the current. There is white paste beneath her nails and a healed split in the web of skin between her thumb and forefinger. The adze at her wrist is older than its handle, which is older than the cord securing it. She replaced the cord this morning after finding a pale, soft place where it passed through the stone.

Her name is Emet. Spoken, it has two syllables. In the streets it is a small inward turn of the hand followed by a touch to the wrist. Her younger brother makes the turn too large when he wants to annoy her.

She is not waiting for the whole slope to harden. That will take years, and parts of it may never harden at all. She is waiting for a particular change beneath the surface: a resistance that yields, then holds. When she felt it yesterday, it was still too deep. This morning she can reach it with the heel of her hand.

There is time to take another breath.

She pulls herself up the stair rope, leaving the basket tethered to the rail. At the nearest house she ducks beneath a low lintel and rises into a bell of air scarcely wider than her shoulders. Someone has left a twist of bitter foliage caught in the ceiling mesh. She presses it gently, releasing its smell.

For a while she does nothing but breathe.

The people of the valley have broad ribs and a way of becoming very still in water. At rest, their hearts beat slowly enough that a child learning to feel a pulse often thinks they have lost it. They can work for several minutes between visits to air, longer if the work is light. A strong swimmer can cross the inhabited terraces without stopping, but arrives with little strength left for anything else.

Emet used to do this when she was young. Now she chooses her route by the breathing places.

Everyone does, eventually.

A newly opened passage may be shorter by fifty arm-lengths and still be inconvenient because its chamber is too far toward one end. A useful neighbor keeps an air bell accessible even when the rest of the house is closed. Someone who ties the entrance shut over a private quarrel will find the matter discussed in chambers all over town before evening.

Yet air is not inexhaustible. After a crowded gathering, people take turns carrying fresh gas to the chamber in clay vessels. The last arrivals are expected to help. They descend holding their vessels mouth-down, careful not to tilt them, while above them the remaining guests clear away cups and leaf-wrapped food.

There are people who always leave before this work begins.

Emet knows most of them.

***

In the water, the valley’s inhabitants look less broad than they do in air. Their arms lie close to their bodies when they travel; their clothes are bound at wrist and ankle, with loose material gathered into flat pleats along the back. Hair is braided, cropped, or wound beneath a mesh cap. An untied length of hair can catch on a rough slab without its owner feeling anything until they turn their head.

Children dislike having it bound. Parents sit with a child between their knees, one foot through a household loop, and work through the tangles while the child twists to watch the doorway.

Their eyes have a clear inner lid. In the open water it lies smoothly over the eye; in air it withdraws, leaving them briefly blinking and blurred. Emet has a small scar on hers. To her, every lamp has a faint second lamp beside it.

They are not graceful in every circumstance. A person carrying a broad door panel is as awkward here as anywhere. Baskets overturn. A sleeve catches an adze handle. Two people coming around a blind corner seize the same rope and hang there, entangled, each waiting for the other to make a sensible movement.

Their street language has signs for these occasions.

Some signs are formed against the body, where they remain visible in poor light. Others require both hands and are difficult to make while holding a load. A refusal can be very economical. An explanation of why the refusal is reasonable usually requires setting something down.

In darkness, words pass through touch. A few taps at the wrist ask who is there. Names can be signed into a palm. The gestures used to guide someone who has become faint are different from those used in ordinary conversation, large and unmistakable.

Children learn those early, although they are not trusted to lead a rescue. Their task is to reach the nearest rope and strike its wooden guide with a stone.

The sound carries surprisingly far.

***

Emet’s apprentice arrives while she is back at the slope.

He is carrying three wedges in one hand, although she asked for six. He signs that the others split.

She holds out her palm.

He gives her a wedge. She turns it over. The cut follows the grain badly, wandering toward one edge. He has tried to correct it by shaving away the thicker side until the wedge is handsome, symmetrical, and almost certain to break.

She gives it back and shows him where to cut the next one.

His name is Seli. He has grown so rapidly this year that he still reaches past things. His elbows strike doorframes. His mother has let down his cuffs twice, and a strip of newer cloth shows at each ankle.

He is good with plants. He can loosen a root mat from its frame without tearing the fine feeding threads, a task Emet finds exasperating. He is less good with wood and very impatient with stone. He chose building because of the outer terraces: the unfinished spaces, the equipment hanging over open water, the possibility of being first to stand somewhere.

Most of the work so far has involved repairing roofs.

Emet makes room for him beside her. She takes his wrist and puts his hand where hers has been.

At first he presses with his fingers. She shifts his hand until the heel of it bears against the slope.

They wait.

A faint warmth comes through the paste. Beneath that, something firmer seems to move away from his touch. He pushes harder, and Emet catches his wrist.

No.

She lets him try again.

This is a large part of what she teaches: how little force is needed to discover what is there. Her own teacher could judge a forming wall through the wooden shaft of a tool. In his last years he could no longer see well enough to find his own basket, but people still brought him to difficult foundations.

He was also a poor estimator of costs, and left his daughter several debts.

Emet remembers both things.

***

They eat in air because swallowing in water is troublesome and because food tastes better when it can be smelled.

The common stems are crisp when young, fibrous when old. Some are split and scraped; others are bruised, folded in leaves, and left beside warm stone until they soften. There is a pale root that tastes of almost nothing unless the cook is patient. There is a dark paste that children love and adults accuse one another of stretching with cheaper ingredients.

A household’s best eating chamber may contain only enough ledge for four people. More can gather if some remain in the water with their faces lifted, but this is tiring, and someone always puts an elbow in the food.

Emet lives with her brother, his husband, and their little daughter. Her own son has moved to the upper gardens. He comes down frequently to borrow tools and less frequently to return them.

At meals the little girl likes to repeat things she has heard in other chambers, especially remarks she does not understand. Once she announced a neighbor’s intention to leave her husband while that husband was helping repair their door.

Since then, the adults have tried to teach her the difference between news and something overheard.

She finds the distinction suspicious.

In the streets she is quieter. Her hands are still small and her signs sometimes ambiguous. When excited, she forgets to leave space between them. Emet must catch both wrists, gently, and ask her to begin again.

***

Other lives occupy the town without participating in it.

The translucent grazers fit themselves beneath roof bindings during the brightest part of the day. Up close, their bodies contain a little tangle of darker organs suspended near the middle. Their feet are tipped with minute hooks. A large one can hold against a current that would carry a person away from the rope.

Children give them names, though one grazer is difficult to distinguish from another. A clean track beneath a window is enough to reassure a child that a favorite has returned.

Builders are fond of them because they remove the film from joints. Gardeners are less fond of them when they climb a seed stalk. No one has managed to keep one where it is wanted.

In the warm seams beneath the terraces, smaller things stir.

Most are visible only as a change in the water, a trembling whiteness that disperses when disturbed. Sometimes a new slab, lifted too soon, exposes a soft moving skin underneath. It contracts from the light. The builders replace the slab and mark it with a loose tassel.

Emet does not know whether this skin makes the stone or eats something the stone releases. Neither does anyone she trusts. Her teacher called it the mountain’s lining, but he called several unrelated things that when he could not remember their proper names.

She washes her tools after touching it.

***

Late in the working period, Seli feels the change.

He looks at Emet before he signs, wanting confirmation. She leaves his hand where it is and tests the slope beside it.

The yielding layer is thinner now. Beneath it, a narrow rib of stone has reached the underside of the lowest step. There is still a gap at one end, no wider than a fingertip.

They place a wedge there—not one of Seli’s new ones—and ease the step until its weight is shared between the rib and the rail. Emet checks the tether, the two joints, the far corner. Then she takes her foot out of the loop and settles her weight onto the stair.

A little mineral clouds around her heel.

The step holds.

Seli is watching her face. She signs for him to try it.

He lowers himself beside her with excessive care. For the first time that day his feet stop moving. They sit shoulder to shoulder, looking across the roofs toward the gardens. Someone is hauling a covered lamp through the lower streets. Farther away, three figures gather beneath a house and disappear one after another into its air.

Seli touches the stair, then the new stone beneath it.

Tomorrow Emet will show him how to leave room around the joint. In time the rib will thicken, press upward, and begin to lift the step out of true. Someone will have to cut it free.

For now she lets him sit.

When it is time to breathe, she touches his wrist. They leave the basket secured beside the rail and pull themselves toward the houses, the unfinished staircase steady behind them.
