The animal that sleeps beneath the opening in the island building is usually mistaken, at first, for something left there.

It lies against the wall in a loose gray coil, with a scattering of reed husks caught in its coat. One end is broad and blunt, the other tapered. There is no visible head. Even when footsteps cross the room, it may do no more than tighten slightly, drawing its outer folds together.

Then a hand comes out.

It has four fingers, dark at the tips, and a broad pad where a thumb might be. The hand feels across the floor without the rest of the animal moving. It finds a husk, turns it over, pushes it beneath the coil. A second hand appears farther along the body. After a while the head lifts from somewhere between them.

The face is wide, short, and sparsely furred. Two amber eyes look almost sideways. Beneath each is a crease that continues to the corner of the mouth. The nostrils close independently, wrinkling the muzzle as if the animal finds one part of the room less agreeable than another.

It watches the doorway. Its hands continue sorting the husks.

Standing, it is about as high as a person’s knee, though most of its length is behind it. The forelimbs are short and unusually free at the shoulder; the hind limbs fold close against a heavy pelvis. Its tail is broad near the body and narrows into a flexible, flattened end. Out of water it walks with an uneven, rolling gait. It can rise onto its hind feet, but prefers to lean against something when it does.

The gray coat is not quite fur. Each strand divides near the skin into several finer strands, making a dense underlayer beneath a covering of smooth, stiff fibers. Dry, these lie in overlapping bands. Wet, they separate along channels through which water drains toward the belly. An animal coming out of a pool can remain dripping underneath while its back is already pale and dry.

There are several dozen of them around the central lake.

They sleep in root cavities, beneath mineral shelves, and inside the few surviving rooms. The building on the island is particularly desirable during the hottest weeks. Its occupant does not own it in any absolute sense. She drives away some visitors, tolerates others, and allows two to enter without looking up. During heavy rain there may be six animals sleeping along the cool wall, with a careful stretch of empty floor between each body.

The one nearest the opening is an old female. A notch divides the pad of her left hand. When she eats, she steadies her food with the right and uses the injured hand for the more delicate work.

Her bed contains very little bedding. Most of the reed husks are the remains of meals.

Inside certain swollen reeds, large insects spend years feeding on the soft tissue around the stem’s air chambers. The animals find them by pressing the side of the face against the stalk. The creases beneath their eyes open slightly. After listening, or feeling, they make a short incision with the lower teeth and pull the grub out with two fingers.

They investigate many more stalks than they open.

In the marsh, one can spend most of a morning following an adult from reed to reed without seeing it feed. It stands in water up to its chest, embraces a stem, rests its cheek against it, moves on. Its tail remains curved around the nearest firm root. Occasionally it closes its eyes.

A youngster following behind will embrace the same stem and press its face against the same place. Sometimes the youngster bites anyway. Water enters the damaged chamber with a quiet gulp, and the adult turns back.

Correction is physical but seldom violent. A hand closes over the young animal’s muzzle. The adult holds it still for several breaths, then guides its head to a different part of the reed.

The young find this difficult to endure. Their fingers continue working throughout.

They eat other things: the firm white bases of aquatic leaves, small fish, bird eggs when they can reach them. Their flat grinding teeth sit behind four narrow cutting teeth that meet like shears. A fish disappears headfirst, accompanied by rapid movements of the cheeks. A grub is opened carefully and consumed in portions. Certain organs are discarded.

These discarded pieces draw small green animals down from the foliage. The reed hunters sometimes wait for them.

For all their slow deliberation, they can move very quickly. A hand strikes, the mouth closes, and the green animal is gone before the leaf it jumped from has stopped trembling.

They are hunted in turn.

One of the large gliding birds will take a youngster from an exposed bank. In deeper water, something narrow and dark moves beneath the grazers, attending their herds. It is usually visible only as a disturbance traveling against the direction of their passage. Adults will enter those pools together, keeping their tails in contact. The young wait among the buttress roots.

A broken chain of contact brings every head above water.

Their alarm call is surprisingly small: two dry clicks made deep in the mouth. It carries poorly through the air but well through a submerged root. At the sound, animals on opposite sides of a tree climb simultaneously.

They know the valley through such contacts.

While resting, they trail a hand in a channel. Before crossing open mud, they press their chins to it. They can distinguish the heavy tread of a grazer from the irregular impacts of falling fruit, and the approach of a familiar animal sometimes wakes them without causing them to open their eyes.

The towers trouble this sense. Close to a platform, an animal repeatedly lifts its hands and sets them down again, trying different places. Most hurry across if they must pass there. Some take long detours.

But they do not fear the towers uniformly. A male on the western shore habitually sleeps in the shadow of one, on a strip of bare rock between the moss and the platform. His coat is darker than that of the lake animals. He brings his food there and washes it in rainwater collected in a hollow.

When a band of shadow begins to climb the tower, he leaves. He does so before there is anything visible to prompt him.

None of the others has adopted his sleeping place.

Their habits vary from shore to shore. In the southern channels, they use flat stones to split hard seed cases. Around the island they wedge the cases into cracks and open them with their teeth. A young animal that moves between these groups may spend a season carrying a stone to cracks where it is unnecessary.

They carry useful things beneath one forelimb, held against the chest. This makes their gait even more awkward. A good splitting stone may travel with an animal for years, polished on one face by the rubbing of its coat.

Other things are carried without any evident use.

The old female in the building has a curved piece of blue material, smaller than her palm. It might have broken from something once, though none of its edges is sharp. When held against the light it becomes almost clear.

She keeps it in a shallow depression behind the bench.

She does not examine it every day. Sometimes weeks pass before she retrieves it, washes it, and turns it between her hands. A visiting youngster once carried it to the causeway. She followed at a pace that made both of them slip, caught the youngster by the back of the neck, and recovered it.

Afterward she sat in the doorway holding it against her belly.

The opening above her bed is part of her daily routine.

She cannot see through it while lying down. To look out, she braces her hind feet against the wall and rests her forearms on the lower edge. The scratches near the floor come partly from this climb, though some are older and lie beneath a thin skin of mineral deposit.

The view does not seem to confuse her. She recognizes birds on the roof, clouds approaching the island, animals crossing the causeway. She occasionally goes outside to meet a visitor she has seen arrive in the opening.

More often she stays where she is.

When the roof birds bathe, she watches the shallow dishes closely. There is one near the northern edge where nestlings sometimes lose their footing. If a bird in the opening begins to struggle there, she drops to the floor and leaves the room.

Several breaths later she is beneath that part of the roof.

She has caught three fallen birds this way. Once she arrived early and waited so long that she went back inside; the bird fell after she had settled down again. Once nothing fell at all. She inspected the ground, then the roof, then the ground once more.

Her surviving daughter has learned to stand beside her at the opening. The daughter looks mostly at the causeway.

At the valley’s rim, these animals are cautious but not helpless. They work the lower shelves where the suspended water eventually touches stone, harvesting pale worms from the mineral ribs. Their tails wrap around shrubs. They test every new reach with one hand.

Youngsters are brought there late.

An adult first lets them approach a shallow basin well back from the drop. It catches floating leaves and releases them into the current. Most circle and return. Some enter the clear bulge where the water begins to hang outward from the rock.

The youngsters reach after those leaves.

The adult pulls them back by the pelvis, firmly enough to make them cry out.

Older animals sometimes fish along the pendant falls. They lie flat on the stone and put one arm into water that has no ground beneath it. Small fish gather there to feed on suspended debris. Through the curved surface, the reaching hand looks immense, then very small, then detached from the animal entirely.

They never enter with both hands.

At night they gather more closely than they do by day. Two or three will sleep touching along the flanks, their tails laid across one another. A waking animal grooms whatever part of its neighbor is nearest. The coat must be kept free of the fine roots that take hold readily in this valley. Animals too sick to groom develop green seams along their backs.

The old female has one such seam between her shoulders, beyond the comfortable reach of either hand.

Her daughter works at it in the cool room while rain strikes the roof. She parts the stiff gray fibers, grips each little plant near its base, and eases it free. Some roots come away carrying flakes of skin. At those, the old female turns and takes the younger animal’s wrist in her mouth.

She holds it without biting.

Then she releases it, lowers her head, and lets the work continue.
