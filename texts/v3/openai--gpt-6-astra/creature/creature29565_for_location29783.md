Behind a slab below the pigeon chambers, something has made a bed of feathers.

Not a nest. The feathers have been pressed flat, mixed with fur and the brittle stems of leaves. There are a few larger quills, their shafts bitten through. The bed lies well back from the opening, beyond a turn in the crack, where neither falling dust nor the light from the opposite wall reaches it.

Its owner is asleep.

At first there is little to distinguish: a length of brown fur, a darker tail brought close along the body, one rounded ear. The animal has fitted itself into the stone so completely that it seems smaller than it is. Its nose lies beneath its hind foot. Now and then the ear moves, selecting something from the enormous noise outside.

It is a stone marten, an adult female. A white patch divides the fur of her throat and extends onto her chest. One ear has a healed notch. There is dust on the outer hairs of her tail, but beneath those hairs the coat is dense and clean.

She has several sleeping places along the broken caverns. This one is useful while the pigeons are breeding. Another lies among the roots of a fig, in a hollow reached from behind the tree rather than beneath it. A third is no more than a dry shelf inside a passage too narrow for her to turn around in. She enters headfirst and backs out.

Her country does not have the shape of the basin.

The broad avenue on the floor belongs hardly at all to her daily movements. She crosses it when she must, travelling low and quickly, but most of her routes run along the walls: behind a leaning slab, up a seam, across the top of an old flowstone curtain. In one place she disappears into a hole close to the ground and emerges much higher, having climbed through a chimney inside the rock.

These routes meet at places with food, cover, or a view of an approach. Between them are long stretches she passes without slowing. A ledge that looks continuous from below may end at a gap she cannot cross. Elsewhere, a few projections too small to cast much shadow make a dependable staircase.

She knows where the pigeons can hear her coming.

On loose rubble she places her feet slowly. On solid stone she moves with a fluid hurry, back lengthening and shortening, tail adjusting behind her. Close to an occupied hole, she may wait with her chin nearly touching the ledge. The pigeon inside shifts. Grit scratches beneath its feet. She lifts her head a little.

Often there is no catch. The bird bursts out, striking the lip of the opening with its wings, and the alarm travels along the wall. She remains at the entrance for a moment, looking into the empty cavity. Then she goes in to investigate.

She eats eggs when she finds them, beetles when they are abundant, small rodents in the leaf beds. In the season of ripe figs, her droppings are full of seeds. She climbs into the crowns after dark and works along branches that bend beneath her weight, holding on with her hind feet while she reaches for fruit.

What falls from those branches feeds smaller residents.

Under the figs, mice keep close to the borders of the fallen leaves. They carry seeds into slots beneath stones, sometimes forgetting them, sometimes returning to a cache until nothing remains but husks. Their paths are tunnels through the litter, scarcely visible from above. A dry leaf lifted at the wrong moment can uncover a whole occupied space: a mouse crouched beside a root, a beetle still moving, a spider withdrawing down the side of a pebble.

Farther from the gardens, beneath the bats, food arrives from the ceiling.

Here the inhabitants work in layers. Beetles cross the crust of the guano mounds. Larvae remain where the material is finer and less exposed. Mites occupy spaces that would look empty even to someone kneeling beside them. At the margins, spiders stretch threads across small fissures; a passing insect need only lose its footing for the whole arrangement to function.

The marten sometimes hunts along these margins. More often she passes above them. Most of what flourishes there is too small to interest her.

As evening approaches, she wakes before leaving her bed. She stretches in the confined space, pressing her forefeet against the stone, then grooms her chest and the underside of one hind leg. A feather adheres briefly to her muzzle. She removes it with her tongue.

At the crack’s entrance she pauses.

The light wells have begun to lose their brightness. Birds are still moving between the walls, but the traffic has changed: more arrivals, fewer departures. From deep overhead comes the stir of bats loosening their grip and crawling toward open space.

She comes out onto the ledge. For several seconds she is plainly visible, brown against pale limestone, looking down into the court. Then she turns behind a projection and is gone.

Later she will reach the rim. There are insects in the scrub above, fallen fruit farther off, other animals whose paths cross hers. The caverns are a shelter and a hunting ground, not a sealed world. Birds bring in what they have eaten beyond the plateau. Bats return with their stomachs full. Seeds arrive inside bodies or caught among feathers.

Near dawn, the marten comes back by a cleft below an old carob. She descends out of the first light, carrying nothing.

By the time the swifts begin to call, she has reached the slab beneath the pigeons. She slips into the crack, turns beyond sight, and settles onto the feather bed. Outside, a pigeon lands on the dusty lip of its hole. A few grains fall from beneath its feet and strike the empty floor.
