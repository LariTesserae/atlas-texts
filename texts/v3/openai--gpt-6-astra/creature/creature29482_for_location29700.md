The keeper of the winch has been in the room with you for some time before you notice her.

She is sitting behind the open door, where the wall makes a shallow recess. Her coat is the color of the floor. One hand rests palm upward on her knee; the other holds a small cup with a lid. She has heard you come in, heard the pebble scrape over the edge, and decided that neither sound requires her to stand.

When you turn, she lifts the cup slightly. There is another in the cupboard. You are meant to take it.

She is not as old as she first appears. The deep lines beside her mouth are mostly the result of holding it closed. Her hair, cut just below the ears, is dark at the roots and almost colorless at the ends. A clear membrane crosses each nostril when she breathes in. It moves so quickly you might mistake it for a trick of the light.

The drink is warm, faintly salty, and thicker than water. She waits until you have swallowed before asking which way you came.

You name the freight yard.

“Across it?”

When you say yes, she looks down at your shoes. There is a sheltered passage along the eastern wall, only a little longer. She tells you where to find it on your return.

Then she puts down her cup, walks to the desk, and records the tension.

Up close, the figures are not all the same. Some differ in the last place. Beside one entry she has made a mark like an open hook.

“That was while you were outside,” she says.

You had noticed nothing.

Neither had she, until she read the instrument.

***

The people of the city are not quite alike, although at a distance their clothes and manner of moving make them seem so. There are broad people whose coats hang open indoors, narrow people who remain wrapped to the chin, people with trembling hands and people who can guide a wire into a socket by touch. Most are short. All have deep bodies, with the lower ribs extending farther down than you expect.

Their breathing is quiet when they are seated. Walking brings out a small, dry catch at the end of each exhalation.

They plan their journeys around places to rest. A doorstep a few centimeters higher than the others may be known across a whole district. People give directions by these things: the rail that is firm, the cabinet you can lean against, the bench inside the unlocked vestibule. Distance alone tells them very little. A nearby building with a steep entrance can be harder to reach than one beyond the freight yard.

No one thinks it rude to finish recovering before answering a question.

Their hands show more difference than their faces. Some have thickened pads on the heels of the palms from opening pressure doors. Some have pale grooves around the fingers where rings of sealing compound are rolled thin. The keeper’s left thumbnail is split along its length. She rubs it with the neighboring finger whenever she is listening closely.

They wear soft shoes with replaceable soles. The soles are cut in kitchens, at maintenance benches, sometimes on the desk beside a ledger. A person can be recognized by the sound of a poorly fitted heel for years before anyone persuades them to have it corrected.

***

They live in fewer rooms than their buildings contain.

A household may occupy a kitchen, a sleeping recess, and a chamber where a warm pipe passes through the floor. The rest remains shut. This does not necessarily mean anyone has died. A room may have been closed because its door no longer fitted, because carrying heat to it became troublesome, or because the person using it moved downstairs.

Inside the occupied rooms, much is covered. Cups have lids. Bowls fit into bowls. A sleeping person draws a fine cloth over the lower half of the face, held away from the mouth by a curved strip sewn into the fabric. In the morning, the cloth is damp. It is folded inward and put into a narrow press beside the bed.

There are colors here that do not appear outside: a green cupboard interior, blue stitching under a cuff, the red underside of a repaired lampshade. Dyes are uneven and difficult to replace. A faded garment may be turned and turned again until the brighter seams lie exposed.

People are particular about these things. One woman dislikes the yellow thread used to mend her coat and mentions it whenever the coat is taken down. Her brother says no one can see it in the street. She says she can see it while putting it on.

Their disagreements are not all old, nor all important.

There are neighbors who do not speak, though they still leave each other the customary portion when a food vessel is opened. There are people who take too long returning tools, and one man in the lower apartments who has made three nearly identical keys because he enjoys being asked which is the correct one.

Visitors are interesting. They are also work. Someone must find a cup, loosen a chair from its accustomed corner, explain which handle must not be turned. Hospitality sometimes comes with audible irritation.

***

Food arrives in sealed cylinders from chambers below the houses.

When opened, a cylinder contains a dense, pale growth, faintly fibrous, with a softer layer against the wall. It is sliced with a loop of wire. Most households warm it in a covered pan and add a dark paste kept in very small jars. Left long enough near a pipe, the cut surfaces develop a rind that some people prize and others pare away.

The chambers must be fed. This is work undertaken in pairs, with gloves and close-fitting masks, and children are not allowed near the open hatches. Nothing brought out of a house is simply tipped in. It is sorted, weighed, treated, and carried to different rooms according to rules that vary with the condition of each bed.

A good attendant can smell a change through a closed sampling valve.

Such people complain that the rest of the city notices food only when something is wrong with it.

There are meals for which a household saves the soft layer from several cylinders. There is a brittle sweet made by reducing one of the chamber liquids in a shallow dish. It sticks to the teeth and makes people thirsty. Parents have been objecting to it for a very long time.

***

There are children.

You are unlikely to see them on your first passage through the city. They spend much of their time in the connected interiors, where they can go farther without crossing bare ground. Their coats have loops between the shoulders so an adult can hold them on a stair without taking either hand.

They are taught early not to shout across a yard. A voice can carry to an inspection station and bring someone a long way for no reason.

This does not make them silent. Behind the thick walls they quarrel, drag stools, imitate the catches in their elders’ breathing. They whisper into disused speaking tubes. Some tubes return a voice clearly; others produce only a low, intimate rushing. There are elaborate arguments about what happens to the words in those.

In one apartment corridor, children have polished a strip of floor by sliding a metal lid along it. The game depends on stopping short of a seam. The lid belonged to something no adult now living has used, but there is an understanding that it must not be dented.

A child brought to the winch house is disappointed by the cables. He expected to see them move.

The keeper sets his fingers against one strand, then against the housing of the bearing. He feels the difference. For a while this satisfies him.

Later he asks to go outside again and look down.

***

The small animals are usually called jointlings, though the word used in the western districts also means a loose piece that cannot yet be thrown away.

They do not enter beds or food cupboards. Their interest is in the dry bloom that forms where warmth, ceramic, and a slight electrical leakage meet. A support that has been disconnected loses its animals gradually. First the clean tracks stop lengthening. Then, over weeks, the shells appear under neighboring fixtures.

People use this as evidence, but not as a dependable test.

One fitter keeps a jointling in a shallow box with an old insulator. She insists it recognizes the position of her hand. When she turns the box, it eventually works its way back to the side nearest her.

Her colleague points out that this is also the side nearest the heater.

She has heard this explanation before.

The animals molt rarely. Fresh ones are almost transparent, with a dark speck inside each segment. For a few hours their many feet can be seen working beneath them. Then the shells cloud and take on the color of whatever surface they inhabit.

Children collect the empty shells until they discover how easily they break.

***

Most adults tend machinery, but not continuously, and not with equal devotion.

An inspection may take several minutes. Reaching it may occupy the rest of a watch. At the station there is a seat, a covered drink, a set of gauges, and often some private work: a sleeve being resewn, a puzzle made from notched plates, a letter whose recipient lives within sight of the window.

The machinery needs attention at irregular intervals. Some adjustments are made once in a person’s working life. Others must be made repeatedly for several days, bringing unexpected company into a district that has been quiet for months.

People enjoy these occasions and complain about them afterward.

They know a great deal about particular things. The keeper can tell when one bearing is warming by laying the inside of her wrist against its cover. She cannot explain why the oldest cable consists of seven bundles rather than six. The drawing specifies seven. The spare clamps have seven seats. Somewhere below the platform, inaccessible without unloading the entire assembly, the bundles divide.

She has never seen that junction.

There are records, but finding a record is work too. Cabinets have been moved away from rooms that lost heat. Labels use names no longer applied to the places they describe. A careful answer may take years, especially if nobody urgently needs it.

Above several doors are the outlines of much larger entrances. People hang shelves within them. Asked what passed through, some say equipment; some say the entrances were built before the floors were inserted.

One old attendant says the tall cupboards in the western blocks were once standing spaces.

He is known for making statements of this kind. That does not settle whether he is wrong.

***

The city loses people. It also receives new ones, although a birth is uncommon enough to alter the errands of several households.

Someone moves closer to help. Someone takes over an inspection route. Small clothes come out of a sealed chest, are aired over a warm grating, and judged badly cut. The child grows into them regardless.

The dead leave more than empty rooms. They leave adjustments no one else thought to write down, acquaintances who now have no reason to cross a district, a particular cup that continues to be brought out by mistake. Their names remain attached to doors long after the occupants change.

At the winch house, the keeper still calls the recess behind the door her mother’s seat.

Her mother preferred it because she could watch the desk without sitting in the faint current from the stairwell. The keeper says this current is no longer there. She sits in the recess anyway.

When her replacement arrives, they compare the reading. He puts one finger beside the open hook in the ledger. She explains that she checked the adjacent gauge and found no corresponding change.

He nods, takes off his coat, and hangs it on the lower hook.

For a while they remain together. They discuss a sticking cupboard door, then someone who has begun walking to the food chambers again after an illness. The keeper finishes her drink. Her replacement has brought a small parcel wrapped in cloth, and he opens it between them.

Inside are three pieces of the brittle sweet.

She says he should not spend his allowance on it. She takes the smallest piece, then exchanges it for another.

Outside, the cables stand taut in the rising warmth. The reading holds. Neither of them goes to the desk for some time.
