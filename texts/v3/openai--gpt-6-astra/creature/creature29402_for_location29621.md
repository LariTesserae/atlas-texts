The woman tending the road has taken off one glove.

Her exposed hand is narrow, with a broad thumb and two pairs of fingers. Across the palm are little transverse folds, like the underside of a jointed stem. She rests it beside the damaged junction and waits. Occasionally one finger lifts. Something beneath the street answers by lifting it a little farther.

She is not listening to the plateau, exactly. She is finding out how much of it is listening to her.

On the stool beside her sits an open tin of food, a roll of spare binding, and a photograph held flat beneath a transparent scrap of horn. The photograph shows three people standing in front of a tower considerably taller than any now in town. One of them is the woman, younger, wearing an extravagant collar. She has brought the photograph because the junction may take all afternoon and she intends, when the traffic permits, to repair its bent corner.

The inhabitants have faces you can read before you understand their anatomy. Irritation is recognizable. So is the careful lack of expression with which a skilled person receives advice from someone passing by.

Their eyes are deep-set, sheltered above and below by short, stiff lashes. A clear inner lid crosses them sideways during spore weather. The mouth is small at rest but extends surprisingly far into the cheeks; when someone laughs, pale grinding ridges briefly show behind the lips. What looks like hair is a growth of fine hollow filaments. Children wear it loose. Adults usually bind it close to the head, threading the ends through ceramic rings so it cannot stand out in a dangerous charge.

Under their clothes they are more substantial than their narrow shoulders suggest. The chest is long. Flexible plates overlap along the flanks, making room for internal chambers to expand. At intervals, particularly after exertion, a row of slits below the ribs opens beneath the clothing. There is a dry, warm sigh.

They walk with their feet almost flat, feeling the surface through their soles. Boots have thin central pads and thick edges. A visitor’s heavy tread looks to them less like confidence than a failure to pay attention.

They do not all move carefully. Adolescents run across bridge lattices before the crossings have finished tightening. Porters hurry. Old people sometimes develop a shuffling step that fails to warn the road properly of their weight, and relatives worry about them.

The gardener’s name is conveyed through the comb at her collar as three soft taps and a lingering vibration. Spoken aloud, it becomes something like Eshet. She answers to either, though the spoken version leaves out a distinction she considers important.

Her work coat has six inner pockets for insect cases. Two are empty, one contains a beetle that has died, and one holds a creature she is not licensed to use on a public road.

She has used it twice.

***

The people are not small versions of the plateau. Their bodies maintain sharp boundaries. They can leave it, cross stone, sleep in a freestanding shelter, survive for a time without contact with living tissue. This independence costs them a great deal of food.

They eat the curls shaved by grazers, certain kinds of concentrated spore, and the soft chamber linings cultivated inside household walls. These last are pulled out in pale ribbons and toasted between charged plates. The smell is nutty, with a sharp mineral finish. Every neighborhood has a different opinion about how much scorching is acceptable.

They also eat animals.

The broad grazers are butchered in rooms insulated from the street. Their suspended dust is collected first, before the supporting tissues lose their charge and let it settle. The best cuts are dense valve muscles. Children like the brittle mouthplates, roasted until they split, though a child chewing one indoors will generally be sent out into the arcade.

At a meal, the inhabitants pass small bowls of powdered salts and bowls of almost identical-looking substances which must never be mistaken for salts. They recognize the difference by holding them near the lips. A cook who has damaged the fine sensory folds inside the mouth may still prepare food, but will ask someone else to finish seasoning it.

There are rich people who eat food raised entirely in detached vessels. There are poor people who regard this as a reasonable precaution and others who regard it as disgusting.

Most households grow at least one favorite thing. Beside a bedroom door there may be a little upholstered-looking swelling that produces a sweet edible lining once every few weeks. The family calls it by a pet name. When they move, they take a graft.

***

Their children begin life unable to tolerate the open air.

For the first months they remain in a flexible casing, carried against an adult’s flank or fitted into a warm recess in the house. Through its cloudy wall you can see the dark internal dust moving in irregular pulses. Visitors bend over it and disagree about whose face is emerging.

A newborn’s casing must be turned regularly. Experienced parents do this while talking, without looking down. An inexperienced one may stop in the middle of a sentence and concentrate completely.

Houses with infants do not lower all their floors at evening. A little section stays raised to preserve the nursery’s pressure, giving such buildings a lopsided appearance. People approaching from the road know to soften their jaw-clicks before anyone tells them.

Older children spend much of their time with the grazers.

The animals are patient rather than affectionate. They accept being leaned against, painted with harmless marking dust, and dressed in discarded latticework. They do not like their jaw-knocks imitated at close range. Every school has a child with a crescent-shaped scar who explains this distinction to younger ones.

A grazer can recognize a particular person by the charge pattern of their hands. It will come to that person even through a spore release, knocking furiously, expecting the person to know what to do.

Sometimes the person does not.

There are accounts of storms in which families found their way to shelter by following domestic animals, and others in which the animals followed families into places no one should have entered. The local histories preserve both. People here are wary of a useful story that has become too reassuring.

***

Eshet lives alone on the fourth floor of a building that is six stories high by day and a little under four by night.

She has lived there for a long time. Her rooms fit her badly.

The house keeps restoring a partition she had removed after her partner died. Every few months a ridge rises across the sitting-room floor. If neglected, it develops a soft upright edge and begins separating the two old sleeping spaces.

She could have the household growth thoroughly retrained. It would be expensive, but not beyond her means. Instead she cuts the ridge back herself. She complains about it to neighbors who know she does not want suggestions.

Other accommodations are easier. Her favorite chair has become a shallow socket in the floor. The wall beside it warms before she gets home. When she is away on a long job, she asks a neighbor to sit there briefly each evening. Otherwise the house begins economizing.

Buildings do not love their occupants. They maintain patterns from which the occupants benefit, and those patterns can become extremely particular.

A house may learn that someone rises at an unusual hour and open a path through its roots before the rest of the arcade is accessible. It may continue doing so for years after that person has moved away. Small nocturnal animals find these openings. Eventually a human habit becomes an animal route, and closing it requires a municipal consultation.

This is one reason the town is so spacious.

Many apparently empty places are still in use by something no longer visible at the hour you pass through.

***

The solitary collectors have inhabitants of their own.

Inside each joint lives a compact colony of pale animals with hooked feet and blunt heads. They rasp mineral deposits from the moving surfaces and pack the useful fragments into insulated nests. When a blade turns, they move together to the sheltered side. A collector without them stiffens gradually, then snaps in the first severe discharge.

The animals sometimes spill out onto a maintenance worker’s sleeves. They weigh almost nothing. Their claws are unpleasantly strong.

Under the old trunk rings live much larger creatures.

Few people have seen one whole. A survey opening may reveal a broad, segmented underside moving across the ceiling of a cavity. Elsewhere, a row of blunt limbs appears through a seam, braces, and withdraws. Their food is the dead supporting fiber buried deep within the plateau. They follow ancient growth layers, widening passages that freight tenders sometimes appropriate.

They are slow enough that a household can spend several years hearing one pass beneath it.

At certain seasons they call. The sound is mostly beyond hearing, but loose objects creep toward the edges of tables. Sleepers dream of climbing. Experienced residents wedge their crockery and wait.

There are maps of their movements, though the maps become unreliable where two old crowns overlap. The animals may be mating there. They may be turning over. No one has yet found a safe way to distinguish these possibilities.

The thin fliers shelter beneath the western rim, hanging by the folded tips of their vanes. Their young are not aerodynamic. They are squat, fierce little things that crawl over one another in warm crevices and bite at any passing charge.

People collect their shed vanes for instruments. A good vane can hold a note through several changes of weather. A bad one splits without warning and leaves a roomful of listeners tasting metal.

***

There are inhabitants who spend years tending a connection they will never use.

The paired doorframes are mostly employed for freight. Passage makes some people ill: their internal dust briefly loses its accustomed distribution, and afterward they cannot judge where their limbs end. Others feel nothing. A few find the experience pleasurable and become a nuisance to the station staff.

The tenders learn to identify an unwanted bud before its destination has become physically possible.

They work in pairs. One cuts; the other watches the space inside the frame. Both wear cords fastened to separate anchors. The cords are not intended to pull them back. They are a way of noticing, immediately, that back has changed.

Eshet’s sister works at the oldest station.

She has never heard anything through the sealed third doorway. She is quite firm on this point. People regularly ask, often lowering their voices as though that will make her answer more interesting.

There is only the wind and the unfamiliar pollen.

Once, however, a grazer brought in to clear the station yard approached the sealed frame and gave the quiet, uneven knocking used between an adult and its young.

Her sister reported it. For several weeks the tenders stopped removing one particular class of bud.

Then they began again.

***

What lives here longest is difficult to count.

The plateau has no central chamber that anyone has identified, no single rhythm running through all its tissues. An injury in one district may change the growth of another far away, but only after a delay that makes interpretation uncertain. Parts of it reject grafts from other parts. Some broad regions seem to sleep while neighboring regions continue growing.

The inhabitants have several words for it, used according to the task at hand. The word used when calculating freight loads is not the one used when teaching a child to step off a warming road. Neither is the word Eshet uses when a repair has resisted her for six hours and finally begins to take.

That word is brief and not especially respectful.

By evening she has fed the junction eleven beetles. Traffic has worn a temporary path around her stool. The illicit insect remains in its case.

She lays her bare palm across the dark stripe one last time.

The damaged tissue contracts. Waste begins moving through it again: husk fragments, a piece of packing membrane, the shed casing of something small. Beneath these comes a slow, unmistakable pressure. The road nudges her hand aside.

She puts on her glove and packs her tools.

Tomorrow she will return to discourage the temporary path from becoming permanent. For tonight she plants two red buds where the traffic has been turning, waits until they take, and walks home through the lowering town.

In her sitting room, the unwanted partition has risen by the width of a finger.

She steps over it, puts her food against the warming wall, and takes out the photograph. There is still time to mend the corner.
