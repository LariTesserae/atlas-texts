The woman working at the threshold has tucked her skirt between her knees. One bare heel rests against the door to keep it from swinging. She strikes the chisel twice, then runs the side of her thumb over the place she has cut.

Her name is Eda. She is not the oldest person in the town, although visitors sometimes take her for it. The reflected light has made her narrow her eyes for most of her life, and the skin beside them lies in permanent folds. Her hair is still dark underneath. When she lifts an arm, you can see the black roots beneath the pale, weathered surface.

The sill is giving her trouble because she postponed it. She knew about it yesterday. She knew about it several days before that, when the door began leaving a white scrape across the floor. Now her supper is ready behind her, and the bowl is taking every little tap of the chisel and delivering it to the other houses.

From somewhere opposite, a man calls, “Lift the bottom pin.”

She does not answer.

After a while she lifts the bottom pin.

There are twenty-eight people living here at present. Whether a young man who has been away for two wet seasons should be counted makes the difference between twenty-eight and twenty-nine. His mother sets out neither a plate nor a lamp for him. She does keep the plants out of his room. This requires work, which she does without mentioning it.

The inhabitants have broad-looking feet because they spread their toes on the wet stone. Their ankles are often nicked. Most wear sandals on the outer path and go barefoot inside the arcade, where they trust their skin more than any sole. In cold weather they wrap their insteps, leaving the toes and heel exposed. The children resent this arrangement until they try to run in fully wrapped feet.

They are not especially tall, or uniformly dark or fair. There is a family with reddish hair and a tendency to nosebleeds, a woman whose skin remains almost black even on the palms, two brothers who look so little alike that strangers hesitate to call them brothers. What they share is a way of moving through narrow spaces: a shoulder turns before the body reaches an obstruction; a hand goes briefly to a pillar; a basket passes behind the back. They can carry a hot dish past someone hauling wet leaves without either person stopping.

Put them in a wide room elsewhere and they tend to sit along the walls.

Their hands tell you more than their faces. The people who tend the channels have pale roughness along the sides of their fingers. Fiber workers have deep clean grooves at the first joints. Eda’s left thumbnail is short and uneven from testing the new stone. The man who told her to lift the pin has a beautifully healed scar through his upper lip and two nearly useless fingers. He gives advice freely because there are many jobs he can no longer do quickly himself.

His name is Senn. He was giving advice before he hurt his hand.

In the mornings he carries the basket of wedges around the arcade. Some wedges are no bigger than a tooth; others are long, polished from use, with a hole through the thick end for a cord. He feels the gaps around doorposts, checks the drain covers, presses his cheek against particular pillars to sight along them. He occasionally lies flat with one arm inside a channel while someone holds his ankles.

The younger residents consider his rounds excessive. Nevertheless, when he is ill, three different people make them, and afterward they compare what they found.

The scored lines on the pillar belong to this work. Beside each is a date and the name of the person who took the measure. Their writing is small because the older inscriptions must remain legible. A strip of waxed cloth hangs nearby to protect the newest notes from splashing water.

A child has added a line at the height of her own shoulder.

It has been crossed out, but not removed.

Her name is Tavi. She is seven and regards most of the town as badly arranged for someone of her size. The useful recesses are too high. The cupboards she can reach contain fiber, spare pegs, or bitter leaves. She has found a route through the backs of three storerooms that allows her to arrive behind adults who believe they have seen her leave in the other direction.

For several weeks she used this advantage to steal dried stalk pith. Then she began arriving simply to frighten people, which brought the arrangement to an end.

She is the loudest inhabitant. Her elders have taught her not to run along the outer walk, not to touch a new shelf, not to put garden earth into the clean channels. They have been less successful in persuading her that a shout should not be answered just because it comes back altered.

She knows which arch gives her voice a deep tail. She knows where clapping makes a sound like someone snapping a board. She has names for these places that nobody else uses.

There are three other children. Two follow her. The oldest pretends not to.

For the adults, hearing one another is mostly practical. A pause in the mallet strokes may mean the fiber needs turning, or that the worker has stopped to eat. A jar breaking brings people to the arcade; a wooden bowl falling usually does not. They cannot identify every sound. The hollow shifts distances, hides beginnings, supplies a low continuation to noises already over. People sometimes knock at the wrong room to ask for quiet.

Private conversations take place behind two mats, with the speakers close enough to smell one another’s breath. In windy weather, couples linger over tasks beside the drains.

There is no convention that everything overheard must be acknowledged. A woman can weep in her room in the afternoon and be asked quite ordinarily whether she wants more greens at supper. She may be grateful for this or furious. The others have to judge.

They quarrel about garden water, labor, children, and the handling of knives. They also quarrel about rooms.

One roofless chamber belongs, by an agreement nobody disputes, to a man called Orrel. He has never slept in it. He sleeps beside the kitchen where he was born and stores baskets under the fitted window of his other room. Each year someone suggests that its sheltered floor would make a useful bed for the pepper-smelling plants. Each year he says he means to roof it.

He has bought two poles for this purpose. They are excellent poles. He turns them occasionally in their dry recess.

Orrel has a slow, pleasing singing voice and is good with very small children. He can get a child to swallow medicine when its own parents have given up. The household that most wants his room planted is his sister’s. Their disagreement is real, but she still sends for him when her youngest has a fever.

Nobody here is only the keeper of a door or the tender of a bed. The woman who manages the sluices makes terrible handles, though she believes hers are the best. Senn enjoys delicate food and complains about its preparation with such precision that people make him cook it himself. Eda keeps a collection of smooth wooden beads brought up from lower settlements. She wears only three. The others she takes out in good light and arranges by color.

Things arrive by the cleft: metal blades wrapped against damp, grain, salt, cloth, a kind of small sour fruit that puckers the mouths of people who have been talking about it for weeks. Loads are carried on backs. The town sends down strong fiber, medicinal leaves, and pale finished vessels made from stone harvested before it hardens fully. A vessel begun at the wrong time will craze during drying. One begun well feels almost greasy beneath the fingers.

Visitors are given a room behind the western arcade. It has the deepest dry floor and the least troublesome door. They are also given instructions about where to empty washing water. This is repeated if necessary. Hospitality does not include allowing soap and food scraps to spread across the bowl.

The sweeping is shared work, but not equally enjoyed.

Eda likes the first hour, when the floor is cool and every bit of fallen matter stands out distinctly. She dislikes the long climb back with a full basket. Orrel prefers to work beside someone. Senn can no longer manage the slope for long, and spends his turn along the shallow upper curve, reaching beneath the spouts.

They remove seeds while the seeds are still free. They lift moth wings, torn leaves, dropped fibers, dead snails. Something already enclosed may have to stay. There are old frond patterns under the surface, the faint oval of a leaf from a plant no longer grown here, a length of thread visible only when the floor is wet.

Tavi once tried to preserve a yellow flower by laying it where the water spread thinly.

Her grandmother found it during sweeping and put it in the basket.

Neither has entirely forgiven the other.

The floor is not forbidden. Children cross it. A dropped tool is retrieved. Someone feeling unwell may sit near the little pool in the warmth of reflected afternoon light. But it is never used for storage, and no household can claim a portion of it. When asked why there are no buildings below, the inhabitants offer answers of varying patience.

Water must be able to spread.

The curve is difficult to build on.

There is room above.

One old woman says her grandfather was told never to begin a house where a person standing at its doorway could not see out over the rim. She does not know whether this was a rule about the stone, the air, or unhappiness.

It is possible to live here unhappily. They know that. The departed young man disliked having every broken dish become public knowledge. Another resident left because there was no one she wished to marry. Someone else went down for a blade and stayed away for eleven years, returning with a husband who spent his first month frightened of the outer walk.

He now carries the largest loads. He still avoids looking down.

The town has places where people used to sit. A recess near the bath fits a missing woman’s basket so exactly that other baskets look misplaced there. A polished patch on a low wall was made by a man who liked to warm his back after rain. Those who remember them can point these things out. Usually they are busy, and walk past.

The oldest resident has begun forgetting names. She remembers the previous shape of the stairs.

“That one was higher,” she says as they help her down.

It was. The tread below has risen around it.

Her room needs less work than most because she likes the light partly shut out, and has allowed the lower half of her window to thicken. Moss occupies the outside edge. A small snail sometimes comes through and travels the damp wall beside her bed. She puts it back among the leaves when she notices it.

In the evening, after the sluices are closed, people can hear her wooden spoon strike the rim of her cup. She stirs long after the drink has mixed.

Eda finishes with the sill before the last brightness leaves the gardens. She gathers the soft shavings carefully; dry ones can be ground for scouring. Then she pushes the door shut and opens it again.

The movement is smooth.

She does it once more, for the pleasure of having corrected something, and goes inside.

Across the bowl, Senn waits to hear whether she will thank him. A spoon scrapes a pot. Someone laughs behind a mat. From the upper walk comes the dragging sound of Orrel turning one of his roof poles, keeping it straight.
