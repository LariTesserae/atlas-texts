The inhabitants are easiest to see just after the rain has withdrawn from a doorway.

The opening clears, but a little water remains suspended across its lower half. It has an uneven upper edge and two or three thicker places where it meets the stone. Behind it, the room appears empty. Then one of the thicker places releases its hold on the jamb, and the water turns sideways.

What crosses the room is more substantial than it first appeared. The transparent part was only its outer surface. Within are close-set folds of a dark, pliant tissue, each edged with a pale seam. They open slightly as the creature moves. Warm water passes between them, emerges through small openings near the floor, and remains behind in separate, trembling heaps.

It takes several movements to get clear of the threshold. There is a pause between each, long enough to suggest that the next will not occur.

Most of the inhabitants are about the height of a low window. At rest they are broad below and drawn together above, with an irregular crest that leans toward the nearest wall. Their bodies have no permanent front. The outer folds can close on one side and loosen on another, bringing a different arrangement of surfaces into use. A resident that appears to turn toward a sound may instead keep its whole body still and slowly develop its attentive side in the appropriate direction.

The pale seams are sensitive. They swell when exposed to air, becoming soft rims which admit water, warmth, and something of the pressure of the surrounding space. In a room with two doors, the seams nearest each opening behave differently.

They have no eyes that can be found by looking for them.

Nevertheless, they notice a visitor standing motionless in the dark. They also notice an empty room becoming smaller on the far side of a wall. A resident may wait beside a closed passage for several hours, then move away just before the passage opens.

Their reflections are much easier to recognize than their bodies.

Below the water, each has a compact central mass and a number of long, diverging extensions. Some reach beneath neighboring buildings. Others disappear into the reflected depth of an avenue. These extensions do not resemble limbs so much as routes repeatedly taken until they have acquired substance.

A creature crossing a square may therefore appear above the water as a small, folded body and below it as something spread through half the district.

The reflection does not always follow at once. Occasionally the body waits while a distant extension draws itself free of a foundation. Such delays are ordinary. Another resident approaching from behind will stop at a considerable distance, sometimes in the middle of an otherwise empty street.

There is no visible obstruction between them.

They move by establishing a place ahead of themselves into which their weight can pass. A fold reaches outward, thickens where it touches stone or water, and bears the body while the former supports soften. On a flooded stair, they may use the risers rather than the treads. In rooms, they often proceed along the lower part of a wall, leaving the floor untouched.

They are slow, but their slowness is not uniform. An inhabitant can remain halfway through an opening for most of a rain, then complete its passage during a moment in which the observer looks elsewhere. It has not hurried. The second part of the journey was shorter for it.

They have difficulties with unfamiliar thresholds. At these they extend a narrow fold, withdraw it, and try again at another height. Sometimes they abandon the attempt and take a much longer route.

The city is no less inconvenient to its residents for having grown around their habits.

---

They spend much of their time in the low rooms behind the residential lanes.

An occupied room can usually be distinguished by the moisture on its walls. In empty rooms, droplets form and gather without much pattern. Around a resident, they arrange themselves in loose bands, leaving intervals of nearly bare stone. These bands move very slowly. Their course can be followed through an adjoining room even when the connecting doorway has been enclosed for generations.

The inhabitants rest with several folds open against these damp surfaces. Their tissue is thinnest there. A dull internal branching becomes visible, finer and more crowded toward the edges.

Heat passes through them.

In the hotter districts, they spread widely and remain close to the floor. Near the central enclosures, they draw their upper folds into the cool skin of the water while the rest of the body hangs in the heat below. They may hold this position for days. The water immediately around them becomes extraordinarily still.

They also tend the colorless plants.

A resident approaches a ledge by raising a small portion of its body along the wall. The pale rim divides into several blunt tips, each of which settles between exposed roots. It remains there through the upward rain. When it withdraws, some of the rust-colored deposit is gone.

They take very little. A ledge may support the visits of a household for many seasons without visibly diminishing.

Young growth is treated differently. Around a newly rooted plant, the creature leaves a bead of cloudy fluid. The bead clings to the wall through rain that strips neighboring surfaces clean. Eventually the roots enter it.

There are disagreements over these gardens. Two residents may occupy opposite ends of a ledge, each with a narrow fold extended toward the same bare patch. Neither advances. The plants between them stay closed.

Such disputes can last long enough for a new mineral shelf to form.

---

A household is rarely gathered in one room.

One member may rest behind a sealed doorway, another beneath the eaves of a building across the lane, a third in the flooded court between them. Their reflections meet below the intervening walls. At intervals, all three bodies tighten along one side.

This is among their most familiar forms of conversation.

At closer distances they use the water held in their outer folds. A small surface is made taut and released. The disturbance travels through the dampness of the room, too slight to make a sound, but sufficient to alter the rims of another body.

Their exchanges have pauses, interruptions, and persistent misunderstandings. An inhabitant may repeat the same contraction several times with increasing force. The listener closes a fold, opens it again, and gives a response that causes the first to withdraw entirely against the wall.

Sometimes they touch.

For this, two residents bring particular inner surfaces into the air. These are pale, densely creased, and ordinarily protected by several layers of tissue. Bringing them out takes time. The surrounding folds must brace against the floor, and both bodies become less able to move.

When the surfaces meet, the creases slowly cease to correspond with the bodies that carried them. A line from one continues into the other. Small swellings cross the join.

They can remain together long after the exchange appears complete.

A third inhabitant entering the room will usually retreat. If there is no other way through, it waits with its sensitive seams turned toward the wall.

The city’s long public halls are used more intermittently. Residents arrive along widely separated routes and settle near the bases of the pillars. A hall that looked deserted may contain twenty or thirty of them, each folded into the dark thickening where stone meets water.

For a long time, nothing happens.

Then the water between two pillars changes level. A few bodies open. Elsewhere, a resident gathers itself and leaves. By degrees, small differences pass through the hall: pressure, warmth, a redistribution of the room’s available distance.

Some gatherings end with no visible result. After others, a lane becomes accessible to bodies that could not previously enter it. A doorway acquires a shallow recess. The inhabitants return home, and the alterations continue in their absence.

They do not seem to command these changes precisely. A new passage may emerge too narrow, or on the wrong side of a court. Residents come to inspect it, testing its edges with the blunt ends of their folds. Sometimes they continue using the old route.

---

Their young are first visible as places where a parent fails to close completely.

A narrow gap persists between two folds. Within it is a surface finer and paler than the tissue around it, gathered into a few deep wrinkles. Water entering the parent passes over this surface before leaving.

For a long time the young one shares the parent’s movements. Then it begins to hold against a wall while the larger body draws away.

The parent stops.

These first separations occur in shallow rooms with several closely spaced openings. The little creature may remain stretched between the parent and the stone, unable to release either. An inner fold of the adult opens beneath it. Eventually it lets go and settles there.

As the young become independent, they are left for longer intervals beside familiar thresholds. Other members of the household pass near them, allowing brief contact. Their reflections at this age are small and bewilderingly mobile. An extension may slip beneath the wrong house and have to be retrieved by a resident whose body is nowhere nearby.

There are rooms in which several young are kept together. The walls are unusually smooth at their height. Little ridges continually form and subside along the floor as the occupants attempt to establish footholds.

They repeat movements that serve no evident purpose: entering the same narrow recess, opening all their folds at once, making a bead of water stand higher than a neighbor’s. Sometimes a whole room of them suddenly closes.

A moment later, one opens, and the others follow.

The adult resting across the doorway does not always respond.

---

Age makes the inhabitants more particular.

An old resident may be able to cross a great accumulating court with ease, yet fail at a recently altered doorstep. Its reflected extensions have become broad and deeply seated. Some lead to buildings no longer present above the water.

Such creatures are often accompanied by a smaller member of the household. The companion goes ahead, rests briefly at each junction, and waits for the older body to find the place it has prepared.

They can lose one another during the exchanges in the central enclosures.

When the reflected city begins to move, residents gather at the wide openings. Many simply hold against the embankments until the current has passed. Others travel alongside a particular arcade or line of windows as it slides beneath the water.

Occasionally a body stays behind while its reflection continues.

It becomes difficult to see. The folds remain, but they no longer quite distinguish themselves from the rain. Nearby inhabitants press against it, opening their protected surfaces. Sometimes this is enough. A dark extension returns beneath the square, and the body grows definite again.

Sometimes the others remain until the place they are touching is only warm water.

There is no object to carry away.

For a while, members of the household continue to pause along the routes the missing resident used. Rooms elsewhere may hold their distance longer than they should. A familiar doorway may admit no one.

These conditions pass unevenly. One lane becomes ordinary again within a few rains. Another is approached with care long after its buildings have moved.

---

A visitor is generally allowed to continue.

The inhabitants withdraw from direct contact with unfamiliar skin. They are especially wary of feet, which repeatedly strike the ground without first finding purchase in it. The disturbance causes their outer folds to tighten.

If the visitor stays, a resident may begin appearing at successive doorways along the route. It remains well ahead, touching a jamb or settling briefly across a submerged step.

There is no assurance that it is offering guidance. It may be attending to damage, or protecting a young one whose reflection lies beneath the street.

But sometimes, after a long passage through the heat, there is a room with water only at its edges. The threshold admits the visitor without resistance. A low thickening of the floor is almost dry.

The inhabitant waits outside.

If the visitor sits, it loosens against the opposite wall. Its pale seams gradually subside. For the first time, its body seems neither poised to cross the opening nor engaged in holding something apart.

Water gathers beneath the lintel.

Before the drop can fall, a narrow fold rises to receive it.
