The woman who repairs the quiet in other people’s houses has a loud laugh.

Her name is Sera. She carries her tools in a canvas roll, with a separate pocket for each one so that they cannot touch. At a customer’s door she stamps twice, waits, then stamps once. The little arrival lamps are unreliable in older buildings, and she knows where to put her foot.

Most complaints begin with a cup moving on a table, or a child who has started sleeping badly. Sometimes a tenant says that someone is walking in the passage around the rooms. Sera does not argue. She takes off her shoes, sits down, and lays a small brass weight on the floor. From a padded case she removes a set of thin spring reeds. She fastens them, one at a time, to the weight.

Usually she finds that a supporting block has hardened, or a mineral crust has bridged the gap between the house and the rooms inside it. Sometimes the tenant has driven a long screw through both walls to hang a cupboard.

She dislikes those visits. People are ashamed of how much they have disturbed themselves.

While her apprentice removes the screw, Sera opens the cupboard and admires whatever is in it.

She is broad, impatient, good at the work. Her left knee troubles her on descending stairs. She has lived on the mountain all her life and would gladly leave it for a month if she could find someone trustworthy to feed her sister’s snails.

***

The residents are not a slow people, although visitors often remember them that way.

Their machinery is slow. Their permissions are slow. Water already admitted to a chamber cannot be recalled because someone has changed their mind. But an afternoon is still an afternoon. People hurry to appointments, grow bored, make purchases they regret. There are furious little quarrels in the insulated rooms of the pressure offices, where clerks have to explain that an urgent application will be reviewed before the next wet season.

Children run almost everywhere.

They learn early not to run with an arm above the shoulder. Games that involve throwing have designated courts, enclosed by screens, so that someone reaching for a ball will not stop work across a district. On the open paths they race with their elbows tucked in. They slide objects along the shallow roadside channels and pursue them from junction to junction, sometimes arriving home with a chipped soap dish that has traveled farther than they have.

Parents attach small mirrors to prams. A person pushing one cannot hear a baby cry.

Older children resent being made to look back at regular intervals. They turn their heads with extravagant misery: yes, still here; yes, I see you; yes, the way is clear.

In public, a child can pretend not to have seen an instruction. This produces a familiar kind of parental anger, expressed very close to the chest.

***

Their hands are conspicuous.

Not unusually shaped, not endowed with any special faculty, but watched. A newcomer looks at faces and misses half a conversation. A resident notices that the porter has changed the position of his thumb and knows the loading platform is closed.

The smallest gestures are household gestures. Couples acquire signs for things that would take too long to write: your brother has come; the room is doing it again; I have saved you some. These do not always survive separation. A woman remarrying in another district may spend years making a motion that nobody there understands.

Formal street-sign differs from these private habits. It has names, numbers, tenses, arguments. In crowded places people lower their slates and sign over them. A good storyteller can gather a circle beside a spillway, where spoken words would be useless, and make the people at the back lean sideways to keep the hands in view.

Insults are often smaller than greetings.

Visitors sometimes mistake restraint for courtesy.

There are people who prefer the public language and become awkward when a café door closes behind them. There are others who wait all day to hear their own voices. Once indoors they talk over one another, sing while washing dishes, call from room to room despite being only a few steps apart.

Sera laughs with her whole mouth open. Her apprentice, who finds this embarrassing, has a delicate speaking voice and a viciously expressive pair of hands.

***

His name is Iven. He is seventeen and wants to build rooms that do not need repairing.

Sera considers this a reasonable ambition, provided he learns to repair the existing ones first.

He draws designs on the backs of discarded inspection sheets. Some hang from cables. Some stand on layered springs. One is shaped like an egg and would require its occupants to keep most of their possessions in curved cupboards.

He has never slept away from the plateau.

His cousin has. She went down to the lowland for training and reported that rain could be heard approaching through trees. Iven suspects she says things like this to make the journey seem worth its expense.

She also told him that during her first week she kept waking because someone in the next lodging was breathing. He believes that part.

***

Hearing varies greatly among them.

Some lose the higher sounds while still young. Some can follow speech indoors into old age. Many have a whistle, a pulse, or a rough flutter in one ear that accompanies them even into the best quiet rooms. The clinics keep separate records for hearing and for the ability to distinguish changes in the works. They are related, but not interchangeable.

An old gatekeeper who cannot understand his granddaughter at breakfast may stop halfway over a square and lay his palm on a rail.

He may also be wrong.

Experience is respected, checked, and sometimes resented. The pressure offices employ people whose work is to compare such reports with instruments and with one another. An elderly resident saying that the western line sounds hungry does not cause a gate to be closed. Six residents using six different descriptions may cause an inspector to miss dinner.

There are families famous for sensitivity to particular streets. There are also families who exaggerate this.

Sera’s father claimed he could tell when freight had been added to the north lift from three districts away. After his death they found that he had maintained a private correspondence with the lift attendant, mostly about football.

***

Much of their work is ordinary city work performed with a few additional precautions.

They teach, keep accounts, cut hair, sell onions, argue about rent. Barbers secure their chairs to the inner floors. Bakers buy water from designated freshwater lines and test it each morning. Tailors reinforce hems that brush wet steps. Fruit sellers charge more for dry storage than for the fruit itself during some months.

People with money buy separation: a larger gap around their rooms, better supporting blocks, a second vestibule, a bed mounted independently of the floor. People without it stuff folded cloth under rattling crockery and learn which side of the mattress is easier to sleep on.

A landlord may advertise a room as quiet because no current tenant has filed a complaint.

This is why Sera is sometimes hired before a lease is signed. She charges less for those visits. Her sister says this is bad business.

Her sister raises snails for the kitchens near the freight exchange. They are not the flat wild snails from under the bridges but a larger, brown variety brought up from below. Their trays contain imported leaf mold, washed gravel, and cabbage ends. Twice a year an infestation of the little local snails appears around the drain, and she spends days picking them out with tweezers.

She has names for the breeding stock. She eats the others without difficulty.

***

The courtyard trees require more labor than a visitor would imagine.

There are drainage committees, pruning accounts, disputes over shade. Leaves are collected before they can block an outlet. Children are permitted to climb only particular branches, painted discreetly on their undersides.

In the district of three trees, the dead one remains in its tank.

It is neither sacred nor protected by a pressure requirement. The roots are entangled with the tank’s old reinforcement, and removal is expensive. Several residents want it gone. Several others like to see the shape of it in winter, although the living trees keep most of their leaves.

A man on the fourth floor hangs strips of red cloth from its branches for his wife’s birthday. He does this with a freight hook on a long pole. The committee sends him the same letter each year.

***

Down at the lower works, another population keeps to the cavities behind the falls.

The keepers call them ledge-runners, though few have seen more than a narrow hind foot or a tail withdrawing into a joint in the stone. Their tracks have four forward toes and one set sideways. Fresh tracks sometimes end at a smooth wall and begin again above it.

They take the translucent insects that gather at freshwater outlets. They also steal grease from uncovered tins, which has settled one argument about their diet and begun several about their intelligence.

The pale shell fragments may belong to them. Nothing resembling a parent has been found beside an intact egg.

One keeper has watched a runner feeding. It was the length of her hand, low to the stone, with a blunt head and skin the color of a soaked paper sack. Along each side lay a fringe of fine stiff hairs. When a maintenance valve opened, it flattened itself before the new flow arrived.

She thinks it felt the change through its belly.

Her colleague thinks she saw two animals overlapping.

Neither account explains why the eggshells are sometimes found on ledges that remain dry behind an unbroken sheet of hot water.

The keepers leave these ledges alone when they can. They mark them on their private copies of the inspection plan, along with loose steps, good places to eat lunch, and the freshwater tap that tastes faintly of metal.

***

Sera has a daughter who is learning the flute.

It was not an obvious choice. The instrument requires quiet, and quiet is already needed for homework, conversation, sleep, and the radio broadcasts her grandfather enjoyed before he died. Practice has to be negotiated.

The girl is not especially gifted. She is diligent for three days, careless for two, then suddenly able to play a passage that seemed beyond her. Iven repaired the catch on her case. She thanks him so formally whenever he visits that he has begun leaving his tools in the outer passage.

One evening Sera comes home to find her daughter seated at the kitchen table, flute across her knees, waiting.

There is a vibration in the room.

It has been there for several days, but only on certain notes. The girl demonstrates. A glass in the cupboard gives a thin answering buzz.

Sera checks the cupboard screws first. Then she takes off her shoes.

Her daughter plays the note again, holds it, runs out of breath. Sera shifts her brass weight across the floor. They repeat this, growing irritated with each other, until Sera finds the place beneath the window where the outer wall has begun to touch the sill.

It is a small repair. She will need a chisel from the shop.

For tonight she folds a cloth beneath the glass. Her daughter raises the flute, tries the note once more, and continues into the next bar.

Sera stays on the floor to listen.
