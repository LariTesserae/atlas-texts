Beneath the broad stone in the middle of the hollow lies an animal whose back might be mistaken for the stone’s underside.

It is not large. A person crouching there could have held it against their chest, though there is no way to lift it without disturbing everything around it. Its body extends into three shallow recesses. Roots pass over its shoulders. The moss along one flank has grown through the longer hairs and made a continuous nap, green at the tips, gray beneath. A small amphibian rests in the crease behind its foreleg, with one black hand spread against the skin.

The animal has a head, but the head is tucked so closely into the body that its position must be learned from the ears.

These are small and rounded, with thick rims. One lies folded beneath a root. The other holds a little water. At the bottom of that water, the delicate inner skin remains faintly pink.

Its fur is unlike the fur of the mammals packed beneath the ledge. Along the back it is coarse, flattened into narrow locks, each following a groove in the stone above. At the throat it becomes so fine that water lies on it as a smooth film. Farther down, where the belly meets the basin, there seems to be no fur at all. There is only pale skin with a few dark flecks embedded in it, like the first grains visible beneath clear ice.

It does not look well protected. There are no plates, spines, or powerful limbs. The little of its abdomen that can be seen is soft. A beetle rests against it, its closed wing cases touching the place where a heartbeat ought to be easiest to feel.

The feet are folded inward.

Each has several blunt digits, not quite alike in length or thickness. Their undersides bear the close, fine ridges of a gripping animal. Yet nothing is gripped. One foot rests against a pebble. Another lies open beneath the body, palm upward, with a snail settled in its hollow. The claws are translucent and worn short. A dark line follows the curve of each, stopping before the tip.

There is no tail visible. Something that could be a tail passes beneath the lowest root, but it could also be the root’s wet, unbarked end. Nothing about the animal requires the question to be settled.

Its face is turned toward the water.

The muzzle is broad and short, with two nostrils almost hidden in the fur. A few whiskers extend across the surface of the nearest pool. Where they touch, the water rises along them in tiny clear sleeves.

Its mouth is closed, though not firmly. Between the lips there is a narrow darkness. No teeth show. At one corner, an insect has placed its front legs upon the lower lip and remains there, neither entering nor withdrawing.

The visible eye is open.

It is brown, an ordinary animal brown, with a round pupil and a moist lower lid. This is perhaps the least expected thing about it. The eye does not resemble a jewel or a cavity in the rock. It has the vulnerable, slightly clouded surface of an eye that could be injured by a twig.

It looks across the basin toward the bases of the rushes.

There is no reason to suppose it is watching them.

Close to its belly are several smaller bodies. At first they appear to be young. They have the same low heads, the same folded limbs, the same fur darkening toward the back. But one is no larger than a moth, and another has the long fingers of the mammals under the ledge. The smallest has no visible hair. Its skin is black and supple, and its throat rests against the larger animal’s wrist.

Perhaps they are unrelated creatures occupying the shelter of its body. Perhaps resemblance here is simply what happens to things that have fitted themselves closely enough to the same place.

There is no nest beneath them. No lining has been gathered, no hollow scraped out. The animal lies on old leaves that retain their veins, beside wood that has softened without collapsing. Under its chin is a seed with a split coat. The pale beginning of a root fills the split exactly.

Nothing suggests that it arrived.

There are no worn approaches to its recess, no snagged hairs farther up the slope. Its body could not pass between the roots as they now grow. Even the stone above would need to be lifted for it to turn over. Yet it has none of the look of a trapped creature. Its joints are easy. Its weight is distributed comfortably. The ear beneath the root is folded along a natural crease.

It may have grown there while the roots grew around it. But the fit is more particular than that. There is room beside its ribs for the amphibian. There is room under the amphibian’s lifted elbow for three pale mites. A fern stem descends between the animal’s cheek and the stone without being bent.

Its presence has left all these small allowances.

The low sound in the hollow is clearest near its body, although that may be because the stone gathers sound beneath it. The fur does not tremble. The throat makes no movement. Listening at its side would not reveal whether the sound came from within it or continued through it from deeper in the mountain.

At the end of the muzzle, one whisker curves downward.

A drop rests on the curve. Within that drop are the rushes, the dark pool, a portion of the animal’s own face. The eye is too small to distinguish there.

The whisker has bent under the weight, just far enough to hold it.
