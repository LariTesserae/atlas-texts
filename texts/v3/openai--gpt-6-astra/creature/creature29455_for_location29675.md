The chief inhabitants spend the day holding the country above them with their feet.

At a distance the bats seem almost without separate bodies. They make a second surface on the limestone, one that tightens and loosens locally, develops a small bare place, closes it again. Nearer, each animal has its own occupation. One works a claw through the fur behind its ear. One sleeps with its muzzle tucked away. Another hangs awake between sleeping neighbors, ears turning independently toward disturbances too slight to reach the floor.

Their fur is softer than the colony looks from below. Across a shoulder it lies in short, dense nap; around the face it thins into whiskers and little creases of skin. The wings, gathered against the flanks, are not simply coverings. A bent wrist protrudes. A thumb hooks the rock while an animal changes position. For a moment, an opened membrane reveals the long fingers inside it, then folds back into an object small enough to fit between two others.

There are disagreements over inches. A bat trying to reach a better purchase puts its weight on a neighbor, receives an open mouth and a sharp cry, and continues sideways. Several bodies stir. The disturbance travels a short way through the roost and expires. Elsewhere, another begins.

In the nursery patches, when there are young, the crowd has a different texture. Small bodies press close to the adults, less securely folded, more intent on contact. A mother returning to such a place must find one animal among an abundance of animals that look, from the floor, interchangeable. Up close they are voices, odors, familiar mouths.

The bats do not know the basin chiefly as the view from a ridge. Their routes pass through its empty middle. A low arch that looks like a distant border is a clearance to negotiate; the edge of a pendent slab is something to turn beneath. Animals leaving the roost release their hold and fall briefly into flight. Those arriving rise toward stone, check themselves, catch, and become part of the ceiling again.

At dusk, the openings connect this suspended population to a much larger feeding country. What returns from outside eventually reaches the floor.

Below the roosts, the inhabitants live inside that consequence.

A beetle works along the edge of a deposit with its head lowered, testing the material immediately in front of it. Its world has almost no distant objects. A crust is an obstacle or a roof. A damp crumb is worth stopping at. Beneath the dry surface, where moisture lasts longer, other small bodies move through spaces that would vanish under the pressure of a fingertip.

The rust-colored mites are not a uniform dust when watched long enough. Some remain crowded around one particle. Others cross the intervening grains alone. Their legs carry them over a relief of pits, flakes, and fibers, each journey disappearing into the scale at which a human observer begins to call the ground smooth.

Millipedes keep more of themselves against shelter. Their flattened backs slip beneath the margins of slabs, their many legs advancing in small coordinated waves. They feed where organic matter has collected and softened. A few inches of exposed stone may separate suitable refuges; an open shelf offers them little reason to continue.

The crickets appear more precarious. Their legs reach far beyond their bodies, and their antennae extend farther still, touching stone before the animal commits itself. A resting cricket can seem caught in a web of its own limbs. Disturb it and the arrangement abruptly becomes force: the body leaves the ledge, strikes another surface, and is still again in a different patch of darkness.

Spiders occupy the little routes between these shelters. Their retreats are easily overlooked beside the size of the arches. A narrow recess and a few threads may be enough. They wait where another animal’s search for food or moisture is likely to bring it within reach.

The snakes are the largest solitary residents one is likely to notice. Even then, a snake may first register as an oddly continuous piece of rubble. Its body follows the sheltered depth of a crack, leaving only a loop or the front of the head exposed. The eyes remain open. Occasionally the tongue appears and is withdrawn.

Near a roost, waiting has value. A fallen bat, a vulnerable young animal, or an adult passing close enough may provide a meal substantial enough to support a long interval of stillness. The snake need not patrol the empty acres.

Most inhabitants do not.

They gather in particular places and stay within reach of what sustains them. Some cross the basin through the air every night; others may spend their lives beneath a single fallen plate. Their activities overlap densely under the roosts, thin out along the damp seams, and stop almost entirely across the broad dry shelves.

A bat hanging above such a shelf shifts its grip. Far below, nothing responds. Under the next overhang, a falling pellet strikes the ground, and eventually something small finds it.
