The woman at the mixing basin keeps a dry pair of shoes in a tin cupboard above the flood mark.

Her name is Sera. She is fifty-three, broad through the shoulders, with a pale band around each wrist where the cuffs of her work gloves end. On working days she wears her hair pinned flat, because the ventilation duct above her station draws loose hair steadily toward the left. Off duty, she parts it on the right. Her sister says this has made her face uneven.

Sera has charge of six valves, two temperature gauges, and a basin whose far end she cannot see when the air is cool. The gauges are reliable. She checks them against a thermometer lowered on a cord, and sometimes against the water on the inside of her wrist. She is not permitted to substitute her wrist for the thermometer. She has trained four assistants and caught every one of them doing it.

Much of her shift consists of waiting after a small adjustment.

She eats, reads, cleans the scale from a fitting, or talks to whoever is working the next basin. When the telephone rings, she wipes her hand before answering. People farther down call to say that a feeding channel has cooled, that a bathhouse has received water too hot to use, that a cultivation rack has begun producing the wrong sort of film. Sometimes they are mistaken about the cause. Sometimes she is.

She lives two terraces below her workplace and one long street east. Going home takes twenty minutes. Coming back takes forty.

***

There are people in the city who seldom see the upper road.

This is partly a matter of distance. It is also because there is little up there that they need. Most households conduct their lives within a few terraces: work, markets, schools, bathing halls, relatives, the room where someone repairs a troublesome appliance. A journey across the city requires decisions about lifts, freight schedules, and which passages will be closed when a reservoir is flushed.

Children learn their addresses as routes before they learn them as words.

Up beside the loud water. Through the tunnel with two lamps. Not the staircase with the birds.

Later they learn the district and terrace numbers, although these do not always help. An old building can have entrances on three officially different levels. A woman on the sixth floor may share a landing with the basement of the house behind her. Deliveries are addressed to particular doors.

People carry less than a newcomer expects. Water makes every bundle heavier; stairs make every unnecessary possession briefly hateful. Shopping is frequent and local. Heavy things are lent, shared, or left where they are used. A family may own a fine large cooking vessel kept permanently at the neighborhood kitchen, where its name is scratched into the handle.

There are also collectors, hoarders, and people who insist on carrying ridiculous purchases halfway across the city. Porters know which households are likely to ask them to move a stone bathing basin to an upper room. They charge accordingly.

***

The inhabitants are not uniformly patient.

They are patient with some things: a valve that must be opened slowly, a lift with a load limit, a sleeping animal too heavy to shift without equipment. They are much less patient with someone stopping to talk on a narrow stair.

Arguments at water crossings are conducted almost forehead to forehead. From a distance they can look intimate.

People learn to turn toward a speaker and watch the whole face. Those who lose their hearing often conceal it for years, helped by the fact that everyone repeats themselves in the streets. In quiet rooms they can be suddenly lost.

The city has quiet rooms. They are expensive to build. A properly isolated floor, a double door, and walls that do not share a pipe can cost more than a view. In the wealthiest houses, a visitor hears the small click of a cup being set down. Some find this luxurious. Others sleep badly there.

Sera’s nephew, who repairs fan bearings, has a room so close to a pump chamber that a glass trembles on his bedside shelf. He says he no longer notices. When the pump is stopped for inspection, he wakes immediately.

***

The large spillway animals have several names, depending on whether a person is discussing them with a child, an inspector, or another person trying to get a cart through a gate.

At close range they are less seal-like than they seem from above. Beneath the heavy neck is a pair of short forelimbs with blunt, divided pads. These can grip the edge of a sill. The hindquarters taper, but not neatly; old animals acquire folds and bulges that make their resting posture look uncomfortable even when it is not.

Their upper coats are dark and sparse. Their undersides are almost naked, crossed by shallow grooves through which the warm water runs. They turn over occasionally, with considerable effort, and expose a pallor like the inside of a shell.

They feed mostly at night. In the settling ponds they push their muzzles through soft deposits, taking larvae, small shellfish, eggs, and whatever edible matter has arrived from the channels above. Around the processing halls, some have learned the timing of particular waste outlets. These animals tend to be enormous.

They can move fast enough to injure someone.

Every few years a visitor attempts to step over one instead of walking around it. The resulting accounts vary in sympathy according to how plainly the animal had been awake.

Females give birth on dry shelves just above the spillways. Their young are narrow, wrinkled creatures with disproportionately strong forelimbs. For their first weeks they hook themselves into the mother’s coat or into the rough mortar beside her. A pup that slips into the water may travel a surprising distance before finding purchase.

Maintenance crews carry a short pole with a padded crook for retrieving them. The same tool is useful for pulling sacks out of drains.

Individual adults are recognized along whole stretches of canal. Some have missing teeth, damaged ears, or pale scars from old encounters with machinery. Workers give them names, though these are rarely agreed upon. An animal called Grandmother at one gate may be known as the Magistrate fifty yards downstream.

Recognition does not amount to ownership. When one dies, the removal crew still has to come.

***

The birds make finer divisions of the city.

The small white lintel birds nest in the drier pockets above warm walls. Their feet leave gray marks around favored perches. They eat insects, scraps, and the thin membranes skimmed from certain fermentation vessels. They steal lining from gloves. They can undo a poorly tied food parcel, but are easily defeated by a lid.

The broad pond birds are kept as well as wild. Domestic strains have heavier breasts, reduced wings, and bright patches around the bill that make them easy to count in mist. They recognize the sound of the feed trolley. So do the wild birds, which arrive ahead of it and must be separated out with swinging hurdles.

Children are employed to help with this in some districts. In others it is forbidden, though children do it anyway for eggs.

The long-legged waders have little interest in people. They stand at the spill lips and watch for animals dislodged upstream. A clear channel can support fewer of them than a neglected one. Residents sometimes complain that a newly repaired watercourse has become ugly because the birds have left.

Under the bridges, the leathery-winged scavengers spend daylight wrapped around themselves. They smell strongly when disturbed. Their young hang in the protected centers of the colonies, pink-faced and nearly bald, passing from one adult’s grip to another’s with an assurance that makes human onlookers nervous.

At dusk they descend to the refuse yards. Their wings are broad enough that the first few strokes seem impractical in a narrow street. Then they are above the lamps, banking around the lift towers.

People who work the evening markets wear washable shoulder capes.

***

Much of the city’s animal life is small enough to be noticed only when something changes.

A bowl left beneath a leak fills with translucent swimmers. A little white shell appears at a tap, then six, then a rough ring that prevents the handle from turning. Dark, jointed creatures live behind warm tiles and emerge when the grout is opened. They flatten themselves so thoroughly that it is difficult to tell whether one has escaped or been crushed.

The flat fish of the channels have mottled backs matching the sediment. Children catch them by placing an overturned basin on the bed and sliding a plate underneath. Adults usually tell them to stop, less from concern for the fish than because reaching beneath an opaque flow is dangerous.

There are bites, stings, infected scratches, and parasites associated with particular trades. Feeding-hall workers inspect one another’s collars at the end of a shift. Drain crews have their clothes boiled. A person who works bare-handed among shellfish develops a detailed knowledge of which small injuries can wait.

The municipal notices are specific. They show the offending creature at actual size and enlarged.

Someone is always drawing spectacles on the enlargement.

***

Food is abundant in some forms and costly in others.

There are pale cakes cut from fermented culture, dense enough to travel in a pocket; clear soups thickened with eggs; shellfish steamed in their own liquid; strips of cured pond bird; soft, sharp-smelling pastes sold in glazed jars. Different microbial harvests have different flavors. People argue about them with the confidence of those who have eaten them all their lives.

Sera dislikes the lilac culture grown in the eastern galleries. She says it tastes of cleaning water. Her sister buys it deliberately.

Fat is saved carefully. A small spoonful browned in a pan can change a whole meal. Street sellers brush hot slabs with it before laying down thin rounds of fermented batter. The rounds blister, stiffen at the edges, and are folded around chopped shell meat or a savory curd.

The queues form before shift change.

At home, eating arrangements depend on space. Some households have kitchens. Others heat breakfast beside the same conduit that dries their clothes and take their evening meal in a communal hall. These halls are not uniformly cheerful. There are favorite tables, feuds, excellent cooks, exhausted cooks, and arguments over whether someone has received a larger portion.

A child can grow up under the observation of a hundred neighbors and still be lonely.

***

People make beautiful things here without trying to make them look alive.

A finely worked basin may hold nothing but water. Its pleasure lies in the depth of the glaze, the shape of the rim, and how a lamp appears in it.

Mineral crusts are cut and polished into translucent panels. Dark metal is worked into screens that cast intricate shadows. Ceramic beads are worn in long strings, clicking softly against coat buttons. A good coat is altered and repaired until only small areas of its original fabric remain.

Feathers are common enough to be cheap, but suitable ones require sorting. There are rooms where people do nothing all day except turn feathers beneath a light, setting aside those with unbroken edges.

Dryness preserves value. Letters, paper ornaments, old photographs, musical instruments, and the wooden casting patterns in the workshops all require attention. Neglect announces itself first as a smell inside a cupboard.

Sera owns a small wooden box inherited from her mother. It contains documents, a broken clasp, and two letters she no longer reads. Once a month she takes everything out and leaves the box open near a warm vent.

She does not consider this an observance. It is how the box is kept.

***

The unvisited parts of the city have inhabitants too.

They are not simply the same animals in greater numbers. In a sealed settling court, generations of grazers may grow unusually thin shells because nothing larger can reach them. A blind fish occupies a reservoir behind a collapsed wall. Pale scavengers follow the air moving through old cable ducts, finding nests that people cannot see.

Occasionally a repair opens one of these places.

Workers shine lamps across water undisturbed for decades. Small bodies withdraw beneath mineral lips. There may be old tools, drowned fittings, a staircase continuing below the surface. The surveyors record what can be used. A sample goes to a laboratory. Someone brings a strange shell home for a child.

Then a door is fitted, or the opening is sealed, or the court returns to service.

Not everything survives the change.

***

In Sera’s building, an elderly man occupies a room behind a nesting balcony. He once worked on the cable railways. Now his knees keep him mostly indoors.

He knows the nesting birds individually and complains about them continually. Their disputes interrupt his sleep. Their droppings spoil the outside of the shutters. One has developed the habit of knocking its bill against the glass.

When maintenance proposes clearing the balcony, he refuses. The work, he says, would make too much mess.

Sera brings him food when she goes to the market. He pays her exactly, unfolding notes from a packet inside his shirt. They talk about the railway, her work, the cost of eggs, and people neither particularly likes.

One evening she finds him at the window with the shutter half open. A young bird has landed on the sill inside. It is still carrying soft gray down around its neck. Its feet slide on the glazed tile.

Neither of them touches it.

Sera puts her shopping down. The old man draws the curtain across the room’s brighter window, and she opens the balcony shutter farther. For a while the bird crouches where it is, breathing quickly. They wait beside the table.

Then it sees the opening.
