The first part of it you see looks like something caught beneath a stone.

A strip of pale material projects from the socket of a loose slab: not quite a feather, not quite a shed skin. The wind lifts its edge. When you crouch, your shadow passes over it, and the strip draws inward.

For a while nothing follows.

Then a foot appears on the other side of the slab. It is broad and very thin, divided into five blunt digits, each with a dark pad underneath. The digits spread against the limestone. A second foot finds purchase farther along, and the animal pulls itself out of an opening that seems too shallow to have contained it.

It emerges in sections. Shoulders, a long depression where the spine ought to rise, hips scarcely wider than the head. Its body is about the length of your forearm. There is no obvious moment when the tail begins. A loose border of skin runs down either side, wrinkled now that the animal has drawn itself together, but capable of lying so flat against the ground that even grit can hold it down.

Seen from above, it has the color and irregular outline of an old flake of limestone.

Seen from the side, it is unexpectedly soft.

The ribs move under a covering of fine, colorless hair. Its feet turn far enough backward to let it reverse into a crack without turning around. Its eyes are small and shaded by thick lids. There are no projecting ears. Just behind each jaw is a bare oval, glossy as a thumbnail, which it presses to the rock.

It does this now.

The whole body settles, and for several seconds the animal is almost indistinguishable from the slab beside it. Only its nostrils move. They open sideways, two short creases beneath the blunt muzzle.

Then it gets up and goes directly to a cushion of plants.

Its progress is not hurried, but it leaves little opportunity to watch. It follows the low side of every swell. Where it must cross exposed stone, it spreads its skin borders and becomes a shallow, traveling patch. At the cushion it lifts one edge with its nose and puts a forefoot underneath.

A larva comes out between two digits. The animal eats it with several quick movements of the jaw, wipes its mouth against its wrist, and puts its head down again.

There are more of them than the first hour suggests.

Their resting places are not the dampest cracks, nor the warmest. They occupy places where a thin bridge of stone connects one broad surface to another: the neck of a basin, the root of a projecting slab, the narrow floor above a buried chamber. A suitable opening may contain a dozen, laid alongside one another with their flanks touching. Their bodies fit the uneven walls so closely that there is little space left for air.

At the mouths of these retreats, the limestone is faintly greasy.

They spend much of their lives listening there.

The mountain’s noise reaches them through their jaws and feet, but it cannot be the same overwhelming sound you hear. The endless rush is familiar to them in all its local differences. One hollow beats against a loose roof. Another sends a fine vibration along a seam. A passage underneath the sedge changes pitch as its walls gather water.

The animals know these places with their bodies. A fallen stone alters a route before any of them goes to inspect it. A bird landing on a hollow ledge produces a brief disturbance that brings several heads up at once. Footsteps are more difficult. As you walk, animals emerge from cracks well ahead of you, then disappear before you come close enough to notice them.

If you sit still long enough, they return.

One may come within arm’s reach and rest its jaw against the same stone that supports your hand. Its eyes will remain half shut. Beneath your palm there is only the broad, continuous tremor; beneath its chin, something apparently sufficient to distinguish you from a loose boulder, a walking bird, or another of its kind.

They communicate through the stone, though not often by striking it.

At a narrow opening, an animal turns sideways and pushes its back upward against the roof. Its loose skin seals the smaller gaps. The passage is partly stopped.

Under your hand, a vibration weakens.

It lasts only a moment. The animal releases its hold, waits, then presses upward twice more. Farther along the basin another opening acquires a pale, soft plug.

The exchange is inaudible in the roar.

At other times they use their forefeet, rubbing the dark pads over a span of limestone no longer than a finger. The movement resembles an attempt to clean something off. A nearby animal may answer without lifting its head.

Courtship takes place on thin stone.

Two adults lie several body lengths apart above the same chamber. For a long time they do nothing you can see. Then one slowly extends a foreleg. The other shifts its jaw. The first draws its skin tight against its ribs, changing how its weight bears on the surface.

Sometimes one leaves.

Sometimes they move closer in small increments until their feet touch. Even then, they continue attending to the stone between them.

Their young are born farther inside the mountain, in abandoned bird passages where old feathers have collected beyond the draft. The young have darker coats and disproportionately large feet. They cannot flatten themselves completely. For several weeks they travel attached to an adult’s underside, clinging to the hair along its belly while their own backs brush the ground.

An adult carrying young looks slightly swollen. It chooses deeper furrows.

Later, the young wait near the retreat while the others feed. They are restless in a way the adults rarely are. They lift flakes of stone, put their noses into empty snail shells, climb onto one another. When a long-legged hunter approaches, the nearest adult plants both forefeet and makes a short, hard movement of its chest.

The young disappear.

One, caught too far from the entrance, spreads itself in a shallow socket. The bird steps past it. You can still see the animal’s outline, but only because you watched it settle there.

They eat larvae, beetles, fallen eggs, the soft bodies of snails extracted from shells too small for the birds to bother breaking. During the brief flowering they work their muzzles into the plant cushions. Pollen gathers in the hair above their nostrils and travels with them between hollows.

Water is taken wherever a mouth can reach it. They lick the undersides of stones. They enter seams too narrow for their shoulders by flattening one side of the chest, then the other. On mornings when vapor beads under the cliff lip, they hang head downward with their hind feet braced inside a crack.

Their skin darkens when wet. An animal returning from the edge may look almost black until the wind dries it.

Occasionally one brings back a feather.

It carries the shaft crosswise, struggling where the vane catches the air. At the retreat it pulls the feather inside and works it into the wall of the resting chamber. Older feathers are already there, held by the pressure of bodies, softened and broken into a felt.

By evening, the first animal has returned to the slab where you found it.

It pauses outside. One forefoot rests on the lip of the opening; the other is folded beneath its chest. Something shifts inside, too far back to see. The animal waits until the movement stops, then slides under the stone.

The strip you mistook for debris appears once more along the edge. It is the border of its flank, spread to close the gap.

A few grains of grit settle against it.

When you put your palm beside the slab, the tremor is still there. After a while it diminishes, returns, and diminishes again.

This time, you keep your hand in place.
