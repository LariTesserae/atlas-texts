The foot has no sole.

What touches the shale is a crowded field of little contact heads, each on its own short stalk, each able to retract. From a distance they make a single dark pad. Close to the lifted foot, you can see them adjusting: testing the air, withdrawing into the shade of one another, extending again. Several hold chips of stone. One holds an iron bead so firmly that its polished side has acquired a dimple.

The animal is not simply choosing somewhere to put its weight. It needs several kinds of contact at once. Some heads grip. Some carry heat. Others settle against the rock and remain almost unloaded, attending to vibrations that would be lost beneath the pressure of the whole foot.

These are the last to leave the ground and the first to return.

The three knocks come again.

Before they reach you through the air, a line of contact heads along the foot’s inner edge draws upward. The animal has heard them. Or felt the part of them that has arrived here.

Its body continues turning.

There is no neck, no distinct head. The apertures beneath the forward overhang are one of several such clusters arranged around the body. For the present, this is its front. When it has reason to move in another direction, a different cluster will open, and the roles of the legs will alter over the next few days.

Its symmetry is only approximate. One corner of the slab is thicker than the others. Two of its legs have an additional joint. The silver vanes are not quite parallel, and a broad strip near the middle of its back carries only blunt sockets, sealed over with something the color of old teeth.

It has a history of damage.

Along one flank, a split has been bridged with overlapping ceramic staples. The oldest are almost buried in new material; the newest still show their fastening pits. Beneath them something contracts in a slow diagonal sequence. The repair moves with the body. It has been incorporated without ever becoming smooth.

Nothing about the animal is finished. Even at this size, its proportions remain subject to local revision. A foot that has spent centuries on a poorly conducting terrace becomes broader. A leg accustomed to reaching across a particular intersection develops insulation along one side. An individual can acquire an asymmetry from a slope that is no longer present.

Some of the great bodies therefore look ill-suited to the places where they stand.

The places have changed.

***

The socket young do not resemble small adults beneath their exposed plates.

If an abandoned shell is lifted, it comes away with a tapering spiral attached, fragile as the interior of a burned seed. The spiral fitted the growing socket. Deep in its turns lie the remains of successive cutting surfaces, each retired when the animal became large enough to rasp a wider circumference.

At the bottom is a space scarcely visible without magnification.

That was where the soft part lived.

For many years, the juvenile’s entire concern is the short distance between this recess and the sunlit surface. It opens and closes minute passages, varying the route by which heat flows through its body. It saves certain metals. It discards others. Its dorsal plate acquires layers so thin that a lifetime of growth may show only as a change in sheen.

Many never leave.

They wear out in their sockets. Their neighbors bore sideways into the empty chambers, and for a while the ground rattles differently over the remains.

The ones that migrate have already undergone a considerable change before their shells open. They have loosened from the socket walls. The spiral has divided along six seams. Folded lengths that once served as internal braces become the first legs.

On the surface, they are awkward. Their plates tilt too far forward. They drag the soft undersides of their bodies over the stone, leaving residues too fine to see except where hundreds have followed the same route.

A hand’s breadth of shade can stop them.

They wait in close ranks at its edge, passing tiny pulses of heat between neighboring shells. The line bunches and loosens. Those at the front advance when the light returns; those farther back respond to the warmth of the bodies ahead.

Sometimes the shade does not retreat.

A raised shale plate settles. A larger animal lowers itself nearby. A crossing brings another plateau between them and whatever part of the sky they were using.

Then the migration remains where it is, a narrow black seam that eventually fills with mineral dust.

But some reach the rims of the hot blisters. There, where the shale stretches without breaking, the ground offers a difference between upper and lower temperatures that persists through the night. The young spread out. They begin to build upward.

The next stage is rare enough to be conspicuous: a creature the size of a fingernail, standing clear of the surface on six translucent struts.

Its first vane is black.

For a long time, it has only one.

***

There are no middling animals in the first stretch of country you cross. Later, on the cooler side of an escarpment, you find dozens.

They stand in a narrow band where two slopes almost meet. Most are no larger than stools. Their legs are long relative to their bodies, and their vanes are crowded, untidy things that twitch in the wind. Their sounds are sharper than the adult knocks. Now and then one produces a thin, sustained squeal and all the others stop adjusting.

The shale beneath them has been worked into overlapping crescents.

They move much more frequently than the adults, sometimes several times in an hour. They are finding positions that a larger body could occupy. One places a foot into apparently empty air, loads it cautiously, then pulls it back. Another leans so far sideways that it ought to fall.

It remains there through the afternoon.

At evening, an orange-lit surface becomes visible beneath its extended legs. The little animal has been standing on two plateaus at once.

The adults retain this ability, but use it with a deliberation that conceals its difficulty. A misplaced contact can join parts of the body to incompatible thermal conditions. On some older animals you see whole branches of vanes closed and glazed over, isolated after such an injury.

The young risk it often. Most of the favorable places are already occupied.

***

The visible adults are not necessarily neighbors.

Two standing on opposite sides of a basin may be separated by an expanse neither could cross. One that seems remote, wavering above the southern drop, may have a hind foot planted beside the animal nearest you.

Their exchanges follow these proximities.

A knock travels through stone, then enters another surface at an intersection and continues elsewhere. Some calls return carrying so many echoes that the original rhythm is almost lost. The animals wait through all of it. Contact heads rise and settle. Internal rings alter speed.

What they distinguish in the replies is difficult to know.

Their behavior suggests that they recognize particular callers. One adult answers every disturbance from a certain direction except a familiar sequence of five unequal blows. At that sound, it closes its apertures and resumes whatever it was doing. Another will interrupt a step, holding the enormous foot aloft until a distant call has ended.

There are sounds that bring movement from every animal in sight.

Once, during the hottest part of the day, a dry cracking begins beneath an empty terrace. The nearest adult turns several vanes edge-on. Farther out, two others do the same. The cracking travels north. As it passes, the socket young fall silent in a strip hundreds of meters wide.

For a moment the terrace cools enough to touch.

Then the rattling resumes.

The great animals keep their vanes turned until evening.

***

Their most vulnerable parts are hidden deep inside.

The copper-colored tissues can be replaced. A broken sleeve can be lined from within. Even the rotating organs have ways of disengaging a damaged ring and growing another around it.

The soft seals are less forgiving.

In an old animal, heat begins to escape where it should not. Fine deposits gather beneath the flank openings. The vanes tilt continually, compensating. It moves toward places with sharper gradients, places the younger adults avoid.

Eventually it stands over a vent that stains its underside red.

Years pass. Its legs widen their stance.

The last movement may be a small adjustment of one foot.

Long afterward, the body continues to turn internally. Heat still passes through the rings. Discharges still cross the back. Some of its mechanisms need no living regulation to repeat what they have always done.

The socket animals climb it.

They establish themselves first in the feet, then along the lower joints. Their rasping changes the pitch of the hollow spaces. Vanes fall. The slab settles by increments until it lies low over the ground, supported by the thickest parts of its own ruined limbs.

Underneath, protected from the worst of the wind, a little vapor sometimes remains until morning.

The rusty film grows there.

***

The animal before you is far from that condition.

Its lifted foot is warm enough to hurt your face at a distance. Dust continues falling between the contact heads. Higher up, beyond the shade of the leg, the vanes reveal their dark evening surfaces one rank at a time.

A small object emerges from a slit beneath the body.

It hangs there, attached by a glossy thread: a pale flake with a thickened center. Then the thread retracts and the flake falls.

It strikes the shale without an audible sound.

Others follow, widely spaced. Most land flat. One catches in a groove and remains on edge, showing a dark underside divided by six fine lines.

The parent does not look down. Its apertures are still directed across the plateau, toward the answering knocks.

By morning, the flakes have moved into imperfections in the stone. One has reached the track left by an iron bead. Another is pressed against the black rim of an occupied socket.

You would have taken them for chips of shale.

The adult’s foot has settled several meters away. Around it, in a region warmed by its underside and sheltered from the prevailing wind, the ground has begun to make a finer sound.
