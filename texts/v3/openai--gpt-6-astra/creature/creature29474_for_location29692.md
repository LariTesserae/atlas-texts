The animals turning the chip are not quite alike.

One has a broad white roof, worn smooth over the middle and jagged at the rear, where a new plate has not yet seated properly. The other is narrower, tiled mostly in rust. A fleck of blue paint stands upright behind its head. This catches on low ceilings, but the animal has kept it through several changes of its other plates.

They work with their mouths and the first pair of legs. The white one braces; the rust-colored one pulls. When the chip slips, both become motionless, though their antennae continue to travel over it. Then they exchange positions.

The gap they are filling is not in either animal’s house. It is between a dead grazer’s roof and a shelf of fresh mineral. Behind it lies a pocket of damp grit containing eggs.

Some of the eggs belong to neither of them. This does not appear to matter. They fit the chip with its smooth side inward, leaving an opening at the bottom through which a very small body could pass.

Then the white one goes away. The rust-colored one remains to eat something from the edge of the opening.

At this distance it is difficult to distinguish care from feeding.

***

Beneath their roofs, the grazers are softer and longer than they look. A startled one draws itself into a compact knot, but an undisturbed animal can extend its head and shoulders almost the length of its house. Its mouth opens downward. Around it are six short, colorless feelers which move independently, feeling into pits too small for the antennae.

They do not eat the iron directly, or not all of it. Much of what they seek grows on the flakes: a dull bloom, thinner than tarnish, which becomes orange where it has been freshly scraped. Their droppings retain little sharp centers. These accumulate in the sheltered corners, and other animals turn them over and eat the bloom that grows there.

A grazer’s underside carries a fringe of fine hairs. Water caught in this fringe can be transported several body lengths before the warmth takes it away. On dry afternoons, smaller creatures follow in the faint damp tracks.

The grazers tolerate these followers until one gets beneath a plate. Then they stop and rock violently from side to side.

Their houses are held together by more than fit. At the back of the neck is a moist patch against which a new fragment is repeatedly rubbed. The substance left on it dries clear and stretches a little before breaking. An animal preparing a plate may spend most of a morning beneath one ledge, turning the fragment, wetting it, pressing it against its roof, taking it off again.

Good pieces are scarce. A thin flake with one curved edge may be carried far past several adequate feeding places.

After a death, the plates loosen in a particular order. First the rear, then the sides. The original plate, the one the animal acquired when young, usually remains attached to the hardened pad beneath it. It can be found at the center of the empty house: disproportionately small, sometimes a different color from every other piece.

There are old roofs here built around a grain of glass.

***

The things beneath the steps have no houses.

Each consists of a narrow body suspended among eight legs, with the joints bent above its back. In daylight their bodies appear empty except for a brown thread. After feeding, the thread thickens and develops dark knots.

They feed on the ceiling of the step. Two minute appendages beside the mouth comb the metal, gathering a film that is invisible until a row has passed over it. Their tracks overlap. There is no unworked patch beneath the lower step except around a small circular hole, which none of them approaches.

Their young occupy the inner bends of the adults’ legs.

At first a young animal is little more than a wet speck clasped around a joint. As it lengthens, its own legs unfold along the parent’s, so that an adult carrying several young looks frayed. During a vibration, the small ones tighten their hold before the large ones lift.

Sometimes the pipe ticks and only a few adults rise. Sometimes the whole colony rises before anything can be felt through the stone.

An animal that has lost a leg still takes the same posture. The remaining limbs spread farther apart. Its neighbors shift to accommodate it, though they resist any newcomer trying to enter the row.

When the washing flood comes, they move toward the bolt without breaking contact. For a while a living mesh stretches between the step and the bolt head. The last arrivals climb over the first. Young are transferred in the confusion, and afterward they are not always carried back by the same adults.

***

The brass-colored predator is blind on the side facing the valve.

That side has grown flat. Its outer skin rests so closely against the metal that a line of green corrosion crosses from one to the other. When it unfolds, the line breaks.

The visible eye is small and black, set well behind the forelimbs. It follows shadows rather than bodies. A grazer can approach it safely in full light and be seized when a shoe passes overhead.

Its long arms end in pads, not claws. Each pad can close around an irregular plate without crushing it. Once prey has been lifted, a much finer pair of limbs emerges beneath the head and begins to search.

The search is methodical. It starts at the newest seam.

Most grazers survive because their plates overlap deeply enough, or because the predator is interrupted. A movement at the valve can make it put down an animal it has held for several minutes. It must return one forelimb to the brass before the bead falls.

It drinks with that limb. The inner surface darkens as water travels along a groove toward its mouth.

There are other curled shapes near the valve, smaller and paler, but most are discarded skins. One has remained caught beneath the adjustment screw for so long that mineral has begun to fill it. The living animal occasionally grips this skin when it stretches.

It has laid three eggs on the dry side of the lever. They are narrow, metallic, and fixed end to end. From below they resemble the remains of a hinge.

***

The cold pocket belongs to creatures that cannot cross the warm floor.

They have flattened bodies with a dark central spot and transparent margins. On the smooth mineral beneath the bracket, they slide with such steady motion that they seem to be carried by water. But the surface is often only damp, and sometimes apparently dry.

A row of minute contractions passes around each margin.

They graze the dark surface and one another’s abandoned skins. When two meet, the smaller usually flows partly beneath the larger. Both pause until their outlines separate again.

Their eggs are held against the bracket in a single uneven layer. Condensation gathers around them. A heavy drop can carry away half a season’s laying.

Below the pocket, on the first warm slope, are tiny clear husks. These are the ones that followed the water too far.

During rain, the pocket’s inhabitants spread widely. They enter the tracks of grazers, cover nest openings, and climb the lower stems of the green blades. They are not fast, but an animal caught beneath one struggles as though caught under a wet cloth. Through the transparent body, its legs remain visible.

When the rain thins, the invaders turn back. Some take routes that were possible an hour before and are no longer possible now.

The grazers do not immediately approach the stranded bodies. They wait until the clear margins have curled.

***

There is also something that lives inside the newest ridges.

It is easiest to find where a ridge has formed badly: a wall across dry ground, a lip facing away from the water. Look along the base and there may be a cloudy thread enclosed in the mineral.

The thread contracts when shaded. Sometimes it divides into several shorter lengths. At other times the apparent divisions disappear, leaving a single cloudy line.

Nothing has been seen entering these ridges. When they split, nothing recognizable comes out. Yet a fresh break may show small chambers lined with a yellowish skin, and in one chamber there may be a dark speck that moves against the light.

The grazers scrape such breaks eagerly.

A ridge does not always survive their attention. They can reduce an immaculate little wall to a scatter of pale chips in a day. Other ridges they leave alone, even when these obstruct a familiar passage. Traffic climbs over them until the surface is polished.

Near the valve there is one low barrier with a notch worn through its center. Before each bead, the notch narrows slightly. After the water has passed, animals gather to reopen it.

They use their jaws, their front legs, and sometimes a loose grain held between them. The work is irregular. On some days no one comes, and the next bead spills into the houses below.

***

What hatches beneath the nearest step is almost without color.

For a while it remains inside the split egg, moving its mouth against the inner skin. Its legs are folded close along its sides. One unfolds and catches on the shell. It pulls, rests, and pulls again.

Above it, the hanging animals have begun to lift.

The hatchling comes free during the vibration. It falls a short distance into damp grit and lies there, collecting black grains on its wet back. A grazer passes close enough to turn it over.

Now its legs can find the ground.

It moves toward the pale underside of an empty roof, stopping wherever its mouth touches something. At the entrance lies a thin triangular chip. The animal gets partly beneath this, raises it, and carries it forward.

The chip is too large. It tips, catches against the roof, and slides away.

The hatchling continues into shelter. A little later its head appears again. It feels along the fallen fragment, finds the narrow end, and begins to pull.
