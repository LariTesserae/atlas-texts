The one behind the upper ledge has a blister on the palm of its working hand.

You see that before you see its face. A hand comes over the clay, spreads three fingers, and waits while the ground beneath it firms. The blister is clear and round, with a grain of dark material moving inside. Then the fingers close. Something hauls itself up from a space that, a moment ago, contained only the shadow under a leaf.

It is about as long as the fourth seed-husk step, though this does not help you judge its size. One foot is still far below it. You can hear that foot searching against a wall, a succession of soft slaps rising out of a depth your eyes cannot find.

The rest of it comes up slowly.

Its skin has the gray-pink translucence of a soaked root. Across its back are patches of darker, tougher tissue, overlapping at the shoulders and hips. Water collects under their edges. The head is broad and low, almost without a neck, with two black eyes sheltered beneath a projecting fold. Where you expect a mouth there is a short horizontal seam. It opens to release a length of cord.

The creature draws the cord out between its fingers. A little more comes, then a little more. At last it bites through, leaving a wet end against its chin.

It has been carrying the cord in its cheek.

There are other things in there. You can see their shapes when it turns toward the needle-hole light: a curved splinter, three hard seeds, something angular that forces the skin outward below one eye. Its cheeks are storage places. So are the loose folds beneath its arms. A peg drops from one of these and strikes the ledge.

The creature stops.

Far below, beyond the seed-husk stair, there is a second, fainter impact.

It listens until that sound has finished. Then it lies flat and reaches after the peg.

Its arm goes down farther than its arm ought to go. The shoulder follows. For several moments all you can see is the patchwork of its back and the two broad hind feet braced in wet clay. Each foot has a divided sole that grips an edge on both sides. One sole opens and closes, testing.

When it returns, it has the peg between its teeth.

It does not look pleased or displeased. It holds the recovered object against its chest until its breathing slows.

Behind it, the new ledge continues to advance.

***

There are several of them.

It takes a long time to be certain, because you never see two complete bodies together. A face appears below the red shelf while a hand is working above it. Something coughs inside a rush wall. Once, beneath the sound of the mallet, you hear the quick double click of two hard objects being tapped together in another part of the country.

They answer one another through the fastenings.

A finger hooked around a cord gives a short pull. Elsewhere, a back stiffens. Three pulls make a worker stop chewing. A sustained tension causes every visible hand to leave the clay.

Then they wait.

A tuber is swelling beneath the lowest green shelf. It lifts the shelf, the retaining wall, and a small chamber woven into the wall. The chamber rises at an angle. From its entrance someone pushes out a bundle wrapped in leaf skin.

Another worker takes the bundle. For an instant the wrapping opens, and you see five small feet held close against a belly.

The sixth is caught in the leaf.

A finger frees it with extraordinary care.

The chamber tears loose almost at once. Its owner lets it go. It swings from one cord while the tuber rolls slowly into the space it occupied.

Only when the movement has stopped does the tapping begin again.

They are often surprised by what the country does. They put an opening where water has already begun to collect. They brace a wall and discover that the brace is now a bridge. One labors for a long while to lift a clay flake, gets its shoulder under the edge, and rests there panting; during the rest, a root enters the gap and takes the weight.

The worker backs away. It rubs its shoulder against a rush stem, then starts work somewhere else.

***

Their young have six limbs.

The smallest cling beneath the adults’ chest folds, soft and nearly colorless, their bellies moving against the larger body’s breathing. They have no cheek stores yet. Their mouths remain open, pressed to damp skin.

As they grow, they begin to carry things.

At first it is a single grain of charcoal. A youngster will spend a considerable time finding a place for it, pushing it into one hollow, retrieving it, trying another. Sometimes an adult takes the grain away. Sometimes the adult sets a hand behind the youngster’s body while it leans out farther than it can safely lean.

The middle pair of limbs are drawn gradually into the sides. You can see them in the half-grown ones: two small, restless hands beneath the storage folds, passing food forward, turning a splinter, kneading the underside of a bruised patch of skin.

In the oldest, those hands can scarcely reach outside.

One old inhabitant lives beside a knot near the entrance, where the heat is lower. The plates along its back are pale and lifted at the edges. It no longer climbs. The little hands beneath its arms remain busy, combing fibers apart and twisting them together again.

Others leave scraps within its reach.

Its cord is very fine. A length of it runs around the rim of a fern bed, crosses an open drop, and vanishes beneath the upper basin. When the old one pulls its work toward itself, a distant bead of water shivers.

It sleeps with the unfinished cord held in its mouth.

***

They eat the things the country has used.

There is a soft brown layer on the undersides of old clay scales. They scrape it off with the lower edge of the jaw. They split lengths of exhausted root and suck the centers. A worker at rest may hold a lump of rotten bark between both feet and turn it slowly while feeding, its working hand free to keep pressure on a peg.

The white animal beneath the steps is tolerated, except when it feeds on a fastening.

Then someone nudges it away.

It is persistent. It comes back along the same route, lifting each of its six feet carefully over the roughness. The worker nudges it again. On the third approach, the worker picks it up.

Held against the light, the animal becomes almost transparent. Green matter fills a crooked tube inside it. Its feet continue to walk.

The worker carries it to a damp patch on the back of a clay flake and sets it down. Both blunt feelers touch the surface. The little mouth begins moving.

Later, when no hand is near, it returns to the peg.

***

The mallet is a piece of dense seed casing fitted to a handle of bone.

It passes from hand to hand. Nobody keeps it long, although its blows can continue for hours. A worker finishes driving a peg and leaves the mallet standing in the mud. Presently a hand reaches out of the vegetation and takes it.

There is a shallow sleeping place behind the hottest ledge. Those who have just used the mallet often go there. They lower themselves chest-first into the wet hollow and cover their heads with their hands. Heat makes the seams along their backs gape.

For a while you can see deeper into them.

There are pale cords beneath the skin, moving against one another. Small dark chambers fill and empty. The hidden middle hands open inside their folds.

Sleep loosens their grip so completely that another inhabitant must sometimes turn them over before the mud closes across their breathing seam.

This is done without waking them.

The one who turns the sleeper usually lies down beside it.

***

You never see anyone at the center of the unfinished basin.

At its rim, workers are constantly arriving. They test the rawhide loop with their feet. They carry away mud in folded leaves. They press their faces close to a new surface and breathe over it, then draw back sharply when it begins to rise.

Sometimes a hand comes out of the basin.

It is difficult to distinguish from the ridge forming beside it. Both are wet, blunt shapes with a shine along their upper edges. The hand flexes once. Someone at the rim takes hold.

What is pulled out has its limbs folded tightly along its body and its eyes sealed. Mud occupies every crease. The worker washes it with water squeezed from a cord, paying particular attention to the mouth.

Most begin to breathe.

One does not, though the washing continues for a long time.

Eventually the worker carries it down through the terraces. At each difficult crossing it shifts the small body to the uphill side of its own. Under the black shelf, behind a wall that has to be partly unlaced, there is a place you cannot see.

The worker comes back empty-handed.

It sits beside the loosened wall until another arrives with cord.

***

When your breath enters, the nearest inhabitants turn their faces away.

One puts both hands over a young one’s head. Another drops flat and catches a loose clay scale before the condensation can lift it. Along the basin rim, bodies lean against the rawhide as the mud begins to swell.

They know this weather.

An old repair runs across the entrance, just beneath the felt: a succession of short cords, each tied to the next, with enough slack between them to take a considerable strain. Several of the cords have darkened almost to black. The newest is pale.

The worker with the blister climbs toward it.

Now its face is close enough that you can see the tiny grains adhering around its eyes. It looks past you, into the summit wind. The cheek on that side draws inward. Its throat moves once.

For a moment it remains there, exposed to the cold.

Then it takes the loose red cord in its mouth and retreats under the felt. It pulls until the flap settles lower, leaving only a narrow opening along the bottom.

Through that opening you can still see its feet.

One braces against the upright. The other finds a purchase in the clay. It works the red cord around an old fastening, pauses, loosens what it has done, and begins again.

Farther in, someone lifts the mallet.

The creature at the entrance holds still for the blow, one finger resting lightly on the knot.
