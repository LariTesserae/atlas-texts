The attendants are easiest to find in the morning, when they go out to uncover the instruments.

They wear loose, pale jackets with dark patches beneath the arms and across the shoulders, where oil and the repeated handling of equipment have defeated washing. Their pockets fasten with cloth tabs. Nothing hangs freely from a belt. Even their keys are kept in little sleeves, each key withdrawn separately, with the others held in the palm.

From a distance they seem unusually deliberate. Up close, they are not slow. A woman can open an inspection housing, exchange a filter, and close it again with startling speed. What she does not do is let the housing take the weight of the lid. Her other hand is already there.

This habit persists outside work. They catch cupboard doors before they meet their frames. They set cups down while still holding a little of their weight. An attendant visiting the towns beyond the ridge may reach involuntarily toward a tray being dropped on a table, as though something living were about to be hurt.

There are twenty-six permanent residents now. In summer there are sometimes thirty, though the spare rooms are not all habitable at once.

Their dwellings occupy the landward ends of the industrial buildings, behind double doors and short passages lined with felt. These quarters were originally intended for shifts of workers, not families. Kitchens have been made in storerooms. The rooms facing north are cold enough that people keep their bread in the bedrooms. Several internal windows look into the machinery halls, so that someone washing dishes can watch a pressure gauge through two thicknesses of glass.

The inhabited rooms are much less austere than the works.

There are patterned quilts, paper lanterns, shelves crowded with unsuitable gifts. A blue glass fish sits on a folded square of wool. Someone has painted a brilliant red border around a door. In one kitchen, all the handles are different because the owner has spent fifteen years replacing them with shapes she likes.

People talk at ordinary volume indoors. The feared disturbance is not every sound, but a particular sharpness passed into the stone. A human voice in an insulated room is usually harmless. A small metal object falling just outside can be another matter.

Even so, newcomers often whisper for weeks.

Children find this embarrassing.

The youngest permanent resident is a girl named Sena, who can distinguish the three generating halls by the pressure she feels through her soles. She says the northern one has a toothache. The engineers dislike this description, although they ask her about it when the weather changes.

Sena is eleven. She does not regard the valley as beautiful in any consistent way. Certain places are beautiful; others are tedious, or awkward, or smell unpleasant after rain. She has a favorite tree and a least favorite stair. She would like to live somewhere with a ball court.

There is a room for jumping in the western building, a former packing bay with a suspended floor. It was installed after a long argument, and its inspection schedule is more exacting than that of several pressure vessels.

Adults use it too, usually when they believe no one else is nearby.

***

The woman who measures the central surveying line is called Iven.

She was nineteen when the first fracture opened beneath the orchard. She remembers the orchard less clearly than she remembers the shed beside it, which had a door swollen at the bottom and a place under the bench where the dog slept. Most visitors want to hear about the day the land rose. Iven tells them there was no single day. For a long time there was a step where there should not have been a step, and people blamed the roots.

She has worked in the valley for forty-three years.

Her hair grows in a white band above the ears and remains dark at the back. One shoulder is higher than the other. She carries her measuring equipment on the lower side, in a rectangular bag stiffened with thin wood. The bag contains three pencils, though only one is officially required. It also contains a spoon, a spare stocking, and a photograph whose face has worn pale where the fabric folds against it.

She walks the routes from memory but never entirely trusts them.

At certain bends she stops before placing a foot, not because anything looks different, but because the next piece of ground is out of sight. She once stepped onto a familiar slab and found it lower than expected. The fall was only a few inches. She broke two bones in her wrist and lay very still afterward, listening for something worse.

Nothing followed. For several years she dreamed that it had.

Iven is not the quietest person in the settlement. She laughs abruptly, with her mouth open, and enjoys hearing stories badly told so that she can interrupt them. She dislikes the reverence visitors sometimes bring to the works. If someone speaks of the valley as sleeping, she will point out that it is doing a substantial amount of work.

She is protective of the plants without being tender toward every individual plant. Each month she cuts the white-backed climber away from an intake grille. It has learned, or appears to have learned, to approach from beneath the adjacent pipe rather than straight across the wall. She admires this development and cuts it back.

In her room are seven pots of herbs brought from outside.

Their soil must be changed regularly. Fine mineral deposits enter on clothing, settle with the dust, and accumulate in the pots. The herbs tolerate this poorly. Growing them is expensive and, by the settlement’s standards, wasteful.

Iven uses them in soup.

***

The small gray animals are most often seen by people who are carrying something and cannot stop.

They are about the length of a hand, excluding a narrow tail that lies flat against the stone. Their fur is darker underneath than above. When one pauses on a slab, its outline becomes difficult to separate from the slight discolorations around a damp seam.

The attendants call them seam-runners.

Their feet are broad and almost bare, with soft, divided pads. Before crossing a fracture, a seam-runner puts its nose down into the opening and remains there for several seconds. Sometimes it backs away. Sometimes it turns sideways and passes along a route that, to a person standing above it, seems no more promising than the first.

They eat the swollen bases of certain fern stems, small grubs from the leaf drifts, and the clear flesh beneath snail shells. They leave the pearly old leaves untouched. Near their sleeping places, one can occasionally find tiny heaps of stripped fibers and salt-rich skins, carefully separated from whatever nourishment they contained.

They have taken readily to the pipe supports.

Each support offers a shaded underside, a dry upper ledge, and a little nightly warmth. Some of the animals line these spaces with fibers pulled from the old felt around service doors. The maintenance staff tried replacing the felt with a synthetic material. The animals would not use it, but they continued to investigate the doors, making repeated trips with nothing to carry away.

Now the worn strips are left in a box beneath an exterior stair.

No one claims this is necessary.

A seam-runner has occupied the wall behind Iven’s bed for at least six years, or a succession of them has. She hears a faint brushing near the skirting after sunset. In winter, when the room is warmer than the wall, it sometimes comes through the pipe opening and sits beneath the radiator.

She does not feed it. There are too many things in ordinary food that it cannot process.

Once, while changing the bed, she uncovered three young in a fold of spare blanket stored underneath. They were pink-gray, their tails translucent at the edges. The adult was not there. Iven stood holding a sheet, then put everything back except the blanket she actually needed.

For nine nights she slept without turning on the bedside lamp.

***

The valley’s other inhabitants are less easy to count.

There are pale snails that remain on the same length of pipe for most of their lives, grazing the films that form where condensation gathers beneath the brackets. Their shells acquire faint green rims from the copper. An engineer once began recording their positions and discovered that the apparently stationary group outside the northern hall replaced itself almost entirely over a year. Where the old ones went was never established.

There are minute insects inside the folded leaves. In the afternoon they remain still, their bodies fitted into the crease beside the central vein. At dawn they emerge wet. For half an hour the foliage is full of them, each moving only a short distance before entering another fold.

The plants themselves are difficult to divide into individuals.

A conifer on one shelf may be connected to a low thicket two fractures away. What appears to be a sapling can be an exposed portion of a root that has turned upward, found light, and begun again. Dead trunks remain held among the living crowns so securely that they continue to provide routes for vines long after their own roots have broken.

The residents mark these connections when they can. A branch that must be removed from a window may be supporting something several yards away. Cutting is done in stages, with days between.

This makes gardening a protracted and occasionally bitter business.

One household has been negotiating with a tree for twelve years. The tree is winning in the bathroom.

***

Supplies arrive over the southern ridge in padded carriers. Flour, fruit, medicines, replacement seals, letters. The empty carriers take back damaged instruments and the small quantities of waste the settlement cannot safely keep.

Much of the electricity leaves without anyone seeing where it goes.

The attendants know the names of the towns supplied by their works, but few have visited all of them. There is a coastal hospital that sends a card each winter. For some years the card showed the hospital itself; lately it has shown a painted bird. The cards stand in a row on a shelf in the common dining room, secured with a length of ribbon.

Meals are noisy enough. People disagree about shifts, money, the fruit, whether someone has taken the last clean towel. Repairs continue during quarrels. A woman may pass a padded wrench to a man she has not otherwise spoken to since breakfast.

Everyone knows which paths a person is likely to take home.

If someone is late, another resident will eventually stand at a window overlooking the appropriate turn. This is not announced as concern. There are several windows with very clean patches in the glass at face height.

Some residents leave because the precautions become intolerable. Others leave for reasons that have nothing to do with the valley. They marry, receive offers, want rooms with more sunlight or less. Their departures are sometimes treated as temporary long after they have ceased to be so.

Sena’s older brother has been away for three years. His soft-soled boots are still in the entrance shelter. They would no longer fit him.

***

On the afternoon of a measurement, Iven opens the instrument box and checks beneath the lid before taking out the pencil.

Small creatures get into things. Moisture gets into everything.

She notes the temperature, waits for the weighted marker to settle, and reads the line. Then she does it again. On clear days, her hand casts a shadow so exact that she can see the tremor of a finger against the pale board.

The distance has increased.

It is not an exceptional increase. She writes the figure beside the date and rests the pencil briefly across her fingers while checking her arithmetic.

Behind her, something moves in the leaves. A seam-runner appears at the edge of the path carrying a strip of felt longer than its body. The strip catches beneath a stem. The animal pulls, stops, reverses, and tries from another angle.

Iven watches until it gets free.

Before closing the box, she picks a fallen flower off the hinge. Its petals are white and thick, already curling inward around the damp center. She puts it beneath the branch from which it probably fell.

Then she checks that she has her spoon, secures the instrument in its bag, and takes the long way home.
