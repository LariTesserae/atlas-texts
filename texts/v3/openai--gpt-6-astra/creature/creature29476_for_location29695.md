Beneath the ledge, where the moss stops just short of the water, there is sometimes a pale lip that was not there before.

It looks like another layer of repair: a thin, soft-looking material laid over the older ceramic, with a little sediment caught along its edge. Water passes over it without breaking. Only when it withdraws does it become clear that the sediment is inside it, suspended in translucent flesh.

The animal is about as long as a hand. Most of that length is tail, though the distinction between tail and body becomes difficult when it spreads itself against the floor. At rest it is broad and low, a loose oval narrowing toward one end. Moving, it gathers into something more definite. A ridge rises along its back. Limbs come out from underneath. The front of it lifts enough to disclose a mouth.

There are six limbs, unequal in size. The foremost pair are short and usually folded beside the mouth. The middle pair do most of the walking. The hindmost are so fine that they can be mistaken for root fibers trailing from its body, until one curls around the edge of a block and pulls.

Each foot opens into a small fan of blunt, transparent fingers. Through them, the dark floor remains visible.

It has no shell. The hard-looking portions of its back are patches of thicker skin, clouded with minute inclusions. Between these patches the body is almost clear. When it has been feeding, a brown strand can be seen well inside it, making a slow bend that does not correspond to any movement of the animal as a whole.

Its eyes are small, set far apart and somewhat underneath the rim of its head. They are useful chiefly for the difference between the shaft’s ordinary brightness and a brightness interrupted. A person passing over the wired glass causes it to stop. A cloud generally does not. It will remain stopped for a long time, with one foot spread against the ceramic and another still gathered for the next step.

This is not perfect concealment. Once you know the animal’s outline, you can usually find it again. But there is seldom anyone here to learn its outline.

It spends much of its time fitted into the shallow hollow around the old pipe plug. The fit is not exact. Its flank leaves a narrow passage through which water continues to move, and its head rests on the lower edge, facing the room. The recess gives it contact along its back and one side while leaving its underside in the current.

Here its skin performs a continual, nearly invisible movement. Fine folds open and close along the flanks, lifting water away from the body and admitting more. Sometimes a tiny bubble lodges beneath a fold. The animal shifts until the bubble comes free, then settles back into almost the same position.

The warmth suits it. So does the constancy of the flow. It need not travel between a place for feeding and a place where it will not dry out. It moves because one strip of film has been grazed clean, because a fern root has begun to soften, because something edible has lodged a few inches farther along a seam.

Its mouth is on the underside of the head, wider than you would expect. It presses this mouth to a surface and works it slowly, taking the loose growth without stripping the surface bare. The small front limbs keep stirring beside it. They separate fibers, turn grains, hold a morsel while the mouth closes.

Occasionally they hold something that struggles.

There are little worms in the retained silt, and soft animals among the moss that are too small to be noticed from the hatch. The creature eats these when it encounters them. It does not pursue them far. A worm drawn halfway out of the moss may escape while the animal is still rearranging its feet to obtain a better purchase.

It remains for a while with its mouth against the empty place.

The roar does not make it restless. Its body is in contact with the floor, the water, and often the wall; it lives within the vibration as thoroughly as it lives within the warmth. Yet a boot placed on the threshold can make all six limbs draw inward at once. The disturbance travels through the foundation with a shape different from the motors’ steady work.

After that withdrawal, the tail continues moving for a moment. Its thin edges fold over each other and come to rest.

If you put a hand in the water and leave it there, the animal may eventually approach. It follows the changed current around your fingers. At the nail of the smallest finger it pauses, touching once with a front foot, then with the rim of its head.

Your hand is warmer than the floor. It is also moving, however carefully you try to hold it still.

The animal slips underneath the longest fern frond and waits.

There may be more than one. A smaller individual could occupy a seam beneath the moss without competing much with this one, and eggs, if it lays them here, would be difficult to distinguish from the clear beads among the roots. But the visible animal bears a recognizable injury: one middle foot opens into three fingers instead of five. The missing portion has healed to a smooth edge.

It is this same foot that can sometimes be seen braced against the glazed crescent at the back of the room.

After the hatch closes, it does not immediately emerge. The altered current must settle first. Then a front limb reaches out and touches the floor.

The rest follows slowly, spreading as it comes into the shallow water, until the pale animal lies fully exposed on a pale block. Its feet cease to be distinguishable from its outline. Water gathers along its upstream edge and passes over its back.

Underneath, the mouth begins to work.
