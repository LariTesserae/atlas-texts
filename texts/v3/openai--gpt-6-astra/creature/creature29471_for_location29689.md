The hunter beneath the pump house has a head wider than the opening through which it comes and goes. To withdraw, it turns one cheek down against the concrete, folds the loose skin of its throat, and lets its shoulders follow separately. The movement looks difficult every time. There is a polished place on the foundation where it has been doing this for years.

Out in the water, it is longer than it first appears. Most of its body is tail: thick at the root, flattened farther back, ending in a thin paddle usually kept folded against one flank. Its hind legs are small. They help it brace inside a crevice but scarcely touch the ground when it walks. On exposed stone it pulls itself forward with its broad forefeet, pauses, and draws the rest of its weight after it.

The black pads on its toes are soft, closely ridged, and sensitive to pressure. Beneath each pad is a little fan of cartilage. A foot laid flat on the pump-house floor receives the movement of the machine through all five toes at once.

It can distinguish the pump from a falling stone. It can distinguish a grazer crossing the foundation from a swimmer striking its underside. The difference between an empty stroke and one that has taken hold of water reaches it before there is any visible change in the pools.

Its eyes are less useful. They register an opening overhead, a shadow moving across an opening, the brief silver disturbance of prey. A transparent fold covers each eye when the head enters mud. Often the folds stay closed after it emerges, giving its face a sealed, worn appearance, like something handled until the finer details have disappeared.

There are small pores along its jaw. In cold moving water these remain open. In still water, a clear bead gathers in each one.

The animal hunts with its mouth almost shut.

A swimmer passing close feels a slight sideways draw, too weak to escape from in time because there is so little warning. The hunter’s cheek expands. Its lower jaw shifts outward on one side. Water and prey enter together, and the water leaves through slits hidden beneath the loose skin behind the head. From above, the whole act resembles a flinch.

Large prey must be held against stone. This is when the forefeet become hands of a sort, though the toes cannot curl far enough to grasp. It pins with the pads and works its mouth along the trapped body. The black crescents sometimes visible on its lips are not teeth. They are plates of hard skin, replaced from beneath as their edges wear away.

It does not eat the large shells. It will spend an hour investigating the base of one, pressing its face into the mineral seam, but it is looking for eggs or the soft little animals that gather in the shell’s outflow. A shell may continue feeding with the hunter leaning against it. Each has become accustomed to certain movements of the other.

Other movements still produce the chain of clicks.

There are three hunters in the hollow, though someone watching for a week might believe there was only one.

The animal at the pump house is the largest. Another occupies the northern pipe, behind the colony that has narrowed its bore. This one is darker, with gray flecks embedded in its skin, and has lost the outer toes of one forefoot. The third is small enough to travel through the passages used by the pale swimmers. It appears in widely separated pools and is seldom seen twice at the same opening.

They tolerate one another in deep water. On the feeding shelves, they leave messages.

Under the tail lies a pair of glands that make a yellowish grease. The hunter presses these against the underside of a ledge, then drags forward a short distance. The mark remains after several changes of water level. A passing animal touches it with the lower edge of its jaw and may wait there for some time.

The marks carry more than ownership. They change when an animal has eaten, when it has been injured, when its body is preparing eggs. During certain weeks, the smallest hunter visits the pump-house foundation repeatedly. It approaches from downstream, touches the concrete, and waits with only its nostrils above water.

The larger animal sometimes comes out.

Their meetings are slow and mostly submerged. One presses a forefoot against the other’s shoulder. They remain that way while the films drift around them. When the pump starts, both withdraw, but not always to separate places.

The eggs are laid well inside the foundation, above the lowest water and below the highest. They are fixed individually to the ceiling of a flat cavity. Each hangs from a short, tough stalk, with enough space around it for water to pass. At first they look like drops that have failed to fall.

The parent keeps this cavity clear. It removes grit with its lips and carries it out in small mouthfuls. It eats the grazers that climb toward the eggs. Mites still arrive, so small they pass between the teeth of every defence the animal possesses. Some feed on the egg coverings without doing much harm. Others enter.

A spoiled egg loses its tension. The parent detects this by touching it, takes it down, and eats it.

The young hatch with fringes along their throats and forefeet that are already disproportionately broad. For a time they cling overhead among the empty stalks. Their bodies are transparent enough to show a dark meal moving behind the heart.

The first pump cycle kills some of them.

Those that release their grip too early are carried into the gravel beneath the floor. Those that hold too long may be left exposed when the cavity drains. The surviving young descend during the interval when the current slackens but the water still touches the ceiling. They do not follow their parent. They disperse into seams too narrow for it to enter.

Farther under the building, beyond the egg cavity, the concrete has cracked around the old intake.

Here the smallest animals are abundant. They live in the soft orange material on the iron, in the felt of bacteria beneath a flange, in the groove worn around a shaft. Some spend their whole lives inside the pump. They are eyeless, nearly colourless, and flattened in different directions according to the spaces they occupy.

One kind resembles a short white comb. It anchors its body inside a bolt hole and extends jointed limbs into the passing water. Another lays eggs in a flexible ribbon along the sheltered side of a valve. When the valve moves, the ribbon folds into a recess. Many generations of eggs have occupied precisely that recess.

The machine is not equally habitable throughout its cycle. A chamber that is safe for an hour becomes crushingly narrow in a moment. A passage fills, draws empty, fills again. The animals living there respond to minute changes in pressure. They retract, fold, release their holds. After a stroke, they extend again into the newly arrived water.

In places, their coverings have become part of the fit between metal surfaces.

The original seals are gone. Around one piston, densely packed tubes hold a collar of mud and mineral grains that leaks very little. Water seeps through the colony continuously, feeding it. The tubes flex when the piston moves. Dead ones are crushed into the collar; new ones grow along its wet edge.

Elsewhere, a crust has stopped a valve from closing fully. The resulting trickle supports a patch of gray film, and the grazers feeding there keep the opening clear of softer deposits.

No animal in the hollow encounters the whole machine. Even the large hunter knows only the floor, the intake cavity, the pulses arriving through concrete. Nevertheless, there are living surfaces wherever the pump requires water to slip past something, and bare surfaces wherever it still requires contact.

Deep inside, iron continues to wear against iron.

When a fresh flake breaks loose, it travels west with the discharge. Small animals investigate it before it settles. Within days its brightest edge has dulled. A film establishes itself in the fracture, then mouths arrive.

The hunter waits through all of this beneath its familiar slab. Its skin carries a sparse growth of bacteria, particularly around old injuries. Tiny scavengers shelter in the folds behind its forelegs. When it rests for a long time, they emerge and pick across its back.

Before the next stroke, it lifts one foot.

The scavengers stop moving. A swimmer farther inside the foundation backs into a crack. Under the hunter’s other foot, the concrete begins to strain.

It has time to lower its head and turn its cheek against the polished opening. Then the water takes hold of its tail.
