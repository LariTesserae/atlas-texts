Beneath one of the leaning groves, where a living branch enters the water and comes up again as a trunk, something keeps a space clear of roots.

The space is not large. A person could put an arm into it as far as the elbow. Its roof is the underside of the branch; its floor is pale stone covered with a thin, evenly combed layer of silt. Roots approaching it turn aside. Some have been bitten through. Others have been folded back while still tender and secured beneath small stones.

There are empty snail shells at the entrance, all with the same narrow opening worn through the whorl.

For much of the day, the inhabitant is the back wall.

Its skin has the color and loose, uneven surface of soaked bark. Small liverworts grow on it. A fern, no longer than a finger, occupies a crease above what will eventually prove to be a shoulder. Water passes over the creature and into the dark behind it without revealing where its body ends.

Then one of the shells moves.

A thin hand has come out from beneath the silt. It turns the shell, feels inside it with two joined fingers, and pushes it beyond the threshold. The hand stays there afterward, palm upward, collecting rainwater falling through the branch.

There are six fingers. They are not arranged in a row. Four surround the palm at unequal intervals, and two grow together at the end of a longer joint. The hand can close around a root from almost any direction. Along its inner surface are white ridges, blunt enough to grip skin without cutting it, hard enough to wear through a shell.

Another hand appears farther along the bank.

Between them, very slowly, the back wall unfolds.

***

On firm ground it would be an awkward animal.

Its body is long and shallow, wider at the front, with a heavy central portion suspended between folds of skin. There are three pairs of limbs. The front pair reach and examine; the middle pair do most of the pulling; the hind pair are short, broad, and frequently remain attached to something after the rest of the animal has moved away.

Across mud, these limbs spread its weight so widely that it leaves less of an impression than a wading bird. Across exposed stone, they become cautious. Each hand touches down, lifts, touches again. Sometimes the creature will retreat the length of a channel rather than cross a shelf no wider than its own body.

The head lies almost flat between the shoulders. Seen from above, it is a broad wedge with a ragged fringe. Seen from below, it has a soft mouth, two nostrils that close inward, and a row of little openings along the jaw through which water is continually drawn.

Its eyes are small and set far apart. A clear membrane passes over each one independently. In darkness they do not shine.

Most of its attention seems to be elsewhere.

There are patches beneath its throat and along its belly where the skin is smooth, pale, and extraordinarily thin. When it settles against a submerged root, these patches flatten to the wood. When it rests on peat, they fill the minute hollows between fibers. A disturbance in the buried stone brings a change to them before the exposed shelves show blue: a tightening, a flush of gray, then a line of tiny contractions traveling backward along the body.

The animal lifts its head.

Several breaths later, the droplets fall from the branches.

***

The one beneath the leaning grove is old.

Its left middle hand is missing two fingers, and the skin around the stump is smooth enough that nothing grows there. It has learned to catch with the right hand and brace with the left. This makes its movements slightly circular. Over many seasons, it has worn a curved approach to its shelter, a shallow groove that fills first whenever the water rises.

It feeds mostly at the boundaries between things.

Where warm water lies beneath cold, it stretches its fringe into the seam. Small animals caught there gather against the fine filaments and are passed toward the mouth. Where an algal veil meets a submerged branch, it scrapes a narrow strip clean. It opens snails patiently, turning them against the ridges in its palms. Occasionally it takes one of the small burrowers when a tunnel floods.

After such a meal it remains withdrawn for a day or more, with the jaw openings working hard.

It does not eat the rootless threads from the central lake. It pulls them out of its feeding fringe with great care and lets them go.

Its own covering is less easily removed. Liverworts have established themselves in pits in the skin. Their attachment is shallow, but the animal tolerates them, even moistens them when a dry wind reaches the basin. Tiny pale grazers move among the leaves. When the creature molts, which happens in separate patches over much of a season, it presses the loosened skin against a suitable bank and holds it there.

Some of these patches continue growing after it leaves.

Around the old animal’s shelter are small gardens carried there on its body. The fern above its shoulder has descendants on three neighboring roots.

***

It spends very little time wholly asleep.

One side relaxes while the other keeps contact with the ground. The hands exchange their holds. Water moves through the jaw. If the stone begins its deeper rushing, all six limbs tighten at once.

At such times, remaining attached is more important than being hidden.

The animal will abandon a meal, open shelter, even a place of relative warmth to find a living root with a firm connection to higher ground. It wraps its hind hands around it and lets the rest of its body trail loose.

There is a posture it adopts when the channels begin to lift: head down, limbs folded close, broad skin stretched into a shallow cup.

The first time it is seen clearly in this posture, it resembles something torn from the bank. Its planted back limbs are almost invisible. The cup fills with rising water. Silt streams past its belly. For a few minutes it hangs beneath the raised channel with the sedges, breathing in a space that had been solid water.

Other shapes sometimes hang farther along.

They are spaced unevenly, one beneath a root mass, two close together under a mat of grass, another so small that it could be a folded leaf. Their hands remain anchored in the bed below. Their bodies sway in the streams curving upward.

As the channel lowers, they flatten themselves against the mud. Water covers them without a splash.

***

The old animal is not solitary, although its nearest neighbor lives beyond the next wooded rise.

Visits take a long time.

A traveler arrives first as a change in the shelter’s water. The resident touches the floor with its throat, waits, then goes to the entrance. Sometimes it closes the opening with its body. Sometimes it withdraws and leaves a hand outside.

A visitor touches that hand.

They may remain joined there through an entire squall.

There is no visible exchange beyond small movements of the fingers and intermittent tightening along the pale undersides. Yet afterward the resident may travel directly to a newly fallen tree it could not have seen, or cease using a feeding place several channels away.

They do not agree readily about space. An unwelcome visitor is pushed backward, a little at a time, with all the deliberation of a root displacing stone. Injuries are uncommon. The white palm ridges leave marks that take weeks to fade.

The old animal admits one particular neighbor. This one is smaller, nearly free of plants, with a pale scar across its head. It enters without feeding at the threshold and always settles on the same side. Their bodies fit badly together. The smaller animal must fold one limb underneath itself, and the old one’s fern is pressed against the roof.

When the water cools, they move closer.

***

Their young develop beneath the loose folds along the adults’ flanks.

At first there is only a thickening there. Later, a line of tiny hands becomes visible, gripping from inside. An adult carrying young feeds often and avoids the central lake. It tests every resting place with its throat before allowing its belly to touch.

Birth occurs in the sheltered air beneath lifted water.

The young emerge pale and tightly folded. Each is about the size of a snail shell. For a while they cling to the adult’s fringe, opening their hands against the air. Then they are placed individually on roots or stiff sedge stems.

The adult stays anchored below them.

As the water returns to its bed, it covers the young gradually, first the feet, then the belly, then the openings along the jaw. Some unfold immediately. Others remain still until a parent touches them.

There are losses. A channel can lower too quickly. A root can slip. Occasionally the water fails to rise for long enough, and an adult carries the thickening along its flank until the little hands stop moving.

Those seasons leave the shelters unusually clean. The adults scrape and scrape at the floor.

***

The old one visits the lake of standing timber near the end of the wettest months.

It goes without its usual pauses to feed. At the shore it grips a root and lowers its head until both eyes are just beneath the surface.

When the deep rushing begins, shapes become visible among the loosened reflections.

Some have the broad, folded outline of its own kind. They move between reflected trunks where no trunks stand. One may approach the bank and lie opposite the waiting animal, close enough that the two heads appear to touch.

The old one opens its damaged hand.

Below it, sometimes, a hand opens with all six fingers.

The contact cannot be seen from above. Rain strikes the animal’s shoulders; water runs through its fringe. Its actual hand remains empty.

It may wait like this until dark.

On returning, it is slow and easily interrupted. Once, it spent a long time trying to enter its shelter through the root beside the opening. Another time it carried an empty shell halfway home, turned it repeatedly between its hands, and set it down in a patch of moving water.

Whatever happens at the lake is not harmless. The smallest animals are pulled back when they approach it. Visitors with new young are met on the outer channels and turned toward the wooded islands.

Still, the old one goes.

***

In the last light, its shelter looks abandoned again.

The silt is smooth. A few shells lie outside. Beneath the branch, the fern has opened a new frond so pale that it is almost transparent.

Far away, the stone begins to gather its noise.

Before the reeds move, before the blue alteration reaches the exposed bank, a hand emerges from the darkness. It searches along the root until it finds the place worn smooth by years of holding.

Then a smaller hand comes out beside it.

The old animal shifts, making room beneath the branch, and both tighten their grip.
