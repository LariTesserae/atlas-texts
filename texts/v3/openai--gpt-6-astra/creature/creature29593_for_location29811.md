The beaver has a pale notch in one ear, visible only when she comes out of the water. Swimming, she holds the damaged edge folded back, and there is little to distinguish her from the others except her size and the slight sideways drift of a willow branch carried in her teeth.

On land she is unexpectedly large. Her back rises into a heavy arch; her belly almost brushes the mud. She places her forefeet carefully and brings the broad hind feet forward in an awkward-looking sequence that works well enough on a slope of wet sticks. Her tail follows behind, dark and sparsely haired, with an old split near its edge.

She is not green, though almost everything around her is. In daylight her outer fur is brown, reddish toward the head, nearly black where it is wet. Beneath it lies a dense coat that the water does not readily reach. After feeding, she sits on a low bank and works through her fur with her hind foot, then rubs her face with both forepaws. This takes time. A beetle crosses the mud beside her. Water gathers under her tail.

She has lived in this part of the basin long enough for several of her old feeding places to be underwater.

One is still recognizable as a row of birch stumps along the bottom of a pool. She cut some of them when their roots stood on firm ground. Now she swims over them to reach the willow on the farther bank. Another place has disappeared under a collapsed shelf of limestone. For several evenings after the fall, she approached through the old channel, stopped where the pale stones filled it, and turned back.

Her knowledge of the basin is detailed but local. She knows the underside of a root that projects into a narrow passage. She rolls slightly to clear it, even when carrying nothing. She knows where a bank offers a low step out of the water, and where the same-looking bank crumbles beneath her forefeet. In darkness she can find the submerged entrance to her lodge without surfacing beside it.

She does not know what has happened to the channels beyond the neighboring family’s pond.

At their boundary, both families leave small heaps of mud and vegetation marked with scent. The heaps are renewed often. A stranger can pass through the basin, but not without being noticed everywhere it pauses.

Her own lodge stands against the roots of a living willow. From above, it has the loose appearance of flood debris caught in a tree. Underwater, the arrangement is more definite: an opening between heavy pieces of wood, a passage rising out of the pond, a chamber whose floor is above the waterline. The air inside is close with the smell of damp wood, bark, and beavers.

There are six of them this year.

Her mate is a little smaller, with thick fur that conceals the scar on one shoulder. Two young from the previous year still live with them. These leave long wakes, handle branches badly when excited, and sometimes work at a dam until one of the adults displaces them. The two new kits remain nearer the lodge. Their fur looks too ample for their bodies. In shallow water they float high, turn abruptly, and climb over one another to reach the same piece of willow.

Inside, they make soft, insistent sounds. From the bank, a person might hear them without understanding that the noise comes from beneath the mound.

The female feeds with a branch held between her forefeet. Her hands are small and dark, capable of turning a stem a little at a time as her incisors remove the bark. The exposed wood is cream-colored, marked by parallel cuts. She lets the stripped piece fall and takes another. In summer she also eats the softer growth that fills the shallows, reaching down for stems and coming up with wet leaves spread across her mouth.

The larger branches require a different effort. She braces, cuts, pauses, and cuts again. Sometimes the stem falls into open water. Sometimes it hangs in the neighboring willow, and she must pull at it from below until the leaves shake and the whole obstruction comes loose.

A cut tree is not always building material. Much of what looks like construction debris is the remainder of a meal.

When she does build, she works with what is close enough to move. A forked branch catches where a straight one would wash through. Mud packs into a gap; water finds the gap beside it. She comes back with more mud. Her forepaws disappear into the softness of the upstream bank, and she pushes the gathered load ahead of her toward the leak.

The dam does not become watertight. It becomes tight enough.

Behind it, the pond rises slowly, covering a shallow approach to the lodge and floating branches that had rested on the bottom. The change reaches places she has not visited that evening. A vole’s opening fills. Water spreads between the roots of a spruce. On the far side of the pond, a strip of moss lifts free of the ground and remains afloat.

She sleeps while this happens.

Others occupy the spaces her work keeps making. Small fish shelter among the dam’s submerged branches. Ducks rest in the slack water beside its upper face. Voles use the dense banks until the water rises, then move their clipped paths elsewhere. An otter occasionally enters the pond and passes straight through the family’s arrangements, searching beneath overhangs and roots. Its scent can keep the beavers uneasy long after it has gone.

None of these animals needs the basin to remain exactly as it is. Each needs some part of it to remain usable long enough.

In autumn, the family brings fresh branches toward the lodge and anchors them in a submerged accumulation. Green wood lies below the surface, within reach when ice closes the pond. The kits, larger now, follow the adults through the entrances. Above them, leaves settle into the dam’s wet face.

Winter reduces the visible settlement to mounds and low ridges. The spring-fed channels stay open in places, dark between white banks. Elsewhere the beavers swim beneath ice, following routes that cannot be seen from the rim.

Inside the lodge, their fur dries. They lie against one another on a floor littered with fine pieces of wood. Occasionally one rises, shifts past the others, and slips down into the entrance.

For a moment there is an empty place among the warm bodies.

Then the young press closer, and below them the swimmer feels her way into the cold, familiar passage.
