The bead reaches the first shell and stops.

For a while it holds its roundness against the broken edge. Then its surface draws out into two points. One explores the shell’s outer curve; the other enters the small darkness beneath it. The rest follows, becoming a transparent seam.

Several shells farther down the heap shift together.

What comes out is larger than the space they appeared to cover. At first you see only a line of pale amber, as though the wet edge of something has caught the sun. The line advances over the floor, bends around a grain of grit, and divides. Between its branches, a body becomes visible.

It is about the length of your forearm. Its back is low and finely creased, the color of the stone seen through shallow water. Along either side are folded limbs, so close together that you cannot immediately count them. Those touching the floor end in broad, soft pads. The pair nearest the front are narrower, with three little tips that meet precisely.

It pulls itself clear of the shells and rests.

Water covers the doorway now, but the animal stands in a dry patch. The edge of the patch follows it, drawing inward behind its last pair of feet. Dust lifted by its passage settles on exposed stone before the flood closes over it again.

The bead has disappeared. In the amber tissue just behind the creature’s front limbs, something round travels slowly backward.

Its head is difficult to find. At one end, beneath an overhanging fold, there are two dark pits and a short, vertical mouth. The pits remain directed toward the floor. Higher up, along the sides of its body, a dozen little areas of skin take on the color of whatever lies beside them. The one facing your hand darkens when you move.

The animal raises a front limb.

Its three tips open, wait, and close on nothing.

A moment later, it does this again.

You become aware of a smell like the white pith beneath citrus peel.

Outside the room, the clear animals on the nearest leaf turn back along its veins. The copper grazers resume their movement. Whatever warning held the hollow has ended, or changed into something they can live with.

The creature lowers its limb and climbs onto the bench.

Its feet make no sound. Each pad settles from the center outward, pressing the last water from between skin and stone. As it passes your hand, you feel a narrow band of coolness. The loosened air beneath the roofless walls has the same quality, spread thinner.

Up close, its back carries a scattering of marks. Some are little round scars. Others are crescents, or pairs of parallel strokes. There are also living things tucked into the creases: dark, spindle-shaped animals no larger than seeds. One comes halfway out, unfolds a fan of hairs, then retreats as the creature bends.

Near the middle of its back is a small bare oval. Nothing lives there. The skin is thickened and faintly polished.

It stops beside your hand without touching it.

The nearest patches along its flank brighten and dim. Its front limbs make a slow examination of the bench, finding the place where your palm has left salts in the stone’s minute pores. It spends a long time there.

Then it goes down to the shells.

***

There are several of its kind in the hollow, although they seldom show themselves together.

The smallest live beneath broad leaves. Seen from below, one resembles a thickening of the central vein, with its limbs extended into the smaller veins on either side. The clear, jointed leaf animals walk across it. Occasionally one pauses at a fold in its skin and picks something out.

The young animal permits this until a picker approaches the bare oval on its back. Then a limb unfolds and gently transfers the picker to another part of the leaf.

Larger ones occupy the spaces under roots and stone shelves. Their bodies can spread very thin. A creature as broad as two hands may lie between a root and the rock beneath it, its entire thickness reduced to a pale line. When the water rises, that line swells. Limbs disengage from one another. The animal lowers itself into the basin in a shape you would not have thought could fit beneath the root.

They travel slowly in the open. Underwater, they make progress by drawing the edge of the body forward and letting the rest catch up. Fine sediment rises behind them in paired curls.

In strong light their skin admits too much of the world. You see a pebble through a flank, then lose it behind an organ the color of weak tea. Sometimes the clearest thing about an animal is the dark, narrow gut running through its middle. After feeding, the gut holds a row of pale fragments.

They eat the hinged creatures that grow beneath the stone shelves.

These are difficult prey for anything with a blunt mouth. Their shells match the mineral floor, and their exposed margins are sealed so closely that a grazer can pass over one without finding an edge. The pale animals locate them with the tips of their front limbs. They explore the stone in short, exact strokes, returning several times to places that seem no different from the rest.

When one finds a shell, it settles over it.

The dry patch forms underneath its body. Water withdraws from the shell’s rim. The animal waits.

Eventually the shell opens a fraction.

One of the three-tipped limbs enters.

The hard click from the room is the sound of a hinge being parted. The animal does this with very little force, having first touched a particular place inside. It eats slowly. The soft mouth can extend farther than its head suggests, reaching into chambers too narrow for a limb.

It cleans both halves afterward.

Most shells are abandoned where they were opened. A few are carried to the room, held under the body by the middle limbs. The animal may reject one halfway there, setting it upright in a crack. Others it brings through the narrow doorway and lowers into the square hollow.

There is no obvious rule to the choice. Perfect shells lie discarded outside. Broken, misshapen ones are carried home.

If you watch for long enough, you notice that the chosen shells tend to have something unusual about the hinge.

***

The individual on the bench takes a shell from near the top of the heap.

It holds it with four limbs and passes the remaining tips over the interior. At a damaged place beside the hinge, it stops. Its mouth opens slightly. A thin amber film appears between the two nearest limbs.

Into this film it presses the shell.

For several minutes it stays still. Small pulses move through the soft tissues along its back. The seed-shaped lodgers withdraw completely.

When the shell comes away, the amber film has hardened into a shallow plate. The animal turns this plate over, examines its edge, and puts it into its mouth.

It does not swallow.

Its jaw works once. There is a click.

The creature sets the shell down and chooses another.

Near the bottom of the hollow, the shells are cemented together. Some have become pale lumps with only a hinge or a point remaining. The animal reaches into narrow gaps among these oldest pieces. Its limb tips emerge powdered white.

The room has been used by bodies shaped much like this one for a very long time. That much is easy to believe. Harder to understand is why an animal opening a shell taken from today’s water should sometimes stop, cross the room, and feel for a particular damaged shape deep in the stone.

The one beside you does so three times before it finishes eating.

***

They gather under the rain roof when the water begins to withdraw.

Often there are only two. Once there are nine, including a small one carried beneath the body of an adult. They arrange themselves along the unequal supports, leaving spaces through which the climbing animals continue to pass.

As the roof releases its stored rain, they lift their front ends into the drops.

The thickened ovals on their backs soften. A clear covering separates from each oval, bulges, and rises. For a little while every animal bears a fragile dome.

Under the domes are tightly folded structures, dark amber and densely ridged.

The creatures turn toward one another. Their domes touch.

You can see a slight movement through the joined surfaces, like the passage of breath between two small bladders. One animal stiffens its limbs. Another lowers itself almost flat. The youngest turns away, then is drawn back by a front foot resting against its side.

The contact lasts until the roof’s dripping thins.

Afterward they separate. The domes collapse without bursting and are worked back into the skin by slow contractions of the flanks.

For the rest of that day, they feed poorly. One repeatedly touches a shell it has already emptied. Another goes to the room and remains with its limbs spread over the heap until nightfall.

The small one, which has never entered the room before, walks there directly.

***

They are not responsible for everything that happens in the hollow.

When the deep movement comes beneath the bank, they stop with the grazers and the worms. Their bodies flatten. Their limb tips seek firm stone.

Sometimes they climb before the water rises. Sometimes they remain where they are and are lifted by it.

A gray pool animal can step over one without recognition. The pale creature draws its limbs inward and waits beneath that broad belly, among the procession of young. Once it reaches up and removes a closed shell caught in a fold beside the gray animal’s head. It keeps the shell. The gray animal goes on.

They cross the ring reluctantly.

At its circumference, the front of a body vanishes while the rear remains visible. The exposed folds tighten. For a moment the animal seems to have been shortened, its feet continuing their patient movements on either side of an absence.

It emerges with its skin flushed amber and rests on the warm runoff.

The little passengers in its creases come out to feed on the black sand.

***

By evening, the animal in the room has become accustomed to your hand.

It no longer pauses when you move your fingers. Once, while carrying a shell, it places a foot against your smallest finger and uses it as a support. The pad is cool, dry, and unexpectedly firm.

The shell it carries is old. A delicate point projects from one end; the matching point has broken away.

It brings this shell onto the bench and sets it beside the mark left by your palm. Then it retreats far enough to watch.

You do not touch the shell at first.

Its front limb opens and closes.

Outside, the flowers lie on the rising water. Something large passes beneath them, lifting three reflections together. The pale creature waits while the room fills almost to the bench.

When you finally turn the shell over, one of its limb tips comes forward and rests against the hinge.

It holds there, allowing your finger to meet it.

Beneath the two of you, in the square hollow, the older shells are beginning to move.
