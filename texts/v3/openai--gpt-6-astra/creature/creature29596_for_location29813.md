The residents keep their outdoor shoes beside the stove, soles turned toward it. In the morning they put them on at the last possible moment. Children are taught to do up their coats first.

Most of the people who live here have the ordinary number of hands. They grow old unevenly, complain about their teeth, and have opinions about which bakery has begun using inferior flour. Their clothes are heavy at the cuffs and hems, where small weights help them hang correctly. A visitor might take these for a local fashion until a woman removes her coat and the unweighted lining continues to follow her across the room.

There are just under two thousand residents. This is the figure used for flour orders, school funding, and representation on the regional council. The fire service uses a larger number.

It is possible to be counted in one building and rescued from another.

The oldest inhabitants came with the foundations. Some were engineers, but most had no specialized knowledge of what was being done. They were offered apartments, wages, and the eventual ownership of the ground beneath their homes. The contract described the ground in the future tense. Several still keep their copies.

One of these first residents runs a shoe-repair counter behind the laundry. She was nineteen when she arrived. Now her wrists are swollen and she works with her elbows close to her sides. She remembers the journey, a bad meal at a transfer station, and the man who helped carry her trunk. She does not remember the town without stone around it, although her earliest wage slips include an allowance for working above an unsupported depth.

She dislikes being asked about this. People always want the part she cannot remember.

She would rather tell you about the first winter, when the communal dining room served cabbage for forty-three consecutive evenings, or the year she left her husband and discovered that he had been claiming the household heat rebate for both of them.

Her husband still lives three streets away. The separation has held better than either street.

***

There are children everywhere once school lets out, although they seldom play in the square. The ventilation housings are too cold to climb, and a ball that rolls behind them can come back with a different amount of wear.

They prefer the roofed passages. Here they play games with elaborate territorial rules, claiming a stretch of drainpipe, a bridge overhead, or the last stair before a landing. A child may own the sound made by stepping on a particular paving slab. Another owns the right to look through a knothole in a boarded door.

Adults tolerate this. A disputed corner occupied every afternoon by six quarrelling children is less likely to disappear.

The children are not remarkably solemn or wise. They cheat. They scare younger ones by telling them the gray animal can get into their sleeves. They dare each other to press coins into the soft municipal patches, then deny knowing why a warning sign has acquired a face.

At school they learn arithmetic, handwriting, and the distinction between a shortcut and a passage that has become shorter.

A shortcut still contains everything along its way.

They are taught to turn back if they notice that something has been left out: the laundry smell, a familiar door, the place where their footsteps ought to echo. If turning back does not restore it, they sit down and wait for someone who knows them.

One boy has learned to exploit this rule. When he has not done his schoolwork, he sits in the alley behind his house and says the bakery was missing. His mother usually catches him because he chooses a dry place.

The older children want to leave. They want broad roads, rain that comes from clouds everyone can see, rooms in which they can shut the door without being called back to the kitchen. They exchange photographs of forests. Some think the photographs have been altered: there is too much space between the trunks, and nothing appears to be holding it there.

***

The most useful people are those who notice a small change without immediately accommodating it.

An elderly man on the south side knows the distance from his bed to the lavatory by the pull in his hip. He has reported three structural developments before the surveyors detected them. A courier remembers how every entrance smells. When a stairwell was reassigned to another building, she continued delivering to its original occupants for four days because the boiled-onion smell remained.

Neither has been persuaded to work for the municipality.

The courier says the pension is poor. The old man says he is already doing enough by getting out of bed.

Officially, the surveyors are the guardians of continuity. Unofficially, continuity depends on a much larger workforce: people who insist a cupboard used to be deeper, who keep a place laid at dinner, who return to a closed shop to see whether it has reopened.

This work is exhausting when done deliberately. The town has a few enthusiasts who record every measurement of their homes. Their apartments are crowded with numbered strings. They are difficult neighbors. A woman who monitored her bedroom for eleven years eventually obtained compensation for a loss of volume, then spent most of the money on a holiday somewhere warm.

She came back looking younger and threw away the strings.

Her bedroom has remained approximately the same size.

***

In the kitchens, the residents are easier to know.

There is a mechanic who sings while peeling potatoes and never while repairing anything. There is a nurse who eats standing up, despite owning four chairs. Above the eastern arcade, two brothers have not spoken for eighteen months but still eat from the same pan, passing it without looking at each other. Their kitchen is among the warmest in the district.

Affection helps, but repetition seems to matter more.

A reliable room accumulates little accommodations. The table has a pale crescent where a particular wrist rests. A curtain is tied back with the same piece of blue cord every morning. Cups are returned to their hooks even when their owners are too tired to wash them.

Visitors are often given a task. Cut the bread. Hold this end of the cloth. Put another spoon out.

It is a courteous way of making them easier to find.

People who remain entirely guests—sleeping in borrowed beds, eating whatever is set before them, never moving an object for their own convenience—sometimes have trouble locating the same apartment twice. They are advised to buy something cheap and leave it there. A toothbrush generally works.

Love affairs leave a confusing distribution of anchors.

The local magistrate has ruled that recovering one’s possessions does not entitle one to enter an apartment indefinitely, however often its door presents itself.

***

There are other inhabitants whose tenancy is less clear.

The long gray animal has a nest, or something the maintenance crews treat as a nest. It occupies a junction where three service passages ought to meet but only two can be entered. Brittle contact residue is pressed into a shallow bowl. Among it are threads, a brass washer, and a milk tooth.

Nobody admits to supplying the tooth.

When resting, the animal looks quite ordinary. Its flank rises beneath short fur. Its ears twitch at running water. It has a scar near one eye, which is one reason the workers think there may be only one of it.

The scar is not always on the same side.

One electrician leaves a small heap of scrap for it at the end of her shift. She says it recognizes her. Her colleagues say it recognizes the sound of the tin. Both statements may be true. When she was away with pneumonia, the animal waited by her locker, its forefeet settled and its hindquarters still making the last few steps toward her.

She was angry when they told her. They had left it there for hours instead of bringing the tin.

Smaller things live in the moss trays. Under a lens they resemble translucent staples, each with a cloudy bead inside. They move only while covered. The baker discovers their progress by lifting a tile, replacing it, and lifting it again later.

They eat a fine white growth that forms on the cold side of the moss. Nobody knows what the white growth eats.

In spring, or during the weeks designated as spring for commercial purposes, the staples gather along the tray rims. Some unfold. For a day or two, the baker finds tiny clear wings on the cooling equipment.

No one has seen anything flying.

***

There are people who were born here and people who say they were born here.

The distinction seldom matters until they need documents.

A few adults have childhood memories for which the town provides no support: a swimming bath, a long sloping street, a cinema with a red illuminated clock. Their accounts agree in incidental ways. The clock was always seven minutes fast. The baths sold orange sweets that stained the fingers.

These residents are not shunned. One manages the cooperative accounts. Another is the caretaker of the pool.

He does not discuss what he sees under the ice. He scrapes it early, before school, and keeps the cart covered on his way to the disposal shed. He is especially careful with pieces that contain daylight.

Once a month he buys a packet of orange sweets from the general shop. They are the wrong kind, he says, but the shopkeeper has stopped trying to find another supplier. He buys them anyway.

His daughter was born in the district’s small maternity room. Her records are complete. She has no interest in the swimming bath and is embarrassed when her father asks visitors whether they have heard of the cinema.

She wants to become a surveyor. She likes the blunt pencils and the authority to stop traffic.

***

The dead require care, though not much more than elsewhere.

There is no cemetery in the new stone. Bodies are transported beyond the district, with an escort and a written list of the places where the deceased lived. For several weeks afterward, someone checks those places each day.

Usually nothing happens.

Occasionally a closed room stays warm. A door handle acquires the discoloration of a recent hand. These occurrences are recorded without being described as visits.

The bereaved do not always welcome them. A man whose wife died in autumn complained all winter that her side of the bed would not cool. He slept badly, pushed to the edge by a warmth that gave way under his hand.

In February it was gone.

He complained about that too.

There is no established ceremony for such losses. Neighbors bring food, wash curtains, sit longer than they intended. The town’s priest is good at practical tasks and does not insist on an interpretation. She has a collection of spare hot-water bottles. She labels them because people forget to return them.

***

What sustains the district is not universal devotion to it. Some residents hate the place. Some are saving to leave. A few remain only because their elderly parents will not go, or because no employer elsewhere recognizes their qualifications.

They still know which steps must be taken together. They still tell a stranger not to lean against that wall.

At closing time, the baker comes out to brush flour from his apron. He checks the trays under the glazed passage, puts back a tile, and tests the guard around the shrub with his foot. Upstairs, somebody argues over the telephone. A child is sent down with money and arrives after the shutters have been lowered.

The baker raises one halfway.

Later, when the kitchens have settled into their small territories of warmth, the service passages become busy. The gray animal moves under the floors. Water gathers where it can. Behind a valve packed with moss, something unfolds and presses for a while against the metal.

In an apartment near the uncertain northern corner, a woman wakes thirsty. She listens before getting up, as she has done since childhood. Through the wall comes the slow ticking of the foundations.

She reaches across her husband for the glass on his bedside table. He wakes just enough to complain.

The water is very cold. She leaves a little for him.
