The animal at the opening is mostly a mouth, but only while it is feeding.

Withdrawn, it becomes a wet knot at the bottom of its tube. Its crown folds inward, each filament lying between two others, and the whole delicate apparatus passes through an opening scarcely wider than the pale thread of its gut. A muscular collar closes above it. For a while there is nothing to distinguish an occupied house from one whose owner died long ago.

Then the collar loosens.

The first thing to emerge is not the crown but a clear, blunt projection that rests against the rim. It touches a new grain caught there, withdraws, touches it again. The grain is too large to swallow. Over the next several hours the animal works it sideways, coating it, turning it until its broadest surface faces the water. Eventually it becomes part of the house.

The crown opens behind it.

There are sixteen filaments on this animal. One is shorter than the others, ending in a thickened bead where something bit it. Along their inward faces, hairs beat too finely to be seen in the blue light. They draw food toward a central groove: broken cells, a fleck of fat, a particle that has already passed through another gut. Unsuitable things travel outward along a different track. The animal handles a great deal more than it eats.

Its body is almost without color. What looks brown inside it is a meal moving slowly through a bend.

It has no view of the shell. Pressure reaches it through the crown and the wall of its tube. Dissolved substances arrive from different directions. A neighbor withdrawing makes a small tug in the water; a worm rubbing past makes a longer disturbance, conducted through the lime. The animal can distinguish enough of these events to remain alive. When a large shadow falls outside, it responds to the change in light, but it also responds to the first few neighbors closing. Sometimes a withdrawal begins where there has been no shadow at all.

Its house leans outward because the water is better there. Each extension has been made by a body reaching as far as it safely can.

Farther in, several of its kind lean the other way.

Their crowns are smaller, though their tubes are old. The passage that once supplied them has narrowed behind a swelling colony of cups, and now their food comes through a crack below. Their newest rims turn downward toward it. One tube has bent so far that its occupant feeds beside its own foundation. Another has reached a dead end against a translucent wall. Its owner continues to test the obstruction, pressing the soft lip of its body against a surface it cannot dissolve.

The cups belong to animals joined beneath their visible parts.

Each has a separate mouth and a ring of fine arms, but the bases communicate through a thin layer of living tissue. Food taken at one opening can sustain growth several chambers away. Along a well-fed edge, new cups rise close together. In a starved recess, the colony keeps only a film against the shell, so thin that the ribs beneath remain visible through it.

This film is easily mistaken for the surface on which the animals live.

A grazing creature removes a little of it. The wound contracts around the scraped place. Two cups nearby close, while a third continues feeding. By the next dim interval the gap has acquired a smooth margin, and a bud is thickening beside it. The replacement will face a different direction. There is more water on that side now.

Among the tubes moves a creature with a body like a curled shaving.

Its back is divided into overlapping plates, but the plates are transparent and its organs interrupt their regularity. A dark stomach sits behind its head. Farther back, several oval eggs are held under the body between thin, moving plates. Its legs keep water passing over them even when it stands still.

It cannot turn around in most of the passages. Instead it reverses, feeling behind itself with its hindmost limbs. In wider places it rolls partly onto one side and walks along a wall. What appeared to be its back becomes its outer edge; its antennae continue searching the same seam.

One antenna is missing its tip. The remaining length has grown a small brush of sensitive hairs around the injury.

It feeds wherever something has softened: on the skin of an old filament, on mucus stretched between cups, on a body that has failed to close its door. Its front limbs bring fragments to the mouth in quick, economical movements. Only here, at the jaws, does it look hurried.

When a crown touches its flank, it pauses. When several crowns touch it together, it pushes through.

The eggs beneath it are not all at the same stage. Some are still smooth and cloudy. In others, the folded outlines of legs have appeared. One young animal is already moving inside its envelope, turning without gaining room. Its mother stops under a ledge and cleans the brood with a hooked limb. She pulls away a strand of fungus, holds it briefly at her mouth, and eats it.

Below the salt boundary live animals she does not follow.

They are broad for their height, with soft backs drawn close to the ground and feet spread beneath them. Around each foot is a narrow clear margin that maintains contact with the uneven terrace. They advance by releasing a small portion of this margin, extending it, and fastening it again. The rest of the body follows so gradually that the movement is easiest to notice by the clean patch left behind.

Their mouths work against the mineral surface.

What they graze is scarcely a layer: bacteria, trapped organic matter, the beginnings of films that would otherwise bind the grains more firmly. Behind them the terrace is paler and slightly roughened. Beside them, eggs lie in adhesive patches. Some eggs have accumulated so much dust that they resemble the surrounding deposit, except that each has a small clear place where the developing animal presses against its covering.

A juvenile crosses the wavering boundary above the basin. Its foot narrows. It draws its feelers close, remains there for a while, and retreats. Higher up there is more food, but its body does not work properly in that water.

One of the adults has been covered along its rear edge by new mineral growth. It can still feed. Its foot pulls against the buried portion whenever it advances, stretching the body into a low arch. Eventually a strip of tissue tears away. The animal moves on with an uneven margin, leaving a pale scrap in the terrace.

By the time that scrap is gone, something else is feeding in the hollow.

The predator in the walls is difficult to see even when it moves.

It is a flattened worm, wider at the head, with two dark points set far apart. Its gut branches into its body, and after a meal those branches make it briefly conspicuous. Before feeding, it looks like a piece of clear membrane peeled loose from a cup.

It travels pressed against surfaces. At a narrow joint it becomes narrower still, drawing itself through until its head can spread on the other side. The head explores without the body following. A small crustacean passes within reach, but is too heavily plated. The worm folds back.

Later, a newly emerged juvenile comes down the same joint.

The worm covers it. There is a brief movement under the flattened body, then a slower one. A feeding tube extends from the worm’s underside. The juvenile’s limbs remain visible around its edge.

Nearby, another juvenile continues scraping a wall.

Most deaths here are found by touch. A familiar obstruction no longer resists. A closed collar loosens and fails to tighten again. Water entering an old tube acquires a different taste. Small mouths gather, and the soft occupant dwindles while its dwelling remains the same size.

The oldest houses contain several kinds of life that their builders never encountered. There are single cells in the pores, worms in the basal hollows, young colonies spread along the inside of abandoned tubes. Some feed on the material binding the walls together. Their work weakens the house from within, making a sheltered place more habitable until it folds.

There are also sleepers.

They lie in sealed cells behind several generations of growth, reduced to dense, rounded bodies. Their crowns are gone. Their guts are nearly empty. A small reserve remains folded into them, enough to begin again if fresh water carrying food reaches their walls. Some will open when a fracture exposes them. Others will be buried more deeply, still alive, until the reserve is exhausted.

Near the opening, a larva turns slowly in the water.

It is smaller than a grain of the milky deposit and has no house. A band of hairs carries it upward until it meets the upper valve. It runs along the surface, touching with its front end. Here the film is too thick. Here another animal’s secretions make it withdraw. Here there is a clean edge, but the current pulls hard enough to lift its body before it can attach.

It circles back among the crowns.

For a time it catches in the little eddy behind the animal with the bitten filament. It could settle there. The water has already been strained, but some food remains, and the tube would shelter its first soft growth. Its front end rests against the wall. The hairs continue beating.

Then the ice presses.

The crowns bend together. The larva lifts from the tube and is carried through the clear lip, out of the shell. It enters water with no wall close enough to touch. Its hairs beat against a current that takes it farther in a moment than it has traveled in its whole life.

Inside, the animal at the opening has withdrawn. Water slips through the space above its collar. A loose particle turns twice and lodges against the unfinished rim.

When the crown opens again, the blunt projection finds it there.
