The people who live here can tell whether a machine has stopped by touching a wall.

This is not a gift everyone possesses equally. Children often claim to feel things they cannot, and people who work in the weaving sheds lose the finer distinctions after years of standing over the looms. But a mechanic arriving home will sometimes pause with a hand against the doorjamb, take off neither coat nor boots, and go back out. Their supper is covered. No one follows to ask why.

They are not especially solemn people. Seen from the far side of a workshop, their conversations can look ceremonial: a raised board, a hand held flat, two fingers turned downward. Close by, the exchange may concern a badly fitted bearing, a lost lunch, or someone who has once again borrowed a tool and returned a different, inferior tool in its place.

Their jokes are frequently about particular objects. A cracked blue basin has been funny in one family for nearly forty years. Visitors are told the story, but it seldom helps.

Clothes are layered, close at the wrists, loose enough across the back for work. The outer fabric is usually dark, though cuffs and pocket linings may be yellow or rose. The weaving works sells short lengths cheaply when changing colors, and people who appear almost indistinguishable in the street become unexpectedly bright when they sit down and unbutton their coats.

Most carry a little square of dry cloth inside the clothing, against the chest. It is used for spectacles, lamp contacts, the inside of a watch, a child’s face. Keeping one thing reliably dry takes forethought.

There are people who have never approached the western edge. There are others who eat their midday meal there, seated a few paces back with their feet toward town. Neither group thinks well of the other’s habits.

The collectors employ a woman named Eda who goes to the precipice on the first free afternoon of every month. She brings nothing to lower into it. She lies on her stomach and looks over until her elbows ache.

At work she is a supervisor, impatient with speculation. She can estimate a rack’s yield from the pattern of tarnish along its lowest plate. She is trying to persuade the council to replace three rows of iron plates with thinner ones of a more expensive alloy. She has measurements, samples, and a growing dislike of the phrase *adequate for the present*.

At the edge she watches cloud.

Her younger brother once asked what she expected to see.

“The side,” she said.

She meant the side of the plateau: the stone beneath the lip, farther down than weather usually allowed her to look. She wanted to know whether the pale seams continued. Her brother, who makes glass, has since made her a small angled mirror on a jointed rod. It has shown her wet rock, a fern growing upside down, and, on one occasion, a snail.

She keeps going.

***

Children here learn the town from below.

They know the spaces under stairs where adults have to stoop, the passages left behind when one house is built against another, the hot end and the tolerable end of a factory vent. They know which cellars contain gardens and which merely contain people’s intention to make a garden.

They are forbidden to enter the water galleries. Consequently, their accounts of the galleries are elaborate and largely wrong.

In school they are taught the lamp signals, how to close a household water valve, and how to distinguish a living cable from an abandoned one. They also learn songs which sound unsatisfactory outdoors and very fine in the vaulted room beneath the school. Once a week, the desks are pushed back and everyone goes downstairs to sing.

Their boats for the returning streams are more sophisticated than scraps of wood suggest. Some have thin stone ballast. Some are folded from discarded insulation cloth and waterproofed with gutter wax. A child may spend weeks preparing a vessel for a flow that does not arrive.

There are races, but the streams do not keep to a dependable course through every crossing, and a boat often disappears under a doorstep to emerge much later in another street. Ownership is painted on the underside.

One boy, Senn, puts roofs on all his boats. He says the passengers must get tired of being looked at.

His grandmother saves him the narrow wooden strips used to separate new panes of glass.

She used to operate the largest pump beneath the eastern nursery. Now her fingers will not straighten, and other people lift her kettles. She resents this most on days when she has already decided not to make tea.

She tells Senn that the returning water was once warmer. The engineers’ records do not support her. She says the engineers did not put their bare feet in it.

***

There is considerable life in the houses that is not entered in any household count.

The pale snails beneath the collectors lay their eggs in the screw recesses of the trough brackets. Workers clean around them when they can. A clogged bracket must still be cleared; affection does not make it drain.

Through a young snail’s shell, its last meal can be seen as a greenish thread. Older shells grow cloudy at the tip. The snails move from the undersides of the troughs onto the metal plates shortly before condensation begins, and for this reason some workers regard them as predictors. Others point out that they occasionally do this on dry evenings and remain there, visibly inconvenienced.

The narrow animal beneath the bakery is about the length of a forearm, with a tail that looks flattened until it is alarmed. Its fur is smoke-brown above and cream beneath. The forefeet have five long toes capable of closing around a pipe.

It does not eat much bread. It eats the soft white creatures that eat flour, and beetles, and the fleshy bases of certain fungi. Given the opportunity, it also steals grease.

The baker calls it a thief while leaving the grease pot where it can reach it. His assistant moves the pot to a high shelf. The animal has begun descending to it along the wall, leaving paired streaks through the flour dust.

There are at least three of these animals. The baker insists there is one. Three would require a decision.

They bear their young in woven masses of hair, root fiber, and stolen packing thread. The young are blunt-faced and nearly naked, with disproportionately complete hands. Before their eyes open, they can hang from a finger.

Few people have seen this. A duct cleaner once brought a nest into daylight by mistake and stood holding it while the mother moved back and forth inside the opened wall. He replaced it, repaired the duct around it, and charged for the extra hour.

***

The gardens belong to households, work crews, associations, and people whose claims would not survive close examination.

Disputes about light are common. A branch may be old enough to have shaded a window before the present building existed, but this does not comfort the person whose beans will not set. Arguments are conducted with diagrams, witnesses, and pruning hooks laid conspicuously aside.

A gardener named Olin tends eleven cellar plots, four of them officially. He grows thick-stemmed greens, sour little tubers, and a pale climbing fruit that bruises if carried in a pocket. He knows which beds need the used cooling water and which must never receive it. In winter he sleeps in a former stairwell behind the glasshouse because his room is too far from the vents.

He is not poor in the same ways all year.

During the cold months, people bring him things to mend, bottles, lamp oil, news. When the terraces warm, he sells fruit and becomes difficult about prices. He has a daughter in the cable works who visits every sixth day and opens all the windows.

Among his beds live small, broad-backed insects the color of wet stone. They draw cut leaves into cracks by walking backward. A leaf may resist for several minutes, trembling at its stem, before abruptly sliding out of sight.

Olin places vegetable peelings beside these cracks. He dislikes being thanked for his clean paths.

***

The bird has a small head, dark feet held close beneath the tail, and wings so long that its turns seem delayed.

Those who have seen it near the western houses describe a rough white neck and a bill with a chipped-looking ridge. Other witnesses report neither. There is some evidence that more than one bird visits, although people prefer to recognize it.

It occasionally lands on a collector frame. Then it becomes unexpectedly awkward, lifting its wings to balance, inspecting its footing before taking another step. Once it remained for most of a morning, fouling six plates and preventing maintenance on a seventh.

People came from three streets away.

Eda made them stand clear of the ladders.

When it departed, she cleaned the plates herself. She kept a feather in a drawer until tiny insects reduced it to a shaft.

***

Those who attend to the old water are not all engineers.

An elderly man carries a folding stool to the main street whenever the stone begins to darken. He sits with his shoes on and watches whatever goes past. Years ago his wife lost a silver fastening in one of the flows. He does not expect to recover it, but he mentions it when asked why he is there.

Two sisters collect the white sand and keep it in medicine bottles. Their collection would scarcely fill a spoon. Under magnification, some grains are pitted and some are very smooth. One contains a dark speck that changes position when the bottle is turned, though that may be a loose particle behind it.

The engineers have borrowed this grain twice.

In the grooves beyond the last gardens, a fine, colorless growth sometimes appears after the water withdraws. It lies flat against the rock and is easily mistaken for a drying film. Touch it with a grass stem and it gathers slowly around the point.

No one has found it before a flow. No one has kept it alive in a dish.

Olin says there is no reason a dish should suit it.

A young engineer has begun leaving numbered slips of glass in the channels. Most come back clean. Two have vanished. On a third, retrieved after an autumn flow, she found a branching mark too shallow to catch a fingernail.

She keeps it on her desk beside several pieces of ordinary scratched glass. When visitors ask to see it, she makes them choose.

***

At night, the town’s inhabitants arrange themselves around the heat.

Late-shift workers eat in kitchens where early-shift workers are already asleep behind curtains. The duct animals unfold and begin their rounds. Snails cross the dark undersides of the racks. Roots press into warm mortar, and the trees hold their leaves still.

Senn’s grandmother wakes when the nursery pump changes speed. She listens for a while, though it has not been hers to attend to for twelve years.

Sometimes she hears the new operator correct it.

Sometimes the uneven rhythm continues, and she gets up, puts a coat over her nightclothes, and turns on the lamp above her eastern window. There is a switch by the frame for making it flash.

Across the hollow, after a minute or two, another lamp answers.
