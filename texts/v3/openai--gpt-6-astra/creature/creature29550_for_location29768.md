The people who tend the pumps can be recognized by the dry place just below each shoulder. They wear pads there, under their coats, for resting against pipes while they work with both hands. Everywhere else they are wet. The hems of their clothes grow stiff with mineral deposits and are cut away a little at a time. An old coat is often short, with a new strip of cloth sewn around the bottom and pockets that have been moved several times.

They carry few loose objects. A dropped nut may be found where it fell, or beneath the surface that received it, or a little farther along the particular distance that belongs to nuts. Tools are tied to the wrist with braided cord. Each worker has a small fork used to clear animals from a thread before a bolt is tightened.

There is no formal way to announce yourself at a pump house. You take hold of the outer handrail and tap twice with something you are carrying. Someone inside rests a tool against the same rail. The two contacts establish, among other things, whether you have reached the same building.

Occasionally you have not.

In that case the person inside may ask you to stay where you are while they come around.

***

Most workers live within reach of a service line, but a dwelling need not have a door onto it. There are rooms above tanks, rooms in the spaces between paired sheds, rooms reached by descending a ladder that has become longer than the building is deep. The ladders are inspected regularly. Their extra length is no reason to condemn them.

A home is distinguished from a workplace by what has been allowed to remain underfoot.

Workshop floors are swept with stiff rubber blades. At home there may be a patch of woven matting, a hollow worn by heels beneath a chair, a child's collection of smooth things in a shallow tray. Nobody moves the tray without asking. Some of the things have been brought a considerable distance at considerable trouble and now lie comfortably together.

Beds are narrow and suspended. Their cords pass through cups of oil to discourage the worms. Before getting in, a person runs a hand along the underside and lifts off anything that has settled there. Most of these animals can be put on the wall. One kind must be carried outside: a soft, flat creature that contracts around the fingers and leaves them unable to distinguish wet from dry for several hours.

Children learn that one by its smell.

They learn the others by having them put into their hands.

The first lessons are distinctions of pressure: something gripping, something resting, something caught. A child who mistakes a trapped animal for an animal holding on will pull when they should loosen. This is corrected patiently. There is much here that can be injured without making any sound.

***

The smallest inhabitants of a house are not kept out. They live around the seams of drinking tanks and in the coarse stitching of boots. Their occupation is useful up to a point. A healthy seam has a fine, even nap of bodies. If they gather into a knot, there is a leak beginning. If they abandon it altogether, someone fetches a bucket before trying the tap.

There are also animals that live in the warmth left by a meal.

They arrive slowly, from beneath the table or along the arms of a chair, and spread themselves under the tin. Each has several thin limbs surrounding a soft central weight. While the food is hot they remain at a distance. Later they draw close enough to touch the metal. They are brushed away before the tin is washed, with the same absent care used to clear crumbs.

One household may have a large one that has come to every supper for years. It will have a name, though the smaller ones will not.

Pets, where people keep them deliberately, are usually channel animals taken young. They grow to the breadth of a stool and learn the arrangement of a room by resting their raised knees against the furniture. They cannot be taught to fetch. An object released into their care is carefully put into water.

They can, however, learn to find a familiar person by the way that person stands.

A visitor may feel a knee come gently against the back of each ankle, followed by a broad body settling across both feet. The animal is comparing them with somebody expected home.

***

The herds have attendants too, though not owners.

These people travel lightly. They wear felt bindings rather than boots and carry poles with wide, perforated ends. A pole laid flat on the water can be moved beneath the feeding film without breaking it. With these the attendants open narrow lanes through crowded basins, separating animals that have settled too closely to permit the smaller ones to leave.

A herd contains many sizes, but it is difficult to tell which are young.

Some of the smallest have the thick, folded mouth edges of long use. Some enormous animals are still soft at the joints and become distressed when they lose contact with a neighbor. The attendants know relationships that cannot be inferred from size. They will move three large bodies to make space beside a small one.

Births occur beneath the water.

What first emerges is a fold of back, then a broad foot laid very carefully against the ground. The mother remains around the new animal for a time. It is not immediately possible to say where her body ends. The attendant kneels beside them and keeps the water still with the flat of a hand.

When the bodies separate, the place between them is unusually deep.

For several days the new animal returns to this depth whenever the herd is disturbed. It can fit there after it has become too large to fit anywhere comparable. Its mother waits nearby, feeding in short passes.

The attendants do not hurry it out.

They have a particular respect for the empty impressions left by herds. Before crossing an abandoned basin, they lower a pole into each hollow along their route. Most return only the ordinary resistance of water and mud. A few close gently on the pole.

Those are given room.

***

There are people whose work concerns the birds.

A sleeping flock can bring down a neglected cable. The birds do not weigh much individually, but they press toward warmth, and a damaged section can collect them by the thousand. Bird workers travel the lines with a little hand bell whose clapper has been removed. They rub its rim against the cable. At the resulting vibration the nearest sleepers unfold, transfer their beaks to a neighboring foot or wire, and make space for a hand.

The birds' faces are almost entirely mouth and the soft structures around it. They examine an offered finger with several delicate contacts before deciding whether to step onto it. Their eyes, if present, cannot be found beneath the feathers.

They smell faintly of heated grain.

Inside a roost there are small, warm chambers formed by the surrounding bodies. Eggs are passed inward to these chambers. No individual sits on them. A bird worker clearing a line may suddenly find an egg being pressed into their palm, and must stop whatever they are doing until another place can be made for it.

This is a familiar cause of delay.

At home, when asked why a repair took so long, a worker may simply cup one hand inside the other.

***

The crews are not all alike in the ground they occupy.

A new worker arrives at a station by the route given to them. After a few years they begin to find shorter approaches, though they may be unable to guide anyone else along them. After decades they sometimes step out of a shed onto a platform that has not yet been built.

They wait, holding the doorframe, until a colleague puts down a board.

This is regarded as a lapse of attention. It happens most often when someone is tired, or thinking about home.

Older workers are asked to test crossings before the iron strips are bolted down. They go over and back carrying different loads, counting under their breath. They know how to put weight into a foot without transferring all of themselves to it. Their reports are practical: another bolt here, a wider board there, do not let two people carrying a tank cross together.

They have personal difficulties that are less easily repaired.

A man may find that the way to his sister's room grows longer each time he uses it. Changing boots helps for a while. Then going without tools helps. Eventually she meets him halfway, on a bench they both remember being closer to her door.

People maintain such benches carefully.

There are acquaintances sustained for years by a place where two lengths of journey still agree.

***

No one spends much time beside the outer engines unless work requires it.

The crews sent there carry food in several small packages rather than one large one. They tie themselves together with a slack rope and do not pull it taut, even when someone disappears beyond the comfortable reach of a voice. A tug may travel differently from a word.

The animals on the great connecting rods are unlike those on the plates. They are pale to the touch: soft, cool bodies that leave the impression of having been uncovered. Their hard ridges run underneath them. When disturbed, they flatten into the metal so completely that a scraper passes over them without catching.

There are larger things beneath the engine foundations.

They can be heard eating during the long interval between strokes. The sound is a minute, regular fracture, repeated in many places along the concrete rim. When a worker lowers a hand into the gap, the eating stops. Something touches each fingertip separately.

Nothing has ever taken hold.

The crews bring back descriptions that differ in small respects. One says there are bristles. Another says the contacts are made by a single surface, folded. A third, who has serviced the same engine for most of her life, says only that the things are larger now.

She has begun carrying longer tools.

***

When a new shelf forms, its first inhabitants are often found already on the underside.

Fine worms thread the wet seams. The little ridged animals occupy the sharp edges, turning together before the next pressure change. A bird may settle on a protruding bolt where no bolt was accessible before. By the time a worker reaches the place to test its weight, something has made it inconvenient to kneel.

They clear a space with the fork, set down a mat, and begin.

There may be room for another step. There may be room for a tank, or eventually a bench. Someone measures twice, using two pairs of boots, and records both results.

Farther in, supper is being opened. A child holds a cup beneath the largest drop on a pipe, waiting for it to fall. In a room over a pump, an old channel animal has risen from the floor and stationed itself beside the door. Its knees touch the jambs. It has felt someone coming home, though along which service line, and from how far away, no one in the room can yet tell.
