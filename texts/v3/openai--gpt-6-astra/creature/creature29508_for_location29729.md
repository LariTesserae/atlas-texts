One of the low rises in the basin has moved since the last thaw.

It has not gone far. A length of exposed stone marks its former position, already dulling beneath a film of new growth. At the other end, the rise has pressed against a bed of needles. Some have folded beneath it. Others stand through openings in its surface, unharmed.

For a while there is nothing more to see.

Then an edge lifts, and a row of feet lets go of the ground.

They are small for what they support: blunt, many-jointed feet with pale gripping surfaces, each fitted into the roots as carefully as a hand into a familiar glove. They release in no obvious order. One near the front reaches forward while three farther back remain still. The body passes over them slowly, redistributing its weight.

The animal is wider than it is long. A large one could cover a shallow pool. Its back is a succession of overlapping folds, densely clothed in the same plants that grow around it. There are needle beds on the oldest folds, soft leaves along the edges, and little patches of bare hide where a movement has recently torn the covering. The hide is dark violet, finely pitted. Moisture gathers in the pits but does not spill from them.

Its head is somewhere underneath.

You find it only when the animal reaches the bare stone. A flattened muzzle extends, touches the surface, and withdraws. Two translucent lobes open beside it. Inside each lobe, tiny dark structures tilt independently, testing the warmth coming out of the rock.

The animal waits.

Behind it, one foot continues to search the place where the roots have ended.

***

They live in the interval between the vegetation and the stone.

Here the air is less thin, though only slightly. Here a small expenditure of warmth can be kept for hours. Their bodies hold the mats up just enough to make a moving chamber beneath them, and almost everything they do takes place in that chamber.

Their mouths are small and numerous.

The one in the head takes solid food: the soft ends of roots, eggs laid against warm rock, pale cleaners dislodged from their cylinders. Along the underside are other mouths, each no larger than a split seed, which open against wet tissue. An animal settling over an active root bed may take half a night to attach them all.

It does not pierce the roots. It wets them.

The saliva softens the outer layers until fluid can pass through. The roots respond by thickening around the contact. After several days, lifting the animal leaves a pattern of low, closed nipples across the bed.

While attached, it feeds slowly. Its underside becomes warmer. The feet relax.

This is when the smaller inhabitants enter.

There are narrow grazers that live among its folds, eating the dead bases of leaves. There are translucent worms that follow the moisture around its mouths. A kind of mite spends almost its entire life in the pits of the hide, turning so slowly that a patch of them resembles a mineral stain.

The animal tolerates most of these. Occasionally a ripple passes beneath its skin, compressing one fold against another. Afterward, the worms gather at the seam.

Its back is not simply camouflage. Some of the plants rooted there occur nowhere else. Their roots enter shallow channels in the hide and travel along them without breaking through. They collect ice while the animal rests on a ridge, then surrender part of the melt when it descends into shelter. In return, they receive salts and the heat of digestion.

A young animal has almost none of this covering. It looks wet, unfinished, much too visible.

An old one may carry shrubs older than any of its feet.

***

The feet are replaced throughout life.

A damaged foot is drawn into the body and digested from the base outward. For a time its claws remain visible, projecting from a little sealed pouch. Beside it, a new limb develops.

The replacement does not always have the same shape.

Animals that remain in deep mats grow long, delicate feet capable of passing between tightly woven stems. Those that cross the ridges have short gripping limbs with hard, spreading tips. Around the pools, the feet flatten into soft cups.

This makes their age difficult to judge. An animal may arrive at a basin with the worn limbs of a ridge-dweller and, over several cycles of frost, become suited to the hollows. Only a few obsolete feet remain tucked beneath the rear folds, too useful to discard, too awkward to use often.

They are patient with these changes. Much of their life consists of reaching a place, fastening themselves to it, and becoming better able to stay.

Yet they do not remain forever.

The root bed beneath a settled animal gradually alters. Some passages widen under its warmth. Others clog with feeding tissue. Small predators establish themselves around the margins. The plants on its back grow thick enough to interfere with the opening of its folds.

Eventually the costs of staying exceed the cost of moving.

It begins by letting the rear mouths close.

Several nights later, it lifts an edge.

***

They know the plateau chiefly through their feet.

Each gripping pad contains a field of stiff hairs sunk into soft tissue. A click from a buried valve reaches them as a direction and a texture. Running water has a different texture. So does a predator pressing its limbs along an occupied channel.

A distant pumping bed may be more distinct to them than a grazer brushing their backs.

They can also feel one another.

When two are attached to the same root network, the repeated contraction of a throat or the tightening of a feeding mouth travels between them. They do not respond to every movement. Most are as unremarkable as breathing.

But a departing animal makes a particular disturbance. As it releases its mouths, it presses its feet down in succession, testing the ground that will bear its weight. Other animals nearby draw their outer folds inward. The passage between them widens by small degrees.

There is no path afterward. The plants recover too quickly.

Sometimes the moving animal changes course before it has come within touching distance of another. Sometimes it waits while an occupied root bed finishes drawing water from a pool.

Their journeys look indecisive from above. Beneath the mats, they are taking place among pressures, attachments, and approaching changes that have no visible surface expression.

***

They meet most often on the sheltered sides of ridges.

One arrives carrying frost in its needles. Another has recently fed in a warm basin. They settle edge to edge, testing each other with a few pale feet.

If they remain, their outer folds loosen.

The contact becomes broad and damp. Heat passes into the colder animal. Water passes the other way. Several small creatures cross between their coats, and one usually discovers that the new back is less suitable than the old one and returns.

Mating happens below this exchange, against the stone.

Afterward, either animal may carry young.

The eggs develop inside a fold that has never opened to the air. There are few of them, embedded separately in a clear tissue threaded with vessels. As they grow, the parent stops feeding from some of its rear mouths. These shorten and turn inward, supplying the brood chamber instead.

The young hatch with feet already capable of gripping.

For a long time they grip only their parent.

They live beneath its folds, feeding on a secretion from the modified mouths. Each is almost transparent. Through its back you can see a small dark gut and the steady contractions of two hearts. Its first plants arrive as fragments dragged inward by mites.

Before leaving, a youngster must acquire a living cover.

The parent assists without selecting it. At intervals it rubs one edge against the ground, breaking stems and packing them into the nursery fold. Much of this material dies. Some takes root. A few seedlings grow into the young animal’s skin channels, and its back begins to darken.

The first departure is usually brief.

A youngster climbs down, moves a little distance under the leaves, and attaches to a root. When the root cools, it follows the parent’s warmth back into shelter.

Later it returns and finds no opening.

The fold has closed. Beneath it, the feeding mouths are turning outward again.

***

The flat predators take them from below.

They are especially vulnerable while crossing a seam in the rock, when part of the underside must be lifted clear. A predator extends a narrow feeding limb into the gap and fastens behind a row of feet.

The attacked animal clamps down.

Often that is enough. The limb is crushed against the stone, and the predator withdraws what remains. Sometimes the attachment holds. Then the animal settles over its attacker and does not move for a very long time.

The struggle is difficult to distinguish from rest.

A few plants may die on the back. One margin loses its moisture. Beneath the folds, mouths close one after another as fluid is diverted toward the wound.

Nearby animals eventually receive the chemical alarm through the roots. By then the contest may be over. They detach anyway, slowly making room around a place that has become unsafe.

***

The oldest animals grow too deeply into their coverings to travel.

Roots pass through the skin channels, emerge at the margins, and join the stationary mat. Feeding contacts become permanent. The unused feet diminish to pale nodules.

For years, an old animal may continue opening and closing its folds. Youngsters shelter against it. Its pulses remain recognizable in the ground.

Then the pulses become irregular.

There is no single visible moment of death. One part of the underside stops feeding while another remains warm. The nursery fold may open once more, empty. Cleaners enter tissues they previously could not reach.

By the time the last heart stops, roots have crossed much of the interior.

The back stays where it is. Its plants continue gathering frost. Beneath them, new chambers form around the spaces left by the organs, and pale animals begin keeping those chambers clear.

Much later, another traveler may settle against the low rise.

It tests the warmth, fits its feet among the roots, and starts to feed.
