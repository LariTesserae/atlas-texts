The tracks are made by the young.

For the first part of its life, the animal is small enough to shelter beneath a dead blade. It has six legs, a transparent abdomen, and a mouth that closes sideways over the films growing on exposed ceramic. Seen among the grazers, it is easy to mistake for one of them. The distinction is underneath: between the rear legs lies a folded organ, pale and densely pleated, which takes up more and more of the body as the animal grows.

Eventually it can no longer eat enough to carry this organ. Its abdomen empties. Its legs become disproportionately long. It stops returning to the leaf under which it has spent its bright hours.

Then, during one of the amber intervals, it walks out of the garden.

Most go only a little distance. The plain offers many apparent entrances: grooves that end in solid material, shallow sockets whose warmth is lethal farther in, openings that admit a small body and close tightly enough to shear it. The young can sense moisture and certain electrical gradients, but neither guarantees a habitable destination. A leak may smell promising until the machinery corrects it.

Their remains are almost invisible. The collection seams take them before they have quite dried.

A successful one finds a joint that breathes.

It waits beside the opening through several cycles. When the plates draw apart, it inserts its head and front legs. When they begin to close, it withdraws. This may continue for days. At last it enters far enough to hook its folded organ around an internal filament.

The legs brace. The abdomen contracts.

What follows looks less like growth than a very slow spilling.

The organ unfolds into the gap, becoming a thin, wet sheet. Its surfaces adhere to the inner faces of the construction. The external body, still recognizable as a little animal, remains at the entrance for a while. Then its contents are drawn inward. The legs loosen. An empty skin blows a few centimeters across the ground.

Inside, the adult begins.

***

An old adult can occupy a patch of plain several meters wide, although there is no point at which you could uncover a several-meter animal.

Its body lies in the intervals between fitted surfaces. Most of it is thinner than paper. It follows the undersides of plates, thickens around a cool fitting, narrows to a thread where a seal is nearly perfect. Portions of it may be separated by a meter of solid construction and connected only by a living filament passing through the bore of an unused fastener.

There are places where it has no tissue at all, only a mineral deposit that conducts between two living edges.

Its color, where enough thickness permits color, is a dull plum. Along growing margins it is clear. Small granules move through it in single file, pause at junctions, and enter one branch or another. Larger reservoirs collect in hollows too shallow to hold a visible drop of water. During a prolonged dry interval these reservoirs shrink, leaving folds that will take years to smooth away.

The animal has no shell of its own. It uses the plain.

Pressure-sensitive pads register the movements of the surfaces enclosing it. It can distinguish a thermal expansion from the approach of a maintenance assembly, and both from the irregular loading of footsteps. Where a plate is likely to shift, the tissue lies furled in a groove, held ready to draw back. Elsewhere it spreads so intimately against the material that lifting the plate would tear it across thousands of points.

This is one reason specimens are difficult to collect alive.

Another is that an adult’s digestion extends beyond its body.

It secretes minute quantities of fluid onto sheltered ceramic. Microbial films develop there; the rice-sized grazers scrape them; the animal absorbs dissolved substances from their waste. In some places it tends the films directly, retracting a membrane to expose them to light, covering them when the local heating cycle begins. In others, roots have entered the same gap, and the animal exchanges minerals for the sugars that leak from their growing tips.

A green cushion above an old adult is often healthier than an apparently identical cushion a few meters away. Its roots suffer fewer sudden dryings. The plates beneath it move less abruptly.

The adult cannot stop a valve from closing.

It can, however, interfere with the measurement that tells the valve when to close.

***

At first these interventions are crude.

A young adult grows across a contact and delays a signal. Usually the result is useless or fatal. A heater remains active. A purge begins. A surface the animal needs is classified as fouled and ground away.

But some contacts respond to resistance rather than simple continuity. Some assemblies accept a delayed answer as evidence that a seal is still settling. Some will wait through hundreds of cycles for a temperature to stabilize within a range the animal can keep it from reaching.

An adult surviving its first few decades acquires a small repertoire.

It learns to make one pocket cooler. It learns which filament can be touched without waking the system beneath it. It learns to release a plate before the pressure across its body becomes dangerous. These are physical habits, held partly in nerves and partly in the shape of the tissue itself. A practiced response travels along a thickened route. An unused route dwindles.

Older animals have habits whose consequences occur far beyond their bodies.

One may slightly alter the angle of a surface panel and thereby leave a downstream garden in shade for an additional hour. Another can cause a buried transport assembly to pause beneath it, long enough for warmth from the assembly’s casing to raise vapor through a cold layer.

None can command the plain. Their reach is full of gaps. A creature able to postpone the movement of thousands of tons may be unable to dislodge a grain blocking its feeding aperture.

They spend much of their lives attending to grains.

***

Their senses are clearest nearby.

Within its own patch, an adult knows the plain as a succession of intimate pressures: a root swelling, a footfall of a grazer, the slight release when a membrane overhead unfolds. It detects dissolved minerals, the products of damaged tissue, the chemical difference between a plant preparing new leaves and one beginning to die.

Farther away, perception becomes uncertain.

The construction carries vibrations well in some directions and extinguishes them in others. A movement on the far side of a basin may arrive more clearly than one just beyond the next joint. Electrical connections change as systems reroute. An adult may spend a century receiving the faint disturbances of a neighbor, lose that neighbor during a routine repair, and begin receiving another whose body lies many kilometers away.

They recognize one another nonetheless.

In occupied districts, the signals passing through certain bronze filaments include small variations that the machinery ignores. These variations repeat with changes. An interruption is answered elsewhere. An answer sometimes causes the first animal to loosen its grip on a contact, admitting heat it had previously been diverting.

There are exchanges that end with one garden becoming warmer and another cooler.

There are others that end in nothing observable.

During a failure, neighboring adults may sustain a sequence of such contacts long after the affected individual has ceased feeding. Sometimes the sequence stops when the individual dies. Sometimes it continues through an empty interval until a young animal settles there.

The young does not immediately know how to answer.

Over the following years, its responses become less erratic.

***

They do not all live in gardens.

Beneath the standing dust veils are adults whose tissues contain so much dark mineral that they appear black. Their outer margins are continually injured by charge. They replace those margins with extraordinary slowness, advancing into a useful gradient while sacrificing the foremost cells.

Others inhabit the soft aprons of collapsed structures. These remain compact, looping their bodies through movable fragments. When material is sorted and carried away, they contract around a few favorable pieces and are taken with them. Such animals may undergo several displacements in a lifetime. Their memories include intervals of motion, compression, and complete sensory silence.

A few settle inside maintenance assemblies.

They occupy neglected recesses behind the moving sensory crowns, where condensation gathers before the heat exchangers clear it. They survive on films, trapped dust, and the small animals drawn into the openings. A mature passenger can press against the crown’s sensors from within.

The influence is slight. The assembly continues its work. Yet it may examine one joint longer than another, or repeat a route it would otherwise have left.

When two occupied assemblies meet, one crown sometimes remains above the surface while the other passes below. Afterward both stay still for a time.

The unoccupied ones do not do this.

***

Reproduction is infrequent and expensive.

An adult gathers material into one protected chamber, gradually withdrawing it from less productive parts of its body. A distant branch dries. A contact is abandoned. A small patch of feeding film is left untended.

Within the chamber, a cluster of young develops.

They emerge with enough stored water to reach the surface. For several cycles the parent keeps a passage open, accepting a loss of vapor it would ordinarily prevent. The young disperse into the garden, where they will spend the walking part of their lives.

There is little resemblance between parent and offspring then. The adult cannot follow them across the exposed ceramic. It senses them only while their feet remain on a plate continuous with its body.

Even that contact soon becomes indistinguishable from the movements of other small animals.

In districts where water has grown scarce, adults sometimes begin this process and reverse it. The developing cluster is absorbed. Tissue returns to the abandoned branches, though seldom to all of them.

A few repeat the attempt many times.

***

They can live for as long as their accommodations persist.

This need not be a peaceful duration. The plain changes around them. Useful circuits fail. Neighboring systems settle disputes in ways that erase whole habitats. An animal must gradually relinquish portions of the place it knows and extend itself into portions it does not.

Very old individuals are consequently uneven. One side may be alert and growing while another consists of a few slow reservoirs joined by mineral threads. A response can begin in vigorous tissue, cross a region scarcely alive, and return much later carrying an instruction appropriate to conditions that no longer exist.

Some old adults heat chambers that have been empty for centuries.

Others remain adaptable until the end.

When a seal finally closes through the center of one, the severed portions may survive independently. Each retains different habits. Each continues signaling to neighbors that recognize the responses, at first, as belonging to a single animal.

Eventually the responses diverge.

***

If you sit beside a healthy green seam for long enough, its inhabitant will notice you.

Your weight is readily understood as pressure. Your shade is measurable. Your breath is stranger: repeated pulses of moisture, rich in substances that rarely arrive together at the surface.

At first the animal will protect itself. A cloudy patch beneath a transparent plate clears as tissue withdraws. An aperture closes.

If you remain through the dimming, a change may follow.

A narrow part of the joint opens beside your hand. The movement is less than the width of a seed. Warm air rises from it. Beneath the edge is something dark and glossy, too thin to have an obvious shape.

It will not reach toward you. Exposed air would damage it.

But if you move your hand away, the opening may close. If you return it, the opening may form again, a little nearer the place where your palm rests.

The animal is comparing conditions.

Below your hand, in a chamber you cannot see, it begins moving water into a fold that has been dry for a long time.
