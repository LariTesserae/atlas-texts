Behind the wall where the pale anemones gather, a strip of darkness remains after the sediment has blown away.

It lies along a horizontal joint, no thicker than a finger, with a swollen place where two blocks have shifted apart. Grit adheres to it. So do three tiny shells, one empty and two occupied. It has the dull, uneven surface of old packing between stones.

When the current reverses, the strip closes.

The movement travels along it slowly. First the swollen place draws inward; then a length beneath the anemones tightens; then something farther down the wall pulls out of sight. A brittle star lifts an arm from the joint and lays it elsewhere. In a minute the darkness has become a narrow gray seam.

There are many such seams in this quarter. Most are only seams.

This one belongs to an animal whose body extends through the wall.

Its exposed parts are small. Within the masonry it widens into sheets, folded tubes, and soft knobs pressed into cavities left by the loss of mortar. In one place it is nearly transparent, stretched across the back of a crack. In another it has thickened into a pale, muscular pad. A portion beneath the lowest block occupies a space large enough for a crab, though it could not be drawn out through any of the openings that now lead to it.

It grew there.

The oldest parts have a tough outer skin embedded with grains. These grains are not carefully chosen. Shell, stone, a broken spine, a fish scale gone opaque: whatever lodges against the skin may remain as the skin grows around it. Farther inside, where the water enters slowly and carries little grit, the flesh is smooth and faintly yellow. Narrow bands contract through it. At a fork in the body, the contractions sometimes pause before continuing along one branch.

There is no head to find by following them.

Along the exposed strip, mouths open when the water slackens. Each is a short soft tube surrounded by fine processes, too blunt to look like hair. They reach only a little way into the current. Food adheres to them: shreds of tissue, small eggs, the softer particles in the material moving uphill. A process curls down into its mouth, comes out clean, and opens again.

Most meals are invisible from more than an arm’s length away.

Sometimes a transparent animal strikes the wall and catches. Then several mouths lean toward the same place. They lengthen beyond what their closed forms would suggest, pale necks emerging from the gritty seam. The prey folds and unfolds between them. A little farther along the wall, mouths with nothing to eat stop reaching.

Inside, food passes through branching passages. So does some part of the disturbance caused by feeding. A well-fed length swells; a damaged length contracts; adjoining tissue changes its work. The whole animal can respond to a hard touch, but the response may take so long to reach its farther portions that the fish which delivered the bite has left the court.

There are places where the response never arrives.

Across a buried doorway, two portions of the same body have grown together without fully joining. Their skins are continuous. Their inner passages are not. One may feed while the other remains shut against a pressure change received elsewhere. Between them is a puckered line that opens a little when either side contracts.

The distinction matters when the wall moves.

During a winter storm, a block settles by less than the width of a shell. The movement crushes a thick strand inside the joint. For a while, fluid leaks into a chamber that has no direct opening to the street. Both severed ends shorten. Tissue gathers over them.

One side contains most of the feeding mouths. The other has a broad pad beneath the wall and a single opening under a loose flag. For several weeks the smaller portion grows thinner. Its inner folds diminish. The pad draws away from the stone, leaving a delicate impression of the surface on its own skin.

Then a new mouth appears beneath the flag.

Water reaches it through a passage scarcely wider than the mouth itself. It feeds poorly, but it feeds. By the following season there are four openings there, and a narrow extension has begun to grow along the underside of the paving.

The animal behind the wall has become two. Nothing in the court marks the event.

Its growth is slow enough that the smaller inhabitants use it as ground. Worms settle in pockets of its outer skin. A young mussel attaches near an opening and is gradually carried aside as the rim thickens. The anemones above it do well because its flesh closes some of the deeper leaks through the wall, leaving a gentler eddy in the recess.

This shelter is incidental. So is its loss.

A feeding edge may advance beneath an anemone until the anemone must loosen its foot and move. A small worm may vanish into a fold that was open when it entered. Where a crust of embedded shell becomes too heavy, the animal withdraws the living layer beneath it. The crust remains for a time, hollow and apparently unchanged. Then it breaks away in a surge, taking its little population with it.

The exposed flesh is eaten quickly.

One of the broad-headed fish knows the lower edge of this wall. It waits for a strong reversal, turns on its side, and drives its mouth into the joint just as the outer lips begin to close. Usually it takes only grit. Sometimes it pulls free a rag of yellow tissue.

After a successful bite, that section may stay shut for days.

The fish returns anyway. Its flank has a pale abrasion from the stone.

There are larger growths farther down, beyond the courts that receive occasional brightness. In the lower districts, an entire stairwell may be lined with the animal’s folds. Water descends through the center, while the mouths occupy the slower margins. In the narrowest places they stand so densely that their touching rims make a soft, irregular lining over the worn steps.

Here the animal is less heavily armored with grains. It hangs in loose curtains behind the stonework. When disturbed, the curtains thicken and draw back, revealing pale patches of wall that have not been exposed to open water for a long time.

Small fish shelter between these folds. A shrimp may spend most of its life in one of the deeper pockets, cleaning particles from the surface and vanishing into the same crease whenever a shadow passes. The animal does not close upon it. Its mouths are too small, and the shrimp’s movements are too familiar a local disturbance to cause more than a brief tightening.

A falling stone is different.

The impact sends a contraction through the lining. Curtains shorten. Water is squeezed from concealed spaces in thin jets. The shrimp is driven into the stairwell, where it hangs exposed for a moment before swimming down.

By the time the flesh opens again, silt has settled on the stone.

At intervals, some of the internal knobs change. They become rounder and darker, each with a small hard point at its center. Food from nearby mouths passes toward them. The tissue between them grows thin.

Eventually they detach.

They leave through openings that have widened over several days, emerging as soft, lopsided bodies no larger than peas. Each has a ring of short beating hairs and, inside, a curled strip of denser flesh. They rise unevenly through the cold water. Most are eaten. Some pass out over the broken eastern margin and are carried into depths where there is little suspended food.

A few lodge on the summit.

They cannot establish themselves on a broad clean surface. The current rolls them away before they can spread. They need a roughness, a sheltered underside, an opening that catches the soft body without tearing it.

One enters the stone bowl near the western balustrade.

For several circuits it travels with the white pebbles, rising along the curve and dropping inward. A pebble strikes it. The soft outer body ruptures, and the ring of hairs stops beating.

The denser strip survives.

It catches beneath the damaged lip, in a pore where the circling stones cannot quite reach. There it remains while the rest of its body loosens and washes away. For days it is smaller than the grains lodged beside it. Then one end flattens against the stone.

A mouth opens into the bowl.

Each circuit brings food past it and threatens to scour it off. It feeds in the interval after a pebble passes. When the pebble comes around again, it draws down into its pore.

Below it, the white stones continue their climbing and falling. The pore extends a short distance into the pedestal, then turns along an old fracture. By the end of the season, a translucent thread of flesh has reached the turn.
