The shelter is occupied before you enter it.

What looks like a thickening of the rear wall is a body standing on its folded feet. Its back is pale and irregular, divided into overlapping shields. Water has dried in the joints, leaving white lines. A narrow fringe along its sides rests against the stone.

It has heard you coming for some time. Your first step onto the roof of a buried chamber was probably enough. It did not leave because the rear wall is the driest part of the shelter, and because leaving would have meant passing you in the entrance.

When you put down your bundle, it lifts its head.

The head is broad, almost rectangular, with a soft, dark mouth underneath. Two small eyes sit far apart beneath ridges of translucent material. The eyes move separately. One follows your hand; the other remains directed toward the basin outside.

Then it places a forefoot on your bundle.

There is nothing tentative about the gesture. It presses, waits, lifts the foot, and presses again somewhere else. The foot opens as it does so. What had seemed to be three blunt toes separates into a fan of short, jointed digits, each tipped with a dark pad. One digit folds backward against the others.

It is testing whether your bundle will bear weight.

If it will, the animal climbs onto it.

This is your first encounter with one of the builders.

***

They are larger than the little grazers in the pools, though not by as much as the shelters might suggest. A large adult is a little under a yard long. Most of that length is trunk. The tail is short, heavy at its root, and flattened underneath; when the animal sits back, the tail and hind feet make a stable three-point support.

The builders and the grazers are related. Their young are difficult to distinguish. Both have the same wide jaw, the same nearly transparent skin over the lower limbs, the same habit of stopping with the chin against the ground.

But a builder’s forelimbs continue developing long after it has reached the size of a grazer. The shoulders broaden. The inner digits become opposable. Beneath the throat, two shallow pockets deepen into muscular recesses in which small objects can be carried.

An adult moving across the plain keeps everything tucked close. Fingers, throat pouches, and the soft folds behind its elbows disappear beneath the lateral fringe. It becomes a low piece of traveling rubble. Only when it settles to work does its body reveal how much has been folded away.

The back shields are not stone. They are tough, layered tissue, cloudy with mineral taken in through food. Their broken edges heal slowly. An old individual may carry several generations of repair: a blue-white central shield, a milkier patch along one side, a thin new margin still flexible enough to dent beneath a finger.

They can endure the water for hours. They can force themselves through slits that look impossibly small, emptying their lungs first and drawing the shields together like an uneven pack of cards. They are strong in compression. A frightened builder is almost impossible to pull from a crack without injuring it.

Yet the folds beneath its body cannot remain wet indefinitely.

The skin there becomes soft, then splits. Salt collects in the wounds. The animal begins to walk with one shoulder raised, or with its tail held clear of the ground. In bad cases it can no longer flatten itself enough to take shelter in a seam.

Dryness is something they have to make.

***

They eat the films on the water and the tender material exposed along a newly opened plate.

Their mouths contain no teeth that meet together. Instead, the lower lip holds a crescent of closely packed scraping points, continually worn down and renewed. An adult feeds with short sideways movements of the head. It leaves broader marks than the grazers do, often with one squared end where it has braced against the stone.

There is nourishment in the films, but little of it. A builder may spend much of a day working around the rim of a single basin, stopping between mouthfuls while the water slowly replaces what it has removed.

They also prise open the small, sealed blisters that form beneath some ledges. Inside is a dark paste with a sharp mineral smell. They eat this quickly and carry portions of it in their throat pockets.

Not every blister is good. Some are opened, touched with the mouth, and abandoned. Others make an animal withdraw abruptly and wipe its lips on gravel. The rejected ones look no different to a human observer.

A fresh fracture draws builders from several basins away. For a few days the newly exposed layers can be scraped before their surfaces harden. Several animals may feed shoulder to shoulder along a break, barely moving except to shift a forefoot when another needs room.

There are disputes. An individual with a good position may spread its fringe over the feeding edge. Another will push beneath it. Their slow shoving can continue for an hour, the participants apparently forgetting to eat.

Sometimes a much smaller animal walks over both of them and takes the exposed place.

***

Their sensitivity to the ground is concentrated in the jaw and the pads of the feet. Along the underside of the mandible is a strip of tissue kept moist even when the rest of the body is dry. Pressed against stone, it receives movements too slight to see.

They do not know the whole plain through its vibrations. A loose bed of shell interrupts what they can feel. Two chambers may give almost identical warnings before behaving differently. Rain confuses them. So does a distant collapse.

They make mistakes, particularly in country they have not visited before.

But on familiar ground they remember sequences.

A low pulse beneath a certain ledge is followed, usually, by a hiss from the narrow mouth beside it. Later the nearest basin begins to drain. If the hiss does not come, the animal waits. It may move to a second listening place, a thumb-sized patch rubbed smooth by many chins.

These patches are among the few enduring marks of their use of the country. Some lie beside feeding grounds. Others are positioned before crossings that appear quite ordinary. A builder approaching one will often stop even when hurried.

The young have to learn which sounds belong together. They are impatient listeners. They put their jaws down, lift them almost at once, and go on. Adults sometimes hold them in place with a forefoot.

***

The shelters take years to build, though very little of that time is spent moving stone.

A site must first remain dry through enough changes of water to be worth improving. Builders add shell to promising patches and return to inspect it. A small heap may be abandoned after one inundation. Another is enlarged a mouthful at a time over several seasons.

They carry gravel in their throat pockets. Larger fragments are dragged backward, gripped between the forefeet and mouth. Their lips are often scarred from this work.

They rarely raise a slab from flat ground. Most shelters begin where a plate already leans above a dry patch. They undercut it, clear loose material from beneath it, and wedge smaller stones along its resting edge. A neighboring plate can sometimes be encouraged to settle against it.

Their tools are wedges, short levers, and abrasive pieces of shell. Tools are left where they are useful. A lever lying beneath a ledge may have been used by animals that never met one another.

The most dangerous part of construction is also the least impressive to watch. A builder lies beside a loaded wedge and works a little mud out from underneath it. Then it retreats. Later it returns and removes a little more.

Eventually the plate moves.

Sometimes the builder has judged badly.

Crushed shields occur beneath old falls. Other shelters stand unfinished around a body that could not be extracted. The living use these places if they are dry, though they tend not to disturb the remains.

An entrance faces a basin because the basin makes changes visible. The lowest threshold stone is set at a level the builder has reason to fear. Often there are other marks below it: pale chips pressed into mud, a scratch on a vertical face, a little shelf carrying two or three loose pebbles.

A rising pool reaches these in order.

Inside, the roof’s hum gives a second account of what is happening beneath the shelter. The animals sleep with some part of the body touching a support or wall. Even when piled together, the lowest one keeps its jaw against stone.

A roof that rattles cannot be trusted to wake them clearly. They may spend days trying to silence one loose contact with grit and small wedges. If they fail, they move.

***

They are sociable, though there is seldom enough food for a large group to remain together.

A shelter may hold one builder for weeks, then six for a night, then none. Certain individuals travel in pairs. Others repeatedly arrive at the same place by different routes. They recognize one another by smell, by the texture of the side fringe, and perhaps by things in their movements too slight for us to distinguish.

Greetings take place at close quarters. One lays its forefoot across the other’s jaw. The second presses upward. There follows a succession of small contacts at throat, shoulder, and wrist.

They communicate this way at length.

The same pressure made at different speeds appears to mean different things. Some exchanges involve both forefeet and leave the participants sitting upright, balanced on their tails. A third animal wishing to interrupt puts a hand between theirs.

Across a gap, they strike the stone. These signals are simpler: repeated taps, a sustained scrape, a hard blow followed by stillness. On a continuous slab they can summon one another from well out of sight. On rubble, a caller may have to move several times before receiving an answer.

They make few airborne sounds. A young one caught under an adult gives a thin, surprised squeak. Adults exhale forcefully when hurt. Their most elaborate conversations would be almost silent in a quiet room.

Here they take place under the endless grinding of the plain.

***

Their eggs need a dry surface.

They lay only two or three at a time, soft, flattened ovals attached beneath a loose piece of shell. The covering tightens over several days until each egg resembles a cloudy bead with one side pressed flat.

A brooding adult carries the shell against its belly when traveling. In a shelter it sets the eggs on the gravel and raises them on carefully chosen fragments so that air can reach underneath.

Visitors examine them. They touch the supporting stones and sometimes replace one. The parent usually permits this, although it may retrieve the original stone afterward.

Hatchlings are tiny, damp versions of the adults, with disproportionately large feet. For a while they ride beneath a parent’s fringe. Their scraping edges have not hardened, and they feed on paste brought in the throat pockets.

The first dry bed a young builder helps maintain is usually the one in which it hatched. It brings stones too large, stones that crumble, wet stones that have to be taken outside again. It may continue enlarging the bed after the older animals have left.

There are shelters with absurdly high floors, the roof so close that an adult must lie flat inside. There are beautifully fitted thresholds attached to chambers that always flood from the rear. Not all their work is skillful. Some of it is inherited and poorly understood.

Some of it persists because a particular animal is reluctant to leave.

***

If the water reaches the threshold while you sleep, the builder at the back of the shelter may wake before you.

It puts its jaw to the wall. It crosses to the entrance and tests the water with the underside of one wrist. Then it returns to the raised gravel.

For a while it moves small things.

An egg-shell, if there is one. A wedge it favors. Food stored beneath a flat chip. These go onto the highest part of the bed. If your bundle is higher still, they go there.

Only afterward does it begin pulling at you.

Its hand is cool and unexpectedly firm. It takes a fold of clothing, releases it, takes another. If you do not respond, it climbs onto your chest and presses its jaw against your breastbone, trying to find a surface through which it can make itself understood.

Outside, water enters between two threshold stones.

The builder strikes you once with the hard heel of its forefoot.

Then it waits for you to move, keeping one hand on the little shell that holds its eggs.
