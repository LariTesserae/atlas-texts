The most settled inhabitant is a female rat with a split in one ear. She sleeps behind the cable trays, but not in the middle of the nest. The middle holds heat. She lies along its outer edge with her belly against the concrete and her tail extended into the gap beneath the lowest tray. When another rat passes through that gap, she draws her tail in without opening her eyes.

She has raised one litter here. Of the young that survived, two still use the chamber. They are nearly her size now and no longer fit comfortably into the arrangements she made for them. The quarrels behind the trays are partly about this: a sleeping place built around small bodies, occupied by larger ones.

Her routes extend well beyond the square. Above the stair is a door with a damaged brush seal. Beyond it, pipes pass through a wall into a basement where there is sometimes food. She brings things down: a piece of bread with a tough outer skin, a greasy paper corner, something that was cooked and then scraped from a pan. Food lodges in the ridges of the cable trays. Crumbs fall through. Cockroaches wait underneath.

She knows the exposed center chiefly as a distance to avoid. Once, startled by a worker, she crossed it and left a diagonal trail in the clay. The prints filled with water. For a while each held a few larvae, separated from the larger puddle. Then the water rose and joined them.

The rats are conspicuous because they have destinations. Much of what lives here stays within the reach of a drip.

Around the cockroaches, the concrete has become stippled with droppings. There are shed skins caught between a pipe clip and the wall, delicate enough to move when a large insect pushes past. The smallest young occupy gaps the adults cannot enter. They press into the roughness beneath washers, into bubbles broken open on the face of the concrete. A bracket that looks crowded can hold another generation without appearing much different.

They come out before the rat returns with food. Antennae emerge, touch the wall, withdraw. One insect follows a line of dampness until it meets another coming the other way. They pause with their heads close together, then pass. Near a dead fly, several feed at once. One holds a wing that offers almost nothing edible and keeps it nevertheless, dragging it backward when disturbed.

Their abundance is supported by small, repeated deliveries. The square receives scraps through the drains, bodies from the lamps, rat droppings, street dirt containing fragments too small to recognize. The timber against the wall supplies something different. Beneath its surface fur, fungal threads enter the wood. It is softening from the wet end inward. A piece that still looks sound can be lifted to reveal a dark underside, pitted and traversed by pale growth.

The water feeds its animals through a further layer of life. Slime grows on submerged grit and on the clay walls of the excavator tracks. Bacteria multiply in the thin mixture of warmth, waste, and water. The larvae browse, filter, or sweep particles toward their mouths. What appears to be a puddle full of restless punctuation is feeding almost continuously.

Some hang from the surface. Others keep their bodies in the silt and expose only what they need to breathe. A shadow passing over them produces a brief withdrawal. A vibration does the same. They return at unequal intervals, so that the dark channel gradually becomes pale-speckled again.

When the pump runs, the smallest hollows become traps. Water leaves through connections too shallow for every animal to follow. Larvae crowd against a retreating margin; some are stranded on shining clay. The cockroaches arrive while that clay is still wet.

In a deeper groove, others remain submerged throughout the pumping. That groove produces flies after the shallower water has failed. An irregularity left by a machine has become a reliable refuge, though it may last only until someone clears the intake more thoroughly or drags a hose across the floor.

Adult flies collect where the air moves least. Some have only recently emerged, their bodies pale, their wings held awkwardly while they harden. Empty pupal skins remain at the water’s edge. Near the lamps, older flies strike the cages until they find a surface on which to rest. A few get into the light housings and cannot get out. Their bodies accumulate above the translucent covers.

There are spiders there too, easily overlooked among the cable ends. Their webs are small and badly placed by the standards of a dry room. Condensation weighs them down; insects too large for them tear through. Yet enough flies arrive that a damaged web can still feed its maker. Beneath one lamp a spider has fastened a retreat inside the cut end of a conduit. Only the tips of its legs show. When the motor starts, it holds still through the first vibration, then resumes handling what it has caught.

The plants occupy the one place where the chamber’s wealth of warmth and water is not enough. They need the narrow fall of daylight. The grass leans out over the ledge. Its older blades lie flat beneath the new ones, making a little damp bed that catches silt and seeds. The seedling has extended beyond the brightest patch; its newest leaves are smaller than the earlier ones. Dust adheres to their surfaces. Street washing cleans them irregularly and sometimes bends the whole plant under dirty water.

A small root has reached the bottom of the mortar seam. It turns there and follows the ledge sideways.

None of these inhabitants uses the whole square. Even the rat spends most of her time within a few inches of an edge. The broad central floor remains mostly a place where heat, water, and falling things arrive. Life gathers where those arrivals are held: behind a lip, beneath a tray, in the low part of a track.

After the worker leaves, the chamber takes a while to become active again. The rat waits longest if the trays have been moved. Cockroaches reappear under the pipe before she ventures out. Flies settle on the fresh mud pressed up beside a bootprint.

By then the print is already changing. Water enters at the heel and darkens the sole. A few grains collapse from the edge. In the tread, something small enough to have escaped notice begins to feed.
