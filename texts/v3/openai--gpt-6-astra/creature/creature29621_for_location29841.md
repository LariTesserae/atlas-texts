The creature in the egg has a folded wing pressed against its face.

Through the crack you can see a closed eyelid, a wet feather, the faint movement of a throat. It is cramped in the ordinary way of something that has not yet come out. When it pushes, the shell lifts slightly along the fracture. When it rests, the two edges settle together again.

The parent bird waits on the rolled edge of the country. It has something in its beak: not food, but a short length of dark fiber.

After a while, someone climbs up from underneath.

First there are fingers around the clay lip. They are broad at the tips, with small, flat nails the color of soaked horn. Then a head appears, and a shoulder, and the person comes over the edge with the unhurried competence of someone entering by a familiar doorway. There must be footholds on the other side. From where you stand, there were none to see.

They are no taller than your chest. Their body is substantial, with a low belly and powerful forearms. A loose garment hangs from their shoulders to their knees, open at the sides. Its weave is so fine that the light passes through it, but where it lies against the body it darkens with moisture.

Their skin is brown, with a greenish cast in the hollows of the wrists. There are no visible seams on the face. On the neck, however, just below one ear, a row of tiny knots disappears beneath the garment.

They glance at you, then at the nest.

The bird gives them the fiber.

They kneel beside the egg and hold one hand above it, making a little shade. With the other they pass the fiber through the crack. There is a brief, difficult movement inside. The person waits. The bird shifts from one foot to the other.

When the hand withdraws, a loop has been pulled loose from the shell.

The crack stays open.

It takes the young bird a long time to get out. Nobody helps it further. It braces a foot, rests, pushes its head into the opening, rests again. The person sits back on their heels. Once, they look away across the gardens, and you notice how tired their face is.

Finally the shell divides.

The bird that emerges is small enough to fit beneath its parent’s wing, but it is not downy. Its feathers are complete, dark and wet, each narrow vane lying neatly against the next. One foot is caught behind its neck. It frees the foot with an irritated jerk, tumbles onto its breast, and begins to breathe more easily.

The person picks the loose fiber from the nest and winds it around a finger.

Only then do they acknowledge you properly. They lift their chin toward the stairs.

It is an invitation to get out of the heat.

***

There are people in the houses.

You do not begin seeing them everywhere once you have seen the first. Most houses remain empty. Most squares hold nothing but their water and their light. But at one doorway a woven screen has been lifted, and behind it someone sits with both feet in a bowl. Farther on, a hand reaches through a gap in a wall to retrieve a cup.

They are quiet, though not solemn. In the house to which you are taken, two people are disputing the ownership of a cushion.

You cannot understand their speech at first. Their voices are low and slightly rough, with pauses inside words where you do not expect them. One speaker keeps a finger hooked through the cushion’s corner. The other has already put it behind their back. Your arrival interrupts the dispute without resolving it.

The room is wider than the roof appeared to allow.

There are sleeping mats, shallow cupboards, a row of little clay lamps without flames. Beneath a bench stands a pair of sandals, one turned over. A bright green strip has been tied around the damaged strap. It is the first repair you have seen that makes no effort to resemble the original work.

Your host brings you water.

They drink before handing you the bowl, perhaps to show that it is safe, perhaps because they are thirsty. The water is cool. When you give the bowl back, a wet print remains where your mouth touched the rim.

One of the people on the cushion leans forward to look at it.

Your host says something brief, and they lean back.

***

The people have not all been made alike.

Some have the supple, faintly grained skin of the person who met you at the edge. Others are darker and glossy across the shoulders, with a texture like a seed coat. A broad woman who visits the house has pale panels on the backs of her hands. Their edges are fitted so closely that you notice them only when she spreads her fingers.

One man’s left ear is attached with conspicuous red thread. He wears a red cord at his waist to match it.

Their bodies are warm. They sweat. They scratch beneath their clothes and press their palms into the small of their backs when they stand. Whatever was done to make them has not spared them discomfort.

Nor has it given them the same expression. There are wary faces and distracted faces, people who inspect you without embarrassment and people who pretend not to. A thin person with an unusually wide mouth laughs at something your host says. Their laughter is nearly silent until the end, when it escapes in a single surprising bark.

They all turn toward the sound.

Then several laugh too.

You begin to recognize them more easily after that.

***

They spend much of their time under cover.

In the gardens, a person crossing an open square moves steadily toward the next patch of shade. Indoors they linger, leaning against walls, sitting close enough for their knees to touch. Screens can be let down over the broad doorways. Some rooms have a second, smaller room inside them, made from overlapping curtains.

A child takes your hand and leads you into one of these.

At least, you think it is a child. They are much smaller than the others, with short fingers and a round, intent face. They have the same finished proportions as an adult, reduced without awkwardness. Their garment has a pocket full of clay counters.

Inside the curtains, it is genuinely dark.

The child puts a counter in your hand, closes your fingers over it, and waits. After a moment they open your hand again.

The counter is still there.

They seem pleased. They take it back, exchange it for another, and repeat the experiment.

Later you see three adults playing a more elaborate version of the same game. A little object passes between closed hands. There is guessing, accusation, suppressed laughter. When it is finally revealed, one player insists on examining everybody’s sleeves.

Here, where water can retain the shape of a splash, they have made a pastime of not knowing where something has gone.

***

They eat the leaves and the heavy pale flowers. They scoop translucent flesh from fruits that open along fastened edges.

The food gives way under their teeth. You can hear it.

After a meal, the serving dishes are as full as before, but the diners loosen their garments and lie back. One falls asleep with a piece of fruit still in their hand. Another takes it away before it can slip to the floor.

They do not appear to find this remarkable.

What troubles them is a sleeping place left empty, a visitor who will not sit down, a door that has been closed without a word. Their conversations often end with someone going to another house. Sometimes that person returns with company. Sometimes they return alone and are given room on a mat.

There are disagreements that last longer than your stay. A woman will not cross a particular courtyard, although there is no fold in it that you can detect. Her brother lives on the other side. When she needs to reach the basin beyond his house, she walks around three gardens.

The animals take that route too. Whether she learned it from them or they from her, you never discover.

***

You do see them work.

Not in the bright avenues, and not at the unfinished baskets waiting beside the doors.

Your host has a small tray that is brought out after the screens are lowered. On it lie needles, slivers of polished wood, coils of filament, and several objects whose purpose you cannot guess. There is also the dark fiber taken from the egg.

A neighbor sits before them and lifts an arm.

Under the arm, where the skin folds into the flank, a fastening has drawn tight. It has made a shallow crease. Your host touches the place, then waits for a nod before sliding a wooden point beneath the thread.

The neighbor closes their eyes.

Nothing is replaced. No new material is added. Your host eases out a little slack, smooths the skin with a damp thumb, and has the neighbor raise and lower the arm several times.

The relief is obvious.

Afterward they sit together without speaking. The neighbor keeps moving the shoulder, cautiously at first, then with increasing pleasure.

You remember the loop drawn from the egg.

There are other such visits. A bird that cannot quite settle its wing. Someone whose mouth pulls at one corner when they try to speak. A small person who sits rigidly on a bench while an older one holds their feet.

The work is delicate and sometimes unsuccessful. Your host will stop, cover the tray, and refuse to continue. Once a patient leaves angry.

They do not fix the walls. They do not straighten the paths or complete the basket. Whatever authority their hands possess, it is narrower than the country’s making.

***

There are old people here.

You would not know them by wrinkles. Their faces are as smooth as the others’, their joints outwardly intact. But they pause before reaching for familiar things, and the younger people let them finish a movement without offering help too soon.

One old person lives near the long pool. Their garment is covered in little loops of unused thread.

People visit to sit beside them. Sometimes they bring an animal. Sometimes they bring nothing. The old person listens with their head inclined, rubbing a loose loop between finger and thumb.

At the back of that house is a room with seven mats.

Six are rolled and tied. On the seventh lies a figure covered to the chin. Their eyes are shut. A bowl of water stands within reach of one hand.

Your host prevents you from going closer.

For the rest of the visit, they hold your wrist—not tightly, but without forgetting it.

***

When you return to the rim, the nest is empty.

Below it, on the smooth clay roll, the young bird is attempting to perch. Its feet slip. It opens both wings, recovers, and looks fiercely at the surface that failed it.

Your host makes no move to help.

Beyond the bird, under the country, there are little dark rectangles in the luminous weave. Now that you know what a lowered screen looks like, you can recognize them as doorways. A narrow ledge runs between them. Someone has set out three bowls. A garment hangs from a peg.

At one doorway two people are sitting with their legs over the brightness. One is combing the other’s hair. They have stopped to watch the bird.

It tries again.

This time one foot finds purchase in a join. The other follows. It stands there, wings half open, astonished by its own balance.

The people beneath the plain wait until the wings have folded before returning to what they were doing.
