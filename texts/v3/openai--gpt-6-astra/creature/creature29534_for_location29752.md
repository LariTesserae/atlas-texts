The surge lifts something out of the undercut shore.

At first it seems to be a pale fold in the water, a thicker part of the sheet that fails to fall with the rest. Then it bends. One end finds the submerged edge of a step, and the water tears away from a long, jointed limb.

The animal comes up facing the basin. It has been holding itself beneath the overhang, with its back against the underside of the rock.

There is more of it than the shallows should have concealed.

Two limbs become four as it lifts its body clear. The rear pair remain widely planted, their ends spread into soft, many-parted pads. The forward pair reach back without the body turning. One finds a groove in the stone. The other hovers, feeling the retreating current with a loose fringe along its inner edge, then settles just where the water thins.

It moves backward up the steps.

Its body is narrow through the middle and broad at either end, pale enough to disappear whenever the glare crosses it. What seemed like a smooth back is a covering of overlapping plates, each no thicker than a fingernail. Their edges have collected limestone. Some are clean and translucent; others carry creamy ridges and little blunt pendants that sway as it climbs. Along one flank, an older deposit joins three plates together. That side bends less easily.

It has learned to accommodate the stiffness. Each backward step describes a slight curve.

On the ankle-deep shelf it stops. Water drains from underneath it in six separate streams.

The end facing the basin rises.

There is a head there, though its outline has been concealed by two broad flaps held against the shoulders. These open slowly, exposing a wet, intricately folded surface. For several seconds the folds ripple from bottom to top. The animal’s body expands. You hear a thin rasp during the interval between crashes, intimate and close, like someone drawing a damp cloth through their hands.

Then the flaps close halfway.

It stands about as high as the bench. Its reach is considerably greater than its height.

You cannot immediately find its eyes. There are dark spots along the rim of the head, but they disappear when the skin flexes. The only feature that remains steady is a narrow gray crescent set underneath, directed toward the stone. It may be a mouth. It stays almost touching the surface while the animal advances.

At the first sheltered hollow it lowers itself.

One forelimb divides at the end.

The separation is so delicate that you may mistake it for water parting around fingers. Six slender tips unfold from a common pad. Each has a small, darkened nail on the underside. They enter the hollow one at a time, explore its back wall, and emerge carrying a clot of pale sand.

The animal places this on the exposed side of the rock.

It repeats the movement twice. With the third clot comes a transparent creature, its feet working furiously against the air. The resident holds still. A smaller tip lifts beneath the creature and turns it over; two others carry it around the stone and press it gently against the sheltered face.

It grips. The hand withdraws.

The next retreating wave takes away the excavated sand.

Only after watching several such operations do you notice how many hollows have the same proportions: a narrow entrance, a deep pocket on the downstream side, a low ridge built up where the returning water might intrude. They occur wherever the stone permits them. Some are empty. Some hold amber films, plant threads, or the flat animals. At one there is a scatter of broken crab shell.

The inhabitant touches that hollow and moves on.

It works between upheavals, but not hurriedly. Often it begins a movement that would seem impossible to finish in time. A hand is still deep in a crevice when the pool goes white. Then its wrist folds, its body sinks, and the water passes above the plated back. The long limbs lie in the grooves as neatly as cords laid in channels.

When it rises, the hand resumes its work.

The chamber has shaped its resting posture down to the last joint.

Or its kind has helped shape the chamber. There are small marks on the inside of the nearest groove, sets of six fine scratches beneath the polish. Where a fresh mineral bead has formed, the dark nails scrape it away.

Eventually it reaches the island that is usually dry.

Here it climbs fully out of the water and becomes awkward. Its pads adhere too well. Each must be peeled from the rock with a slow rolling action, and the weight of its plated body draws its middle downward. It uses a forelimb to support itself while the other reaches under the rim of a shoulder plate.

Something comes free with a small crack.

The animal holds the fragment out. It is a piece of its own mineral crust, curved and thin, pinkish on the inner face. Under the plate the newly exposed skin is flushed almost violet.

It rubs the place with the soft underside of a finger.

For the first time, its movements have the unmistakable quality of discomfort.

The next jet will strike this island. You have begun to recognize the preliminary filling of the low arch, and the animal plainly recognizes it too. It turns the sore patch toward the basin, braces on three limbs, and raises the loosened plate.

The jet hits.

For a moment the resident is only a shape within white water. When the island clears, it is crouched with its head low and the plate lying flat again. It rests there through two ordinary surges.

A cream-colored crab comes out beneath it.

The crab’s feelers touch the animal’s underside. It picks at something caught between two plates, then sidesteps toward the tender patch. A long finger descends and bars its way. The crab tries the other side. The finger follows.

After a little while, it is allowed to continue elsewhere.

From the bench, you can now see that the resident carries a small community on its back. Brown filaments occupy the bases of the oldest crusts. In the wetter seams, transparent bodies contract away from the light. One high plate supports a tiny rosette with three leaves, its roots disappearing beneath the limestone rim.

The animal can reach most of these places. It leaves them intact.

When it finally approaches the screen, the sweet smell grows stronger.

It comes from the folds beneath the head. Droplets collect there, thicker than the surrounding water. The animal catches one with a fingertip and draws it along a groove beside the bench. There are several such grooves, shallow and amber-stained. Within a minute, something fine and translucent begins to move at the back of one.

The resident lowers itself onto the level ground.

Its front limbs remain between you and its body. The fingertips rest lightly on the stone, ready to bear weight. Its head tilts, showing the gray crescent underneath.

Seen close, the crescent is made of hundreds of narrow slits.

They open toward you.

The flaps at its shoulders become still. The dark spots along its head emerge again, little cups of pigment recessed in clear tissue. Some face the basin. Three face you. One appears to watch the place where your feet were before you drew them back.

A fingertip touches the edge of your footprint in the grit.

It brings the grit beneath its head and holds it there.

You remain seated.

After a time it reaches toward the end of the bench.

The row of depressions is not quite a match for its hand. Four fingertips settle easily. A fifth rests against the outer rim of the last hollow. The sixth curls beneath the bench, where you had not thought to look for wear.

It presses down.

Through the bench comes a faint series of impacts: two close together, a pause, then a longer trembling pressure. They are almost lost in the roar, but the stone carries them clearly into your thighs.

The resident waits.

Beyond the pool, somewhere inside a passage that turns out of sight, there is an answering tremor.

You feel it through your feet before you understand what it is.

The animal’s shoulder folds loosen. It shifts nearer the screen and settles its weight, leaving the worn portion of the bench unobstructed. Its long rear limbs arrange themselves along the dry strip beneath it. One reaches farther than the other; the stiffened flank stays toward the warmth.

This is an old individual.

You can see younger growth beneath the crust, repairs at the joints, a missing dark nail replaced by a soft hook. There are places on its back where whole small gardens have grown and vanished, leaving roots sealed into the mineral. The little rosette on its shoulder leans toward the basin even when the animal turns away.

For a while neither of you moves.

Then, before the transparent creatures withdraw, before the pebbles begin to tick, one of its fingers lifts from the bench and touches your ankle.

The contact is warm and faintly adhesive.

It draws your foot back another inch.
