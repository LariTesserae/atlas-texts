What lives there is easiest to see when a pool has almost finished draining.

Look beneath the edge of a flange, where a narrow strip of wet material remains attached to the iron. At first it resembles an old gasket squeezed out of its joint. It is gray, a little translucent along the lower edge, and embedded with particles of rust. The water drops away from it. For a moment it hangs unsupported.

Then the edge thickens. A crease travels along it, slowly enough to be followed with a finger, and the whole strip draws back beneath the flange.

It leaves a clean crescent on the metal.

There are other places where this happens. Under the settling trays. Inside the mouths of pipes that have been disconnected at both ends. Along the underside of the curved cover, where the lifting ring has remained upright. The exposed portions are usually no wider than a hand, though on a day when several basins empty together, a length of it can be seen against the eastern wall. There it has the appearance of a heavy apron pressed into the angle between wall and floor. Vertical folds descend into the silt. Some are rounded and full; others have collapsed so flat that grit rests in their creases.

It is difficult to tell where the apron ends. Its color changes with the material against which it lies. Over brick it is dull brown, over clay almost white. Against iron, the outer surface contains so much rust that a flake taken from it can be picked up with a magnet.

The living substance is underneath.

A fresh edge is the color of the inside of a mussel shell, without the sheen. It bruises yellow under pressure. If disturbed, it gives off a faint smell resembling the water left in a vase after the stems have softened. The smell does not carry far in the hollow, and the surface soon closes over.

Most of its body occupies spaces that were never intended to remain accessible: the clearance beneath a pressure vessel, a settlement crack behind brickwork, the narrow interval between an original drain and the larger pipe fitted around it. It is very thin in these places. Elsewhere it gathers into cushions, or hangs in folded sacs inside abandoned chambers. The same tissue that passes beneath a slab as a film may fill the well beyond it to a depth of several feet.

Water runs through it.

There are channels enclosed within the flesh, and channels held open between the flesh and whatever it has attached itself to. These can be shut independently. Beneath one of the small square covers, a person once found six soft openings arranged around a bolt. Five closed when daylight reached them. The sixth continued to discharge clear water, with a regular hesitation in the flow.

The person replaced the cover.

Its movements are made by transferring fullness from one part to another. A cushion under a pipe subsides; a fold beside the wall becomes stiff enough to raise a plate. Something that looked like a seam in the clay rounds upward and holds its shape while water passes on either side. This accounts for some of the changing firmness underfoot. A foot placed on apparently solid mud may be resting on a swollen portion of the animal. When that portion empties, the mud loses its support.

It does not always empty immediately.

Along the old foundations, it has been compressed so often that it has developed a different surface. There the skin is thin, dark, and closely attached to the masonry. It does not swell much. The polished footholds remain usable, although the spaces beside them may alter several times in an hour. Under a standing person, very small adjustments continue: a slight hardening beneath the front of a boot, a release beneath the heel. These can be mistaken for the person’s own efforts to balance.

It has no single place from which to look out.

The submerged edges bear minute pits, generally clogged with silt. When washed clean, each pit contains a pale bead set far back in a soft collar. A shadow passing over them produces little response. A hand entering the water produces more. The collars turn toward the disturbance, though the beads themselves remain fixed. A droplet falling nearby causes them all to withdraw.

Farther inside are stiff hairs, sparse and colorless, which lie across narrow water passages. They register the pressure of a flow and the interruptions carried in it. A worm moving in the sediment makes a different interruption from a piece of grass. Footsteps on the bank reach these hairs through water confined beneath the foundations.

Much of what it knows of the world arrives compressed between hard surfaces.

The motor’s tremor is part of that world. So are the distant loading and unloading of the shore, the flex of the bowed wall, and the little changes that pass through the bank before the inlet visibly rises. Its body extends into the bank along the cable, though no one has established how far. At one point where the cable sheath was repaired, the surrounding material had to be cut away. It adhered in layers. Between the layers were fine chambers full of clear fluid.

The early rise in the inspection well may be a consequence of what it feels there. A small contraction deep in the bank could displace water into the yard several minutes before the tide reaches the visible inlet. The measurements inside the pump house establish the interval, but nothing about its purpose.

It feeds at surfaces.

Very fine matter gathers on its exposed skin: broken algae, softened wood fibers, fragments from the drains. In places where the current is slow, the skin raises a nap almost too short to see. Particles settle into this and are worked inward. A grain of sand is eventually released. A fleck of organic material disappears.

Larger food requires a pocket. One forms around a dead sandhopper or a drowned fly and closes with no more movement than a bubble collapsing. The pocket remains visible for hours as a small, dark blister. Later there may be a leg or a piece of wing on the mud.

The pale creatures under the roofing felt graze on its older surface. It tolerates them there. Sometimes they cluster along the boundary of a patch that is about to shed, lifting its edge with their mouthparts. The cast material is leathery and almost transparent when rinsed, but in the hollow it quickly takes on clay and becomes indistinguishable from the ground.

The shorebird eats these grazers when drainage exposes them. Its feet leave little forked impressions across the newly uncovered skin. The animal beneath does not close around them. The bird is quick, and the skin in those places is broad and slow.

It can withdraw more sharply from damage. An iron bar pushed into one of the fuller folds produces a contraction that travels beneath several adjacent slabs. Water rises from unrelated openings. A tray tilts. For perhaps a minute, the hollow seems busier everywhere except at the place touched, where the tissue has become small and tightly puckered.

Afterward it restores its contact with the iron cautiously, first with a clear film, then a thickened rim.

There are old injuries. One sac beneath the pump house has healed around a length of wire. Another is divided by a brick driven through it during a collapse. The two halves still fill and empty, but at different rates. Sometimes this pulls the brick against a metal bracket and produces the brief ticking heard outside.

The pump assists some of its circulation. How necessary it has become is uncertain. When the installation is switched off, the nearest folds remain full for an unusually long time. The western compartments drain and stay drained. After a day or two, moisture begins to bead along the mortar inside the brick house, including above the old water stains.

Restarting the motor restores the familiar pattern gradually. There is no immediate convulsion. One narrow passage opens, then another. A piece of silt detaches from a valve seat. By the following morning, the pools are again changing levels independently.

Whoever maintains the motor has learned to wait before deciding whether a repair has worked.

In the control cabinet, behind the lubrication instructions, there is a spare bearing wrapped in greaseproof paper. Below it lies a blunt wooden wedge. The wedge is used to hold a soft fold away from the motor footing while the mounting bolts are checked. One side has been worn smooth by handling. The other carries a shallow impression of the fold, as though the wood had spent a long time pressed against a thumb.

There may once have been several animals here. There may still be. In certain wells the flesh is finely ridged; in others it is smooth. Parts contract when neighboring parts expand, and sometimes a passage remains closed for weeks while both sides continue to live. Where two exposed edges meet, they pause, adhere, and eventually lose the visible seam between them.

No small, complete version has been found.

What has been found is a piece beneath a loose tile: a gray patch no larger than a coin, furnished with three little cavities and anchored around a grain of brick. After separation from the surrounding mud, it continued to fill one cavity and empty another. It lived in a jar of water from the hollow for several months. It grew along the bottom rather than upward, making use of the angle where glass met glass.

In fresh seawater it stopped moving. Returned to its original water, it resumed before evening.

The largest exposed folds have an old, abraded appearance. The sheltered undersides are tender. New tissue is always being laid down somewhere under the yard, extending a few inches into a loosened joint, abandoning a passage that has become too narrow, coating the interior of a chamber whose roof has just fallen in.

Its shelter is also the shape its body takes. It cannot be lifted free and laid out without cutting it apart.

At low water, beneath the curved iron cover, there is room to see a portion hanging clear of both mud and metal. It is thicker there than elsewhere, suspended in several unequal lobes. Each has a faint branching pattern inside. Water gathers at their tips but does not drip. When the motor starts, the smallest lobe draws upward into the others. A broad fold opens behind it, revealing a clean, pale interior.

It remains open for a little while.

Then water begins to rise through the floor, and the fold settles out of sight.
