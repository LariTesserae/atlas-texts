The first one you meet is clearing grit from a door pivot.

It has taken the door off its pins and laid it on three small stones. Kneeling beside the empty socket, it works with a slender pick, pausing after each stroke to lift out the loosened grains on a damp fingertip. There is already a neat crescent of them beside its knee.

You may have passed it earlier. At rest, with its back turned, it looks like a folded piece of the slope. Its outer surface is pale, finely layered, darkened here and there by water. What seemed to be a broken edge of stone is the ridge of a shoulder. What seemed a mineral seam opens when it reaches for another tool.

It notices you before you recognize it, but does not interrupt its work. The door must be rehung before the afternoon change. It sets a bowl a little closer to you with the back of its wrist, an unmistakable invitation to keep it from being kicked over.

Only after you have stood beside it for a while do you see that it is breathing.

The movement is slow and occurs along the flanks. Overlapping plates separate slightly, exposing a dull violet material beneath, then settle together again. No air seems to pass through its face. A film of moisture forms between the plates, gathers at their lower corners, and is drawn back inside.

Its head is blunt and somewhat flattened. Two dark recesses under a projecting brow contain organs you take for eyes, though they catch no light. A narrow opening lower down serves as a mouth. The chin has been repaired: a wedge of yellowish stone is bound into the pale material with stitches finer than hair.

It is not especially tall. Standing, it would reach your chest. But it seldom stands all the way up.

Its legs have an extra fold below the knee, and its feet are broad, divided almost to the heel into two grasping halves. The hands have three strong fingers and a shorter opposing hook. The fingers can spread flat against wet paving, each joint bending backward a little, giving them the purchase of a soft brush.

It uses its hands for walking whenever the ground becomes uncertain.

For the moment, they are occupied with the door.

When the socket is clean, it rubs a pinch of purple powder into the cup. Then it lifts the slab. The effort makes the plates along its neck rise. You move to help, and it stops you with one open palm.

It waits.

A thread of water in the floor channel hesitates, thickens, and begins to creep toward the rear wall.

Now it lifts again. The door comes easily. It slips the pivot into place, lowers the upper pin, and tests the balance with a fingertip.

Then it looks at you.

You have the impression that you have arrived in the middle of an ordinary morning, and have already demonstrated a dangerous ignorance.

***

There are others.

Not many, compared with the number of shelters. Some courts have been empty for so long that the door sockets have filled with the same crystals as the ledges. Elsewhere, a bowl has been rinsed recently, or a worn handline has acquired a new length of braid. Occupation is easier to recognize once you know that loose tools are always put into shallow depressions, never simply left on a slab.

Most of the inhabitants keep to the western fins during the brightest hours. They sleep in the blue interiors, their bodies flattened onto the raised shelves, arms tucked beneath them. A sleeping one looks smaller than an awake one. Its joints close tightly. Its face withdraws under the brow.

The sleeping shelves have grooves across their width. Into these, the sleepers fit the ridges of their backs.

When the pull changes, a body need not wake merely because its bed has become a wall.

Some shelters contain additional sockets above the sleeping place. These are for young ones, whose grasp is unreliable. Adults fasten a broad mineral-fiber band across them, tight enough to prevent a fall but loose enough to permit the slow opening of their sides.

The abandoned sandals would fit them. Their bare feet grip well, but long journeys on the brilliant wet paving damage the softer surfaces between the toes. Footwear is worn on safe stretches and removed for difficult ones. The little perforated plates spread to accommodate the foot, and can be folded into a packet no larger than a fist.

Ties wear out much faster than plates. In an inhabited room, spare ties hang from notches beneath the roof.

Their possessions are mostly things you have already seen, without understanding how much variation there is among them. A bowl may be for drinking, for grinding, for measuring a route, or for holding something that must never be allowed to become dry. They distinguish these at a glance. They find your habit of picking up whichever vessel is nearest almost as troubling as your willingness to cross a flat court without first touching the water.

***

They drink often and eat very slowly.

In sheltered places they cultivate thin films on slabs of selected stone. Nothing rises from the surface. There is no leaf or filament to see, only a darkening that develops over many seasons, purple at the edges and copper-bright where a scraper has passed.

The wild varnish under the pools is related to this food, but too dense and bitter to be taken directly. They grind it, wash it, spread it thinly, and carry the slabs between different seeps. Some must spend part of each day facing downward. Others are kept in nearly still water.

The moving water of the western lanes once powered mills for this work. The unattached wheel still turning between the fins belongs to a house whose grinding chamber has collapsed. No one has dismantled it. Sound from its paddles reaches several neighboring rooms, and they use it to judge how the afternoon is proceeding.

A meal consists of a little scraped paste and a great deal of water.

The paste disappears into the narrow mouth. For a while afterward, a warmer color shows beneath the flank plates. They sit together during this time, often with their shoulders touching. An adult may feed a younger one directly from the scraper, taking care to blunt its edge first.

They can go a long time without food. They cannot tolerate prolonged dryness.

An injured traveler is laid across the current in a floor channel, face supported on a small stone. Water passes under the body and through the loosened plates. The others remain nearby, turning it at intervals so that the flow reaches each seam.

This is also how they wash the very old.

***

They are not immune to the basin.

They slip. Doors trap fingers. A poor choice of route can leave someone clinging to a handhole for half a day, waiting for the path to become a path again. Several have scars where an outer plate tore away under a handline. One elderly inhabitant of the northern court has a foot that never fully closes.

What they possess is a finer warning.

Inside their bodies, beneath the pale armor, there are long chambers partly filled with dense fluid. As the local pull begins to alter, the fluid presses against sensitive folds. They feel an approaching change as you might feel a muscle beginning to cramp.

The sensation is directional, but not precise. Water still tells them more.

Every journey begins at a groove stone. They pour, watch, touch the standing face, and sometimes pour again. A traveler may wait an hour beside a favorable-looking groove because another person has put a hand on the stone and quietly disagreed.

Their old paths preserve knowledge too complicated to explain at a doorway.

One short passage is avoided when a certain scarp has stopped chattering. A shelf is safe while the hollow booming beyond the ridge remains low, but not after its pitch rises. There is a crossing where the proper signal is silence from three particular crystal combs.

To you, the afternoon noise conceals events. To them, it announces which routes are closing.

Their voices work within that noise.

At close quarters, they make soft clicks and a low, granular murmur. Across a court they strike the paving with a heel or the side of a bowl. Messages travel through the fitted stones more reliably than through the air. Indoors, with one hand resting against the wall, a person can follow a conversation in the next room.

They are capable of shouting. It is an ugly, tearing sound, made by forcing the flank plates apart.

You hear it once, when a child ventures onto an overhang whose pebbles have begun to loosen.

Everyone moves.

***

The young are darker than the adults and have very little of the pale outer covering. Their limbs look too supple for the country. They can squeeze through drainage holes and come out wet, carrying grit in every seam.

They are fascinated by small objects that behave unpredictably.

A round pebble may occupy several children through an entire shift. They set it on different surfaces, withdraw their hands, argue, and retrieve it from increasingly inconvenient places. Adults interrupt only when the experiment approaches a crack through the floor.

One child has learned to place a pebble on the underside of a ledge shortly before the pull changes. It takes visitors there and invites them to knock it down.

When they fail, it makes a rapid series of clicks, folding its hands beneath its chin.

The adults make the same sound more quietly.

There are fewer children than sleeping bands in the shelters. Yet the bands are maintained. Frayed ones are replaced even in rooms that currently house only one old person.

You cannot tell whether this is hope, custom, or simply sensible use of a room.

***

They visit the eastern lake, but seldom linger.

Their shadows behave ordinarily there. It is the stones they carry that leave remnants.

A bowl set down during a drink may retain its dark circle on the white silt after being lifted. Sandal plates leave a scattering of small marks, briefly resembling tracks. The people avoid putting tools in the water unless they intend to leave something behind.

There are places where they do so deliberately.

Near the shadow of the collapsed shelter, a low stone platform stands just clear of the lake. Here they lay out the possessions of the dead: a scraper, a bowl, perhaps a door handle polished to a particular hand. The objects are lowered into the shallows one by one and allowed to rest.

Later, the objects are recovered.

They are useful. Someone will need them.

The dark shapes remain, moving gradually away from the place where they were made. For several days, relatives return to look. They can identify a bowl’s shadow from a slight unevenness in its rim, and sometimes follow it along the shore until the sun becomes too fierce.

The bodies themselves do not go into the lake.

They are placed in wet recesses above the inhabited courts, wrapped loosely in fiber. Over a long time the softer material leaves them. The plates separate. Water lays new minerals over the old surfaces.

Eventually there is a pale hollow full of ordinary-looking stone.

A fragment may be brought down and used to mend a damaged joint. The yellow wedge in the door-maker’s chin came from such a recess. When asked about it, the door-maker touches it, then points toward a shelter across the court.

You never learn whether the gesture means parent, companion, teacher, or merely the person who once lived there.

***

At evening, when the grinding begins far below, they stop what they are doing.

No one runs to look through the cracks.

The children are brought away from the open paving. Bowls are moved from edges. Those working on a roof descend before the first dust falls. Inside the shelters, people spread their hands on the floor.

They remain like that until the sound has passed.

Perhaps they are listening. Perhaps pressure in the stone tells them something the sound cannot. Once, you try to imitate them. You feel the prolonged vibration through your palms, a faint aching in the bones of your wrists, and nothing you can interpret.

Beside you, the door-maker shifts one hand a finger’s breadth to the left.

Afterward, it sits for a long time without rising.

The others resume their work. Someone brings fresh water. A young one comes to lean against its shoulder and is gently moved to the other side, away from the mended chin.

You had thought their lives were arranged around waiting: waiting for routes to open, for food to ripen, for a body to heal. In the days that follow, you begin to notice how much they do within the short intervals available. They carry a slab to another seep. They visit. They quarrel over a borrowed wedge. They cross a court to hear a story they have plainly heard before.

And they repair things.

On your last morning, the door-maker is working at an empty shelter well beyond the inhabited fins. The path there is inconvenient. No recent tracks disturb the grit inside.

It has brought a new length of handline, coiled over one shoulder, and a bowl to replace the split one beneath the standing stone.

You help carry the bowl into position.

The groove is clogged near its foot. The door-maker clears it with the same little pick it used on the pivot. It pours in water, studies the flow, then adds a thin shim beneath one side of the bowl.

Inside, the sleeping shelf is dry. The door turns reluctantly, but it turns.

Before leaving, it places the pick in a recess by the entrance. There is another tool beside it, badly worn, whose handle bears the shallow impressions of a different hand.

Then it leads you back by a route that seems at first to be uphill.
