The animal beside your leg has a chipped place on its forward rim. New material has grown beneath it, pale and slightly translucent, but the old damage remains as a notch. Three grains of dark sand are lodged there.

It is about the size of a serving dish. Up close, its resemblance to stone is imperfect. The gray of its back lies in layers, some cloudy, some clear enough to show a dull green branching beneath. Its edges are covered in short, dense fibers. Where these touch your trouser leg, they flatten and slowly lift again.

For a while you cannot find its head.

Then two plates part. A blunt wedge of flesh emerges, carrying a row of dark points along either side. The points do not quite look like eyes. They remain directed toward the ground while a smaller organ, folded beneath the face, opens against the cloth.

It tastes the salt there.

The contact is cool and surprisingly firm. After a moment the organ folds away. The head follows. A little air leaves from somewhere under the shell, and the animal settles more closely into your shadow.

It has not mistaken you for another of its kind. Its movements against a neighbor would be different: a brief pressure, a release, then a second pressure answered from the other side. Here it explores once and rests. Whatever else you may be, you are presently large, still, and keeping the sun off a patch of ground.

***

The broad-backed animals belong to several kinds.

Some have a definite front and spend their lives turning laboriously toward whatever interests them. Others can bring out a feeding head from either end. The largest have several small mouths beneath the perimeter, each served by its own cluster of limbs. Watching one move, you cannot always tell whether it has chosen a direction or merely continued the movement of the first feet to find purchase.

The palm-sized plated creatures are not all their young. Many are adults. They slip beneath the greater bodies to feed on softened films and the debris caught in skin folds. Their shells overlap loosely enough to let them bend around a stone. In danger, they draw together into a hard little oval, leaving their legs entirely enclosed.

The true young of the large grazers look unfinished. Their backs are soft and nearly colorless. A pinkish line contracts inside them. They cannot retain enough water to cross much bare ground, so their first journeys take place beneath adults, passing from one damp underside to the next.

Sometimes a youngster reaches the edge of a group and waits there, its front half in the light.

An adult may move to cover it. More often, nothing happens until the ordinary rearrangement of bodies provides a way through.

Many young do not survive their first dry interval. Their shells leave thin, curled flakes among the grit.

***

Touch carries farther here than sound.

Beneath the soft border of a shell are rows of pressure-sensitive pits. A neighbor’s weight, its warmth, the minute tightening before it begins to rise: all are perceptible through the joined margins. An animal can prepare to move before the body beside it has displaced any ground.

This is why a crowded resting group seldom traps its smallest members. The warning travels through the pavement, and narrow spaces open ahead of them.

It is also how disputes begin.

A broad-backed grazer returning to a favored hollow may find it occupied. It presses its rim beneath the stranger’s and holds it there. For a long time neither seems to move. Then one shell begins to tilt.

These contests can last through the warmest part of a day. There are no striking limbs, no sudden rushes. The weaker animal gradually loses contact with the cool ground. At last it withdraws, dragging a fringe of damp dust behind it.

The good resting places are not interchangeable. One hollow collects dew early. Another lies over a crack that holds moisture deep into the dry season. Some animals return to the same depression so often that their undersides and the stone acquire corresponding shapes. Dislodged, they may spend hours fitting themselves to a place that never fits quite well enough.

***

The colonies on their backs have histories of their own.

In one shallow basin live pale scavengers with antennae longer than their bodies. They shelter their eggs beneath a lifting plate on the western side. A second colony, darker and shorter-legged, occupies the grit around the basin’s edge. Between them is a narrow strip kept almost bare.

When food falls there, both colonies approach. Individuals touch antennae, retreat, and return with others. The boundary shifts by the width of a grain.

Their host may carry this dispute for years without responding to it.

Elsewhere, small burrowing creatures irritate the living seams. The shell-bearer rubs against a raised band of bedrock until the infested plate is scraped clean. Residents flee across its back. Eggs, dust, and months of accumulated shelter fall together onto the ground.

The oldest hosts are not necessarily the gentlest places to live. Their worn shells offer deep refuges, but a crack can suddenly reach living tissue. Fluid wells up. White scavengers gather. A familiar hollow becomes unsafe before its inhabitants have finished abandoning it.

On a healthy animal, though, the back can support a colony through several generations. Some of its residents never touch the plateau itself. Their whole territory travels a few body lengths in a season.

***

The builders of the hollow nests are smaller than their structures suggest.

They have broad foreheads, short digging limbs, and a wet-looking band around the mouth. Pressed against a wall, this band leaves a bead of mineral-rich secretion. The animal draws its head sideways, smoothing the bead into a ridge, then waits for it to stiffen.

A repaired doorway may contain the work of several hundred such passes.

No builder sees the whole roof. Each responds to what is close: an edge exposed to dry air, a crack admitting too much light, the touch of another animal waiting behind it. From inside, the walls are not smooth. They contain shelves, pockets, and narrow turnings polished by repeated passage.

Some chambers hold young. Some hold damp vegetable matter brought in after rain. Others are empty except for an adult lying across the entrance, its body regulating the draft.

The three knocks heard at evening come from a builder striking the wall with its hardened forehead. Nearby occupants stop moving. After a pause, some begin again in a different passage.

Other sequences receive other responses. It would take a long stay to distinguish a summons from a warning, or either from the sound of an animal trying to shift something too large for a doorway.

***

The tall browsers are less patient than they look.

A raised foot may be waiting for space, but it may also be testing the faint air rising between shells. Sensitive folds above the spreading sole detect moisture. The animal places its weight where the ground will yield least, then lowers its long face to inspect the seams.

It eats plants, certainly. It also eats young shell-bearers.

The narrow lips can reach beneath a rim and draw out a soft juvenile without disturbing the adult above. The movement is quick enough to disappear within the browser’s otherwise measured progress. A swallow passes down the long throat. The foot lifts again.

The winged animals take larger prey. On landing, they fold into an angular canopy beneath which small creatures gather. Some merely rest there. Others are caught by a limb that closes inward with a speed absent from flight.

After feeding, the flyer may remain until morning. Its shade becomes shelter again. The little animals continue to arrive, keeping closer to its outer edges.

***

Around the empty oval, even these visitors alter their routes.

A browser approaching it slows, touches the stone with one open foot, and places that foot elsewhere. A shell-bearer turned toward the interior does not recoil. It makes a gradual correction until it is traveling along the margin.

No smell distinguishes the place. Beneath a hand, the stone feels ordinary.

After sunset, when the neighboring shells still release stored heat, a coolness gathers there close to the ground. Dust blown across it sometimes settles in faint curved lines. By morning the lines have usually vanished.

It may be something beneath the rock. It may be a condition of the rock itself. The animals give you only the boundary. None stays long enough inside it to reveal what the boundary is for.

***

By full dusk, the animal beside your leg has turned slightly.

Its chipped rim now rests against your ankle. On its back, a creature no bigger than a seed emerges from a seam and crosses the shallow dome. It stops where a single bead of moisture has formed, drinks, and returns by almost the same route.

When you stand, the shell-bearer remains still. The place beneath you is cooler now, but it is also exposed.

Eventually a foot unfolds. Then another.

It moves toward the crowded drainage line, carrying the three grains of dark sand in its notch, the green branching beneath its plates, and the small inhabitant that has already withdrawn for the night. Ahead, two resting shells ease apart. The opening is barely wide enough.

It enters edge first.

For several minutes, the neighboring bodies adjust around it. Then their rims settle together, and only the chipped place lets you find it again.
