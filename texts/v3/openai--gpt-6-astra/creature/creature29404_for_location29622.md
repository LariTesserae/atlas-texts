The beetle carries dust in the joints of its armor. Its back is nearly black, but the edges of its wing cases are brown where the thin light passes through them. One antenna is shorter than the other. At the broken end, it has nothing with which to feel the stone, and so it travels a little crookedly, turning its intact side toward whatever gap it is considering.

It is small enough to disappear behind a grain that would scarcely trouble the skin of a human palm.

Even so, there are places it cannot go. It follows the seam beneath the roof until its back touches stone, then lowers itself and tries another step. The passage tightens. For a while it remains there, antenna moving in the space ahead. Finally it reverses, six feet finding their former holds in a different order.

Behind it, a mite enters the same gap without slowing.

The mites are not all alike. Some are round and dark, with legs tucked so close beneath them that they resemble moving seeds. Others are pale, almost soft-looking, and carry their legs widely over the dust. A few have long hairs that brush the walls before their bodies arrive. Under sufficient magnification, each would become an elaborate animal. Here, without magnification, their differences appear mostly as differences in motion: a steady crawl, a quick advance followed by stillness, a laborious turning around of something that seemed to have no front.

One has a yellow particle adhering to its back. It takes the particle under an overhang and brings it out again. For several hours, this is how that particular mite can be recognized. Then the particle comes off.

Beneath the dead fly is a pale larva.

It has worked into the protected angle between the fly’s abdomen and the stone. Its head is darker than its body. When it feeds, the movement is concentrated there: a small drawing inward, a pause, another drawing inward. The rest of it remains pressed against its meal. Through its skin, something cloudy and brown shows along the gut.

The fly is enormous beside it. A folded wing extends beyond the sheltering body, catching enough entrance light to reveal a few veins. One leg reaches uphill and ends in a pair of claws still shaped to grip. The larva feeds where the joints and membranes offer access. Over time, the fly becomes a roof held up by its harder parts.

Other animals come beneath that roof. A mite passes close to the larva’s mouth and changes direction. Two springtails remain in the shadow of the thorax. There is room for them all, though not always in the same place.

The springtails have the color of something that has never needed to be looked at. Their bodies are faintly translucent, their antennae delicate, their feet almost lost against the pale deposits. They graze with their heads lowered. Where the roof holds a little moisture, they linger.

A disturbance can make one vanish from its position. Its jump is too fast to follow in the narrow light. But this is poor country for jumping. Above it is a ceiling; beside it, a blade of sulfur; below, a crevice from which climbing out may take much longer than falling in. Most of the time it walks. It tests the edge of a grain and steps down carefully.

The spider has accommodated itself to one recess so closely that its outline is difficult to separate from the recess’s shape. Its abdomen occupies the deeper part. Its legs reach forward and sideways, bent at different angles according to the available room.

A line runs from one foreleg into the passage.

The mountain’s tremor reaches that line through both its anchors. Wind adds other movements. Sometimes a loose particle strikes the silk and hangs there, giving the spider a brief, useless message. Sometimes the spider draws the line toward itself, waits, and releases it.

When prey does arrive, the movement is small and local: a snag, a struggle, an uneven pulling that continues after the first contact. The spider comes out far enough to gather what is caught. Its legs seem suddenly too numerous for the passage. Then they fold back into the recess, taking the disturbance with them.

There are inhabitants here that never cross a passage at all. They adhere to stone in films thinner than the dust lying over them. Where warmth, moisture, and the vent’s chemistry permit, microbial cells divide. Elsewhere a patch dwindles or persists without visibly changing. A grazing animal can pass across one of these surfaces and leave no mark that the entrance light would reveal.

The beetle returns to such a patch. Its mouthparts work against the stone.

During a change in the machinery below, it stops. One foot remains lifted. The tremor strengthens, becomes finer, settles. The beetle puts the foot down almost where it had intended.

By night, the residents occupy a slightly different hollow, though the rock has scarcely moved. The cold has entered along the roof. A ledge used during the afternoon is empty now. The springtails have gathered farther inward, and the beetle has worked its way behind a splinter where its body fits with little room to spare.

Deeper still, fresh sulfur narrows a crack.

Along the tolerable margin, the animals rest, feed, touch one another, turn aside. An egg lies wedged among three grains, its surface holding a little dust. There is no clear route from it to the entrance. There need not yet be one. Whatever hatches will begin with the space beside its head.
