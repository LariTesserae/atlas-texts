The first one is in the room with you during the rain.

It lies along the joint between the floor and the back wall, where a narrow strip of darkness remains even when the doorway is full of light. You have looked at it several times. What makes you look again is the disappearance of a small white chip of limestone. The chip was beside the joint. Now it is moving along it, steadily, against the flow of water.

A hand comes out to turn it.

There are four fingers, very short, with broad ends. They close around the chip without lifting it, rotate it once, and settle it into a shallow depression. Water gathers behind it. A little of the flow leaves the wall and joins the groove beside your foot.

The hand withdraws.

For some time nothing else happens. Then the dark strip separates from the wall.

The creature is about as long as your forearm, though length is difficult to judge while it is folded. Its body is low and broad through the ribs, narrowing behind into a muscular tail. Three pairs of limbs lie close against its sides. The front pair are smaller than the others. At rest, all six fit into hollows along the body, leaving almost nothing for an edge of stone to catch.

Its skin has the color of wet silt. Along its back are overlapping plates, thin enough to bend, with pale margins like the edges of shale. Grit has collected between them. In one place a minute green growth follows a crease.

The head is blunt. Its eyes sit far apart beneath ridges that can close over them, and the mouth extends farther back than you expect. Beneath the jaw, soft folds open and shut.

It does not turn its head toward you. Instead it places both front hands flat on the floor.

You feel an unreasonable need to stop shifting your weight.

When you do, it moves again.

***

They live chiefly in the thickness of the city.

There are spaces beneath the paving that have never been rooms: gaps left between bedrock and a level floor, channels beside foundations, long cavities where a course of softer limestone has dissolved. There are also rooms that have become such spaces, their entrances blocked by settled debris while a hand’s breadth remains beneath a lintel.

The inhabitants can pass through these places with their backs brushing the ceiling. Their ribs overlap slightly as they compress themselves. In a particularly narrow opening they empty their lungs, draw the legs into their recesses, and advance by a slow movement of the whole body. On the other side they remain still while the folds beneath the jaw pulse.

They are neither wholly creatures of water nor comfortable away from it. They can cross a dry court, but do so quickly, keeping to shadow. In deep water they swim awkwardly. Their feet reach for a bottom that is no longer there.

What suits them is a moving film over stone, enough to wet the belly, with air immediately above it.

The city contains miles of this condition.

A flooded avenue that looks impassable to you may be poor territory for them as well. They use the curb beneath the surface, or the ledges inside its drains. Conversely, a nearly dry stair can be a busy route if seepage runs beneath its outer edge. Their occupied districts do not correspond to the neighborhoods visible from the ridges. A single inhabited tract may pass under three deserted quarters and emerge at a spring on the far side of a hill.

Some houses contain them only in winter. Some support the same gathering for longer than any exposed wall remains unchanged.

They do not fill the ruins. Whole slopes are empty.

***

Most are smaller than the one in your shelter.

In a court below, after the rain, you find several no longer than a finger. They are feeding on the submerged face of a step, braced against the current with their hind feet. Their backs are almost transparent. Through them you can see a dark loop of gut and, farther forward, two small movements that do not keep the same rhythm.

Each has a broad pale mouth, which it presses to the algae and draws sideways.

One loses its hold. It tumbles down the step, opens all six limbs, and catches against a stem of sedge. The others continue feeding. Presently it climbs back by a longer route.

Adults eat the small things washed into the channels: larvae, worms, drowned insects, the soft bodies that live beneath stones. They also graze, especially late in the cold season. Around a well-used refuge, the limestone is cleaned in low crescents. Above the reach of their mouths the algae remains dark.

Food is brought inward from the marshes. You may see a strand of roots moving against the water, then discover the creature beneath it. The roots are packed with little snails. Sometimes two inhabitants carry a clump between them, each pausing whenever the other pauses, until it has passed through an opening apparently too small for either.

Inside are shallow feeding basins. These require very little water. A raised grain in the floor is enough to retain it.

The young gather there with their heads pointed upstream.

***

Their hands are not equally skilled.

The front hands can pick a seed out of silt. The middle pair hold the body steady and carry things beneath it. The rear feet are broad, with blunt nails that engage irregularities too small for you to notice. When an adult climbs a wet wall, there is no haste or slipping. Each foot tests its place before accepting weight.

They keep objects in dry pockets above their resting channels.

A pocket may contain three rounded pebbles, a curved piece of shell, and a limestone splinter worn thin at one end. It may contain nothing but a little heap of coarse sand. These are useful distinctions. Coarse sand slows a leak without closing it. A shell lifts a mat of roots. A flat pebble placed upright can divide a current that would otherwise enter a sleeping hollow.

The creature in your shelter retrieves its white chip before it leaves. By then the surge from the upper streets has passed. Removing the chip restores the small flow along the wall.

It carries the stone beneath its chest, held by the middle hands.

You begin to notice other arrangements.

Two pebbles stand edge to edge under a drain mouth. Behind them, silt has settled into a tongue that directs the water left. A rush stem passes through a crack with both ends trimmed. At the bottom of a stair, loose grit has been swept into a ridge no higher than your smallest fingernail.

Much of the city’s drainage works without attention. Some of it works because attention is still being given.

***

They detect one another through the stone.

Their palms contain pale pads, visible when a foot lifts. At rest, at least one pair of these pads remains pressed to the floor. A moving creature pauses at intervals to listen in this way, its body slightly raised so that the rush of water against its belly does not interfere.

Their own signals are made with the hard underside of the jaw.

You hear one only once: three faint contacts in the vault behind you, spaced irregularly. It sounds like a pebble settling.

The answer comes too softly for you to hear, but the creature beside the wall turns toward it.

Water changes the reach of these signals. A full joint carries movement differently from an empty one. A loose slab answers differently once rain has weighted the silt beneath it. They test familiar places repeatedly, learning the day’s connections.

After a shower, exchanges travel through streets that were separate in the morning.

It is then that the adults emerge.

They do not all go downstream. Some climb toward the arriving water. Others wait at junctions, touching those that pass with a front hand beneath the jaw. Two may remain in contact for several minutes. A third works around them without apparent interest.

There are places where many stop: a roofed landing, a broad seam under a retaining wall, the underside of a bridge so low that you took it for a threshold. The floors there are worn smooth across their whole width.

They bring food to these places, and chips of stone, and young carried flat against the belly. The young grip folds in the adult’s skin with all their feet. When put down, they retain the shape of the body they were holding before slowly uncurling.

***

The very old become pale.

Their plates thicken and lose flexibility. They can no longer enter the narrower channels, and settle in more open chambers where the water remains dependable. Some are longer than your arm. They lie with their hands spread over several joints in the floor.

Other inhabitants visit them.

At first this appears to be feeding, and sometimes it is. A visitor leaves a snail or a strip of softened root within reach. But often nothing is brought. The smaller creature places its jaw against the floor beside one of the old one’s hands. There follows a long period of slight movements: a finger lifted, the throat tightened, the jaw touching down again.

Then the visitor departs, sometimes by a route different from the one it used to arrive.

In one chamber, an old inhabitant has lost a front hand. It listens with the blunt end of the limb pressed into a bed of fine gravel. Someone keeps that gravel clear of silt.

***

Whether their ancestors built the visible city is difficult to say.

The surviving rooms are larger than they need. The stairs have risers they must climb one at a time. Yet many thresholds contain secondary passages precisely suited to their bodies, and some of those passages were made when the stones were laid. You can see it in the untouched bedding around them.

Other openings are later work.

In a sheltered drain, where weather could not easily have reached, the limestone bears countless shallow scrapes. Each is small. Together they have lowered the floor by several inches.

Elsewhere a channel has been abandoned halfway through. The tool marks stop at a yellow band in the rock. Water found another way before the work was finished, or the workers did.

They show no interest in restoring a roof simply because it has fallen. A collapse may provide better chambers than the rooms it buries. They clear what must remain open, shore up certain small cavities, and leave the rest to settle.

Their work can take a long time. At a seep above the marsh, one creature holds a flake against the limestone and draws it back and forth, making a sound too slight to separate from the water. It rests often. The groove beneath its hands is already deep, but you cannot tell how much of it was made today.

***

Toward evening, the streets seem empty again.

The last water from the high terraces arrives warm from stone that has caught the sun. It enters the lower drains in a brief, perceptible change of temperature. Heads appear beneath ledges. Hands are set against the paving.

Far north, rain is still crossing the plateau. By tomorrow its water will have reached districts you cannot see.

In your shelter, the groove beside your foot has diminished to a silver thread. You move a little to make room for it, although it was never in danger of touching you.

Something taps beneath the threshold.

After a while, farther inside the wall, something else replies.

The room has places for both of you. You occupy the high, dry middle of the floor, where a body can sit without interrupting anything. Along the edges, through passages kept open by hands no broader than a leaf, the inhabitants continue moving between the rain and their homes.
