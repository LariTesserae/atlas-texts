There are animals here that spend most of their lives keeping another animal’s mouth open.

They live beside the intakes of the great armored bodies in the channels. In the intervals between compressions, when the water slackens and a little of it runs backward, they can be seen braced across the openings: pale legs against dark flesh, a narrow body trembling under the effort. When suction returns, they fold flat. Brine passes over them. Their legs remain locked.

A large channel animal may carry twenty or thirty. From a distance they resemble damaged parts of its armor.

They are not attached to it. They can leave.

Each has six limbs, though the front pair are so short and so deeply tucked beneath the head that they look like mouthparts. The middle legs end in broad, grooved pads. The hind legs are longer than the body and bend twice before reaching the ground. At rest, these lie folded along the shell, their joints making two small humps behind the head.

The shell is white when new. Within a season its edges darken, and black grains accumulate in the seams. Old individuals are almost indistinguishable from the stone except where a plate has been recently shed.

Underneath, they are soft and yellow-gray. Their bellies bear rows of shallow cups. These do not grip. Each contains a bead of fluid held beneath a transparent skin, and in each bead hangs a darker speck. The specks shift when the ground moves. An animal lifting its belly from the stone loses most of what it knows about the country.

This is why they dislike swimming.

They can do it, badly, with all six legs spread. Swept into a channel, one will usually turn belly-up and wait to strike something solid. Many drown within reach of a surface they cannot recognize.

On stone they are deliberate and difficult to dislodge. They advance by planting the middle feet, extending the hind legs, and drawing the body forward between them. Every few steps they settle their undersides to the ground. A procession of them appears to hesitate repeatedly over nothing.

They are listening to the pumps.

A healthy intake does not open all the way. Its rim tightens before each compression, leaving a narrow passage through which the water accelerates. The pale attendants brace themselves across this passage and keep it from closing too soon. They also remove grit from the valve folds and eat the soft growth that gathers there.

Their work is not gentle. The short forelimbs have hooked ends. An attendant feeds by holding a fold of living membrane between them and scraping it with the hard lower edge of its head.

The host flinches. Sometimes its whole flank contracts.

The attendant stops scraping but does not let go.

An intake without attendants gradually loses its regularity. Its knocks become double knocks. Warm water escapes through openings meant to draw cold water in. The neighboring animals shift away, leaving the failing one a little more room than it needs.

Then the smaller creatures that live beneath its belly begin to depart.

An attendant will stay much longer. It continues cleaning a valve that no longer shuts, braced against a current too weak to flatten its shell. Eventually it walks inside.

No one could mistake this for being swallowed. It goes in during the outward flow, feeling ahead with its long hind legs turned forward over its back. Sometimes it returns carrying a strip of dark tissue. Sometimes several enter, and the host’s rhythm improves.

Sometimes none come out.

Their young live elsewhere.

The eggs are laid in stone impressions, preferably those that resemble no living animal nearby. A parent tests a hollow by settling into it, rocking very slightly, then moving a body-length away and testing the stone again. It may repeat this for hours. A hollow accepted one day may be abandoned the next, even if nothing visible has changed.

The eggs themselves are flat and nearly clear. They overlap like scales in a patch no larger than the parent’s underside. Water cannot lift them. Each is joined to the stone by a short, soft stalk that enters pores too small to see.

During a discharge the stalks darken first.

The young hatch with their limbs already spread. For several seconds they remain in the shape of the clutch, touching one another at the feet. Then the outermost individuals begin to walk, and the patch unravels.

They do not seek the warmth of the channels. Their belly organs are still cloudy and poorly formed; the pressure of a nearby pump would keep them flattened until they starved. Instead they climb onto the mobile grazers and settle in the polished depressions where neighboring shells rub together.

There they eat films, cast skin, and the soft matter trapped in damaged joints. A grazer may carry a dozen without appearing to notice.

For the young attendants, a moving host is a way across ground they could not cross alone. It is also a prolonged education in danger. They feel the grazer flatten before a discharge, accelerate before a rising tide, and stiffen when the stone beneath it begins to bulge.

Their first clear response is to hold tighter.

Later, it is to let go.

The difference determines which reach adulthood.

A grazer passing over one of the folding predators becomes light at the feet just before the seams rise. The young attendants release themselves at that instant. Most fall into the closing folds with everything else. A few are thrown outward by the first upward stroke and strike the wet stone beyond.

Those survivors lie still for a long time. Then they begin to look for an intake.

Finding a host is not merely a matter of entering a crowded channel. Every great animal has a different rhythm, and an attendant must fit its movements into that rhythm closely enough to avoid being crushed.

A newcomer waits beside an opening, lowering its belly to the armor between pulses. It touches the valve with one forelimb. Withdraws. Touches again.

The resident attendants may strike at it.

They do this with their hind legs, delivering short, sideways blows without abandoning their braces. Along an old intake, the stone-colored shells are often notched at precisely the height of a newcomer’s head.

Admission is easiest after injury. When a valve tears or a plate is displaced, there are more edges to hold than the residents have legs. A waiting animal can take one of these edges and remain. If the host survives, it has acquired a place.

Once established, attendants seldom leave voluntarily during the warmer months. They feed, shed their shells, and mate in the intervals when their host sleeps. Mating is accomplished side by side, with both animals retaining at least two feet on the intake rim. Afterward they resume their positions, sometimes on opposite sides from before.

They recognize their host even after it has moved.

They do not follow its sediment or its heat. Displaced individuals will cross several warm outflows, stopping at each, then continue toward a weaker current farther away. They can recognize a familiar compression through a considerable thickness of stone.

In winter this becomes less useful. The pumps slow until the intervals between them are long enough for frost to fasten a foot to a plate. Attendants lose limbs. Some are sealed alive beneath the glaze around an opening, still moving their forelimbs in a chamber scarcely larger than their bodies.

Their companions scrape these chambers open when they can.

They eat the trapped legs first. A frozen limb comes away cleanly; the living part withdraws beneath the shell. Once freed, the damaged animal moves deeper into the intake, where short legs can reach both walls.

Thus the smallest, darkest attendants tend to live farthest inside.

There are individuals with no full-length limbs left. Their shells have thickened into uneven wedges. They advance by flexing the edges of their bellies against the membrane, and their sensory cups have enlarged until they occupy almost the whole underside.

These old animals no longer help to hold the opening. They lodge behind it.

They are present in some of the hosts that stop pumping earliest before an electrical storm.

When the deep knocks come, however, the old ones emerge.

Not all of them. Not even most. Here and there, along miles of crowded channel, a blackened wedge works its way past the younger attendants and drops into the water.

It sinks immediately.

On the bottom it waits through several compressions, letting the pale silt settle over it. Then it moves away from the host, pulling itself along with whatever remains of its limbs.

The journey can take days. Some cross only a few yards. Others pass beneath packed bodies and through channels that are dry for half of every tide. The younger attendants do not accompany them.

They go to the basins where new stone rises.

There, among impressions of shells and bellies, the old animals spread themselves as flat as they can. Their undersides swell. The membranes over the sensory cups become so thin that the dark specks are visible from above, through the gaps in the shell.

Sometimes the rising slab bears a hollow that fits one exactly.

Sometimes the hollow is longer than the animal, with places for legs it no longer has.

It settles there anyway.

As the basin fills, sediment gathers against its sides. Small grazers cross its back. A tide may cover it and withdraw without producing any visible response. Beneath it, the fluid in the sensory cups moves slowly, though the stone is still.

Then another knock comes.

Close to the animal, the water draws inward. Its shell plates separate a little. What escapes between them is neither a call nor a pumping stroke, but a brief, low tremor that can be felt through a hand resting on the same slab.

Several others answer across the basin.

Afterward, some of the old attendants return to the channels. They are pale underneath again, and ravenously hungry. They enter the first intake that will admit them.

Others remain in their hollows as the stone rises around their edges.

By the next tide there is no gap into which a forelimb could be inserted. Only the top of the shell is exposed, wet and dark, with a little water gathering along one side.

Far out in the channels, a young attendant pauses in the middle of cleaning a valve.

It loosens one foot, sets it down again, and waits for its host’s next compression.
