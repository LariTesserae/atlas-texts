What lives beneath the roots is easiest to mistake for a thinner part of the root.

It occupies the places where two great wooden surfaces approach without touching. There, in the blue clearance between them, something pale spans the water. At a distance it resembles a seam of connective tissue, a strap holding one swelling to another. But its edges are free. There are toes along them.

The toes are blunt and widely spaced, with a little dark nail sunk into the tip of each. They rest against the wood without gripping it. Between them, the pale material gathers into folds.

This is an animal’s underside.

Its back faces the deeper water. It is green-grey, finely pebbled, and interrupted by smooth oval patches through which the internal anatomy is visible: clear partitions, a few broad blood vessels, organs the colour of wet chestnuts. No part is armoured. Across the shoulders, if those are shoulders, the skin has the thick, yielding appearance of the inside of a cheek.

The central body would fit inside a modest house. In this place that makes it a small creature.

Its limbs do not fit inside any house.

Six leave the body at uneven intervals, although there is nothing malformed about the arrangement. Each has its own proportion and direction. Two extend into the narrow darkness beneath a root buttress. One lies flat along a terrace, following every softened irregularity of the wood. Another reaches upward to the underside of a leaf so distant that the limb disappears before its point of contact can be seen.

They are not tentacles. There are joints in them, long shallow changes of angle beneath the skin. Toward their ends, they divide. The divisions divide again, becoming flat, delicately fingered structures laid across the living surfaces of the plant.

An entire slope is occupied by the hand of this animal.

Its head is close to the central body, almost withdrawn between the bases of the limbs. A broad jaw gives it an expression of mild obstinacy. The nostrils are sealed crescents. Along the lower lip hangs a fringe of short translucent rods, each with a dark thread down its centre.

There are four eyes.

Two are small, set high and angled into the immense open water. The others face forward beneath a soft brow. These are not immense eyes, not the luminous globes of an animal meant to collect the last available light. They are ordinary-looking eyes, brown around the pupils, with a fine pale rim where the globe meets the lid.

The lids are unequally lowered.

That slight asymmetry does more than anything else to make the creature unmistakable. The long limbs might belong to the plant. The exposed organs might be another of its mechanisms. But this face has a particular shape of attention.

One of its larger eyes is directed toward a silver fish beside the root wall.

The fish is perhaps twenty metres away. Its tail is curved. A narrow stripe along its flank catches the daylight, and a darker stripe below it does not. The animal beneath the root has its jaw slightly open, revealing small square teeth and a tongue with a deep groove down the middle.

It is not poised to eat the fish. Nothing in its posture has the concentration of a strike. Its mouth is simply open, as a hand elsewhere is open.

Along the lengths of its limbs are other organs of perception.

Some are cups, scarcely larger than the toes, with thin membranes stretched across their interiors. Some are seams of black tissue fitted into the elbow folds. Others resemble clusters of unripe berries, each berry clear except for a single metallic speck.

The cups face the water. The black seams lie against the roots. The clear clusters occupy the branching points.

Through these structures the animal possesses the roar.

The sound is not merely loud to it. It has breadth and contour. The low presence within a root, the higher density beneath a leaf, the deep concentration among the green swellings: these are distinct, as a face is distinct from the space beside it. Where a limb touches wood, the enormous labour of the plant is available to the creature with an intimacy that hearing alone cannot suggest.

Its whole anatomy is fitted to that intimacy. There are scarcely any muscles in the farthest branches of the limbs. Instead, there are layers of nerves, broad translucent bands laid one above another. Near the body they thicken into white cords. Under the smooth skin of the neck, the cords enter a structure folded so closely that it resembles the dark tissue beneath the leaves.

The resemblance is not identity. The animal is not an organ of the plant.

Its toes have their own pads. Its blood is enclosed within its own vessels. A thin film of water separates even the most closely fitted surfaces. Along one palm, a transparent crustacean stands between skin and wood, its feelers extended into a space neither larger body has occupied.

There are others of its kind.

Across the basin, what looks like a pale fork in a stem is another limb. Farther down, a scatter of smooth patches belongs to a back almost lost against the root behind it. Their bodies differ greatly. One has a short, heavy face and limbs thick nearly to their ends. Another is narrow through the torso, with toes arranged in fans. On a third, the clear windows in the skin reveal a set of organs unlike those of its neighbours.

These differences have the particularity of individuals rather than separate species.

Their reach overlaps.

A slender branch of one animal’s hand lies across the wrist of another. The upper skin is faintly indented at the contact. Neither supports the other’s weight; neither prevents the other from falling. There is no useful task to assign to this small conjunction, although the surrounding anatomy is so extensive that it is tempting to give every part a function.

The two surfaces touch.

Elsewhere, kilometres of limb occupy the rootwork without encountering another animal at all.

There is no hidden society conducting itself behind the stillness. No messages travel from body to body. No counsel waits in the dark beneath the largest swelling. Yet solitude is not quite the condition of these creatures. Each has the others present through the wood, the water, the precise interruptions their bodies make in the continuous sound.

Their awareness does not need to travel out and return.

The distant hand and the brown eye belong to one undivided extent of experience. Cold at the fingertips, light upon the back, the fish beside the wall, the pressure of another wrist: none has to be brought before a mind elsewhere.

And there is room in that awareness for things that do not concern the animal.

Below its longest limb is an opening in the floor of the sea. Roots surround the lens of water, and beyond it the clouds are white and densely folded. One blunt mountain tip projects through them. It is too far away to matter to the creature’s body. It offers no food, shelter, purchase or company.

One of the little upper eyes faces it.

The eye is clear. Its pupil is narrow in the generous daylight. Along the lower lid rests a minute crust of salt, perfectly fitted to the wet edge.

The creature has that mountain, too.
