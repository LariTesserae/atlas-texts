The animal that comes to the pool is smaller than the sound of its approach.

For some time there has been a faint scraping beneath the shelf beside you, interrupted by long silences. Once, something hard rolls against something else. Then a nose appears below the stone: bare, blunt at the end, moving a little from side to side. Whiskers touch the mineral rim. A forefoot follows, its toes spread wide, and the animal draws itself out as though being pulled through a sleeve.

It could sit in two cupped hands. Most of its apparent size is fur.

The fur is brown, but not the brown of the dry rock. It has a reddish undercolor that shows where the shoulders bend, and a dark, greasy parting along the spine. Dust adheres to its flanks. Around the mouth and beneath the chin, the hairs are short and almost white. Its ears are small enough to disappear when it turns away. Its eyes catch what little light there is, two wet points set well forward in the head.

It does not look blind. When you move a finger, it stops.

For several breaths nothing happens. Its nose remains over the water. Only one whisker moves, trembling where a drop has caught it.

Then it drinks.

The tongue is very small and very quick. Between bouts of lapping, the animal lifts its head and works its mouth. It drinks from the cool end, beside the accumulation of wings, although reaching that edge requires it to stand with one hind foot braced on the slope. Its tail lies along the rim behind it. The tail is shorter than the body, thick at the base, sparsely haired, with a pale scar near the tip.

When it has finished, it does not retreat immediately. It noses among the wings. One disappears into its mouth, comes out wet, and is discarded. A second is pushed aside. Beneath them it finds a soft-bodied insect you had not seen.

There is a brief, close crunching.

***

There are others, though you would rarely see two together.

Their paths run along the bases of walls and underneath the overhangs, crossing open stone only where there is no sheltered alternative. On a dusty shelf, these paths are narrow smudges. In wetter places they are almost polished. Each animal uses several resting pockets, moving between them as the warmth shifts or the bedding becomes fouled.

A sleeping place requires a small entrance and a wider space behind it. The animal carries in feathers, dry insect husks, and the brittle ends of stems that have found their way down from the entrance. It does not arrange these materials delicately. It pushes into them, turns, pushes again. After many days the hollow holds the shape of a curled body.

One nest lies above a crack through which warm air rises continuously. Its occupant sleeps against the outer wall, not directly over the heat. Another is tucked behind the pale apron beneath a swiftlet ledge. That one is warm enough only while the birds are home.

The animal beside the pool is an adult female. The outer edge of her left ear is missing. When she turns that side toward you, the ear looks folded.

She knows the chamber as a succession of passages, surfaces, and encounters. A broad hump in the floor is crossed on its left side when dry and on its right when condensation has made the left side slippery. A certain gap can be entered headfirst but must be left backward. Beneath one ledge, beetles collect after the bats begin to stir. The nearest drinking cup sometimes contains water and sometimes only a cool smell.

She checks it either way.

Her whiskers extend beyond the width of her shoulders. In a narrow passage they bend backward against the walls, and she slows until each step is almost separate. Her forefeet have short, curved claws; the soles are naked and finely ridged. She can climb rough stone, but on a smooth slope she travels low, belly nearly brushing the surface.

The deep seams stop her. At their edges she stretches forward, testing the opposite lip with her nose. If she cannot touch it, she usually turns aside.

Usually is important. There are bones below some of those seams.

***

She eats often, in small amounts.

Crickets are taken by a quick sideways movement of the head. She holds large ones beneath her forefeet and bites behind the head while the hind legs kick against her cheeks. Beetles require more work. She turns them over, worries at the softer joins, and sometimes abandons an individual whose shell defeats her.

She eats larvae, spiders, dropped eggs, anything soft enough and small enough. A dead bat can keep her returning to one place for days, though she must approach around the beetles and retreat when a living bat falls close by. She is not capable of overcoming a healthy adult. Once, disturbed beneath the roost, she seized a dragging wing and was beaten against the floor until she let go.

Since then she approaches fallen bats from the head.

Her appetite gives her little leisure. She rests in short intervals, wakes, grooms, searches. In the warmest passages she pauses with her mouth open. In the cold draft near the entrance she fluffs her coat and presses closer to the wall.

She seldom goes as far as the fern.

Beyond it, the passage widens toward the outside and shelter becomes unreliable. Air moves against the grain of her fur. Sounds arrive without traveling through stone. On one visit she found a moth stunned by cold and carried it back whole, its wings dragging on either side of her head. On another, blown ice struck her face. She fled before the grains had melted.

***

Now there are young in the pocket above the warm crack.

Three were born. Two remain.

They are dark and smooth-faced, with folded ears and feet that seem too large for them. Their fur is beginning to lift from the skin. When their mother enters, they search beneath her with abrupt, forceful movements, pushing each other aside. She lies awkwardly against the curved wall until they settle.

She has pulled more feathers into the entrance, but not enough to stop the air.

Soon the young will follow her. At first they will travel only the length of the shelf, stopping to investigate grit, biting things that cannot be eaten. A cricket’s sudden movement will send them backward. Their mother will continue hunting and return to find them where the passage narrows.

There is not much room for another adult here. When they are larger, she will drive them from the nest. One may find shelter beyond the pool, where a smaller chamber has its own warm seam. One may follow the cool air upward. During wet weather, insects gather behind the fallen slab, and there are breaks in the rock farther down the mountain where a small body can get out of the wind.

For now, they sleep.

Their mother leaves the pool and passes close to your boot. She smells the leather without touching it. Something in the unfamiliar odor holds her for a moment; then a cricket scrapes behind the shelf.

Her head turns. The rest of her follows, compressing beneath the stone.

The scarred tail is the last part to disappear. A moment later, a little grit spills from the crack.
