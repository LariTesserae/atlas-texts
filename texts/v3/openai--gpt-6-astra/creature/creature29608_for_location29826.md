At first its back is mistaken for the rounded corner of a pier.

It has the same exhausted ocher color, the same fine pores, even a faint horizontal marking where one course of brick ought to end and another begin. But the line does not continue into the masonry on either side. It bends downward. Below it is another line, closer to a fold than a joint.

The creature has fitted itself into the angle so completely that there seems to be no place from which it could begin to move.

Its exposed surface is warm. Everything is warm here, but this warmth answers pressure. Where a hand rests, something slowly firms beneath the skin, not pushing the hand away, only taking up its weight.

Then the corner becomes a shoulder.

The movement is small and difficult to follow. A ridge withdraws; a shallow recess deepens; a length of ocher surface that appeared to belong to the wall comes away from it. No dust falls. The creature has not been cemented there. It has been holding that position, perhaps for a very long time, with a precision greater than the eye requires.

It is much thinner than its apparent bulk suggested.

Most of what seemed to be its body was the pier enclosed by it. Unfolded, it consists of a narrow trunk and several broad, jointed limbs, each capable of flattening against another. Its thickness varies. At one moment a limb has the substance of an arm; at another it is a long, dense flap sliding edgewise through a gap too narrow for a wrist.

There is no visible machinery to this change. Its substance gathers where it is bearing weight and thins where it is passing between obstructions. The folds remain. They travel over it slowly, like creases being worked from one part of a heavy cloth into another.

It does have a head, though it is usually held against its chest, and in that position is difficult to distinguish from a closed hand.

When lifted, it shows a blunt face with no projecting nose and two shallow depressions beneath a heavy brow. The depressions are covered in the same skin as the rest of it. They may be eyes. Nothing in them reflects the violet light.

Under the jaw are fine overlapping plates.

These are almost always parted.

The sound enters there.

It enters elsewhere as well: through the broad ends of the limbs, through the flattened breast, through a row of soft-looking indentations down the spine. Wherever the creature touches the building, it acquires another way of hearing it. It seldom has fewer than four points of contact. Even when moving, it leaves one limb extended behind until another has found a suitable surface ahead.

To cross a passage, it first lays a palm on the opposite wall.

Palm is only an approximation. The end of the limb spreads into several unequal lobes, with a deep hollow at their center. The hollow settles over a brick. The lobes press the neighboring joints.

It waits.

Then the rest of it follows.

There is no haste in the movement, but neither is there hesitation. In places it travels with its head below its body. In the hand-wide passages, it lengthens and goes almost flat, making two or three turns at once. Those carefully floored and vaulted channels are not too small for it. It passes through them with the ease of something using a familiar stair.

Yet the passages were not necessarily made for this creature. Some bear the polish of a different body, or of its own body when it had another shape.

It can remain still long enough for these distinctions to become difficult.

Its skin has small irregular pits, none fresh. There are darker patches where the ocher surface has worn through to something nearly black. At the inner folds the material is smoother, with a dull sheen like clay rubbed by a thumb before firing. It has no hair, no damp mouth, no smell distinguishable from warm brick.

Once fully extended, it is longer than a person is tall. Folded beside a threshold, it could be stepped over without notice.

There may be more than one.

A visitor who watches it disappear beneath a low arch might later find a similar fold of ocher flesh high on a support, with no apparent route between the two positions. But there are routes inside the walls, and the creature knows the turning of ducts that no light has reached. Counting it would require access to more of the building than a person could survive entering.

It behaves as though alone.

This is not the same as being lonely. There is no searching in its movements, no pause directed toward an expected arrival. The contacts it makes with the piers are sufficient to occupy it. Sometimes it holds one position with the jaw plates spread and the whole length of its back pressed against an arch. Sometimes it draws away abruptly and crosses to another support, as if something urgent had occurred.

Nothing audible has changed.

For it, the chord is not continuous in the way it is for human ears.

A foot on a threshold closes a circuit of pressure through the floor. A hand against a vent takes something from one note and gives it to another. Even a body standing still alters the chamber, absorbing, obstructing, carrying sound within its wet cavities and fragile bones.

The creature knows someone has entered long before it uncovers its face.

It cannot hear their voice as speech. A shout reaches it as a complicated injury to the steady pressure, brief and full of internal breaks. It flattens its jaw plates when this happens. Then, cautiously, it opens them again.

Quiet breathing interests it more.

It will follow a visitor at a distance of several turns, keeping to surfaces they have touched. Where a shoulder has rubbed the wall, it places one of its hollow palms. Where a person has sat, it lowers its breast.

The traces do not last long. It attends to them while they last.

In the central chamber it becomes less able to hide.

The six piers do not offer it one continuous surface, and the raised slab leaves an open interval it must cross. Here its full arrangement can sometimes be seen: the small folded head; the narrow trunk; the limbs branching at unequal heights, with no clear division between those used for standing and those used for reaching.

It lays itself over the slab.

The shallow curve receives its breast and abdomen. Two limbs reach backward to the nearest piers. Another pair extends forward. The remaining folds settle along the brick edge, one inside another.

It rests there with the deepest note passing through its center.

This is the closest it comes to sleep. Contact is maintained, but its responses become slow. A disturbance at the perimeter may cause only one lobe of a palm to lift. Its face loosens. The covered hollows beneath the brow grow deeper.

There are marks on the underside of its limbs that cannot be seen at other times.

They resemble fingerprints.

Not a covering of them, and not an ornament: a few small impressions, grouped near a joint, where someone might have supported a soft object while lifting it. Some overlap. One has dragged sideways.

The creature cannot explain these marks. It does not turn them toward the light.

If a visitor stays beside the slab, enduring the pressure in the teeth and chest, it eventually raises its head. One limb disengages from a pier. The end folds inward, losing its broad, load-bearing shape.

It takes a long time to arrange.

First there is a hollow, then a projecting ridge. The lobes narrow. Two remain joined and are worked apart with a slow motion beneath the skin.

What reaches toward the visitor is not quite a hand. It has too many knuckles, and the thumb is set too low. But it is small enough to be held.

The creature keeps every other limb firmly against the masonry.

When the offered hand is taken, its plates open beneath the jaw. The pressure of its fingers changes by minute degrees. It adjusts each point of contact until the visitor’s pulse reaches it clearly through the crushing sound.

Then it holds very still.
