The small brown animals have pale bellies and hands almost as large as their heads.

They are easiest to see just after dawn, when they come out along the undersides of branches to lick water from the bark. Their fur lies in overlapping ridges, each hair flattened and slightly oily. In still air they look untidy. In a gust the ridges close, and the animal becomes a smooth brown knot with four long-fingered feet tucked beneath it.

They do not leap between branches. They reach, take hold, test, and only then release the grip behind them. Even the young do this. A newborn can hang from a strand of its mother’s belly fur before its eyes open.

People call them holders. There are other names for them when they get into the food baskets.

Their nests are woven into the forks where creeping trees have rooted again. A good nest has several entrances and no opening that faces directly outward. Inside are shredded stems, shed fur, the translucent skins of insects, and sometimes something stolen: a length of fine cord, a strip torn from a sleeping mat, a piece of softened leather that somebody has been searching for all morning.

Holders eat the tender bases of fern shoots. They also gnaw the swollen roots of certain creepers, but only after the roots have begun to rot. A gardener who finds clipped stalks may hunt through the growth for an hour, swearing softly, then stop at a hollow root and feel along it with a hooked finger. Sometimes the animal has found a sickness before the gardener has.

For this reason, and because they are difficult to catch, holders are tolerated up to a point.

The point differs from house to house.

***

In one of the houses beneath the western stone lives a woman named Eda, her brother, his daughter, and, at present, a young man whose own roof is being repaired.

“At present” has lasted most of a year. His bowls have acquired niches. Nobody mentions this except when they are angry with him.

Eda keeps the condensation slab clean. It is a broad piece of dense gray stone set against the back wall, where the earth stays cool. Moist air reaches it through a narrow passage. Each morning she wipes its underside with a cloth that has never been used for anything else, then rinses the cloth into a covered jar. The bowls beneath it receive less water than a visitor expects. A household can watch them for an entire conversation without seeing a drop fall.

There are other jars buried under the floor.

The house smells of roots, warmed clay, and smoke. Its ceiling is low at the entrance but higher over the sleeping platforms. A person can stand there, though visitors often remain stooped for several days, distrustful of the branches overhead. These branches move. Fine dust falls after a hard gust; small roots slowly find their way through the roof covering and must be cut before they thicken.

Eda knows which creaks require her to get up.

She sleeps through most of them. Her brother wakes at every one, lies listening, and complains in the morning that nobody else takes the house seriously.

They have lived under this roof for thirty-one years.

***

The gardens are not wholly wild and not wholly planted.

People move cuttings from one hollow to another, wedge useful roots into cracks, and carry baskets of humus to places where a tree has made sufficient shelter. A plant may be tended for several years before anyone takes food from it. Some of the broad creeping leaves are cut in strips rather than harvested whole, leaving the central vein and most of the blade alive. The strips are folded around mashed tubers and steamed in closed pots.

There are sour fruits that ripen beneath the leaves, close against the stone. There are peppery young stems, and bulbs that must be soaked through several changes of water. Those bulbs are eaten only when water is plentiful. In a dry year they may stand untouched beside a house where everyone is hungry.

The richest food is a soft white grub found in the dead centers of old runners. Children locate it by pressing an ear to the stem during a lull. Adults claim they can do the same, but usually ask a child to check.

Much of the work consists of keeping one thing from taking too much of another.

Too many holders kill new growth. Too few leave the dead runners full of grubs, which eventually hollow out the living ones. A creeper allowed to cover an entire depression may shade out the plants beneath it and leave a splendid green roof over almost nothing edible. Cut it back too far and the soil begins to leave.

Eda is particularly good at seeing where to cut. She is not good at explaining it. Her brother says this is convenient.

When she teaches his daughter, she has the girl lie beneath a mat of creeper and watch while she loosens the outer roots. From below, the girl can see the load pass through the plant: a runner straightening, a fork twisting, a thick root pulling sideways in its socket. Eda stops after each cut.

The lesson takes most of the afternoon. They return dirty, carrying very little food.

***

Smaller lives occupy the sheltered thickness of everything.

Under a leaf there may be a colony of waxy insects, each sealed to its feeding place by a transparent rim. Among them move flat predatory beetles with claws that fold into grooves along their legs. Minute flies breed in the amber pools. Their young can survive for a time in wet leaf litter when the water drains, but the adults are short-lived and seldom travel far under their own power.

At certain seasons, the pools have a gray edging of drowned wings.

The broad birds rising out of the gulfs feed along the boundaries between currents. From above, a person sees a dark back and a small head turned sharply downward. From below, on the rare occasion a bird passes over a garden, its body is surprisingly pale. The wings are scarred at the edges. Old birds have missing feathers that do not seem to trouble them.

They nest below the brows of the stone, not on top of it.

Once a year, a few people descend to collect eggs from ledges that can be reached. The work is done early, while the upper rock is still cool. A collector takes one egg from a nest of three, none from a nest of one, and sometimes comes back with an empty sling because the route has changed.

The birds remember collectors. Whether they distinguish one human face from another is disputed. Eda’s brother believes they do. There is a particular ledge he will no longer visit.

He says the rope there is badly placed.

***

Human beings here have the usual range of bodies: tall, short, heavy, narrow, quick, painful. Age does not make everyone wise about the wind. Some old people become reckless because they have crossed the same place safely so often. Some young people are exhausting in their caution.

What almost everyone acquires is a habit of keeping contact.

A hand rests in a root fork while its owner talks. An ankle hooks behind a stool leg. Sitting outside, people tuck a tool beneath a thigh rather than laying it beside them. Babies spend much of the day against someone’s chest, secured inside a broad carrying cloth, and learn early to seize a collar when the adult carrying them changes posture.

Children practice falling on the sleeping mats. They are taught to spread their weight, find something with a hand, keep their feet from going over an edge. Later they practice on a sloping patch of rock well inside a garden. The games become elaborate and occasionally cruel. A child known to fear an exposed crossing may be challenged to fetch a seed case from just beyond the safe holds.

Adults interfere when they see this. They do not always see it.

There are arguments over routes, gardens, marriage, borrowed tools, and whose turn it is to clear the passage carrying damp air to a shared cistern. People make promises in perfectly good faith and then discover that the wind prevents them from keeping them for six days.

Someone expected for supper may arrive the following afternoon, furious and hungry.

There are households within sight of one another that meet less often than households connected by a long sheltered channel. Distance is given in routes as readily as in walking time. A place can be close by the fern beds and very far by the bare stone.

The oldest paths have names that no longer describe them. The Lower Orchard contains no trees. At the Three Steps there are seven, the others having been cut after a fall. Eda knows where one route used to pass before a whole rooting limb died, but she has never seen anyone use it.

She points it out sometimes. Her niece can scarcely believe that people once went that way.

***

In the houses, speech returns to an ordinary volume.

Visitors who have spent a day being touched, pulled close, and shouted to are sometimes surprised by the amount of talk after dark. Stories are interrupted. Instructions are repeated without need. People sing while mending things, because outside a person can sing at full strength and hardly hear whether the note is right.

The young man living with Eda’s family has a pleasant voice and knows too many verses of one song. He is also the best cord-maker in the household. These facts help each other.

Fine cord is spun indoors, where the separate fibers can be laid out safely. The broad ropes are braided in sheltered channels, pegged at intervals to the rock. Old rope is unpicked for useful lengths, but no recovered length goes into a crossing rope. It becomes a basket handle, a binding, a soft pad beneath a load.

Eda has a necklace made from small pieces of polished green stone. She wears it under her clothes outside. Indoors, when there is company, she draws it out and arranges it across her chest. One of the pieces is poorly drilled and always turns backward.

Her niece likes that piece best.

***

There are things the people do not know about their home.

They do not know how far down the warm passages go. Objects lowered into them knock against unseen walls, become wet, catch, and sometimes come back smelling of a mud no one has seen. Seeds arrive in the vents that resemble none of the plants overhead. Once, a blind white animal came out beside a hearth and died before morning. It had six legs, or eight; the account depends on who is telling it.

Nor do they know whether the holders travel beneath the stone between gardens. A marked animal once appeared across a gulf, but the marking might have been mistaken.

These uncertainties furnish many evenings.

Meanwhile, roofs need cutting free of roots. Food needs gathering. A bowl set beneath a drip must be moved half a finger’s width because the water has begun to follow a different crease in the slab.

Eda’s niece notices this before anyone else. She shifts the bowl and stays crouched beside it until the next drop falls cleanly inside.

Then she returns to the argument at the table, where everyone is wrong about something she considers important, and has been waiting for her to finish.
