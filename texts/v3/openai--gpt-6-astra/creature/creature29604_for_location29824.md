The gecko has a tail a little too short for its body. The replacement is smoother than the rest of it, without the fine pattern that breaks its back into patches of stone and shadow. Whatever took the first tail is no longer here, or is here in another form: inside a beetle, beneath a leaf, worked into the dust.

It has chosen a shallow hollow where its belly touches the wall and its feet can rest on four small irregularities. There it can remain so still that an ant climbs over one toe. Its eyes do not close. Now and then its tongue passes over one of them, briefly making a bright surface in the dimness.

The gecko knows the recess by contact and interruption. A route is a succession of places where its feet hold. A hunting place is an edge beyond which something moves. It does not need to know the jar is a jar. The curved lip shelters moths; the hollow beneath it is too narrow for its head. Once, reaching in, it got a mouthful of dead leaves. It shook them loose and waited.

Under those leaves lives a beetle with dust packed into the grooves of its wing cases. It looks unfinished, as though it has just been dug out of the wall. Its legs are glossy beneath the dirt. When disturbed it draws them close and becomes a hard, inconvenient particle. The ants examine it, find no purchase, and continue around it.

There are smaller beetles the gecko has never seen. Their whole lives occur beneath the dry skirts of the plants, among leaf bases that overlap like badly stacked bowls. They eat what has ceased to be leaf but has not yet become floor. Some are pale when they emerge from their old skins. For a few hours their bodies are soft enough that the narrow passage which protected them yesterday could injure them today.

The plants make such passages without ceasing to fill them.

Each rosette holds its younger leaves upright in the middle, close around the point from which more leaves will come. Farther out, the older ones flatten, wrinkle, and give up their stored water. A single plant contains several seasons of weather. At its heart is the small, tight growth of the present; underneath are the remains of years when rain arrived differently.

One of the houseleek’s daughters has reached the dust beyond the broken jar. Its connecting stem lies across the rim, thin and dry-looking. Beneath the little rosette, roots have begun fastening it to a floor that will not stay still. Ants excavate beside it. A grain falls away. A root tip follows the newly exposed darkness.

The ants belong to more than one household. At certain crossings they pause with a sudden attentiveness absent from their ordinary haste. Two workers touch, recoil, and turn. Elsewhere, a dead fly moves steadily uphill with no visible means of movement until its wing catches on a stalk and the carriers spill out around it.

Their nest extends beyond the space a kneeling person could inspect. What looks like its deepest wall is only the beginning of some of their journeys. They pass into cool joints behind the hot stone, through chambers narrower than the thickness of a fingernail. There are workers there whose bodies have never crossed daylight.

The new wasp is still at the mouth of its hole.

Mortar dust clings to the hairs of its face. It cleans one antenna, then the other, drawing each through the bend of a foreleg. Behind it is the chamber that contained almost everything it has experienced: food placed there before it could feed itself, the casing of its transformation, the barrier it has spent the afternoon opening. Ahead is an immense brightness, already fading.

It walks onto the sill. Its wings separate.

For a moment it shares that small ledge with a mite, a husk, and a fragment of pigeon feather. Then it is gone, and the ledge has room for something else.

Above, the pigeons settle badly. One wants the place occupied by the other. Their dispute sends a little sift of debris through the floor, including a broken seed that lands between two green leaves. By morning it will have been found. The feather remains caught on a strand of silk, turning whenever air enters from the lane.

A spider approaches it, tests the strand, and withdraws. Later it will anchor another thread nearby. The feather will become part of the arrangement, though it serves no purpose the spider intended.

None of these inhabitants occupies quite the same place. For the pigeon it is a chamber with an awkward corner. For the gecko, a warm wall and several good edges. For the plants, a few dependable cracks. Beneath them, an animal small enough to travel between grains of dust encounters a fallen seed as an obstruction that may require a long detour.

Night reaches each at a different time.

The flowers lose their light first. The wall continues to warm the gecko’s belly. Deep in the jar, a beetle pushes past another beetle, their bodies making a faint dry click. Roots remain pressed against stone. In a chamber behind the mortar, ants turn their pale young and carry them a little farther inward.

By then the entrance looks empty from the lane. Only the fallen lintel is visible, holding the day’s heat, with a thread across one corner that briefly catches the light from an opened door.
