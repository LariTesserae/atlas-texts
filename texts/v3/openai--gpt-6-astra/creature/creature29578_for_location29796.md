The first grazer to reach the crack does not put its head inside. It places one foot on either side and draws its body forward until the split passes beneath its belly.

Through its skin, the gut is visible as a bronze thread. The thread straightens. Something traveling along it stops.

For a while the animal remains that way, straddling the new opening. The feet nearest the crack begin to look farther apart than the others, although their dark little claws have not moved. Then it withdraws. It rubs its underside against the stone, leaving a smear in which several bright points continue to shift.

The next grazer eats the smear.

These are the most numerous of the larger inhabitants: low animals with blunt heads and so many short legs that, walking, they appear to flow around obstacles. An adult can flatten itself beneath a film of water thinner than its own mouthparts. Out of the film it becomes plump, almost cylindrical, and the legs tuck into pockets along its sides.

Its back is not smooth. Transparent folds run across it, each holding a little water when the surrounding stone goes dry. In those folds live the fan-bearing animals. They fasten themselves by a single stalk and lean outward, opening their fringes whenever the grazer moves.

On a young grazer there may be only three or four. On an old one, the fans overlap. Their stalks thicken; debris lodges between them; other creatures settle in the debris. Eventually the grazer carries more living material than its own body contains.

This changes where it can go.

A heavily inhabited animal cannot squeeze beneath the nursery ledge. Its passengers catch against the roof. Such animals work the open crust, turning slowly at the boundaries of their allotted patch. When a hot pulse comes, their backs grow restless before their legs begin to move. Fans close. Stalks bend. Tiny hooked bodies emerge from the folds and travel toward the head.

The grazer follows them.

Sometimes the passengers disagree. A pale line forms down each flank, and the host stands between two directions until the water settles the matter.

There are creatures that spend their whole lives in one of these folds. They hatch there, feed there, leave their capsules along its lower edge. The grazer’s passage from the seep to the outer lip is, for them, a change of season. A journey inward may remove half a generation. On the return, the survivors pick over the empty stalks.

Others move between hosts whenever backs touch. Seen closely, a crowded feeding patch is full of these crossings: minute bodies balancing across the brief bridges made by grazing animals, waiting with their front limbs raised when the gap becomes too wide.

One kind carries its eggs in a fan.

The eggs occupy the places where the finest feeding hairs would otherwise be. As they grow, the parent catches less and less. By hatching time it has become little more than a stalk beneath a cluster of folded offspring. The young open their own fans before detaching. For several hours the parent feeds through them, its body briefly filling again.

Then they leave.

The empty animal sometimes grows a new fringe. More often it is eaten by something living lower down in the fold.

***

The yellow segmented animals keep to faster water.

Their mouths are muscular cups surrounded by small teeth. Attached to the stone, they lift the rest of the body into the current, allowing water to pass between their segments. Each segment carries a pair of translucent plates. These are usually mistaken for legs until one of the animals releases its mouth and is swept sideways, helplessly, with all the plates still opening and shutting.

It has only two true feet, tucked close behind the head.

With these it handles mineral flakes.

At the nursery, the yellow animals work head downward. One foot braces against the ledge while the other turns a splinter in the mouth. They probe a blocked channel repeatedly, changing the angle after each unsuccessful push. If the obstruction gives way suddenly, the worker may be carried off with it. Another takes its place.

They do not attend every capsule. Some they pass over even when those capsules are drying. Others they touch with their mouths, linger beside, and return to after clearing several neighboring channels.

The distinctions are difficult to see. A favored capsule may contain a yellow shape; it may contain a broad, transparent one; it may seem empty except for the bead.

An animal approaching with a useful flake can find the ledge crowded against it. Mouths remain attached to stone. Bodies lie close together. There is no opening through which it can bring its tool.

It waits.

Eventually one of the workers moves, or the newcomer leaves.

Near the back of the nursery are capsules whose occupants have developed too far inward. Their limbs remain visible, folded around a point of light, but they are small in a way that cannot be accounted for by age. Water passing over them takes a moment longer to emerge below the ledge.

The attendants bring them the thinnest flakes. They insert these beneath the capsules and tilt them toward the cooler flow.

Occasionally a folded limb becomes abruptly larger.

Occasionally the capsule collapses, and the attendant stands over it with the flake still in its mouth.

***

The flat predator is not alone beneath the ledge.

There are others on the undersides of the grazing shelves, on the leaning splinter, inside a hollow grain of salt. The youngest are round. They have six hooks and a mouth that occupies most of the back. As they grow, their margins extend unevenly around the surface to which they cling. A predator living on a ridge becomes long and narrow. One beneath a broad shelf develops lobes.

They can move, but do so rarely.

Before moving, a predator lifts each hook and sets it down again. Small animals scatter from beneath its rim. Then it draws itself loose, contracting into a thick, wrinkled shape that bears little resemblance to the clear surface it was a moment before.

Its underside is crowded with scars.

Some are ordinary attachment marks. Others are tiny sealed openings. When the predator stretches across new stone, these spread into thin white lines.

The view through its feeding mouth varies. Usually there is only darkness or the blurred interior of the prey. Sometimes the clouds are visible. Sometimes the opening shows black rock wet with morning sun, though the actual entrance has been in shade for hours.

On one occasion it shows a nursery ledge.

An attendant stands there holding a splinter. The splinter is broken at an angle familiar from the foreground. Before the animal can turn, the mouth closes.

The small creatures that drink at these openings approach cautiously. They touch the edge with one limb, withdraw, and touch it again. A few extend their whole bodies across the aperture without entering. Others pass through so quickly they appear to have slipped.

The predator takes none of them.

When an injured grazer crawls onto its back, however, the rim rises at once.

***

Those returning in the hottest beads are not quite as they were when they fell.

A grazer may come back with its folds empty. It runs rapidly over the crust, lightened of a population it has carried through many pulses. The first fans to settle on it remain closed for a long time.

Another returns bearing passengers no animal near the entrance carries. These have stiff, branching stalks and small red centers. They occupy the warmest folds. When the host approaches cooler water, they pull themselves down against its skin with enough force to leave dents.

Some returning animals have no mouths.

They move among the grazers, following the scraped arcs, pressing the front of the head against the bronze film. After a time the head splits, and something very fine begins to work inside the opening.

Others arrive with worn claws and fragments of old mineral lodged in their joints. The mountain around them is fresh, but what they carry is dull and rounded. The nursery workers sometimes pry these fragments loose. They use them until the current takes them away.

A few returns cause a disturbance.

The nearest animals stop feeding. Passengers crowd onto the sides of their hosts farthest from the newcomer. Even the fixed fans bend away.

The returned creature may be outwardly unremarkable: clear body, jointed legs, a little frost disappearing from the back. It crosses the cleared stone without haste. Wherever it pauses, the water draws into a deeper meniscus.

Usually it goes inward.

Long afterward, the fans open again.

***

By evening the new crack has acquired inhabitants.

The bronze film has reached its upper edge. Two grazers feed there, avoiding the place where the first one stood. Lower down, a yellow animal has attached its mouth to a projection and is testing the flow with its plates. A transparent capsule catches in a notch. It was laid elsewhere, but stays.

Inside the crack, something moves across the entering water.

It is visible only when grit passes behind it: a clear, many-jointed body stretched between the walls. Its feet are set at unequal distances. Some touch stone close to the surface. Others appear to rest much farther away.

It draws one of the distant feet nearer.

On the outer stone, the yellow animal releases its mouth. The grazers flatten. Every fan on their backs closes.

The creature in the crack waits through the next hot pulse without moving. Water travels along its body and gathers at a joint. The drop grows heavy, elongates, and touches the floor.

Into that small connection it lowers its head.

By morning there are feeding marks on both sides.
