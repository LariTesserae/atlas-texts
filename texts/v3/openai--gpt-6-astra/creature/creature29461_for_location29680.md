The person who made the bridge is lying beside it.

From the entrance, their body belongs to the bare floor. Light passes through the back and shoulders and comes to rest on the angular grains beneath. Only where one limb overlaps another is there a faint thickening, a color like the edge of a block of beeswax. They are small enough to lie between the bridge and the nearest stem without disturbing either. Their knees are drawn up. One arm is under their head.

The other reaches toward the handrail.

Seen from low down, against the black basin, they become easier to distinguish. There is a cheek, broad and almost flat, and a small ear folded close against the skull. The ear has several inner rims, nested with the precision of the partitions inside a shell. No hair grows on the head. Fine, colorless filaments lie along the scalp instead, all inclined toward the gardens.

Their eyes are open.

The eyes are the most solid parts of them: dark, oblong, set a little farther apart than seems comfortable in such a narrow face. Within each is a second, smaller darkness that does not reflect the apricot ceiling. Both eyes are directed toward the point where the handrail joins the farther support.

There is something wrong with that joint. The lashing is secure, but one turn of fiber lies across another instead of beside it. It makes a tiny raised place under the polished rail.

Their extended hand stops just short of it.

The hand is not transparent. Its palm is the color of pale clay, its fingertips darkened by work. There are four fingers and a thumb, though the thumb divides near its end into two soft, blunt tips. One could hold a length of root between those tips while using all four fingers to draw a knot tight. The nails are small plates with squared edges. A notch in one matches the width of the holes in the repaired cup.

They wear a sleeveless garment made from overlapping husks. Each husk has been trimmed, pierced, and joined to its neighbor with a stitch so fine that the garment initially appears to have grown around them. Under one arm is a patch of a different shade. The maker was economical with it: the damaged piece remains underneath.

There is a pocket against the chest.

Inside are two short fibers, a chip of shell, and something round wrapped in a much softer scrap. Through the body and the pocket, its outline can just be seen. It may be a bead. It may be a pellet of clay rolled until no corners remained. Whatever it is, the wrapping has been folded over it four times.

Nothing else in the hollow has been wrapped merely to keep it safe.

They are alive in the same difficult way as the animals. The flesh beneath the ribs has the fullness of a breath, and retains it. A shallow crease at the corner of the mouth suggests concentration rather than ease. There is no sleep in the face. Nor is there the strain of someone forcing themselves to remain motionless.

One might think, at first, that they are listening to the clearer thicket.

But their attention is less selective than that. It occupies the entire room. The sharp voices under the leaves, the deep animal holding its young, the ceiling’s high cry: all are present in the set of the face. Even the broad, less crowded intervals have a place there. In those intervals their own sound is audible.

It is a low sound, barely voiced, with a roughness toward its upper edge. Close to the bridge it resembles someone humming with a small object held between their lips. Near the seat it sounds younger. Beside the cups there is a little break in its texture, the shape a voice takes around an amusement too slight to become laughter.

These are not successive sounds. There is nowhere one gives way to another.

The room holds all of them.

The cups suggest a history, though it is impossible to arrange it. The maker’s divided thumb would fit the print in the clay. The shallow recesses keep the cups upright on the angular floor. Three are within easy reach of the seat; two stand farther away. Each has a lip carefully smoothed on one side.

Perhaps there were others here.

There is no second mat, but the stone is wide enough for two of these small people if they sat close. The polished place overhead extends beyond where one head would rest. In the pocket, the wrapped object is pierced through its center.

None of this establishes an absence. The hollow is full of provisions for actions that do not occur. A cup can remain offered to an empty place without becoming abandoned.

Under the nearest leaf, one of the ivory animals faces the maker. Its forelegs rest along a rib. It has been given a minute collar of braided fiber, loose enough that no part presses against its body. A longer strand runs from the collar to a husk loop on the maker’s garment. The strand lies slack across the floor.

There is no practical reason for it. The animal cannot stray. The maker cannot draw it closer.

Yet the braid is unusually beautiful. Two pale fibers turn around a darker third, and the ends have been tucked away where they will not scratch.

The bridge, too, exceeds any need. Its maker could have stepped over the crack. So could most of the creatures large enough to use it. Still, the three pieces of bone have been fitted level, and the rail follows a gentle curve suited to the travel of a hand.

Here is someone who has cared about the passage from one side to another.

Now their fingertip and the imperfect knot are separated by less than the width of a salt grain. Their expression contains the small, absorbed dissatisfaction of having seen what needs correcting. It contains, equally, their pleasure in the bridge.

Neither feeling will displace the other.

Beneath their cheek, caught between the transparent skin and the floor, is a single blunt-edged husk. They put it there before lying down. Even here, where nothing wears or withers, they preferred something soft under their head.
