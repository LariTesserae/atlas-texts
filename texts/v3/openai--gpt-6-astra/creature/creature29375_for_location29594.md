The stone-carriers have a bare patch beneath each wrist which they place against the rock before crossing it.

The rest of the hand is narrow and dark, with three long grasping fingers and a shorter finger that folds across them. The wrist patch is pale, almost translucent. On an old animal it becomes thick at the edges, but the center remains soft. Even while feeding, even in a quarrel, one of them will occasionally lean forward and hold both patches to the ground.

Then the others wait.

They are about the length of a human thigh, though their postures make size difficult to judge. On a ridge they travel low, knuckling forward with their bellies clear of the stone. In the trees they lengthen unexpectedly. A folded reach opens from the shoulder; the hind limbs extend; an animal that seemed squat below is suddenly hanging across a gap, holding a branch with one foot and feeling for the next with both hands.

Their fur lies in narrow, oily ridges. Between the ridges the skin can be exposed by a slow muscular ripple. This is how they cool themselves. A resting group is never quite still: strips of skin appear and disappear, limbs exchange places, a belly turns outward from the warm wood. The young sleep on top until they become too heavy. Then they are pushed into the middle, where they protest with a grinding sound made behind closed lips.

Their faces are long, but most of that length belongs to the mouth. The eyes sit close together above it, black and wet beneath heavy brows. Along the upper lip grow two rows of short bristles, directed forward. These can find a bead of water on the underside of a leaf without breaking its surface. The tongue follows, fitting between the bristles.

A thirsty stone-carrier may spend an hour beneath a single branch, harvesting droplets too small to see.

They do not often drink from the ground. Too many things wait in those mouthfuls of water.

One is a flat, transparent animal that fastens itself across the drinking lips. It is scarcely larger than a thumbnail before it feeds. Afterward it becomes a soft, red button, tucked inside the cheek where the tongue cannot easily dislodge it. Stone-carriers with these passengers stop eating hard food. They drool, lose weight, and begin to smell different.

Their companions examine them.

The examination is rough. Two hold the head while a third pulls the lip outward and scrapes beneath it with a sliver of root shell. Sometimes the parasite comes away. Sometimes the patient bites through a finger.

Missing fingers are common. So are old scars around the mouth. An animal may refuse to sit beside another for days after being treated, then present the same cheek to it when the swelling returns.

Their stones are carried in a fold of skin low on the abdomen, supported from beneath by one hand when they run. A good stone is dense, blunt at one end, and narrow enough at the other to enter the seam of a root nodule. The carriers know its orientation without looking. They turn it inside the fold while listening, thumb rubbing a familiar depression.

Youngsters carry unsuitable things: porous fragments that crumble, stones too large to lift effectively, smooth nodules stolen from feeding sites and mistaken for tools. Adults usually let them struggle. But when a youngster strikes and produces a sharp ringing note, every nearby head turns.

Fresh stone can break into edges that pass through a hand almost without resistance.

An experienced carrier tests a new hammer against old shell before using it. It listens to the impact through its fingers as well as its ears.

The root nodules are not simply stores of water. Inside each hard rind is a pale living mass threaded with chambers. Some chambers hold sweet fluid. Some contain the thick larvae of insects that enter through pores too narrow for an adult carrier’s nail. Others are dry and packed with bitter fibers.

The carriers can distinguish most of these conditions by tapping.

A feeding group produces an irregular conversation of knocks: testing, opening, testing again. A listener can hear the moment a promising nodule is found. The tapping stops. Feet shift. Someone gives a low nasal complaint as another body presses too close.

They share badly, but they feed together.

One animal opens a nodule while another holds the surrounding root under tension, preventing the whole structure from bouncing beneath the blows. The holder gets the first access to the broken seam. This arrangement is continually renegotiated through shoves, lip movements, and small, painfully exact bites.

Fluid is lost during these arguments. Beneath every feeding party waits an assortment of creatures prepared to catch it.

There are six-legged cups with flexible rims, living bowls that creep into position and tilt themselves toward the work. There are pale worms which emerge only far enough to touch a wet shell fragment and then retract, dragging it underground. There are beetles whose backs carry dense mats of absorbent hair. Once soaked, they lumber away with their legs barely reaching past their swollen covering.

The carriers sometimes eat the beetles. Usually they are too occupied with one another.

Their greatest competitor cannot climb.

It moves through the forest on four short, deeply jointed limbs, with a belly protected by overlapping plates. Its broad back often supports ferns. These are not rooted in the animal itself but in the accumulated scraps caught between its plates: dung, leaf pulp, discarded insect skins, soil brought up from fissures. On very old individuals, the growth conceals everything except the head.

The head is a low wedge with a moist, muscular rim around the mouth. It presses this rim against a root, makes a seal, and cuts inward with teeth that work from side to side.

A stone-carrier may labor for half an hour over a nodule that this animal opens in three bites.

When one arrives at a feeding place, the carriers climb just beyond its reach and strike the branches. It pays little attention. Occasionally a frustrated youngster hits its back with a hammer. The ferns shake. Insects pour from the plates. The animal continues eating.

But its passage leaves torn roots, and torn roots leak.

The carriers follow it afterward.

So do the long-legged hunters.

These are much lighter than their height suggests. Most of the body is a narrow trunk suspended between high shoulders and hips. Their feet spread into flexible hooks that grip bark, hide, or the uneven edges of stone. They can stand astride a grazer while it moves and remain almost level, each limb adjusting independently beneath them.

They hunt downward.

The head hangs on a loose neck, turning among the traffic beneath their bodies. Their jaws open only a short distance. They take small prey: a cup-animal swollen with fluid, a worm too slow to retreat, a young carrier separated during a tremor.

A hunter will remain above a feeding party for a long time, apparently interested in nothing. Only the angle of its head changes.

The carriers watch the head.

When it drops below the hunter’s shoulders, they gather their young inward. When it rises again, the group loosens. Feeding continues throughout. There is not enough time or food to treat the mere presence of danger as an emergency.

During ground movements, hunter and hunted sometimes occupy the same branch.

At those moments the hunters fold their necks tightly against their chests. A dangling head is easily struck, and a fall into moving rock is worse than a missed meal. The carriers cling around them, stones trapped between belly and bark. After the trembling passes, the animals separate carefully. The youngest carriers must be pulled away. They have mistaken temporary safety for a property of the creature holding still beside them.

The immense browsers are different again.

A stone-carrier may know only the underside of a particular individual: the scars above one knee, a strip of loose skin, the odor of the crease behind its forelimb. Its back remains somewhere in the leaves.

The browsers move slowly enough that smaller animals maintain routes across them. Certain insects breed only in their nostril rims. A little hook-footed animal lives under the jaw and feeds on pulp expelled during chewing. When the browser lowers its head, this attendant runs up the cheek. When the head rises, it returns.

Stone-carriers climb the browsers in dry periods.

They are seeking the wet corners of the mouth. It is dangerous work. A carrier braces two feet against the cheek, grips a skin fold with one hand, and reaches toward the moving lips with the other. The browser scarcely notices unless a nail enters a tender crease. Then its head turns, slowly but with enough force to sweep a whole party through the branches.

Older carriers approach from the scarred side, where there is more to hold.

Some browsers recognize the sensation of small hands and pause their chewing. Others rub their faces against trunks until the visitors drop.

The carriers remember which is which.

They remember much of their country as a succession of bodies and contacts. A root that sweats at the coolest hour. A fork that holds five sleepers but not six. A browser that permits drinking. A stretch of rock where a certain low vibration precedes the opening of a crack.

This knowledge does not survive every upheaval.

Sometimes a familiar route ends in a wall of fresh fracture. Sometimes the sleeping tree is still standing but its roots have been cooked below ground. Its leaves remain fleshy for several days while its grip loosens.

A group may return to such a tree repeatedly, climbing into the old forks and descending again. One animal settles. Another pulls at it. They strike the trunk, press their wrists to the bark, smell the air around the roots.

Eventually they leave, or the tree falls with them inside it.

After a fall, the survivors search the broken crown. They lift leaves and pull at hands. They retrieve stones from bodies that will no longer grip them.

For a while, an adult may carry two hammers: its own in the belly fold and another held awkwardly against its chest. The extra weight makes climbing difficult. At feeding places the second stone is set down within reach, then collected again when the group moves.

Sooner or later it is left behind.

A youngster finds it. Or a root grows around it. Or a new split in the ground takes it down among polished bones and the pale tips of roots.

Above, the group has already reached another feeding place. One of them braces a swollen nodule. Another raises its hammer, pauses, and lowers its free wrist to the stone.

The young crowd close enough to feel the adults holding still.

Under all the rasping and shrilling, something has changed.
