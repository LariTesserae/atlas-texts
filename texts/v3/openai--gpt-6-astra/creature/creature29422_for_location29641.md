A crab lives in the split beneath the lowest stair.

Its shell is the color of wet cement, except at the joints, where a little amber shows. One claw is smaller than the other. When it comes out, it pauses with its rear legs still inside the crack, close enough to withdraw at the first change overhead. Its eyes rise above the stair’s worn edge. Its mouthparts keep moving.

The crack extends farther into the concrete than the crab can travel. At the back, it narrows around a corroded reinforcing rod; farther still, water finds a passage. Even at low tide the innermost surface stays damp. The crab has enlarged the entrance by carrying out grains of sand and softened mortar. What it removes accumulates in a small fan below the step, where passing water repeatedly takes it away.

Several other crabs occupy the same stair. They meet often and tolerate one another poorly. A claw lifted in the entrance is usually enough. Sometimes two brace against the concrete and push, their legs slipping until one finds purchase. The loser retreats a few inches and begins feeding again.

They eat what adheres: softened scraps, a film on a stone, matter caught against a strand of algae. Each claw brings up so little that the work appears ceremonial. Over hours, a clean patch spreads beneath them.

Their young do not begin here. They spend their first lives suspended in water, carried through gates and around the bay in currents too large for them to oppose. Some pass through the district’s screens. Some are eaten at the sluices. The ones that eventually settle beneath these stairs arrive small enough to shelter in the roughness of the concrete. There is no journey home, only a place where the water leaves them and they manage to stay.

Inside the pump house, a woman rinses a cup at a utility sink.

She has worked nights here long enough to recognize a clogged intake before the alarm sounds. The pump develops a roughness she feels through the chair legs. Tonight it is running evenly. She sets the cup upside down beside the tap and writes the start time in a ruled book whose lower corners have swollen from the damp.

Her boots remain under the desk until she has to go outside. In sandals she crosses the room, steps around a damp patch beneath the air conditioner, and looks through the glass toward the outlet.

She sees the heron first. The fish gathering below it are harder to distinguish.

The heron has learned how long to wait after a pump starts. At first the disturbed silt hides everything. Then the current clears, and small fish come forward to hold their places in it. Their tails work continuously. A fish that loses its position slips backward, turns, and tries again from the side. Occasionally one flashes against the dark water, giving away both itself and the bodies around it.

The bird’s feathers lie close in the heat. Seen from the office, it looks thin and slightly disordered, with a loose feather projecting from one shoulder. Up close there is considerable weight in the folded wings. Its feet spread across the mud, each toe settling separately. Between strikes it scarcely moves, though its attention travels all along the outlet.

Beneath the office window, a gecko occupies the back of an electrical sign. During the day its belly rests against the wall in a gap scarcely wider than its body. Dust has collected around its toes. At night it comes out onto the sign’s upper edge, where the lamp delivers insects.

A second gecko sometimes approaches from the cable conduit. Both become conspicuous then: tails lifted, throats moving, pale bodies stiff against the paint. The dispute is brief. There is room along the wall, but only one particularly good place beneath the light.

Much of what lives here has such an address.

A mussel’s is a shaded seam that receives water twice a day. A larva’s is a shallow pool with no fish in it, provided the pool lasts. A shrimp’s changes as the sun rises: first the open margin, then the curb’s shadow, then the cooler water under a slab. The district contains miles of apparently interchangeable surfaces, yet a difference of an inch determines where a body can remain.

Other inhabitants have no visible outline at all. They multiply on the submerged stairs and in the warm sediment, on the undersides of floating scraps, in water too salty for the fish. They make the surfaces slippery. They turn sunlight and dissolved material into something a mouth can gather. When conditions change, their colors change too, and a pond that was brown becomes reddish over several hot days.

By sunrise, the woman has put on her boots. She walks out to check the screen.

The heron lifts away before she reaches the canal. Fish scatter at her shadow. Along the stair, the crabs withdraw so quickly that their disappearance resembles water draining from a rough surface.

She hooks weed and a strip of plastic from the intake, waits for the excess water to run off, and drops the tangle into a bucket. Two small shrimp remain caught in it. She notices their movement and tips them back.

Then she goes on toward the gate. Behind her, one crab appears in the split beneath the stair. For a while only its eyes are visible. At last it sets a foot on the wet concrete and reaches down to feed.
