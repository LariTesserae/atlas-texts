It is not quite transparent.

The shell lets the amber light through, but reluctantly, clouding it at the joints. Where the legs fold beneath the body there is a little accumulation of whiteness, as though the animal has been bent too often along the same creases. Its eyes are dark. Its stomach, when there is anything in it, makes a second, less orderly darkness behind the head. The brown line of the gut continues almost to the fan of the tail.

One antenna is shorter than the other. At its broken end there is a tiny thickening, too small to catch the light.

The shrimp fits inside the missing brick with room to turn, although it rarely turns all at once. It transfers its feet, draws the abdomen sideways, pauses with the tail against one wall. Then the head comes around. The maneuver takes place deep in the pocket, where a few grains of sand remain through every pump cycle. Out at the mouth, a loose grain would be gone immediately.

Its feet find differences in the masonry that would not register against a human fingertip. A glaze of mineral over mortar. A pit left by a dissolved grain. The edge of a deposit that has grown since the last time it stood there. It holds by several such differences at once.

The pocket is not uniformly safe. A thin current enters along its upper edge, bends down the back, and leaves near the bottom. When the pumps accelerate, this current becomes strong enough to lift the shrimp’s tail. It has to lower itself and take a new hold. There is one position, diagonal across the rear corner, in which the water presses it against the wall instead of peeling it away.

That position has become the place where it spends most of its life.

It did not arrive knowing it.

Somewhere in the system, in a junction or a wet seam beside a gate, there was another place in which a shrimp could live. From there, a small animal entered moving water. It may have let go to reach a surface less than a body length away. It may have been dislodged by a pressure change that began under a street it would never approach.

The journey need not have been long. A few seconds would have been sufficient to carry it beyond any possibility of returning.

At the chamber’s curved wall, the current brought it close enough to something rough. Several feet caught. For a moment the whole animal streamed from those points of attachment. Then it folded its abdomen and got another foot down.

There was no arrival after that. There was the work of not being taken farther.

Now it feeds along the rear of the pocket, holding with some legs while others pick at the surface. The movements are minute and busy. Material passes toward the mouth, is handled there, rejected or eaten. Much of what it gathers is of no use. A flake may be turned several times before it is released.

Food comes without a recognizable season. For three days the water may carry almost nothing. Then a valve is opened and fragments arrive: softened plant tissue, a clot of microorganisms, something pale with an edge that yields under the mouthparts. The shrimp cannot follow this abundance to its source. It can only reach into the part that passes its hole.

After a good interval, the thread through its body grows darker.

Its antennae occupy more water than the rest of it. They extend beyond the shelter, meeting changes before those changes reach the legs. They bend and recover, touch the rim, sweep particles from the head. When the flow becomes too strong, they trail back. The shorter one makes a smaller arc.

The chamber’s tremendous noise does not have to become terror in so small an animal. Much of it is simply the condition under which a foot can or cannot remain attached. Some disturbances provoke an immediate recoil. Others pass through the body without interrupting the work at the wall.

The dangerous moment is not always the loudest.

After a long, steady run, the pumps slow. Pressure releases. Water that had been holding the shrimp into its corner slips behind it instead. The animal rises a fraction from the masonry, legs extended.

It catches again.

Above it, the trapped air spreads along the ceiling. The washer falls against ceramic. A particle that has spent hours circulating through the room enters the pocket and lodges beside its head.

The shrimp begins to examine it.

There are times when its shelter becomes inadequate even though nothing outside has changed. Its shell has grown tight. Feeding diminishes. It stays farther back and works its limbs in movements that do not quite become walking.

Eventually the old covering opens behind the head.

Getting out requires room, purchase, and a brief success against every force that usually helps it stay in place. The shrimp draws free of its own legs. Its antennae come out of their fine sheaths. For a little while, two shrimps seem to occupy the recess: one pale and sharply articulated, the other soft, poorly defined, laboring beside it.

The empty one retains an excellent posture.

The living one is almost unable to hold itself.

This is the chamber at its most dangerous. The rim of mortar can damage it. A grain of grit can damage it. The ordinary current can fold an appendage that has not yet hardened. It retreats to the rear corner and remains there while water passes through the new shell and the body expands.

If the next surge enters badly, there will be no sign afterward of what went wrong.

But sometimes the timing is sufficient. The shrimp hardens. It begins to move its mouthparts. Later it feeds on what it can recover of the discarded shell.

A piece escapes and catches at the pocket’s mouth: the transparent casing of several legs, moving briskly in the current. For an hour this fragment appears more active than the animal behind it. Then it is pulled away.

The shrimp emerges a little larger. The damaged antenna may be longer now, though still unequal to its partner. Its familiar corner receives a slightly different body. The feet must find new places.

There is no view of the whole chamber from where it lives. The pipe, the rungs, the nail in its tiny shore of air do not assemble into a room around it. The nearest rim of mortar conceals most of the water. Beyond that rim are passing particles, changes in illumination, forces that arrive through the surfaces it touches.

Once, during a quiet interval, it ventures far enough out for its entire abdomen to clear the recess.

It stands on the pale repair mortar. Below it, the yellow tile shows through the water. Its long antenna reaches down past the edge of the vanished brick and touches nothing.

For several seconds it feeds there.

Then the wall begins to tremble. Before the bubbles leave the pipe, the shrimp has moved backward into the hole. It settles diagonally, takes hold, and lowers its tail.

The surge pulls one antenna flat along its body.

The other, sheltered by the rim, continues to move.
