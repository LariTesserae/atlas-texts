The large basin animals are not quite as flat as they appear. Much of each body is below the waterline, in a hollow that cannot be seen while the animal occupies it. When one rises, the plain seems briefly to have lost a piece of its surface.

It lifts itself on eight limbs.

The front pair are blunt and muscular, suited to pulling. The others are folded beneath the belly, with long, soft soles that spread under load. Standing, it is still low enough to step over, although it would be unwise to put a foot where it might settle. Its underside is pale gray, intricately pleated, and wet with a secretion that keeps the ceramic from adhering to its skin. For several moments after it moves, a pattern of those pleats remains in the water.

Then the basin refills.

An adult can stay in one place for many days. This is not sleep, or not entirely. Beneath the skirt, its limbs are busy. A foot closes a small outlet, opens it, closes it again. Folds of the belly spread across the warm floor. Water passes through channels made jointly by the animal’s body and the molded ground.

It feeds along almost its whole underside. The mouth in its blunt head is used for other things.

There are two eyes, recessed beneath clear lids, but it seldom turns to look. More important are the small pits around its muzzle and the narrow band of sensitive skin running along the edge of its skirt. A grazer moving there is felt as a sequence of distinct touches. A predator is a longer pressure, broken by the placing of its feet. Another basin animal sends a disturbance through the water before it comes close enough to touch.

The large animal knows its immediate surroundings in extraordinary detail. It knows which of its basin’s inlets is a little warmer, where the water draws fastest, which strip of its back has dried enough to itch. The distant roofs, the bright ceiling, the vapor standing over another district may not exist for it in any useful sense.

But a loose filament caught beneath a rear foot is an event.

It will spend a considerable time removing one.

***

The animals on its back are not passengers of a single kind. Some never leave the body on which they hatched. Others move between hosts when two skirts meet, or cross the ceramic during the brief wet interval after a large animal has passed.

The chalk-white mites live on the driest skin. Their legs end in split pads, one half gripping while the other advances, so that they can walk against the slow stretching of a breath without being displaced. They browse on a powdery secretion released from pores too small to see. Along the boundaries of their colonies, their bodies become faintly pink from the light transmitted through the host’s skin.

The glossy worms need more moisture. They occupy the valleys between skin folds and withdraw when a fold begins to open. A whole population can disappear from sight as the animal inhales, then emerge again while it breathes out.

There are also small, amber-colored creatures with disproportionately heavy forelegs. Each keeps a shallow pocket in the host’s skin clear of debris. When threatened, it folds itself into the pocket and seals the opening with its broad head.

These pockets are present on young basin animals before anything lives in them.

They do not all become occupied at once. A juvenile may carry empty ones for a long time, touching other animals, resting beneath crowded bridges, waiting without knowing that it waits. Eventually an amber creature arrives, examines an opening with its forelegs, and backs inside.

Once established, it rarely moves farther than the length of its own body.

To the host, it seems to register as a small, persistent point of comfort. An animal with an irritated pocket will lean that part of its back toward another animal, or press it beneath the attention of the grazers. When the pocket is occupied again, the leaning stops.

***

The blue grazers have more individual variation than their numbers suggest.

Some work outward in rings. Some keep a straight course until they encounter another body. Some return repeatedly to pores they have already emptied, testing each with a brief lift of the mouth fringe. Their feeding patches overlap, and disputes are settled by pressure: one animal sets its head against the other and pushes until a set of adhesive feet releases.

The defeated grazer may retreat only half a body-length.

They have no need to drive one another far away. The next patch of ceramic is also feeding ground, and the fluid beneath it has already adjusted to their presence.

Young grazers are almost colorless. Their blue develops first around the digestive chamber, then spreads into six branching stains toward the legs. For a short period, they look like clear animals carrying dark animals inside themselves.

They gather beneath adults, where the nutrient film is thinnest and easiest to take up. An adult feeding in a crowded nursery lifts its feet differently, peeling each pad slowly from the ground. Young bodies flow into the vacated space before the foot has settled elsewhere.

There is no recognizable act of care. Yet a nursery moves at a different pace from an ordinary feeding ground. Introduce a single newly released juvenile among adults and the change passes outward, animal by animal, until a patch the size of a hand has become slow.

The adults resume their usual movements after it leaves.

***

The predators can live among this abundance without appearing hurried.

Their heads are narrow enough to enter the feeder gaps. Behind the mouth, the body broadens slightly around a pair of organs that detect chemical changes in water. An animal preparing to shed, an injured animal, and a healthy animal that has merely stopped feeding all leave different traces.

A predator follows one trace with minute sideways movements of its jaw.

Up close, its mouth is delicate. The opposing jaws carry rows of fine gripping surfaces rather than conspicuous teeth. They close around a grazer and hold while the predator braces its feet. Adhesive pads release with a faint sequence of clicks. The prey is turned, compressed, and swallowed.

Sometimes the predator selects badly. A grazer holds on. After several attempts, the predator lets go and continues along the channel.

It does not return in anger. The grazer remains flattened against the plate for a while, its mouth fringe tucked beneath its head. Eventually it begins to feed again, first with the outermost filaments and then with all of them.

Predators rest in the dry upper portions of their recesses. Their bodies curl around a raised ceramic tongue, leaving the lower groove open for runoff. Often several translucent scavengers wait beneath a resting head.

The predator does not eat these, although it could reach them without moving its feet.

When it sheds the fine lining of its mouth, the scavengers take it away.

***

The pale fliers are the only common animals that regularly leave a surface.

Even they fly close to something: beneath a deck, beside a pipe, along the face of a pressure housing. In open country they travel just above the water, where their reflections join them into brief, four-winged figures.

Their bodies are small inside their broad wings. At rest, the wings fold into layers like damp paper, enclosing the abdomen and leaving a hooked foot exposed at either end. A row of roosting fliers looks less like a gathering of animals than a long, uneven gasket.

They do not sing. Courtship begins when one presses its folded wing against a neighbor’s and produces a vibration too faint to hear. Sometimes the vibration travels through several animals before one answers. Then there is a patient rearrangement of feet along the ledge.

Their young develop in wet cavities beneath the roosts. Before their wings expand, they resemble the silver channel animals, though without hooks. They cling head downward in the outflow, feeding on what passes them.

On the day of departure, a juvenile crawls against the water into a dry slot. It remains there while its wings unfold into a space exactly sufficient to contain them.

Its first flight may last less than a second. It crosses to the opposite wall and hangs there, opening and closing the new wings.

For a long time afterward, it is reluctant to let go.

***

Bodies also emerge from the warm vessels behind the service windows.

They leave through low, water-filled passages rather than through the galleries where the replacement parts are handled. A basin animal comes out folded narrowly, its empty digestive tissues making it light enough to float. It draws itself along with the front limbs while the others gradually straighten.

The water in these passages carries a strong chemical signature. Adults recognize it. They move aside, making openings through feeding grounds otherwise so crowded that the ceramic cannot be seen.

A newly released animal has no inhabitants on its back. Its skin is uniformly damp. It pauses beneath every obstruction, touching the underside with its muzzle, and sometimes tries to enter a gap too small to admit its shoulders.

There are suitably sized basins nearby, but finding one is still something the animal has to do.

It may pass an empty basin several times. It may settle across an inlet instead, find the pressure uncomfortable, and pull itself free. Once in the hollow, it continues adjusting long after its weight is fully supported.

The first grazers arrive at the skirt. The first mite finds a drying patch. Water begins to take familiar paths beneath the belly.

By the time the animal is feeding steadily, it no longer looks new.

Elsewhere, animals release their young into numbered depressions or carry them beneath folds of skin. The two sources mingle. Nothing in the behavior of a grazer shows whether it developed inside another grazer or in a vessel behind a wall. A predator tests either one with the same patient jaw.

***

For all the precision of their surroundings, the animals are not precise.

They hesitate. They favor one limb. They abandon a comfortable basin and return to it. Two large animals sometimes rest with their heads pressed together although equally warm, equally well-supplied hollows lie empty nearby.

One may follow another through several districts, losing contact at narrow crossings, searching the water, finding it again.

What passes between them is difficult to distinguish from feeding, cleaning, or the ordinary adjustment of crowded bodies. A muzzle moves along a skirt. A forelimb is laid across another forelimb. Both animals remain still while water gathers against them and escapes through the channels provided.

Eventually one leaves.

The other may leave too, or stay.

The machinery accommodates either choice. Beneath the ceramic, valves alter their openings. Fluid arrives at the occupied pores. An emptied basin cools by an amount too small for a hand to notice.

Up on the surface, the animal that stayed extends its blunt forelimb into the other hollow. It rests there for a while, with the pale sole turned upward.

Small grazers approach the newly shaded strip beneath it. One touches the sensitive edge of the limb and stops.

After a moment, the limb lifts just enough to let it through.
