The woman who looks after the pump lives behind the winding shop, in two rooms reached by passing sideways between a wall and a rainwater tank. She is not especially old, though people who came to the district as children remember her being in charge of things then. Her hair is usually clipped up with a large brown clasp. When the clasp breaks, she repairs its spring rather than replacing it, and for several weeks afterward it closes hard enough to hurt her fingers.

She owns rubber boots but mostly wears sandals. The boots are for opening certain inspection covers, not for rain. In rain she carries her sandals and walks barefoot, feeling for the joints between paving stones. There is a place near the footbridge where one stone tilts under a person’s weight. She steps on its outer edge without looking.

Her work is less continuous than the pump’s. There are readings to take, grease to apply, things to listen to from particular positions. A knocking heard at the window is not the same knocking heard with one hand against the pipe. Sometimes she stands for a minute with her fingertips resting on a flange, looking distracted, as though she has forgotten why she entered the room.

She is quite capable of forgetting. She has left tea on the workshop shelf until a pale skin formed across it. She once searched for a spanner while holding it under her arm. But she remembers the sound the pump made before its last bearing failed, and the weather that week, and who supplied the replacement that was supposed to fit.

Above the workbench she keeps a calendar advertising domestic water filters. Nothing is written in its squares. Measurements, telephone numbers, and sums are recorded in a school exercise book wrapped in a plastic shopping bag. The bag has become cloudy where her thumb rubs it.

Other people live here too, and not all of them hear the machinery as she does.

The machinist across the lane hears it only when it stops. His own hearing has a thin, persistent whistle in it; he turns his better ear toward customers and sometimes agrees to things he has not understood. He has a careful face and a surprisingly loud laugh. The bitter gourds above his yard belong to his wife, who objects when he hangs wire from their trellis. They have argued about this often enough that he now hangs the wire from a separate length of pipe immediately below it.

Their son does deliveries outside the district. He comes home late with dust on his trousers that is paler and drier than anything found in the lane. He wants them to move somewhere a van can reach the door. His mother wants a different kitchen. These are not quite the same wish.

Two children live in the house beside the iron gate. The younger one can tell, from the damp rising through a certain crack, when the alley behind the house will flood. This does not make her cautious. She likes the first shallow water, before it becomes brown, when the paving is still visible and a bottle cap can travel from one doorway to another. Her older brother considers such games beneath him but will spend half an hour freeing the cap with a stick.

They have been told not to touch the gate wheel. They touch its spokes in passing, lightly, to establish that the prohibition has limits.

The pump keeper knows their footsteps overhead when she is down beside the lower pipework. She knows the dragging foot of the man who sells gas cylinders, the rapid uneven tread of someone carrying a hot pot, the pause before a visitor commits to the narrow bridge. Yet there are people in the district whose names she does not know. A room above the welding shop is rented by the month. Its current occupant washes a blue shirt every evening and hangs it inside the window. Sometimes he leaves before dawn. Sometimes he is still asleep at noon.

She does not inquire. What matters to her is that he has been shown which drain must not receive cooking oil.

People bring her broken appliances because she works with the pump, although she does not think these are related qualifications. She will repair a fan if the fault is obvious. She refuses televisions. A rice cooker has sat on her table for so long that its owner has begun referring to it as hers.

She has interests that the lane gives no evidence of. In a biscuit tin beneath her bed are photographs from three trips to the coast, all taken in hard sunlight. In one she is standing beside a hired bicycle, wearing white trousers and laughing at something outside the frame. She likes large, bare restaurants with cold air pouring from ceiling vents. Given a free afternoon, she would rather sit in one of those than tend a garden.

Nevertheless, plants gather around her rooms. A neighbor gave her a cutting in a chipped mug; it rooted, outgrew the mug, and was transferred to an old washbasin. Now it partly obstructs her bedroom window. She keeps meaning to move it. At night its leaves touch the mesh with a small dry tapping that can be heard only when the rain has stopped.

Her room contains a tall wardrobe standing on four unequal brick piers. The lowest shelf is empty. She learned that much from a flood, but not every precaution has stayed with her. There are shoes beneath the bed, papers in a cardboard box, a good bag hanging lower than it should. To prepare completely for water would leave little room for living.

Toward evening, she switches on the footbridge lamp from a switch just inside the pump-house door. Then she makes another round, not a formal inspection. She moves a rag away from the belt guard. She checks that a window hook has caught. At the intake she pushes a mat of stems aside with a forked pole, waits for it to turn, and pushes again.

From somewhere behind the workshops, someone calls to ask whether she has eaten.

She says she is coming.

For another minute she stays by the grille. A length of clear plastic has wound itself around one bar below the surface. She cannot get the pole beneath it. She tries from the other side, catches it at last, and lifts it out trailing weed.

By the time she reaches the kitchen, the lid has been put back on her food. She washes her hands at the outdoor basin, rubbing carefully around her nails. Then she sits beneath the fan someone else repaired, draws the plate toward her, and asks what she has missed.
