The people are easier to find by their washing than by their houses.

They hang it low, stretched between pegs so the wind cannot lift it. A household’s clothes make a pale rectangle outside its door: shirts with reinforced shoulders, long undergarments, the broad foot-wrappings worn inside, where an unprotected heel can bruise a young floor. Children’s clothes are secured inside sleeves of netting. There are always more drying than you would expect.

The fabric is alive when new. It needs light, and occasionally a weak solution of salts. After years of wear it stops renewing itself and becomes softer, easier to mend, warmer against the skin. People save their oldest cloth for babies.

You may pass three streets of washing before you see anyone.

Then a woman comes out carrying a chair.

She is small, with gray hair cut close around her ears and a long upper lip that makes her look displeased until she speaks. Her hands are broad and ordinary. There is a pale scar across one knuckle. Nothing about her suggests that she belongs to a different order of life from you.

She puts the chair in the middle of the street, sits down, and takes off one shoe.

A seed husk has worked its way under her heel.

For a while, this is all she does. The mill strikes. She turns the shoe over and knocks it against the chair leg. Somewhere out of sight, another person coughs.

Her name is Sere. She is taking the chair to a house that has begun to forget its dining room.

***

There are fewer inhabitants than there are places prepared for them.

The city has been this way for longer than Sere’s grandmother lived, but nobody speaks of it as abandoned. Certain quarters are resting. Others have been taken out of service. Whole avenues are maintained for traffic that comes twice a year.

An empty house can remain empty if someone visits it.

Sere has forty-three houses on her route. At each, she opens the doors, walks the stairs, and sits in the rooms whose dimensions are becoming uncertain. Sometimes she brings weights. Sometimes she brings another person.

A bedroom is best maintained by sleeping in it, but the municipality no longer pays for overnight attendance except in buildings of historical importance.

The dining room requires six people on Wednesdays.

They do not have to eat, although they usually do. One brings bread made from ground storage kernels; another brings a jar of sharp, dark paste. There are white slices of something fermented until its original texture has disappeared. The meal is modest, and the conversation is often about who has failed to bring anything.

Sere’s chair goes at the narrow end of the table. It is heavier than the others.

The absent sixth diner was a man called Olan, who died during the previous cold season. He had attended for nineteen years, first as a paid occupant and later because he liked the company. Without his weight, the end wall has advanced almost a handspan.

They have advertised for a replacement. So far, the applicants have either lived too far away or wanted to come on Thursdays.

***

The inhabitants are not especially solemn.

Distance can make them seem so. Seen from the far side of a square, a person is a dark upright mark moving slowly through immense green space. You cannot see that she is laughing, or carrying a hot bowl awkwardly, or pretending not to notice someone she owes money.

Up close, they interrupt one another. They complain about the taste of reservoir water. Young people alter their clothing in ways that interfere with its growth. A fashion for very narrow cuffs briefly left half a generation with swollen wrists.

There are public arguments about whether the mills have become louder.

Older residents say yes. Mill workers say the older residents have simply moved closer, which is sometimes true in a literal sense: a block can creep toward a pressure channel over the course of several wet seasons.

The workers have the broadest faces, or seem to, because they keep their jaws slightly open against the pounding. Their teeth are capped in pale, dense material. Some wear little padded braces beneath the tongue.

Away from the mills, they speak too softly.

A turbine fitter visiting Sere’s Wednesday table will sometimes move his lips through an entire sentence before remembering that nobody else can hear him. Everyone waits. He starts again, embarrassed, much too loudly.

Their hearing often deteriorates, but not evenly. One man can hear a dropped pin and cannot hear his wife. Another has lost ordinary speech almost entirely but can tell, from two districts away, which pressure gate has a damaged seat.

Neither considers himself deaf.

***

Children live here, though the city seems badly proportioned for them.

A child sent across a square on an errand has time to become thirsty, distracted, resentful, and remorseful before reaching the other side.

Parents arrange stages. Get as far as the intake. Wait beneath the signal cage. Cross when the arrows agree.

Small children wear address tabs stitched between their shoulders. These give a name, a household, and the route by which the household is currently reachable. The tabs have pockets for amendments.

They learn the sounds before they learn the maps.

Six blows and a scrape is the northern mill clearing its chamber. A long horn followed by two short ones means that something large is coming along a freight route, even if the route looks empty. A bell that sounds once and then seems to sound again from inside your mouth means stop walking.

They also learn games their parents have forbidden.

They race their shadows in the square where shadows sometimes continue independently. They push hoops into a short arcade and argue about which opening the hoops will use. They lie flat over pavement seams to feel the deep air lift their shirts.

Sere dislikes finding them in her empty houses.

They leave small objects in rooms to see whether the objects will return: a knotted string, a seed case with a face scratched into it, somebody’s much-regretted shoe.

Once she found seven children sitting perfectly still in an upstairs bedroom.

They said they were helping.

They had heard that an occupied room stayed open, and had decided to preserve one that was scheduled to be grown shut. It had a good view of the freight yards.

For three weeks they took turns being there.

Eventually a parent brought bedding.

***

The small gray animals are commoner than they appear.

There is one beneath almost every occupied block. Sometimes there are several, but they are difficult to distinguish because they do not seem to retain quite the same outline from one sighting to the next.

Close up, they have narrow heads and a row of dark pits along either side. What looks like fur is a dense covering of short, blunt filaments, worn smooth on the belly. The legs fold underneath in pairs. When an animal is still, you can see four. When it runs, that count becomes unhelpful.

They do not seem frightened of people. They object to being watched from directly above.

If you lean over one, it stops. If you kneel beside it, it may continue past your knee, carrying a little strand of white material in its mouth.

No one has established whether the strand is food, bedding, or part of the animal itself.

The ramps beside the drains began as a precaution. During a severe contraction, the drain lips rose too far above the pavement, and people found the creatures waiting below them. Hundreds stood motionless in the open until a woman laid down a broken shutter.

Now the ramps are customary. Children decorate them. Householders argue about whose responsibility they are.

One lives under Sere’s kitchen.

She calls it Button, though she has never succeeded in calling it to her. She leaves a shallow lid of water beneath the cupboard. The water disappears, but it might simply be absorbed by the lid.

When she is ill, the animal comes farther into the room.

It does not approach her bed. It stations itself where the floor joins the outer wall, head turned toward the pressure pipes, and stays there until she gets up again.

She finds this comforting. Her son finds it ominous. Neither has persuaded the other.

***

Not everyone has an address that can be written on a child’s tab.

The city employs listeners at troublesome intersections. Most are elderly, but the work is not a retirement occupation. It requires the ability to distinguish an approaching person from the sound of someone who passed there long ago.

A listener sits in a shallow booth with three open sides. Beside the chair is a slate, a clock, and a padded mallet for the warning bell.

Some are very good.

One can recognize individual footsteps weeks before the walker arrives. She uses this mainly to avoid her brother.

Others make mistakes. A delivery is held for an hour while everyone waits for a procession that never appears. Someone crosses too early and comes out of the next street with wet clothes and no memory of water.

There are procedures for this. The wet person is brought indoors, warmed, and asked not to make any important decisions until the following morning.

Occasionally somebody returns who was not expected to return at all.

Not often. Not reliably enough to make promises.

Sere once opened a house in the reservoir quarter and found a man eating at the kitchen counter.

His clothes were unfamiliar, although much mended. He had set out two cups.

He knew the house’s former number. He knew where the cupboard had been before it was moved. He was irritated to learn that the water supply had been disconnected.

She fetched him water from the basin.

By the time she returned, the kitchen was empty except for one cup, which was warm.

For several months she visited that house first.

Then she put it back in its proper place on the route.

***

The people who descend into the plateau are readily recognized, even when they have washed.

They wear their hair covered. Their outer clothes close without loose ties, and they carry lamps in cages designed to remain open under pressure.

The deepest crews smell of wet leaves rather than cut stems.

They maintain conduits, clear constrictions, and inspect the old load-bearing ribs. Their maps are narrow things: a sequence of passages, valves, ladders, and places to turn sideways. There is no useful map of everything around them.

On returning, they often spend an hour in an open square before going home.

Their families know not to meet them at the shaft mouth.

One of Sere’s Wednesday diners worked below for thirty years. He says there are inhabited rooms down there.

Not ancient rooms with furniture preserved in them. Inhabited rooms. Swept floors, damp laundry, bowls turned upside down to dry.

He has never seen the occupants.

The others ask the questions he expects. Where do they get light? What do they eat? Why have they not come up?

He says there are supply lines that do not appear on the service charts.

This is not an answer, but he offers nothing else.

He carries sugar in a little folded packet and puts some in every drink, even broth. His wife died before he retired. He wants to move to the warmer side of the city, but the house he has chosen will not be ready for several years.

***

Most lives are spent nearer the surface.

People fall in love while waiting for a street to become continuous. They separate and disagree over which of them should keep a room that has grown around their furniture. They save for houses, although houses are plentiful, because a reliable house near water and work is not.

They grow old at different rates, as people do.

The weather enters their joints. Their fingers stiffen. A familiar walk becomes too long. Friends begin arranging visits around the availability of sheltered benches.

Sere’s son wants her to give up the outer part of her route.

She says the houses will close.

He says someone else can keep them open.

Neither mentions Olan’s empty place at the table.

On the mornings when her knee is bad, she takes a little wheeled frame. It carries weights, wedges, a flask of water, and a cushion. The frame belonged to her mother, who used it for entirely different work. One handle is polished almost white.

At each house Sere lets herself in and announces her arrival, even when the records say nobody has lived there for a hundred years.

Then she puts down her things.

She opens what can be opened. She checks the ties around the windows. In a room that has begun to narrow, she places her palms against opposite walls and stands for a while with her arms extended.

Sometimes, through the walls, she hears another person doing the same.

She has learned not to knock. Knocking can start something that takes a long time to stop.

Instead she talks a little about the weather, or the difficulty of getting decent bread near the western works.

Once, the person on the other side laughed.

It was an ordinary laugh, abruptly stifled, as though they too had been warned about making contact.

Sere stood there longer than necessary that day.

When the evening horns began, she took up her frame and went home. Her window was lit. Someone had brought in the washing, and beneath the kitchen cupboard the little gray animal had left a white strand curled beside its water.

She bent to look at it, then thought better of touching it.

Outside, the first long adjustment passed through the avenue. The floor shifted beneath her feet. She held the table steady until it was over, keeping one hand around the cup her son had set out for her.
