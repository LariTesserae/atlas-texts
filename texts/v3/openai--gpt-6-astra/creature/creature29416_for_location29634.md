The first sign of the residents is the cups.

They are left beside the blue channels, usually upside down on a folded square of cloth. Some are glazed white. Others are made from the cut ends of pipe, with handles attached through holes drilled near the rim. Each has a small defect that makes it recognizable: a chipped foot, a crooked handle, a patch of green glaze beneath the lip. Where a walkway is being drawn into a wall, the cups have been moved a little farther along.

Occasionally there is one set apart from the others, on a higher shelf.

You can wait beside these places for a long time without seeing anyone drink.

The residents are easier to notice when they carry something. A shallow basket moving along a parapet resolves, after a moment, into a basket held against a body. What looked like a vertical seam behind it becomes a separation between two legs. A face turns, and the small dark interruption of the mouth makes the whole figure suddenly difficult to overlook.

They are roughly human in height, although there is considerable variation in how much of that height they use. At rest they tend to settle: knees bending, shoulders descending, the long middle of the body gathering into two or three broad folds. A resident waiting for a lift may be no taller than the goats that graze nearby. When the lift arrives, they take hold of the doorframe and rise into themselves.

Their skin is a warm gray where it is protected. On the hands and face it carries a pale, finely cracked layer, like the bloom on old enamel. This wears thin at the fingertips. The exposed pads are dark and soft, capable of spreading over a rung or drawing together to pick a beetle out of a valve.

There are no obvious nails.

Their clothes account for some of the difficulty of seeing them. Most wear long, overlapping panels of felt, the edges stiffened with narrow ceramic beads. These garments hang square when the wearer stands and fold neatly when they crouch. Dust collects on them in the same narrow lines it makes around inspection doors.

But a resident in dark clothing is only a little easier to distinguish. Their stillness seems to let the eye finish the building across them.

They recognize one another readily.

A woman with a blue repair down one sleeve lives above the orchard, behind a cover marked with the safe operating pressure of something no longer present. Her door is too small for her shoulders. She passes through it sideways, slowly, with a motion that makes her seem for a moment very tired.

Inside, she has enough room to stand.

There is a table fixed to the wall, two cupboards, a hanging bed, and a deep niche containing several objects wrapped separately in cloth. The room has a window onto a staircase. Every afternoon, rain briefly blurs the glass.

She lives with a smaller resident whose age is difficult to judge. He has the same long hands, but his wrists are thick and his surface is still smooth. When he is absorbed in a task, he allows his feet to broaden on the floor until the toes are scarcely distinguishable. She touches his ankle as she passes, and he gathers them back under him.

They eat at the table with their cups between their plates.

Much of their food comes from the district’s slow processes. There is a soft growth beneath certain lengths of warm pipe, peeled off in gray sheets before it hardens. There are the white beetles, kept for several days in perforated tins and fed clean mineral flakes. The orchard fruit is sliced thinly and laid across a heated grate. Its interior remains clear as it cooks; only the edges become opaque.

They also eat something dark and granular stored in heavy jars. This arrives on the carts from elsewhere. When a jar is opened, the smaller resident leans forward before the lid is fully off.

The woman makes him wait while she smells it.

They have preferences. They leave things on the sides of their plates. One of the orchard trees produces fruit that nobody takes except an old resident from the lower pump terraces, who comes up with a padded basket and spends an hour choosing.

The woman’s work concerns thresholds.

Each morning she carries a straightedge, a roll of blue cord, several wedges, and a little box with a padded interior. She visits doors in the district’s newer portions. At each, she lays the straightedge across the sill, stretches the cord through the opening, then goes around by another route to examine the other side.

Sometimes this takes only minutes. Sometimes she returns home without her basket and collects it the following day.

She does not appear to be checking whether the two sides agree. They often do not. She records the difference by pressing marks into a strip of soft metal.

At a satisfactory doorway, she sits on the threshold and drinks.

At an unsatisfactory one, she drives in a wedge and hangs a tuft of dark thread at eye level. The animals generally avoid these openings. Residents stop, examine the thread, and choose whether to continue.

Once, at a door beside the extrusion works, she takes a small bell from the padded box.

She holds it on one side of the opening and shakes it. Nothing is audible. On the other side, several seconds later, there is a faint, unpleasant rattle.

She closes the door immediately.

For the rest of that day she keeps touching her teeth with the tip of her tongue.

Other residents have occupations less easy to identify. A man moves seats from one landing to another, sometimes while people are sitting on them. A pair of heavily dressed workers spend weeks tending the cupboard shadows. They lift each black deposit with broad, flexible blades, examine its underside, and either lay it back or divide it between smaller recesses.

They speak quietly while they work. Their voices have a dry, close quality, audible at the elbow and almost lost a few paces away. Laughter travels farther. It can be mistaken for a handful of washers sliding across a table.

The shadow workers are frequently interrupted by requests.

A household with a new child needs more darkness than its cupboard contains. Someone whose room is approaching a warm conduit wants a thinner piece. An elderly resident brings a covered dish in which a little shadow has gone hard around the edges. The workers soften it between their palms while she watches.

Children sleep in these kept shadows.

For the first part of their lives, they are difficult to see even at close range. A parent opens a cupboard, bends into it, and lifts out what appears to be an extra fold of cloth. Then there is a hand gripping the parent’s collar, a pale cheek, two eyes that close firmly against the light.

They are carried beneath the outer clothing until they can hold their shape while awake.

Later they spend much of their time around ladders. They climb down headfirst with the gray pipe animals, though the adults discourage this near deep reservoirs. The children have games involving dropped bolt caps, forbidden floorplates, and the question of which staircases can be made to return an object before it has finished falling.

There are injuries.

In the woman’s lower cupboard is a little splint shaped to fit two fingers. The smaller resident dislikes the sight of it. He turns it toward the back whenever he puts something away.

They gather socially where the construction is oldest. There, the floor is less likely to alter during a visit, and the walls have acquired enough irregularity to hold hooks.

People bring stools, warm dishes, pieces of troublesome equipment. Repairs take place in the middle of conversation. A story may pause while six people watch a seized spindle being eased from its housing, then resume without anyone repeating the last sentence.

They exchange news of moving rooms, good fruit, a stretch of pipe that has become impossible to sleep beside. They complain about the carts. The one carrying the upright door is especially unpopular. It sometimes stops across a crossing for days, and knocking only causes it to wait longer.

They remember routes that no longer exist.

An old woman can describe the journey to a friend’s kitchen through what is now the solid base of a cooling tower. She still begins the directions confidently. Someone touches her sleeve when she reaches the missing passage, and she sits for a moment with her mouth open, looking toward the place where she would have turned.

Their homes must be moved occasionally.

Well before a room is withdrawn into a larger structure, the occupants begin loosening its possessions from the walls. Neighbors come to help. Shelves are carried away with their contents still arranged upon them. A sleeping niche may be cut out whole and transported under a cover.

The last thing removed is usually a small patch of flooring.

It is worn darker than the surrounding surface, shaped by years of standing at a stove or beside a bed. In the new room it is fitted wherever it will lie flat. People step onto it when they first arrive, even if it has been placed in an inconvenient corner.

Some residents refuse to move.

For a while they can still be visited. Their doors narrow, their rooms shorten, and conversation takes place with knees tucked up against furniture. Later, cups are left in shallow niches outside. These are sometimes found washed.

The woman above the orchard leaves such a cup on her way to work. She never stays long. She rests two fingertips against the seam, waits, and continues down the stairs.

When the great press completes its evening stroke, residents throughout the basin pause.

Hands remain on dishes. A thread is held half drawn through felt. In the orchard, the old fruit-picker steadies his basket against a hopper.

They wait until the beads of water have flattened again.

Then, in homes close to the new paving, someone opens a cupboard and feels along its back. Others check a familiar mark on a doorjamb, or put a palm against the floor beneath the bed.

The woman returns late that evening. Before taking off her coat, she goes to the little room’s window.

The staircase beyond it has gained a landing.

She stands looking out for some time. Then she calls the smaller resident over and lifts him so he can see.

By morning they have put a pot on it.
