The animal at the rim does not come to inspect you immediately. It continues what it was doing before you arrived.

You hear a stem drawn taut, a series of short bites, then the soft recoil of leaves released from tension. After a while there is chewing. After that, nothing you can distinguish from the hollow’s other sounds.

It is still there.

When it moves again, it goes the long way around the pool. Its feet make a faint peeling sound on the wet stone. It stops twice, and at each stop several of the insects nearest it fall silent. Their calls resume behind it, so that you can follow its progress by a small interruption traveling through the margin.

It approaches your shoes before it approaches your hands.

Something touches the seam of one shoe, withdraws, and returns farther up. The contact is warm, firm, and divided into several blunt points. It holds the leather while you shift your weight. It seems interested in the fact that the outer surface moves separately from the foot inside.

Then it pulls.

Not hard enough to unbalance you. Hard enough to establish that the shoe is attached.

If you reach down, you encounter a shoulder where you expected a head.

The creature is low and broad, about the size of a person crouched under a table. Its back slopes toward the rear. The surface beneath your palm has a dense, close nap, smoother in one direction than the other, with sparse longer hairs that bend before your hand reaches the skin. Along its sides the coat parts into narrow bare channels. These are warmer and slightly damp.

It has four weight-bearing limbs and a smaller pair held beneath the front of its body. Those are what touched your shoe. Their ends spread into thick, flexible digits, without nails that you can feel. The walking feet are wider, with soft rims around a harder center. They grip by settling onto the stone. Lifting one requires a deliberate loosening, which accounts for the peeling noise.

The head is tucked low between the shoulders. You can touch the crown while it continues working at something near the ground.

There is no projecting muzzle. The mouth lies beneath a firm overhanging fold and opens sideways farther than you expect. When the animal takes a stem from you, the working edges inside feel neither sharp nor soft: a row of closely fitted ridges, like the worn teeth of a tool. It braces the stem with its small hands and draws it through those ridges. The skin comes away in a continuous wet strip.

You may never find its eyes. It does not offer its face for examination, and a finger moving toward the mouth is gently but decisively caught.

Its listening is easier to recognize.

Two long folds run back from the sides of the head. Usually they lie almost closed. When an unfamiliar sound crosses the hollow, they loosen and lift. If your wrist happens to rest against one, you feel a thin surface trembling inside.

The whole animal becomes quiet when it listens. Its jaw stops. The small hands stop. Even its breath is briefly withheld.

Then it decides, and resumes.

***

There are several of them in the hollow, though it takes a long stay to learn how many.

Their movements overlap. One animal can sound like two when it works with its hands while walking, and two resting together can breathe so nearly in time that you mistake them for one. They do not continually announce themselves. Most of their calls are small sounds made at close quarters: a dry cluck beneath the mouth, a closed-throat murmur, three quick taps of a hand against stone.

Each has a recognizable step.

One drags the outside of a rear foot. One releases its feet with unusually loud little snaps. The creature that first examined your shoe pauses before crossing the pool’s outlet, even when the water is low. It feels downstream with one hand, waits, and only then steps across.

They sleep within the vegetation, but not simply underneath it.

There are chambers in the deep folds of leaves, shaped by repeated occupation. Living stems arch over them. Older leaves form the floors. A sleeper enters backward, catches the upper growth with its small hands, and draws it down over the opening. The chamber does not quite close. There remains room for breath and for the long hairs along the animal’s sides.

When it wakes, it spends considerable time pulling fragments from its coat and eating some of them.

The animals smell of warm bark, damp wool, and the pear-scented creeper. That last smell varies. Individuals rub the vine between their hands and work the crushed stems into the bare channels on their flanks. Whether this repels parasites, helps them recognize one another, or simply feels pleasant is difficult to determine. They are thorough about it. A companion will attend to places the animal cannot comfortably reach.

They disagree during this process.

One leans away. The other follows. A hand is pushed aside, returns, is pushed aside again. Eventually there is a sharp double cluck and both become still. A moment later the grooming continues somewhere else.

***

The stripped stems are only part of their food.

They open the thick, softened layers beneath the living leaves and remove pieces of the warm fibrous material there. Some they eat. Some they roll between their palms and tuck back into the growth. They bite through roots with a patience that makes the work sound almost mechanical, then suck the cut ends.

In the pool, they turn stones.

This is done with great care. A hand explores the exposed underside while the other holds the stone at an angle. Small shelled animals are detached and put into the mouth. The stone is then lowered into approximately its former position. An animal that lets one fall too quickly may spend a long time feeling around beneath it afterward.

They also take the little hard bodies from plant roots.

Not many. They follow a root to its end, pinch something free, and hold it in the mouth without chewing. A faint ticking comes from behind the closed lips. Later they return a hard particle to the margin, pressing it into the wet matter with one broad digit.

Where they have done this repeatedly, new roots gather thickly.

Their hands are nearly always carrying something. A pellet of sediment. A bitten stem. A folded leaf containing water. They set these things down in particular places and return for them, sometimes much later. What feels like litter beside the bench may be a meal interrupted several cycles of warmth ago.

The pellets pushed out by the ring around the outlet are collected. Most are broken apart and worked into the plant beds. A few are taken to the dry rim and left there.

The animals do not inspect the ring itself. Their fingers pass over it with the familiarity of feet crossing a threshold.

***

The smallest young ride underneath an adult.

You discover this when the creature beside you appears to develop another hand.

It emerges between the larger animal’s forelimbs, catches your finger, and holds it with startling persistence. The adult continues feeding. Beneath its body is a warm hollow in the coat, occupied by something with fine skin and feet already broad enough to grip.

The young one’s listening folds are loose and disproportionately long. They brush your knuckles. It explores with its mouth, discovers that you are not yielding anything edible, and makes a thin irritated sound.

The adult answers by pressing one small hand against its back.

Older young travel along the margin on their own, though rarely far from an adult. They are noisier than their elders. Their feet detach abruptly. They spill water. They pull at leaves that are still supporting other leaves and bring down whole pockets of collected moisture.

For a while after such an accident, everything nearby calls at once.

The young animal waits beneath the downpour, making small movements of its mouth. Then it begins again.

The adults intervene most quickly near the outward lip.

There, a wandering youngster is caught around the middle and drawn inward. No warning call necessarily precedes this. The adult may have been feeding with its head turned away; one of its hands is simply in the correct place when needed.

Even grown animals seldom put a whole foot beyond the point where the slope reverses. When they reach into the growth hanging down the outer face, they spread low against the rock, three feet planted, the fourth braced against a seam. The feeding hands work beyond the edge.

They know the drop without needing to fall into it.

***

The open center is important to them.

They cross it, drink in it, occasionally leave something there to soak. But they do not build sleeping places in it or drag their food into a heap on its stones.

When a length of vine grows far across the floor, one of the animals eventually bites it through and draws it back to the margin. This is not prompt enough to look like tidiness. The vine may remain for a long time, becoming familiar beneath your feet, before it is removed.

The empty space carries information.

When a bubble breaks in the pool, listening folds lift all around it. Sometimes an animal puts its mouth close to the bedrock and gives a short, low cough. The sound passes through your soles more clearly than through the air. Elsewhere, another animal shifts its feet.

They seem able to hear things in those returns that you cannot: water beneath a slab, perhaps, or a body interrupting the smooth sweep of sound across the hollow. If you lie down in an unfamiliar place, someone will usually come to touch you.

The bench is not unfamiliar.

An animal may find you on it and leave you alone. It may also climb up beside you, turning several times before settling with its rear against the warm, seamless surface. The bench accommodates its weight without an audible creak.

Sitting together requires negotiation. The animal occupies more room relaxed than standing. A foot presses against your thigh. You move it; it puts the foot back. Eventually it tucks the limb beneath itself and lets its flank rest against you.

Its body warms in small waves.

As the next rise of heat passes through the mountain, it becomes more wakeful. You feel muscles tightening under the coat. It lifts its head before the first drops fall.

***

Sometimes one visits the post.

It stands on the inward side, presses the underside of its head to the smooth shaft, and rests both small hands against it. Its feet move a little until they are firmly placed.

There is no visible attention to give, and no sound you can reliably separate from the hollow’s continuous noise. After a while the animal leaves.

Another may come soon afterward. Sometimes none comes for several changes of warmth.

The youngster tries this too, reaching around the post with hands that do not quite meet. It stays only briefly before biting at a root nearby.

You cannot tell whether the adults are operating something, receiving something, or enjoying a sensation. They do not bring food to the post. They do not sleep beside it. When you stand there for a long time, an animal may wait behind you, touch your calf, and wait again.

If you move aside, it takes your place.

***

The one with the hesitant step at the outlet eventually grows accustomed to you.

It still examines anything new you bring. Wet cloth receives prolonged attention. Metal is touched with the mouth and abandoned. Once it takes a knotted cord and spends much of a warm interval undoing the knot, then leaves the straightened length beside your foot.

It has an old injury beneath one shoulder, a patch where the nap grows in several directions around a firm ridge. When it rests against you, it usually places that side outward.

It likes the thick bases of the pepper-scented leaves and dislikes having water dropped onto its head. Before lying down, it feels the bench with the backs of its little hands. It does this even when it has only just stood up.

In time you learn its approaching step well enough to make room before it touches you.

One interval, while the hollow is gathering rain, it arrives carrying a tough stem. It sits beside you and strips the rind. Small pieces fall against your leg. Beneath its chewing you hear the pool, the insects, another animal moving carefully past the post.

It pauses when you shift.

You settle again, and its listening folds relax against its neck. One hand searches along the bench until it finds your wrist. It rests there lightly while the other turns the stem, bringing an unbitten portion to its mouth.
