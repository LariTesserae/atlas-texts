The people who live here are not necessarily the people you see crossing the roofs. Some of those arrive before daylight on the lower freight line and leave with their lunch tins empty, never having climbed high enough to see either mountain. Some of the residents work elsewhere. In the morning they descend through six or seven buildings to catch a passenger car, carrying dry shoes in bags.

You can sometimes recognize a resident by the way they wait.

At a junction, a visitor watches the approaching vehicle. A resident watches the little lamp above the drain. They know that a certain delivery cannot pass until a platform has lowered, and that the platform cannot lower until its runoff has cleared. There is time to fasten a cuff, unwrap a sweet, finish a sentence. Then, without any apparent decision, they step back.

They are not all patient people. They quarrel with lifts. They press call buttons repeatedly. But they have a practiced sense of which delays can be hurried.

***

One of them is a woman called Irena, who lives on a level designated Four in one stairwell and Seven in another.

Her rooms occupy the end of a former testing hall. The rest of the hall still tests something: small pumps, now, although when she moved in it was pressure vessels. From her kitchen she can hear the pumps starting in groups. The sound is very low, more an unevenness in the refrigerator’s hum than a separate noise. When the testing stops for a holiday, she sleeps badly for the first night.

She is sixty-three. She has a broad face, short hair that remains flattened on one side after she takes off her rain hood, and one finger that will not straighten. Her work clothes are good, expensive clothes, repaired badly. She can seal a flexible pipe joint in a space too narrow to turn her head, but her sewing comes out in tight, puckered knots.

For most of her working life she maintained drainage controls. Now she trains the people who do it. Two mornings a week she goes out with a tablet, a thermos, and a young technician who is expected to explain each fault before she touches anything.

“Upstream,” she says, when they are looking too closely at the thing that has broken.

It is not a philosophy. Usually there is a blocked screen upstairs.

Her own kitchen tap drips. The replacement cartridge has been sitting beside the sink for eleven days.

The apartment has two windows. One looks into an alder trough, at almost exactly the height where a person standing outdoors would expect to see a cellar window. The other looks across an enclosed gap into a packaging office. At night, if both rooms are lit, Irena and the office supervisor can see one another clearly enough to exchange gestures. They have known each other for nine years without learning much beyond names, working hours, and which of them has had a cough.

Irena puts her hand up when she draws the blind.

The supervisor generally puts his up too.

***

There are homes with broad roof gardens and homes whose only daylight arrives through wired glass at the end of a corridor. There are apartments so warm that their occupants keep basil through the winter, and others in which condensation darkens the backs of wardrobes.

Heat is one of the district’s unequal possessions.

A floor above a drying room may be lovely in January and almost uninhabitable in August. When a production line closes, a household can lose the warmth it has depended on for twenty years. Buildings are insulated afterward, ducts redirected, arguments held over who should pay. Meanwhile, people buy slippers and move their beds away from the walls.

The more desirable rooms are not always the highest. High rooms have light, but they have wind and long journeys home with groceries. A sheltered apartment beside a reliable lift can be worth more than a view.

People describe where they live by a combination of official address and current inconvenience.

Above the blue tank.

Past the bridge that is still locked.

Use the nursery stair; the other one smells of sealant.

A delivery address can acquire a whole paragraph during repairs. Relatives keep old versions and telephone angrily from the wrong door.

Inside, the rooms are less unusual than the routes to them. There are washing racks, chargers, cheap bowls, photographs curling in their frames. Someone has put a polished black dining table in a room scarcely wide enough to walk around it. Someone collects model ships. Someone leaves every cupboard open while cooking.

Not every possession folds away. People become particularly attached to things that are difficult to move.

***

Irena’s granddaughter stays with her on alternate weekends.

The girl is nine and knows how to read the district’s warning lights, though she sometimes pretends not to when told to come indoors. She calls a nearby pedestrian bridge the slow bridge because once, when she was six, it moved while she was watching. It has not moved since. Its official name appears on a plaque behind a currant bush.

She has favorite places that are not intended as places at all: a widening of the path beside a warm wall, a step from which she can see into the top of a lift car, the space beneath an exterior stair where discarded cable ties collect.

The adults say there is nowhere for children to play. The children can list twenty places, most of them forbidden for different reasons.

They race floating things through shallow channels. They make elaborate distinctions between a race conducted during rainfall and one conducted on scheduled discharge. They know which seeds turn in circles and which travel point-first. A child who uses a piece of packing foam may win, but there will be an argument about whether it counts.

They are less casual about height than a stranger might expect. A gap with a visible bottom twelve meters below is familiar; an open hatch into darkness is frightening. They crouch beside such openings and call into them until someone comes to see what is wrong.

Irena’s granddaughter has seen exposed soil on trips beyond the district. What interests her is not that plants grow in it. Plants grow everywhere. It is that someone can dig without first obtaining a drawing.

She once brings home a stone from a mountain path. For several weeks it sits on Irena’s table, leaving grit whenever it is moved. Then it becomes the weight that holds down a troublesome corner of the kitchen blind.

***

Many residents work with living things, but this does not make them uniformly gentle.

A nursery worker can discard a thousand failed seedlings before lunch. A roof gardener may hate the birch outside his bedroom because its leaves block his drain. There are people who grow rare alpine flowers in trays and people who pour wash water into the nearest planter despite the notices.

The greenery is work. Its beauty is not lost on them, but neither is the cost of its weight.

In the leaf season, crews pass with narrow carts, lifting wet material out of gutters by the armful. Their gloves acquire a sour smell that persists after washing. Compost workers come home with warm faces and cold ears. People who repair greenhouse screens have little paired scars across their knuckles.

Office workers live here too, and nurses, cooks, a woman who translates equipment manuals, a man who does not work and resents being asked what he used to do.

There are teenagers who want to leave because everything is damp and everybody knows their parents. There are teenagers who like the concealed stairwells, the late shifts, the possibility of walking for an hour under cover while rain passes overhead. They have learned where the cameras cease to overlap. Their graffiti appears on surfaces no maintenance plan considers accessible.

One inscription has survived behind a transparent section of newly installed duct. The duct makes it impossible to paint over. For a while people photograph this. Eventually it is only something passed on the way to work.

***

The district has mice.

Not many, and not everywhere. The food stores are well sealed, the service shafts trapped, the warmest spaces inspected often. But there are old partitions whose interiors nobody can reach without dismantling something still in use.

Irena hears a mouse behind her kitchen wall one winter. It scratches for three evenings near the pipe chase. She takes everything out of the low cupboard and finds nothing.

Her granddaughter wants it caught alive. Irena says they first have to establish that there is a mouse.

For another week they refer to it as the possible mouse.

Then the sound stops.

There are also small flies in overwatered pots, woodlice beneath trays, slugs that emerge from the deep planting beds and leave silver marks on doors. Life persists in the intervals between cleaning schedules. Sometimes it is tolerated and sometimes removed with considerable effort.

A bird nesting above a loading light can delay work for weeks. The workers may be genuinely fond of it and still wish it had selected another light.

***

What distinguishes the long-term inhabitants is less a love of machinery than an accumulation of consequences.

They remember when a roof was open, when a passage was public, when the present row of shops was a staff canteen with yellow tables. They remember a winter in which a valve froze and water entered three apartments through the ceiling lights. An elderly man still unplugs everything during a particular combination of frost and rain. The drainage has since been rebuilt twice.

Knowledge becomes lodged in individual people.

A woman at the bakery knows whom to call when the west lift stops answering. A retired cleaner remembers a door concealed by insulation. Irena knows that a pipe labeled as an overflow was connected to the wash system seventeen years ago and never relabeled.

This can make them useful. It can also make them impossible.

They oppose sensible alterations because they distrust the people proposing them. They insist on unnecessary precautions and occasionally prove right. Younger workers complain that nothing is written down, then discover three contradictory records and begin keeping notebooks of their own.

Irena does not always enjoy being consulted. Sometimes she lets the call go unanswered. Sometimes she answers and says she has retired, although she has not quite retired and everyone knows it.

***

One afternoon, the crew arrives to repair the membrane outside her kitchen window.

The alder roots have entered a seam. For months, water has been finding its way into the testing hall, where a bucket occupies a marked square between two machines. The tree must be lifted, its rooting layer reduced, the trough opened and relined.

Irena has signed the access form. She has moved her herbs indoors. Nevertheless, when the crew begins, she stands at the window in a manner that makes them work self-consciously.

Without the soil, the trough looks shallow and disreputable. There are bottle caps in the aggregate, a fragment of old cable, the compressed remains of something woven. The tree hangs in a sling, its branches dripping onto the workers’ shoulders.

For the first time in years, daylight reaches the lower half of Irena’s kitchen wall. It reveals grease beside the stove that she has not noticed.

She cleans it while they work.

By evening the alder is back, held upright with new straps. It has fewer roots and a smaller crown. The foreman says it should recover, but they will have to watch it through the first dry spell.

After the crew leaves, Irena opens the window. The sill is gritty. Beyond it, fresh soil lies dark against the pale edging of the repaired trough.

She reaches out to retrieve a label they have left caught in a twig, then pauses with her hand around the branch. There is more movement in the tree than there used to be.

From inside, her granddaughter asks whether the window can be closed. She is cold.

Irena lets go and brings her arm back in.
