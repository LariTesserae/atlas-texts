The animal beneath the center is thinner than the crack it uses to come out.

At first, only an edge appears: a damp, smoke-colored margin advancing between two plates of stone. It could be water if water paused to let a beetle pass. Along its leading edge are short hairs, each with a clear bead at the tip. These touch the floor separately. A hair finds a grain of sand and withdraws; another finds the polished track left by generations of larvae and lies down along it.

More of the animal follows. It spreads without seeming to pull anything after it. Beneath its skin, pale structures loosen from a tight arrangement and move apart, like the ribs of a fan opening underwater.

It is not transparent everywhere. There are cloudy patches, a rust-colored branching vessel, little yellow bodies clustered around a thicker place near one side. Through the clearest portions, the shallow pits remain visible, slightly enlarged.

The larvae carrying their dead approach it without slowing. At its margin, they release their burdens and back away.

Sometimes they back away too late.

A fold travels along the animal’s edge, turns over a larva, and holds it with exquisite care. The larva kicks. For a while its movements are visible through the fold. Then the folded place becomes opaque, and the animal advances another fraction of an inch.

There is no ceremony about this. The other larvae keep working.

Fully extended, it would cover much of the pale oval, though it seldom extends fully. Parts of it stay beneath the floor. Its thickness changes continually as material is drawn into one region and out of another. A portion that looked like a stain rises into a blunt, muscular knob. Elsewhere, a substantial ridge dwindles until an insect can step across it without lifting its body.

The exposed surface dries quickly. Within minutes, it acquires the color and fine powdery texture of the rock.

This accounts for some of the hollow’s apparent emptiness.

An observer who remained through several evenings would begin to recognize the small inaccuracies: a pit on the wrong side of a seam; a mica flake that appears in the same place after being brushed away; a shallow depression that slowly closes when a moth settles inside it. The animal reproduces what lies beneath it, but loosely, from an imperfect familiarity. It is particularly bad at shadows. When a leaf’s shadow crosses its back, a dark shape sometimes persists there after the leaf has moved.

By morning these errors have faded.

Its mouth is underneath, and there are several places where it can bring the mouth to the surface. The larvae know most of them. They bring husks, eggshells, the hollow abdomens of insects emptied by other hunters. Mineral fragments are accepted too. A pale chip may remain inside the animal for days, moving by degrees toward the rust-colored vessel, becoming smaller and rounder along the way.

It rejects some things immediately. Bitter seeds. Certain mites. Once, the severed head of a particular beetle causes its entire western margin to recoil. For the rest of that night the beetles of that kind cross the center unharmed, walking over folds that close around almost everything else.

At intervals it grows a temporary head.

A thickening gathers near the fern. Two short stalks push upward, darkening at their ends. Beneath them, a slit opens and works from side to side, taking in air. The whole structure is scarcely taller than a bent finger, and usually lasts only a few minutes.

With this head it investigates things it cannot investigate through the floor.

It watches the lizard.

The lizard watches back.

Neither seems capable of catching the other under ordinary circumstances, but each has eaten portions of the other. The lizard’s missing toes are old injuries. Along the animal’s eastern edge is a row of pale indentations which never quite disappears, even when that edge has been drawn under the stone and made into something else.

Their encounters are brief and concentrated. The head rises. The lizard shifts its weight. Both remain still until a passing insect gives one of them something more immediate to do.

The animal’s greatest activity occurs when the upright gathers moisture.

Well before the seams darken, slender cords tighten beneath the floor. Soil lifts almost imperceptibly around the fern. The animal draws its exposed portions inward, making itself thick and narrow along the route to the vine.

Under the roots it has a part that does not change shape much.

This part fits against the buried base of the upright. It is tough, blackened, and divided into close-set chambers. The walls of those chambers pulse when dew forms above them. Water passes through some; others contain air, or a clear jelly in which bright grains travel in short, abrupt runs.

A root has entered one chamber and come out through another. The animal has grown a smooth collar around it.

Here its flesh is scarred in regular lines. Some of the scars match ridges on the buried object. Others have no counterpart now visible. The oldest tissue is almost as hard as the upright itself, and has to be shed in small pieces. Those pieces are carried away by flat gray scavengers, which hide beneath them while eating their softer undersides.

The animal cannot draw itself free of this attachment. Whether it ever could is difficult to tell.

When rain falls, it behaves as though it might.

It contracts toward the vine with such force that the floor gives a faint ticking sound. Its broad margins gather into ropes. The temporary head becomes larger than usual, and the dark tips of its stalks separate into clusters of little shining points.

It climbs.

For a few minutes, a heavy, awkward body can be seen among the leaves: folded flesh, gripping lobes, a wet underside with hundreds of minute openings. In this shape it seems poorly made for the hollow. Too much of it hangs unsupported. Parts lose their hold and have to be gathered back. The vine bends, shedding water.

The head reaches above the lip.

Cold rain strikes it. The shining points turn toward the open plateau.

Then the chambers at the foot of the upright contract, or the animal tires, or the rain weakens. It lowers itself. The ropes loosen. Its body runs back across the warm floor, slowly enough that beetles stranded on its folds can climb off.

For several days afterward it eats heavily.

There are smaller animals like it in the hollow.

Most live beneath flakes of stone and never grow larger than a thumbnail. They have the same beaded hairs, the same tendency to reproduce a patch of ground slightly incorrectly. Their cloudy internal structures are simpler. Some are eaten by the larger one. Others approach its edge and remain there through the night, their tissues meeting along a seam that becomes impossible to find.

By dawn, one may have departed with a yellow body inside it that it did not possess before.

One may also have left behind a third animal, smaller than either.

Their dealings are hard to distinguish from feeding. A small one can spend hours buried in the larger one and emerge unharmed, while another is absorbed so gently that it continues extending its sensory hairs until there is no separate body left to extend them from.

Beneath the warm oval, where the stone lifts a little from its bed, they lie together through the dry hours. The larger animal occupies the low roof. The smaller ones cling below it. Between them move mites, pale worms, and scavengers carrying pieces of food too large to turn around with.

There is very little unoccupied space down there.

Near sunset, before anything stirs on the exposed floor, one of the larger animal’s thin lobes reaches upward through a pit. It feels around the opening, rests briefly against the warm rim, then turns outward.

The first larvae find it already waiting.

They put down what they have brought.
