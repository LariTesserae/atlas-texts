In the late afternoon, some of the pale shapes beneath the cliff shelves turn themselves over.

They have been lying with their backs against the warm stone, their limbs gathered over the softer parts of their bodies. From the basin they look like another layer of fallen rock. When they wake, a dark hand appears first, or a narrow head lifts from between two folded joints. One reaches out without otherwise moving and touches the underside of the shelf above it. Others remain asleep until the shadow has covered them completely.

Their backs are not shells, though they resemble shells at a distance. The skin there is thick, divided by shallow creases into broad, irregular fields. Fine dust collects in the creases. On an old individual, small plants sometimes take root in this accumulation; these are usually picked out by a companion, but not always. There is one in the eastern settlement who carries a tuft of stiff grass above the right shoulder. It has been trimmed several times. Its roots must itch.

The pale surface gives way, along the sides, to a thin brown skin that folds easily. Under the arms it is almost purple. They have four short walking limbs and, farther forward, a pair of longer limbs whose hands can reach the ground but ordinarily do not. At rest the whole animal is low and rather broad. Standing to work, it raises its front half, unfolding more height than seemed to be present.

A large adult can reach a person’s chest. Most are smaller.

Their heads are narrow because much of what one expects to find in a head is elsewhere. They breathe through openings along their flanks. Their strongest sense of vibration lies in their walking feet. Beneath the jaw hang two soft, dark lobes, usually folded together, which open slightly in damp air. The eyes are small and deeply shaded. They see well across the basin, but at close quarters they are always touching things.

A hand rests on a wall before its owner goes through an entrance. A foot tests a slab while the other three remain on gravel. Two of them passing in a confined space may lay their fingers briefly against one another’s sides.

They can move very quietly. Their children cannot.

The children have disproportionately long hands and a troublesome eagerness to use all six limbs at once. They scramble over sleeping adults, dislodge pebbles, and knock their hard backs against low ceilings. The three clicks sometimes heard beneath a ledge are often an adult telling a child to stop. The instruction has several degrees of force, distinguishable chiefly through the stone.

Their settlements occupy the scooped lower faces of the cliffs, above the heaps of fallen rock but below the shelves used by the fliers. Little has been excavated. A hollow has been deepened here, a loose slab secured there. Partitions are made of thin stone, fitted into grooves with wedges of felted plant fiber. Seen from below, a dwelling is mostly shadow.

Inside, it is crowded.

Sleeping places, food trays, old tools, bundles of fiber, flat stones selected for purposes not immediately apparent: all occupy the same few ledges. Objects hang from pegs at the exact height where a raised forebody will bring a hand to them. The youngest sleep in shallow baskets whose rims prevent them from rolling onto their backs, a position from which they have difficulty righting themselves.

There are colored things in these rooms. Pink chips from the interior of freshly broken stone are fitted into wall cracks. Beetle wing cases are sewn along the edges of carrying cloths. Some households keep pieces of translucent mineral in slots through which the last sunlight passes. For a few minutes each evening, red or yellow patches lie on the occupants’ faces and hands.

They watch these patches with an attention they rarely give the sunset itself.

Most food is collected at dawn and dusk. They eat the amber growth of the seams, but not by licking it directly. A thin flexible blade lifts the swollen film from the underside of a stone. The blade is wiped on a porous cake of ground seeds and dried roots, which absorbs both the food and some of its moisture. These cakes are carried in folds of skin beneath the forward arms.

Fresh ones smell faintly of resin. Old ones are very hard.

They also gather the white-backed beetles, dig for the storage organs of cushion plants, and trap small animals under weighted slabs. A herd animal brought down by one of the stone-colored predators draws their attention from a considerable distance. They wait until the hunter has fed, then approach in a group, carrying poles.

There is nothing ceremonial about this. It is dangerous, unpleasant work, and individuals who are good at it are sometimes tiresomely proud.

The broad undersides of the flat grazers are particularly valued. They yield a tough, supple membrane used for water pouches and the diaphragms of listening tools. No household likes killing one near its own dwelling. The animals become accustomed to the inhabitants and will press themselves against a sleeping adult for warmth. Consequently, a fair amount of trade consists of one settlement bringing another meat that nobody there recognizes.

Their own young know these arrangements earlier than adults think.

They do not drink often, but thirst changes their appearance. The soft folds along the body draw inward. The dark lobes under the jaw become narrow and stiff. Someone badly thirsty has difficulty judging the position of a hand without looking at it.

This is one reason they attend so closely to the waterworks.

They did not build the pale structures. At least, none of the surviving accounts says that they did, and several contain quite specific stories of discovering them already in operation. Those accounts disagree about nearly everything else.

Their understanding is practical and uneven. They know which outlets can safely be cleared and which must never be scraped. They can distinguish a change of destination from an ordinary adjustment by placing a membrane stretched over a small frame against the outer wall. They know that one building must be approached from below after a cold morning, although it can be approached from any direction in the afternoon.

They do not know what would happen if this rule were ignored. The person who last tried did not return along any of the paths watched by the others.

Not every structure is tended. The partly collapsed one has been left alone for longer than the oldest living inhabitant remembers. Sometimes its threshold is swept. The shrub growing there is carefully worked around.

At another, near the head of a broad western gully, a keeper arrives each evening carrying a roll of tools and something to eat. She is recognizable by a scar across two of the pale fields on her back. One of her rear feet has fewer toes than the other.

She touches the building in five places. She waits. Then she puts a thin strip of dark material beneath the outlet and watches it curl.

The strip is cut from the shed skin of one of the flying animals. It reacts to moisture too slight to feel. The keeper has several, differing in age and thickness, each stored between smooth stones. She distrusts the newest one.

Inside the building, blue light falls across her hands.

She does not walk directly toward the inner doorway. She advances along the wall, stopping where a pale mark has been rubbed into the floor. From there she reaches sideways into what seems, from the entrance, to be empty space. Her fingers encounter something. There is a small click, a pause, and another click farther away.

Some evenings this is all she does.

On others she comes out rapidly and lies flat against the terrace with every limb spread. Those waiting nearby do the same. The air above their backs acquires a dense, cold weight. Dust moves toward the doorway rather than away from it.

Afterward she may say that the building is drawing well.

The keepers are not a separate kind of person. They quarrel over food, have children, neglect their homes, and occasionally leave the work because they cannot bear the interiors anymore. A replacement learns by accompanying someone else, sometimes for years. Instruction includes much standing still.

The work rewards sensitivity, but also a certain dull persistence. A brilliant individual may notice a new relation between two clicks and touch the wrong surface before anyone can stop them. A less inventive keeper may spend forty seasons bringing a reliable evening darkening to the seams.

Both kinds are remembered.

The basin is divided into gathering grounds, although their boundaries would be difficult to draw on a flat map. Some follow cracks. Some pass between particular plants. Others apply only when a given waterwork opens onto cloud, or only to the morning harvest. A household can own the right to lift a stone but not to take what grows beneath it.

Disputes are common.

They can argue almost silently, feet planted against a shared slab, each sending a rapid sequence of taps through it. The hands move as well, indicating distances, quantities, the shape of a former plant. To someone watching from ten paces away, three angry adults appear to be studying the ground.

Occasionally one lifts all four feet in succession and walks off. This is an unmistakable refusal to listen.

They have laws against diverting seams. Such laws would not be necessary if nobody wanted to do it. A little packing of dust beneath one edge, a fresh chip wedged where two plates meet, can favor a private hollow for several evenings before the change is noticed farther down.

The damage is not always easy to reverse. Water may continue along its altered route after the wedge has been removed. Then neighbors who would prefer never to touch one another again must spend days working side by side, warming one stretch of stone, shading another, waiting for the thread to resume its old course.

Some seams bear the names of the people who spoiled them.

Others bear names nobody can explain.

Their histories are kept partly in speech and partly in things held while speaking. A notched piece of pink stone, turned through a particular sequence in the hands, carries a list of winters. A loop of knotted fiber records the households that once shared a waterwork now dry. Without someone who knows the account, these are simply stones and knots.

The old teach the young by giving them objects to hold. An inattentive child may learn the motion perfectly and miss the story altogether. Later, in adulthood, the hands remember something the mouth cannot supply.

This troubles them. They do not regard forgetting as natural merely because it happens often.

There are also stories told with nothing in the hands. These are less carefully preserved and more readily enjoyed. Many concern journeys into the waterworks. In one, a traveler finds a place where water covers every surface except a small warm stone, and spends a miserable night sitting on it. In another, someone returns from an interior carrying a second shadow that refuses to lie on the ground.

Children try to make their shadows do this. At low sun, with the help of a vertical slab, they can almost succeed.

The inhabitants rarely leave the plateau. Their bodies manage poorly in cool, wet country, where the pale skin softens and small infections take hold in its creases. But they are curious about what lies below. Travelers returning from the lower slopes are asked about trees: how closely they stand, whether their tops touch, what the ground feels like beneath so much moving shade.

Seeds are brought back and planted near the settlements. Most fail. A few survive for years in sheltered hollows, watered from personal rations and fussed over by people who insist they are not wasting anything.

One dwelling contains a wooden bowl.

It is much repaired. Nobody uses it for water.

Near midnight, when the stone finally begins to cool, the settlements are most wakeful. Food is broken up and shared. Tools are passed between households. The soft clatter of work reaches only a short way into the basin.

Farther out, solitary figures move between the pale buildings. They carry no lights. Sometimes one stops with a hand suspended above a seam, feeling the cooler air immediately over the water. Sometimes two meet and remain together for a while, their walking feet touching the same plate of stone.

Then they separate, each following a route that is visible only in fragments.

By dawn, most have returned to the cliff hollows. One or two are still outside when the amber films begin to swell. They make a last small harvest, wipe their blades, and turn toward home.

The children have already woken. From beneath the shelves comes the irregular sound of someone learning, unsuccessfully, to be quiet.
