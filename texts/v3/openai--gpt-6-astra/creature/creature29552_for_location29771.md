The most numerous inhabitants have almost no outline until they move. A mite at rest is a slight thickening of the flour. Its legs are finer than the cracks between grains; its body holds a little of the window light without seeming to cast any back. When it starts across bare stone, it becomes an animal. When it reaches the meal again, it becomes difficult to distinguish from what it eats.

There are several kinds. Some are rounded and slow, their bodies swollen behind a small working head. Others run on longer legs, pausing at the margins of shed skins. These do not feed on the flour. They feel along an edge, enter beneath it, and sometimes come out carrying another mite, which continues to move for a while.

Under the washer, where the meal has been pressed into a firm ceiling, they live upside down as readily as upright. The distinction matters less there than the width of a passage. A single husk can separate two populations that seldom meet. A beetle shifting it brings them together.

The beetles are the conspicuous animals, although none is large enough to cover a letter on a printed page. Most are brown: not the same brown, but shades of dry bread crust, translucent horn, old varnish. Freshly emerged ones are lighter. For a few hours their joints look unfinished, their wing cases soft and faintly wrinkled. They stay beneath things until they darken.

One has lost the tip of an antenna. It tests the stone unevenly, touching farther ahead on one side than the other. This gives its progress a small recurring turn, corrected every few steps. It can still find food. It can still force its head beneath a fragment that looks too low to admit it, flatten itself, and pull the rest of its body through.

Flour gathers on the beetles wherever a surface will hold it. Their eyes remain dark. Their feet clean themselves by use, emerging briefly from the dust as polished hooks. When two meet in a narrow trough, neither always gives way. They press together, lift, turn sideways. One climbs over the other. The lower beetle carries the added weight for a few steps without changing direction.

The passengers around its leg sockets stay attached.

These travelling mites occupy places the beetle cannot easily reach. Close against a joint, a passenger is sheltered from the rubbing walls of a passage and from much of the host’s grooming. It does not need the beetle to go far. Beyond a ridge of compacted flour there may be a fresher deposit, a dead moth, a pocket crowded with animals of its own kind. What looks from the aisle like one patch of dust contains many possible destinations.

The larvae are less definite in their movements. A head appears, followed by a pale, jointed length that seems to have been drawn out of the meal itself. The head is harder and darker than the body. It works at something too small to identify. Behind it, the soft segments shorten, swell, and lengthen again.

A larva in a silk tube feeds with most of itself concealed. Its shelter is untidy from outside, furred with flour and bits of husk, but the passage within is fitted closely to its body. It can draw back quickly. The exposed head vanishes; the mouth of the tube remains, apparently empty.

As the larva grows, the fit changes. It extends the passage or leaves it. Abandoned silk continues to bind the flour after its maker has gone, making little roofs beneath which smaller animals gather. Eventually a beetle breaks through, or a fresh fall loads the roof until it settles. A structure that took repeated journeys and many attachments to build becomes a firmer patch underfoot.

The spiders live where this traffic narrows.

The one in the pupal casing is easy to mistake for part of its lodging. Its forelegs lie along the split rim, brown against brown. Sometimes one leg lifts without the body appearing. It touches a thread and rests again.

Inside, the spider has turned. Its abdomen occupies the space where the moth’s abdomen formed; its legs fold against a shell made by another animal’s change of shape. The casing is dry, light, and just large enough. Silk fastens it to the debris so that it does not roll.

The spider beneath the iron is more exposed. Dust collects on its web, adding weight and making some strands useless. It shifts its feet, draws a strand inward, works at it with its mouth. Its snare is never finished for long. A falling husk can wreck one side. A beetle can drag away an attachment without being caught.

What it usually takes is smaller: something that walks into a low thread, pulls, and finds another thread against a second leg. The spider approaches over the parts of the web that still hold. Beneath it, the caught animal disturbs a few grains. Farther away, the ordinary traffic continues.

The ants are visitors, but repeated visits have given them a place here. Their route is kept open by use rather than construction. At the mortar crack, their bodies pass close enough for antennae to touch. Those carrying nothing can flatten themselves into the irregular opening. Those carrying food must turn it.

A dead insect’s leg may catch three times at the same projection. The worker pulls, backs away, takes a different grip. Another worker touches the load and passes. For a moment the leg points outward into the hollow, larger than the head carrying it. Then it folds or breaks, and both disappear.

Not everything living here stays long enough to become familiar. A newly emerged moth rests with its wings still short and thick, fluid extending them while its feet hold to the cocoon. For this interval it needs space it has never needed before. The larva could feed and grow beneath the washer. The moth must leave that shelter to finish becoming itself.

At the open edge, its wings lengthen among grains of flour. They acquire their flat shape, their faint pattern, their powdery fringe. One touches the underside of the bearing. The moth steps sideways and touches it again.

Below, a beetle enters the cocoon it has vacated. It advances until the passage closes around its shoulders, backs out with silk adhering to its head, and goes beneath the washer.

There are eggs there, too, fixed in recesses or loose among the meal. Most cannot be seen without moving the things that protect them. Beside them lie empty eggshells, cast larval skins, a head capsule with nothing behind its jaws. The living bodies fit among these remains. A mite crosses the hollow of a shed skin and is briefly visible through it.

Another delivery of flour begins. The nearest beetle lowers its head. The spider draws farther into the moth casing. Under the washer, a larva keeps feeding, its shelter admitting only a faint new dust through the gaps around the rim.
