The one that lives nearest the stairs sleeps behind the third shutter, where the bottom edge has pulled away from its guide. The gap looks too narrow to admit anything larger than a hand. This is misleading. Most of the animal’s height is a way of holding itself.

At rest it spreads low, its weight carried on a succession of folded limbs. There are six of these, though the front pair are seldom used for walking. Its back is covered in overlapping pads of dark skin, bare at their centers and fringed with short, colorless hairs. The pads slide across one another when it turns. Underneath, it is pale and surprisingly creased. It can flatten enough to pass beneath the shutter, then rise on the other side to something like the height of a seated person.

It does this slowly. A limb comes through, finds purchase, and waits. Then another. The broad head follows sideways, one cheek pressed to the floor. Last comes the abdomen, which seems for a moment certain to catch.

There is an old scar there. Sometimes it does catch.

Once free, the animal stays beside the shutter until the machinery has completed a cycle. Its eyes are small, wet, and set far apart, suited to distinguishing the maintenance lamp from the dark around it but not much more. It holds its front limbs slightly raised. Along their inner edges are fine combs of stiff hairs. These receive the movements of air close to the floor.

Sound reaches it through other parts of the body. The undersides of its walking feet have soft central patches, and beneath its throat hangs a loose fold of tissue that it can lower against the paving. When it does so, the head seems to melt downward.

It spends a good deal of its life in this position.

The chamber is full of distances it can measure without crossing them. The stream behind the grille is far away through the air and close through one buried length of pipe. The gray animals are nearly silent until they put their weight on the basin’s rim. The delivery carrier announces itself while still inside the wall, by a vibration that arrives along the shutter guides.

Occasionally the animal makes a mistake. A loose fitting can resemble something moving. Since a section of conduit was replaced above the balcony, it has stopped using one of its old routes. It still pauses at the place where that route began, lifts a foot, and sets it down again.

Its usual circuit takes most of an hour.

First it goes beneath the stairs. It presses its mouth to the crack below the plant, where a little moisture gathers around the root. The mouth is on the underside of the head: a short transverse opening bordered by four firm, mobile lobes. These can close around a pipe or peel a layer of softened material from stone. The six leaves are untouched. The dry fallen ones are sometimes carried away.

Then it follows the wall toward the basin, keeping two feet on the paving and two against the shallow curb. It passes the damp oval without entering it. At the basin it drinks from the overflow, drawing water into its mouth in a series of small pulses. The loose skin beneath its throat tightens with each one.

The shrimp-like animals retreat under the rim. It probes for them, patiently but without much skill. Its front limbs end in three short digits, broad and blunt, better for levering than catching. Usually it gets sediment. It eats that too, working the copper-colored grit between hard plates inside its mouth.

Most of its food comes from behind the closed frontages. There are films growing on warm pipework, pale nodules in insulation, soft accumulations in filters that have not been changed for a long time. It has access to several of these places. Others have become inaccessible as its body has thickened.

It still reaches into one narrow service opening every time it passes.

Near the delivery aperture, it becomes very still.

The carrier does not always bring anything it can use. Sometimes it emerges clean. Sometimes a corner holds a translucent smear, or a folded scrap, or a little mass of fibrous material pressed flat by transit. The animal waits through the sudden light with its head turned away. As the carrier crosses the shelf, one front limb rises and sweeps the near edge.

This movement is quick enough to be difficult to see.

The outer digit on that limb is missing its tip.

Afterward, whether it has obtained anything or not, it withdraws beneath the balcony and rubs the limb against its mouth.

There are others farther along the perimeter. They do not often enter the square together. One is smaller and almost uniformly pale, with a habit of moving through the drainage channel even when water is running in it. Another has a high, stiff ridge along its back and cannot flatten fully. It uses an access door whose lower hinge has failed.

They recognize one another by touch and by what they leave on the walls. Beside certain corners, the dark surface bears dull patches at ankle height. Each animal presses the side of its head there as it passes. The patches smell faintly of warmed wool.

When two meet, they stand with their heads beside one another for a long time. Their front limbs move over the folds beneath each other’s jaws. Sometimes they exchange a small piece of food. Sometimes one takes what the other is holding, and both remain perfectly still afterward, gripping it.

Their quarrels are mostly conducted through the floor.

A foot is lifted and brought down flat. The other animal answers. In the machinery’s roar, a person standing nearby might notice only a faint tremor in the knees. The exchange can continue for several minutes before one turns aside. Actual injury is uncommon, but the pale one has a split in a mouth lobe, and the resident nearest the stairs no longer permits it behind the third shutter.

That shutter shelters three young.

For now they are little more than pale bodies with disproportionately large feet. Their back pads have not darkened. They spend much of their time attached beneath the adult, gripping folds of skin and feeding from small glandular patches between the rear limbs. When it goes through the shutter, it gathers them forward first. The scar on its abdomen is particularly sensitive to their feet.

As they grow, it leaves them in a hollow made from cable sheath, dry leaves, and thin strips of material stolen from the carrier. The hollow rests on a warm foundation block. One strip is bright blue. The adult repeatedly pushes it beneath the other bedding; the young repeatedly pull it out.

During the five seconds when the dragging sound stops, all three flatten themselves.

They have learned this before learning to drink from the basin. The deep report unsettles the foundation block, and anything standing upright is likely to fall. Afterward they climb over one another and mouth whatever the vibration has shaken loose.

The adult is less cautious. It uses that interval to cross open ground. For those few seconds it can distinguish small movements that the machinery usually obscures. It has caught gray animals then. The ears and tail are eaten last, or left behind.

The bench is part of this crossing.

It climbs onto it from the end nearest the cylindrical post, hooking its front limbs around the seat and drawing the rest of itself up in stages. On the bench it can lift its throat clear of the pervasive vibration and listen through its feet alone. It rests there with its limbs folded beneath it, warming the seat, its head inclined toward the stairs.

People still come down occasionally.

The animal detects them at the bend. A person’s weight arrives as a series of widely spaced shocks, accompanied by sliding cloth, disturbed air, and the small irregular contacts of things carried in pockets. It usually leaves the bench before they can see it.

If someone stays very still, it may return.

It approaches from the side rather than the front and stops just beyond reach. Soap troubles it. Rainwater on shoes interests it. A bag set on the floor will eventually be touched.

It has no general understanding of what people will do. One visitor left food in a shallow lid for several weeks. Another struck it with the end of a tool. For a long time afterward it retreated from the same metallic note, even when that note came from an empty carrier.

Behind the third shutter, among the bedding, it keeps the lid. When the foundation block is too hot, it drags the lid onto the cooler paving and settles the smallest young inside it.

Then it lies across the gap, not quite asleep, with one front limb extending into the chamber. The comb of hairs opens against the floor. Water begins to fall from the ceiling seam, and it draws the limb back a little, out of the path of the overflow.
