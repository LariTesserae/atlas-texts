The pale animal at the western lip has six feet, though only four are visible. The hindmost pair remain tucked beneath the shell, holding the stone. It can rest this way without effort. Each foot closes when the muscle relaxes, so that sleep, exhaustion, and even death tighten its grip.

Its shell is not quite white. Close to the body it is the yellow-gray of the older rock; farther out, successive bands lighten until the newest margin is almost translucent. Dust has settled into a shallow groove across its back. At one side, an old fracture has healed crookedly, leaving a raised seam that catches the sun before the rest of the shell.

Nothing about it is made for looking into the distance.

Its eyes are four dark pits beneath the forward rim, useful for distinguishing an opening from a wall, a moving underside from a still one. The plains below are probably no more than brightness. What has brought it to the edge is something in the stone.

It sets down one forefoot, lifts the other, and touches the newly exposed pink surface.

Beneath each foot is a soft, densely folded pad. On familiar routes the folds stay shut, exposing only a tough central sole. On unfamiliar ground they open. The animal samples the rock through them: traces of salts, the faint products of living threads, the difference between a surface warmed by sunlight and one warm all the way through.

This stone has almost no taste of life.

The animal remains over it for a long time.

***

Under the shell, it is less like a tortoise than like a folded piece of pale leather. There is no long neck. The head is a blunt muscular lip that can press into a crevice or flatten against a fan. Its mouth lies underneath, surrounded by short, stiff brushes. When it feeds, the brushes seal against the surface and the rasp works inside them. Almost nothing escapes.

What it takes from the copper undersides is not the plant itself. A thin living film gathers there, sustained by substances the fans release too slowly to form a visible deposit. The film thickens along sheltered ribs. A grazer may revisit the same rib many times before there is enough to eat again.

Its route is therefore full of interruptions that are not mistakes.

It passes good feeding places without stopping because it fed there recently. It waits beside an apparently bare stem because a fan above is slowly changing its angle, exposing a strip that has been inaccessible for years. At a junction it presses its mouth to the floor, then turns away from the stronger scent of food. Another animal has already gone that way.

They do not maintain a queue, but each leaves enough of itself on the stone to discourage wasteful following.

Their droppings are small, hard, and nearly dry. They leave them in the soft corners, backing carefully into places where old root tissue has already accumulated. Those corners support the rust-colored cushions. The youngest grazers spend much of their lives there, too light to crush the growth, feeding among its minute folds.

A mature animal will sometimes stand at the entrance to such a corner for half a day. The young move beneath it. Its shell becomes another roof, and its feet receive their movements through the floor.

There is no outward sign that it counts them or recognizes them. When it leaves, it places each foot in an empty spot.

***

The flat animals overhead are quicker, but only when they need to be.

At rest, one can be mistaken for a thickening in the fan. Its six legs fold so completely into the body’s margin that the outline becomes a single shallow oval. The green on its back is a living coat: tiny growths held in pits in the skin, kept close enough to the surface to receive light when the animal moves across an opening.

Most of its underside is sensitive. It can tell which roof rib has a grazer beneath it, which is being strained by a growing stem, which carries the irregular movements of another of its kind.

A court that seems empty to a visitor may be difficult for one of these animals to cross. The floor is bright, the ceilings do not meet, and the separate plants carry separate messages. At the last overlapping edge it pauses, stretches two feet into the gap, withdraws them, then turns sideways to find a narrower crossing.

Occasionally there is none.

Then it drops.

The body flattens further, the legs spread, and for an instant the animal is a pale, six-pointed shape in the sun. It lands without a sound except, perhaps, the faintest contact of its hard mouthparts against stone. For the next several moments it does not move. The unfiltered warmth of the court touches its entire back.

Only afterward does it hurry.

Hurrying, here, means that a visitor would have no difficulty seeing it go.

These animals eat the small white lives around the joints. They take few at a time. A mouth presses to a cluster; one white body disappears; the others draw tight against the stem. The predator moves on, leaving the living patch capable of closing over the vacancy.

Their own eggs lie near the central fracture, attached by broad, clear bases. Through the membranes, the young look like crumpled versions of the adults, their legs folded across their bellies. Just before hatching, an egg becomes cloudy. The young animal has drawn almost all the remaining fluid into itself.

The empty skin stays on the rock until something eats it.

***

The white creatures at the stem joints do not all remain there forever.

Some become embedded so deeply that only a narrow dark seam is visible. Their lower bodies enter the plant’s outer tissue, while their exposed surfaces harden against the dry air. They draw very little from the stem. Around them, the plant forms a dense collar that is harder for smaller burrowing mouths to enter.

Others never settle. They wander through the hollow stems, grazing the inner walls and gathering in chambers where the temperature scarcely changes.

A few develop the four translucent vanes.

Long before they emerge, they can be seen through thin portions of the stem: folded gray shapes occupying spaces that once held several white grains. The settled ones nearby become motionless. The plant slowly dries a small patch of its own wall.

Eventually the patch comes away.

The winged creature climbs out through the new opening and waits while its vanes stiffen. It carries little reserve. There is enough for several descents, perhaps one brief climb in a favorable current, and the beginning of a new cluster in another stem.

Most move only from one district to the next.

Sometimes one rises above the wall.

Inside the enclosure, its departure makes no general disturbance. A flat animal may lift its head at the sudden release of weight. The stem sways back a fraction. The opening remains, and eventually something else uses it.

***

The larger inhabitants are long-lived, though much of that life passes in a condition a visitor might mistake for illness.

A grazer can remain wedged beneath a low roof for weeks, feet locked, mouth drawn shut. Its body becomes thinner inside the shell. When food is available again, it does not rush out. First the soft parts swell against the old margins. Then the sensitive folds of one foot open. Movement resumes by degrees.

The shell records these stoppages imperfectly. A broad band may represent a favorable stretch of feeding; several narrow ones may belong to a period when a roof thickened overhead and shut off the film on which the animal depended. An individual carries the history of a few passages, not of the whole enclosure.

The pale grazer at the western edge is old enough to have used a doorway now filled by solid trunk.

For a long time after that passage narrowed, it continued to approach it. It would stop with its shell touching both sides, rasp briefly at the base, and reverse. Eventually its route changed. The trunk still bears the polished hollow made by those attempts.

Its whiteness comes from feeding near the newer wall, where a different mixture of minerals enters the fans. The older, darker shell persists near its center. Its soft body is much the same color as that of the young beneath the cushions.

Now, on the lip, it opens the folds of its forward feet again.

A buried thread has reached the broken surface. Too fine to see without kneeling close, it follows a pale band in the rock and ends beneath the animal’s left pad. There is almost nothing there to eat. But the stone no longer tastes wholly empty.

Behind the grazer, evening warmth begins to leave the upper roofs. The flat animals press closer to the ribs. Gray vanes close beneath a shelf. Deep in a stem, a loose fragment of wall shifts against a cluster of white bodies, and several of them turn toward it.

The grazer moves one foot beyond the lip and finds the beginning of a narrow ledge.

It tests the ledge, then brings its foot back.

When darkness reaches the western wall, it turns—not cleanly, but in several small adjustments, the crooked seam of its shell scraping stone. It descends into the nearest passage and stops beneath a copper roof.

On the pink rock behind it remain two faint marks from the open pads. By morning they will have dried. In the shelter of one mark, the root thread has begun to branch.
