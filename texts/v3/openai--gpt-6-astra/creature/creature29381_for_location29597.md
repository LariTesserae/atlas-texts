The resident is easiest to find when a valve has been left half a turn out.

For a while nothing happens. A broad leaf trembles in the altered current. Then something beneath the leaf takes hold of the valve stem, and the wheel begins to move back.

What holds it looks at first like a strip of cloudy packing material. It is flat, almost transparent, with a thicker cord along each edge. The end divides into three unequal tabs. These settle between the spokes, tighten, release, and settle again. There is no haste, and no fumbling. When the valve reaches its customary position, the strip remains draped over it for several minutes.

Eventually it withdraws beneath the planting plate.

There is only one animal of any appreciable size in the chamber. It is larger than the maintenance workers initially believed.

Most of its body lies in the intervals between things: behind the porcelain terraces, inside the clearance left around a duct, along the narrow space where a stair meets the wall. Its resting shape is a loose stack of translucent folds. Water passes between them. A person looking through a gap may see several meters of it without recognizing a body, because the far side of the animal is as clearly visible as the near side, and beyond both are the ordinary pipes and brackets.

Where its tissue doubles over, it has a faint apricot color.

It has no skeleton, but it does have hard parts. Small cream-colored plates lie beneath its surface in widely separated rows. To pull itself through a tight place, it sets these against the structure and draws the rest of itself after them. The plates make a very quiet succession of clicks against porcelain. Workers sometimes mistake this for a cooling pipe.

There is no consistent front to it. The part approaching a task thickens, gathers several of the marginal cords together, and becomes capable of a finer grip. At rest these arrangements loosen again. An extremity that spent the morning sorting roots may be a broad, idle membrane by evening.

It does, however, have one place that it keeps protected.

Low in the body, among folds usually tucked behind the storage tanks, is a cluster of opaque tissue about the size of two hands held together. It is the yellow-gray of wet paper. Fine channels enter it from every direction. When a worker comes too close, the folds draw across it, not quickly enough to suggest panic, but with unmistakable intention.

This is the part around which the rest turns when it sleeps.

Its food comes from the brown liquor behind the walls. It takes very little. At one service coupling, an old gasket has been displaced just enough to expose a damp seam, and the animal holds a fringed portion of itself there. The workers have marked the coupling with a green pencil so that nobody will tighten it.

It does not eat the living plants. It handles them continually.

A root pressed against a hot housing will be moved. A leaf caught under a comb will be lifted free, unless the leaf has already yellowed beyond some distinction the animal can make. It separates the fine red stems where their density begins to obstruct a circulation grille. The stems are not broken. Each is laid through a different opening and held there until it has acquired the new bend.

Some of these actions agree with the maintenance schedule. Others do not.

For six weeks it repeatedly removed a healthy climbing plant from the same cable. The workers put the plant back twice, then installed a small mesh guard to keep it in position. The animal eased the guard off its clips and left it on the landing.

When the cable sheath was finally inspected, water had entered beneath it.

There is no instrument in the chamber that could have detected the fault at that stage. There are instruments that should have.

The resident appears to know the place through contact. It rests thin portions of itself against the pressure diaphragms, the motor housings, the backs of the planting plates. Changes travel through it as small contractions, visible only where light catches the thickened margins. At times nearly its entire body is occupied with listening.

During a shutdown, it leaves its recesses.

This is unsettling even to workers who know it well. Surfaces thought to be empty begin to peel. Lengths of tissue come away from the underside of stairs. A pale breadth lifts from behind a grille and draws itself through openings much smaller than the whole. For an hour the chamber seems to be surrendering a lining.

Gathered together, the animal would fill a maintenance cradle. It never quite gathers together. Some part is always stretched back toward a machine that has not yet restarted.

It dislikes complete silence.

When the main circulation pump was replaced, it spent the eleven-hour interruption holding the old pump’s disconnected shaft. The pump lay on a cart in the dry landing. The resident reached it through the doorway, with a small brass service frame fitted around the length of body that crossed the threshold.

The worker who had fitted the frame stayed beside it.

On the dry side, the animal’s tissue hung inside a sleeve of water. Its three tabs held the shaft without attempting to turn it. Now and then a contraction passed up the extended part and disappeared into the chamber.

When the new pump began, it let go.

Afterward, the old shaft was left beneath a terrace. The animal moved it behind the tanks. It remains there, slowly acquiring a skin of mineral deposit.

The resident also keeps a cracked valve handle, a white glove, and three of the soft wedges used to protect glass blocks during installation. These objects are not arranged in any obvious order. It sometimes carries one to another level and leaves it there for a day.

The glove is the oldest.

It belongs to a woman who no longer makes the climb up the mast. When she worked in the chamber, she wore a different color from the other maintenance staff, and she was in the habit of letting the animal hold her wrist while she read a gauge. It does not approach the other workers in quite that way. With them it touches the tool first.

When her replacement wore white gloves, it followed him through an entire shift. He found the attention difficult and changed to blue.

On the governor casing, among the penciled dates, there are now other marks. They are pale strokes made by dragging a hard plate through the soft surface deposit. Most are short and nearly parallel. One crosses three others. Nobody has cleaned that portion of the casing.

The animal makes them while waiting for the wheel.

It does not copy the numbers.

High above the accessible stairs, where the vine has joined itself into a ring, a narrow portion of the resident lies along the supporting cable. It has been there for months. It is difficult to distinguish from the pale climbing stems, except that it occasionally changes which side of the cable it occupies.

The ring did not form without assistance.

The animal held the vine’s growing end against a damaged place farther down the stem. It held it there through several shifts of workers, through a nutrient flush, through the replacement of a window seal. By the time anyone noticed, the two surfaces had begun to join.

A worker brought up a hooked knife.

The resident covered the graft with its body. The knife was lowered. Later there were discussions on the service landing, and a temporary isolation frame was ordered. It arrived in a padded case and has not yet been installed.

Within the slanted water, something is growing.

It is not attached to the vine. At first it resembled a little residue from the graft, a fleck of milk-colored matter caught inside the ring. Now it has a clear margin and two thicker cords. It holds itself near the bubble, on the side that is uppermost according to the water inside.

When it moves, the adult becomes still throughout the chamber.

Valves remain untouched. A root slips back against its housing. Behind the tanks, the feeding fringe lifts away from the coupling. All the little tasks continue mechanically without their attendant, while the narrow extension high on the cable presses closer to the ring.

The small body has begun to reach for things.

There is very little within reach: the vine, the bubble, the end of one stem distorted by the boundary. The adult has brought it a soft packing wedge. Passing the wedge through took most of a morning. For a while the object caught at an angle that made its two ends appear to belong to different pieces.

Now the young one rests against it.

Below, in a rack beside the storage tanks, one of the spare doorframes has been loosened in its padded clamps. Its serial plate is clean. The resident has cleared the roots from the terrace nearby and moved the old pump shaft out of the way.

Every few days it returns to the frame and tests its weight.
