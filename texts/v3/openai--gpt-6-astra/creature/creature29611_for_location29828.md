A woman comes out of a doorway carrying a bird by its feet.

It is alive and furious. It strikes at her wrist with its bill, but she holds it far enough from her body that it cannot get purchase. With her free hand she lifts the door flap, looks back into the house, and makes a gesture to someone inside. Then she walks to the end of the passage and releases it over the water.

The bird turns immediately toward the roofs.

She watches where it lands. She has a broad face, cropped hair, and a healed notch through one eyebrow. There is nothing ceremonial in what she has done. It is the third time this morning. Before going back inside, she picks a feather from the wet skin of her forearm.

These are people whose bodies are usually partly bare. They wear short hide aprons, belts with bone toggles, sometimes a shoulder covering when they go beyond the warm shore. Clothing that hangs loose gets caught, soaked, or fouled. Hair is cut with flakes of glass, unevenly around children’s ears, more carefully on adults who have someone willing to take the time. Some people leave a strip long enough to bind back. The bindings are narrow hide thongs, rubbed soft with fat.

They are not all broad or powerful. There are thin old people with enormous-looking knuckles, soft-bellied people who work sitting down, adolescents whose elbows seem to arrive ahead of the rest of them. But most acquire a particular way of placing their feet. They test a surface without appearing to stop. On a narrow crossing they turn slightly sideways, keeping the load close to the belly. A child can recognize a familiar adult from the movement of their shoulders above a parapet.

Their feet are scarred. So are their shins and the backs of their hands. Small cuts matter here. Each household keeps a dry recess for sharp tools and things used to cover wounds; maintaining that dryness takes attention. A person who comes home bleeding is likely to be met first with irritation, then care.

The woman with the bird is especially good at removing splinters of volcanic glass. People come to her holding one hand cupped in the other. She sits them in her doorway, where the light falls across the skin, and makes them stay still. Her patience for this is considerable. Her patience for explanations of how the injury happened is not.

Her household includes her sister, two children, an old man, and a younger man who began sleeping there several years ago and has gradually stopped keeping his possessions anywhere else. They occupy three connected chambers. One of the chambers used to belong to a different household; the blocked doorway remains visible from the lane.

The old man is not related to everyone in the house in the same way. This is important to them and difficult for a visitor to follow. He owns two traps he can no longer lift, remembers agreements concerning four others, and maintains that a landing beneath the neighboring house was once shared. The neighbors agree that it was once shared. They disagree about what follows from this.

He is nearly deaf.

In the town, this is less isolating than it might be elsewhere. He can follow a quarrel across a courtyard if everyone keeps their hands visible. At close quarters he watches mouths. What he misses are remarks spoken behind him, the small additions that soften an instruction, the muttering by which people excuse themselves after complying. He sometimes believes the household is more obedient than it is.

The children exploit this, though not always successfully. He has become sensitive to the way they look at each other.

Their gestures extend well beyond directions through the lanes. There are signs for a frayed fastening, a dangerous animal, a missing tool, a person who is pretending not to notice something. Some belong to particular working groups. The divers use movements that mean little above water. Two sisters cleaning fish together can sustain an exchange with almost no speech, using the angle of a knife, a lifted chin, a pause before passing the next body.

But the people are not silent, and they do not regard speech as an unfortunate necessity. They enjoy it. In sheltered rooms they sit close enough for knees to touch and talk directly into one another’s hearing. A storyteller may have to repeat a passage for people at the far end of a sleeping platform. Someone will complain that it was better the first time.

Laughter travels badly through the uproar, so it is often something seen: mouths open, shoulders moving, a person bent forward with one hand against the wall.

Much of their humor concerns competence. A boast made before a difficult task can remain attached to its speaker for years. There is a man who once announced that a particular bull seal would move if approached without hesitation. Children who were not born when he made the attempt know the phrase. They use it when someone is about to lift a pot that is too hot.

The seals are familiar without being tame. Individuals are recognized by scars, missing teeth, the shape of a pale patch, the ledges they prefer. Some have names. Naming them does not imply affection. A heavy female with a torn nostril is called something equivalent to *Your Problem* by the households nearest her resting place.

People kill seals, too, outside the crowded lower lanes where a wounded animal would be dangerous. A carcass means meat, fat, hide, sinew, bone, and a long period of work. The good cutting edges are brought out. Children are sent away from the busiest part of the terrace and then summoned back to carry manageable loads. Arguments about shares may begin while the animal is still being opened.

There is usually enough to eat, but food becomes bad quickly in the warmth. Abundance creates work that cannot be put off. Shellfish must be opened, fish dressed, fat rendered, hides scraped. Drying is unreliable except on exposed ledges with a steady movement of air. Every household knows the difference between a food’s strong proper smell and the first wrong sweetness of decay.

Some people are better at this than others. There are houses from which one accepts food gladly and houses where one finds a reason to have eaten already.

The birds know these distinctions in their own fashion. They gather early at careless houses.

Possessions are mostly small enough to fit in wall niches or hang from pegs. A good length of wood is valuable even before anyone knows what it will become. Straight pieces are examined with the eye and the palm. People remember their arrival: a rib from a broken boat, a branch brought in after rough weather beyond the bay, a handled implement traded along the coast and eventually dismantled.

An old handle may outlast several blades and pass through several hands. Its shape records them. One side is polished by a thumb; another has been cut back to suit a smaller grip.

People are attached to such things quite specifically. They do not cherish every useful object equally. Someone may lend a fine hook without hesitation and refuse to part with a battered shell cup. A woman may keep her dead brother’s belt but replace its worn fastening because he would have considered the original one badly made.

The shells around doorways also have histories. A row can mark years of collecting, or a single fortunate haul, or a child’s insistence that two mismatched shells ought to go beside each other. Some doors are ostentatious. Some are neglected. One man regularly removes and resets his border because he is never satisfied with it, to the entertainment of his neighbors.

The town’s close construction makes a household’s habits consequential beyond its walls. A blocked drain raises water in another room. A roof repaired badly sends drips onto someone else’s bedding. Fish waste abandoned at the wrong turn draws birds into a passage everyone uses.

There are established obligations for clearing channels, but an obligation still requires someone to perform it. People delay, complain of sore hands, claim to have done more than their share. Others do the work angrily and make sure this is remembered. The person most admired for settling disputes is not necessarily the person anyone most enjoys eating with.

The woman who removes glass from wounds is often asked to inspect drainage disagreements. She gets down on one knee and puts her hand into the opening. She will listen to an account, but prefers to feel where the water is catching.

Her sister dislikes this public usefulness. It brings people to the doorway at all hours.

Her sister is the one who wants to leave.

Not urgently, and not because she despises the town. She knows the fishing grounds, likes the old man, and has a particular affection for the younger child, who brings her interesting crab shells with most of the legs missing. But boats sometimes arrive from farther along the coast. Their crews describe other landings, colder water, cliffs that break apart differently. One crew member has described a place where a person can walk a long distance without choosing each footfall.

She asks questions that the others find repetitive. How far? What do they eat there? Is the ground really soft everywhere, or only in hollows?

She has begun setting aside things that could travel.

Meanwhile, she works. She repairs the hide lashings on a trap, holding each wet strip between finger and thumb to judge its remaining strength. Beside her, the child arranges crab fragments in a shallow shell. Under the floor the water keeps moving. Someone outside calls, and she lifts her head, unable to make out the words.

The woman from the doorway touches her shoulder and points toward the landing.

A boat has come in. They can see only its upper edge between the houses, a stranger’s raised arm, something long being passed ashore. The sister puts down the trap.

“Finish that first,” the woman says close to her ear.

For a moment she does not move. Then she bends again over the fastening, draws the strip tight, and tests the knot with both hands.
