What lives there has a resting place, although it is easy to mistake that place for another repair.

It lies beneath the gallery, where three pipes pass through a mass of dark, fibrous material. Two pipes are cold. The third is hot enough to have left a pale channel through the mass. At first the material resembles the packed bases of the folded-leaf plants, except that nothing grows out of it. Its exposed surface is made of overlapping pads, each with a dry rim and a slightly moist center. Grit has lodged between them. One pad bears the rectangular impression of a bolt head.

This is the side it sleeps on.

You would have to return at intervals to discover that. On one visit, the impression is near the floor. On another, it faces outward. The pipes have not moved, and the mass has not visibly changed its position, but something has turned within the narrow space available to it.

It spends much of its time accommodating heat. The outer pads lift a little from the body; a seam opens beneath them; dampness appears and is immediately lost. The hot pipe settles deeper into its channel. By morning the seam has closed.

There is no clear boundary between the animal and the bed in which it rests. Some of the fibers can be followed into mortar joints. Others end in blunt, polished tips. Still others are ordinary roots, caught against it and held there gently enough to continue growing. When it rises, a number of these come away with it. A few remain stretched between its body and the wall until, over the course of an hour, they slip free.

It does not rise to anything like its full height. There may be no occasion on which it does.

Its body is long and low, with several places at which it can bend sharply. Beneath the overlapping pads are limbs that differ from one another. One terminates in a broad sole, divided into shallow lobes. Another narrows to a tough, flexible length that can be wound twice around a handrail. The foremost pair are usually held together, making a blunt wedge. Separated, they reveal pale inner surfaces covered with small ridges. It uses these to hold things without crushing them.

The limbs are not arranged for traveling over a floor. They are arranged for being supported.

As it moves along the maintenance path, it keeps contact with the wall, the rail, and at least two patches of sound masonry. Its weight passes gradually from one support to the next. A foot may remain behind for several minutes after the rest of the body has rounded a corner. The narrow limb tightens on the handrail. A loose brick receives a tentative pressure and is abandoned.

It knows the hollow’s unsafe surfaces very precisely. It will cross a sheet-metal cover near the inspection booth, but only with its weight distributed along the folded edges. It avoids a perfectly ordinary-looking stair. Where the path has broken away, it places one limb into the vegetation and finds something firm a considerable distance below.

On those journeys it appears smaller than it does at rest. Much of its bulk is space: channels between folds, pockets that can close, a deep underside that draws up around its limbs. Passing through a narrow opening, it sheds dust from surfaces that have not been exposed for years.

There is no face at the leading end. There is, instead, a shallow cleft it keeps turned toward the nearest continuous sound.

Fine, stiff growth lines this cleft. In silence the growth droops. Against the grinding of the loose plate, it stands apart in orderly rows. The creature pauses at junctions in the pipework and touches its cleft to the metal. Sometimes it stays like that through an entire change of pressure, receiving whatever distinctions are lost to a person standing in the chamber.

Its own movements are nearly inaudible beneath the machinery. Only when the grinding note lowers can you hear the pads coming apart: a faint series of damp releases.

It eats at the basin.

The descent takes a long time. It braces itself on the steps, lowers its front into the apparently empty oval, and stops at the dark waterline. There its outer covering changes. The dry rims soften. Pale joints become visible between the pads. The submerged portion is displaced slightly to one side, as the hanging leaves are displaced, though there is still nothing to see between its body and the tiles.

Along its underside, folds open that are much finer than any part exposed to air. They sway separately. Occasionally a slow contraction passes through them, drawing them together into a thick seam before they loosen again.

Whatever it takes from the basin does not leave it visibly fuller. Instead, after feeding, it can move more readily. Its limbs release their supports sooner. The cleft at its leading end stays open without effort. For a few hours the surface of its body has the deep, nearly black color of wet bark.

Small flakes detach from it in the basin. Some drift below the waterline and disappear from sight. Others catch on the roots and remain there, dark on one side, pale on the other.

The growth around the basin is densest where these flakes collect.

It tends that growth, though not consistently enough to be called a gardener. It tears away healthy shoots and carries them into darkness. It leaves dead stalks standing when they seem to serve no purpose, then removes one whose base has begun to lean against a pipe. Once, with the ridged surfaces of its forelimbs, it holds a folded leaf open through the greater part of a day. Afterward the leaf remains open by itself.

Its attention follows contact and pressure. A vine may grow across its back for a season without troubling it. A single root tightening around a valve stem brings it out from beneath the gallery.

It does not cut the root. It inserts the narrow tip of a limb between root and metal and waits. The root grows around the obstruction. The limb gradually thickens where it has been inserted, holding a space open while the valve rises. Much later, when the clearance is sufficient, the creature withdraws.

Some of the fibrous repairs along the handrail were made this way. They are pieces it left behind.

At the point where the rail enters the trunk, the creature pauses on every circuit. It presses the join from below, then from above. The trunk has begun to lift the rail as it thickens. The adjustment required is minute, and the creature has been making it for a very long time.

The inspection booth also receives its attention. It goes inside, although the doorway makes entry difficult. There is a polished hollow in the floor beside the desk where it settles. One forelimb rests against the lowest row of switches. It does not move them.

There are limits to its care. When a red lamp fails, it remains failed. The fallen lettering on the desk means nothing to it. It has preserved an unobstructed approach to a cabinet whose contents have long since become a single mass of rust.

A person might live beside it for months without being certain of recognition.

It will not come when called. Loud speech closes the cleft at its leading end. It dislikes being touched on the dry edges of its pads and will slowly draw the touched surface underneath itself.

But if you stand on the damaged stair, it reaches up from below and presses against the underside of the tread. It holds there until you step away.

Whether it is supporting you or protecting the stair is difficult to establish.

When the valve inside the wall finishes opening, the creature is usually already at the basin. It has felt the approaching change through several separate lengths of pipe. It lowers one forelimb to an exact depth and leaves it there while the leaves unfold around the chamber.

Near the overflow, a root has grown across the lip. The first movement of water bends it flat.

The creature catches it before it can lodge in the drain. For a while it holds the root above the invisible surface, allowing the smaller fibers to trail downward. Then it finds a place for it against the basin wall and keeps it there, patiently, until it begins to adhere.
