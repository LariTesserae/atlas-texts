What lives there is easiest to see where a root crosses one of the new cracks.

The root is round, grey-brown, and slightly wrinkled. Beneath it, farther down than the daylight reaches, there is another surface. A torch shows a dull yellow fold pressed against the underside of the stone. It has the fine, close grain of the skin inside a mushroom stem. Along its upper edge, where the lifted slab bears down on it, it is almost white.

There is no visible movement. If the torch is left shining into the crack, however, a little moisture appears on the fold. After several minutes it withdraws, leaving a wet impression against the stone.

The root stays where it is.

Most of the inhabitant is thinner than this. It occupies the bedding beneath the floor, spreading between surfaces that were laid flush together. In some places it is no thicker than the skin that forms on cooling milk. Elsewhere it has gathered into pads. These are what lift the slabs.

A pad takes years to form. The last part of the lifting can happen in an afternoon.

It does not seem to be particularly strong. Where the floor is sound, it follows joints or turns aside. It has never cracked the concrete plinth beneath the machine. But it can keep a small pressure in one place through a length of time in which mortar softens, iron expands and contracts, and a stone finds a new way to bear its weight.

Once a gap opens, the tissue enters it. The next expansion begins from there.

Its colour varies with its thickness. The thin portions are nearly transparent, carrying branching stains the colour of weak tea. The thicker folds are yellow, then grey at their crowded bases. A freshly exposed edge sometimes has a pinkness that disappears before one can decide whether it belonged to the tissue or to the light.

It has no skin in the ordinary sense. Whatever part is exposed becomes a surface. Whatever part is covered softens again.

This makes it difficult to remove a sample. A piece brought upstairs soon develops a dry outer layer on every side, including the cut. Inside there remains a small quantity of clear fluid and something soft that contracts as it is handled. It will live for a while in a warm jar. It does very little there.

Below, it does a great deal, but seldom on a scale convenient for watching.

Water moves through it in shallow channels that close behind the passing swell. The channels are not permanent vessels. A route used for months may flatten, while another opens a finger’s breadth away. Near a reliable drip, the tissue thickens into a wet, translucent margin. Beyond a dry groove in the floor, it may stop as neatly as paint at a ruled line.

The plants root where these margins reach them.

Their roots do not enter its body. Between root and tissue there is a dark layer, very thin, which remains damp even when the surrounding stone is dry. Fine root hairs crowd into that layer. When an old leaf falls, the same dark material gradually appears underneath it. The leaf sinks a little, loses its stiffness, and is held close to the ground until its outline is gone.

There are separate plants here. They germinate, compete for light, shade one another out. A vine can die while the broad-leaved trunks beside it continue to grow. The inhabitant beneath them has preferences that do not always favour the vegetation. Occasionally it withdraws from a whole bed. The leaves droop over several days, and the smallest shoots disappear first.

The person who tends the chamber sometimes moves a collecting pan to such a place. Sometimes this helps.

Near the service pit, the body is much thicker. It fills the old corners and rises inside the masonry in low folds, hidden by the overlapping fans. What appears from the landing to be black soil is, beneath the first loose layer, a continuous yielding surface.

It is warm, though not as warm as the machine.

There are depressions in it that retain their shape after being pressed. A fingertip leaves a pale hollow. By the following morning the hollow has become shallower and wider, as though the pressure has spread through the material rather than sprung back. A deeper mark may remain for weeks.

The oldest parts have many such hollows. Some fit tools no longer kept in the chamber.

Beneath the fan plants, several folds lie against the buried pipes. Here the tissue is dense and smooth, with long parallel creases. As the casing grows hotter, these creases slowly separate. Fluid enters them. The body thickens along the pipe and thins a little at its farther edges.

The fans above open first because their roots rest directly over this change. The tall plants receive it later, after warmth and moisture have travelled beneath the intervening floor.

A person staying from morning until night might notice only that the room has become more difficult to cross.

The inhabitant has no single resting position. There is always somewhere in it that is filling, somewhere being drawn thin, somewhere hardening around an obstruction. Even when the instrument’s red mark remains steady, its distribution changes. A slab that rocked underfoot last week may now be held firm. A valve may become a little harder to turn.

It seems to distinguish recurrent disturbances from new ones.

The foot of the stairs is dry and clear, although warm pipes pass beneath it. The usual route to the machine is clear too. At one narrow point there are two shallow depressions in the tissue under the floor, spaced like successive steps. They have persisted through changes in the surrounding growth.

When the caretaker began taking a different route around a tilted slab, the leaves along it gradually lifted. Nothing comparable happened on the far side of the same bed.

This took most of a year.

The caretaker does not claim that it was making room.

They come down with a small lamp, a cloth bag of fittings, and drinking water they keep on the stair. Before touching the valve, they rest the back of a hand against the machine. They inspect the gauge, clear the gutters, and look into certain cracks without shining the lamp directly down them.

They have learned not to pull anything loose merely because it appears loose.

Once, a length of disused cable came away from the wall easily for several feet, then tightened. By the next visit, the nearest collecting basin had tilted enough to spill its water onto bare stone. The cable was left in place. A bracket was put under the basin.

Such accommodations account for much of the newer metal in the room.

There are limits to what the inhabitant tolerates. Cold water poured directly into a crack makes a wide area of it turn opaque. The plants above close and remain closed long after the floor has warmed again. A prolonged interruption of heat draws the yellow folds down out of sight. The body becomes thin, dark, and difficult to distinguish from the old damp bedding beneath the slabs.

The machine has never been allowed to cool completely.

No one now tending it knows how much of the chamber would wake again if it did.

In one corner of the service pit, under the smallest fans, a fold has recently risen above floor level. It is about the length of a hand. Its upper surface has hardened into a shallow trough, pale at the rim and brown where the water rests.

It lies beneath the chain that no longer reaches its basin.

At first the drops struck beside it. The trough was only a blunt swelling then, and the water ran over it into the roots. Over successive visits its nearer edge rose. A narrow crease deepened through the middle.

Now a drop lands in it every few minutes.

The impact leaves a bright, rounded bead. For a while the bead holds its shape. Then it flattens and travels along the crease toward the masonry, where a soft lip rests against the stone.

Nothing closes over the water. Nothing can be seen to swallow.

By the time the next drop falls, the trough is empty.
