A woman coming up from the spring carries two covered vessels on a wooden yoke. The yoke has been repaired at both ends and polished black where it rests across her shoulders. At a turn in the stair, she stops without setting it down. A boy is descending with an empty basket, and there is room for him to pass only if she turns slightly toward the wall.

He does not thank her. He belongs to her household; they have been arguing since breakfast. But as he squeezes past, he lifts the lower vessel over a projecting stone so it will not strike.

The people of the city make thousands of such accommodations. They know where a load will catch, which step rocks underfoot, whose animal must be allowed to go first. A stranger sees a confusion of walls and interrupted paths. Someone who lives there sees a place to stand while a herd passes, a shortcut available until a particular family shuts its yard, a sheltered ledge on which to put down something heavy.

They carry much of what they own.

Children begin with small loads: a bundle of dry dung, a pot wrapped in cloth, a younger child who keeps slipping down their back. Adults carry sacks against the shoulders or suspend them from forehead straps. Porters have thickened skin where these straps bear. Some have a permanent forward inclination even when unburdened, as though listening to someone shorter than themselves.

Their clothing makes them look broader than they are. Beneath the outer felt are layers of woven wool, softened by wear and stiffened again by smoke, grease, and frost. A good coat passes through several people. Its last owner may be a child wearing what was once a sleeve as a separate garment. Fastenings differ from basin to basin: bone toggles, braided ties, little metal hooks that can be worked with numb fingers. People recognize a neighborhood by how a collar closes.

There is color, though a distant observer might miss it. It appears beneath a lifted arm, inside the fold of a cap, in a strip of red binding repeatedly transferred from one worn coat to the next. Someone has spent a winter embroidering blue birds along the hem of a wedding garment. Most of the time they are hidden under an everyday overcoat.

Faces weather unevenly. The cheeks darken and chap; the skin beneath a scarf remains pale. Young herders come home with deep cracks in their lips and startlingly white skin around the eyes when they remove their snow shields. The well-to-do can afford ointment, finer wool, rooms with less smoke. Their children cough too, but there are more places to move them when a room becomes unhealthy.

Breathlessness is familiar enough that people distinguish its kinds. There is the harmless stopping of an elderly neighbor on the steep stairs, and the pause that makes someone take the basket from her hand without asking. Lowland visitors sometimes arrive talking expansively and become silent after the first ascent. They are given a place to sit. Later, if they recover well, the story of how much they talked is told in their presence.

Much of the city’s humor concerns burdens: badly balanced loads, proud people forced to carry undignified objects, animals that have understood an errand better than their owners. The man carrying a door across three districts will hear about it for years if he takes the wrong stair.

***

A household extends farther than its rooms.

It includes a strip of roof reached through someone else’s yard, a share in a grain store, the right to shelter six sheep in a cousin’s enclosure, and an obligation to help clear the water channel above the next terrace. A marriage can rearrange all of these. So can a quarrel.

Rooms are added when there is money, labor, or a child who must be given somewhere to sleep. A family may spend ten years saying that it will build upward. During those years the stones collected for the work become a seat, a drying platform, and a place where a daughter meets someone her parents dislike.

Indoors, people live close to things being kept.

Sacks occupy the dry wall. Fuel is stacked where a leak cannot reach it. Cheese hardens on shelves beyond the dogs’ noses. Harness hangs overhead; boots are opened and inspected beside the fire. A guest may sleep beneath a valuable beam whose purchase is still remembered, although the buyer has been dead for two generations.

The warmest room is seldom quiet. Here wool is picked apart, accounts are argued over, infants fed, leather softened, grain sorted. People who speak little in the street can talk for hours once the door is shut. There are accomplished mimics, tedious storytellers, singers who must be coaxed, and singers whom nobody can stop.

Privacy takes practice. A person can withdraw by turning toward the wall with a piece of mending. Everyone may agree not to notice tears. Children learn that an adult apparently asleep beside the hearth may remember every word spoken.

Not every crowded household is a close one. Some inhabitants arrange long journeys partly to get away from their relatives. Some marry into a distant basin and find that the city is large enough to begin again.

***

Water brings people into relations they cannot simply abandon.

Each district has people who know its channels well enough to hear a change in them. One old keeper stops beside a covered conduit and places the iron tip of his staff against a slab. He cannot tell everything from the sound, whatever children believe, but he can tell when there is much less water than there should be.

He remembers repairs nobody else remembers. Under one house the channel divides around a buried boulder. Under another, a badly fitted joint admits fine sand whenever the thaw is strong. The owners of that house resent him because he insists on entering their storeroom at inconvenient times. They also send for him first when the flow slows.

Access to water is recorded, witnessed, inherited, and disputed. A household with a generous share may have very little else. Another, outwardly prosperous, depends on the goodwill of three neighbors. There are officials who can recite an allocation back through several generations, and women waiting at a basin who know exactly where the official account leaves something out.

In a poor snowfall year, the waiting becomes longer. Lids remain on vessels. Arguments begin over wet paving, washed clothes, an animal brought to the wrong trough. The city’s patience has limits, and scarcity reveals where those limits are.

It also sends people outward. Caravans leave with wool, hides, salt from distant basins, and goods that have already crossed several ranges. They return with grain, timber, oil, metal, dyes, unfamiliar illnesses, and songs that young people learn before their elders approve of them.

There are residents who have crossed the whole plateau yet never visited certain quarters of their own city. There are others who have spent seventy years within a few basins and can identify a merchant’s origin by the way he knots a sack.

***

The animals have their own map of the place.

A pack beast remembers a feeding court after years away. It turns toward the entrance against the rope, dragging its handler half a pace before submitting. Sheep learn which gateway leads home and occasionally divide a procession by insisting on it. Goats discover routes across unfinished masonry that nobody intended to be routes at all.

The dogs distinguish between belonging and permission. A grain merchant may be allowed through a gate each week without ever becoming someone the dog welcomes. Children from the household can fall over it, pull its coat, or tuck cold hands beneath its forelegs. A child from two passages away is another matter.

Some dogs accompany the herds. Others remain attached to an enclosure so completely that, when its owner moves, they return to sleep outside the empty gate. They are fed there until somebody takes possession.

Smaller lives gather around the provisions. Mice nest in felt where a fold has escaped inspection. Pale insects live in the seams of bedding. Birds wait until a sack is opened, then descend with an urgency quite unlike their puffed stillness in the cold. Ravens recognize the people who throw scraps and the people who throw stones. A particular raven has learned to lift an unattended covering, and three households blame one another for teaching it.

In summer, children bring home things from beyond the walls: a flowering cushion dug up with too little root, a beetle in a closed fist, a young burrowing animal that bites through its carrying cloth. Most do poorly indoors. The children are told this beforehand and grieve nevertheless.

***

The old people sitting in the sun are not all remembering the same city.

One recalls a pasture where there are now six terraces of houses. Another remembers those houses being built and maintains that the pasture was mostly gravel. A woman watches a mason repair a wall she helped raise as a girl. She offers advice until he moves his tools to the other side.

Their knowledge is useful, but unevenly wanted. They know which families once owned a spring, which winter killed the orchard in a sheltered court, where a room collapsed during a feast. Younger people have new disputes and sometimes deliberately take a route an elder warned against.

There are ambitious inhabitants, impatient builders, wasteful sons, careful daughters who become wasteful once they possess something of their own. There are people saving to leave for the lower valleys, where trees grow without walls around them. There are lowlanders who intended to stay one season and are buried here.

At evening, those who have been outside return with the cold still held in their clothing. For a while it can be felt coming off them in the room. Someone moves closer to the hearth to make space. A bowl is put into their hands before they have finished explaining where they were delayed.

Behind the wall, an animal shifts its weight. A child complains that its bell will keep her awake, though she sleeps through it every night. Upstairs, a man checks the felt around a shutter and pushes a loose edge into place with the handle of a spoon.

Then he sits down among the others. His place has been kept for him, and there is news he has not yet heard.
