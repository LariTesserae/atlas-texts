The woman who receives strangers has a loud voice and very little patience with breathlessness. She does not hurry them; she simply goes on talking while they stand bent beside the entrance stone, so that by the time they can answer she has asked several questions and begun to suspect them of evasion.

She wants to know who sent them, whether the lower path is passable, and what they have brought. These are not ceremonial questions. Her brother went down eleven days ago with a load of repaired buckles, and she is worried about him. She has put on her better apron because someone saw the strangers coming. There is flour along one eyebrow.

When they can walk again, she takes them inside. She moves sideways through a place where two jars narrow the passage, touching one lid as she passes. In her house she gives them a covered cup and leaves them alone long enough to drink without being watched.

Her name is Issa. She is not the head of the settlement, though visitors often spend their first day believing she is.

There is no single person whose permission is sufficient for everything here. Issa can find a stranger somewhere to sleep, but cannot promise them work. The broad-backed man who measures out grain can authorize repairs to the storage room, but his mother must be consulted before anyone moves the pots on its roof. A quiet, rather shabby woman keeps track of what the households owe one another. She remembers amounts without remembering to collect them, until suddenly, at an inconvenient moment, she does.

Authority belongs partly to age, partly to competence, and partly to whoever is prepared to keep an argument going. A boy of nineteen has acquired considerable influence because he can reach the seepage channel beneath the houses and is no longer willing to do so merely because someone calls him a good boy.

He comes out of the fissure hot, dirty, and cross. For an hour afterward he sits near the western entrance with his shirt open, refusing errands. His grandmother sends food to him by a younger child. She considers this a concession; he considers it lunch.

The people are not all small, although the houses favor smallness. Tall inhabitants develop particular ways of folding themselves. A long-legged basket maker sits with one knee beside his ear. A woman with heavy shoulders enters every doorway headfirst, turning as she goes, and has worn a pale patch on the back of her coat from the same projecting stone. Children duck long before they need to, imitating adults.

Most are broad through the chest. They walk the outer paths at a pace that seems almost idle until a visitor tries to accompany them. They have their own infirmities: aching joints, damaged eyes, teeth worn down by grit in meal. One old man has not climbed to the rim in six years. He asks each returning person about the weather beyond the hollow, then disputes the answer.

Their faces look different indoors and out. Below the roofs, where they spend most of their time, age can be difficult to judge. On the exposed shoulder of the mountain, light finds every crease, every scar, the redness at the edges of the eyes. People who looked sleepy in the lanes become intent and oddly private in the open. Conversation thins. There is wind to work against, and work to finish.

They do not consider themselves quiet or especially patient.

Two sisters have quarreled over a roof drain for so long that neither now gives the same account of how the quarrel began. They still share a grinding stone. Each wipes it carefully before and after use, making the wiping conspicuous. Their children move between the houses without observing the feud, except when it offers some advantage.

Someone steals food. Not much, and not often enough to establish who it is. Someone adds too much grit to the material sold as rich planting soil. There is a man who will lend any tool and then ask after it so persistently that borrowing becomes worse than doing without.

There are also people whose kindness is difficult to refuse. A widow with a painful hip keeps taking in other households’ children when the adults are busy. By evening she is exhausted and sharp with them. They love her, avoid her when they can, and bring her news she has specifically told them she does not want to hear.

The children know the settlement as a much larger place than adults do.

There is room behind the oven wall for three of them if the smallest goes in first. There is a route over the roofs that bypasses the woman who assigns chores to anyone she sees. Beneath one balcony, a loose packing stone conceals a collection of beetle cases, blue thread, a tooth, and a disc of metal nobody has yet identified.

They are forbidden to follow the rodents under the grain platforms. They do it anyway, and emerge with dust in their hair and extravagant accounts of snakes.

One girl has learned to distinguish the lizards along her wall. She recognizes a missing toe, a pale mark, the angle of a healed tail. She believes they recognize her in return. They certainly recognize the small flies she catches for them. When her father rearranges the pots, she makes him put one back because it belongs to a particular lizard. He protests, then puts it back slightly crooked to show that the decision remains his.

Animals live close enough to become familiar without becoming tame. Doves foul sleeping cloths. Sparrows pull fibers from fresh repairs. The flour beetles are killed, the wall snakes usually spared, though not by everyone. A household that tolerates a snake may still drive it out when there is a baby sleeping near the floor.

The creatures have individual reputations. So do the plants.

A swollen-trunked tree in an upstairs room is said to sicken whenever its owner goes away. In fact, she gives such elaborate watering instructions that those left in charge are afraid to do anything. There is a red-flowered thorn no one likes tending, inherited by a young couple along with the room it occupies. They complain about it continually. In its brief flowering season they invite people in to see it.

The climbing plant that passes through three houses is the subject of an intermittent dispute about pruning. One household wants more light; another fears losing its flowers. The third has concealed a cracked lintel behind the leaves and would prefer that nobody touch anything.

For all their care with vessels and roots, the inhabitants are not reverent about scarcity. They grumble about carrying snow. They spill things. A child can overturn a bowl and be comforted before anyone begins scraping the damp grit together.

Wastefulness is judged partly by who is doing it. An old woman rinsing her dead husband’s cup receives a latitude that would not be extended to a daughter washing dust from her hair.

Food comes up the path on human backs and on the backs of small, ill-tempered pack animals that do not enter the crowded lanes. Grain, dried pulses, salt, oil: the settlement could not live on what grows in its jars. People go down to work, to trade, to marry, to get away from particular people. Some return every season. Others send goods and increasingly formal messages.

Fine repair is one of the things the settlement sells. Broken clasps, harness fittings, knife handles, split wooden vessels: these arrive bundled together, with explanations of what may be spent on each. Several households do this work. They compete for it and occasionally finish one another’s orders when illness intervenes. A customer far below may never know that three different people have worked on the same buckle.

The young do not all wish to leave. Nor do those who leave all wish to return.

There is a boy who wants to see a river broad enough that a thrown stone cannot cross it. His aunt has seen such a river and says it smelled bad near the town. This does not discourage him. There is a young woman who has already spent two years below and has come home with new sleeves, money sewn into her clothes, and habits that irritate her family. She opens shutters they prefer closed. She will not explain to everyone why she returned.

At night, when the larger flowers open, people remain awake in the warm lower rooms. A repair continues by lamplight. Someone tells a story badly and is corrected from the other side of a wall. A child, almost asleep, hears its own name and wakes enough to object.

Later, even the settlement grows quiet. The birds settle. The mill stops. Small feet still move behind the plaster.

Issa makes a last check of the guest’s cup. She has learned that people from below sometimes ration what they have been given too anxiously, then wake with headaches and blame the height alone. She sets a little more beside the sleeping place, puts the lid on, and takes away a coat to mend a tear she noticed at supper.

In the morning she will complain about the quality of its cloth.
