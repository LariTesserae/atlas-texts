The oldest inhabitants remember when it was possible to walk straight across the hollow.

They do not all remember this fondly. There was nowhere to get out of the wind, and the first winter several roofs went into the marsh. But one old man still gives directions through rooms that have since been built. He tells a child to go past the split stone and bear left at the black pool. The child knows the stone as the wall behind her aunt’s sleeping bench. She has never seen the pool. It is under the meeting house, where something must be lowered on a cord to discover how deep it is.

Most of the adults were brought here young or were born during the building. Their knowledge begins at different depths. A woman remembers the foundation beneath a neighbor’s hearth because she helped carry it. Her son knows only that the hearth must not be struck hard on its eastern side. His son has been told that something there dislikes noise.

They are ordinary people, not especially small, though visitors often remember them that way. They stand close, turn sideways without interrupting a conversation, and sit with their knees gathered out of the path. Even in the open marsh, two townspeople meeting may leave no more than a hand’s breadth between them. On a causeway, where there is room to pass, they still wait for the other to settle a load before coming through.

Their clothes are short enough to clear the gutters. Working hems are stiff with pale dust. People who kneel at drains tie pads of folded reed mat around their shins; people who carry stone wear a second thickness across the shoulders, held on with cords and easily replaced. There is little long, loose hair below roof level. Children have it cut unevenly with the precious blades, or bound into small tight bunches that will not catch beneath a stair.

Their hands are less tidy. The cold water opens cracks beside the nails. Sharp rock leaves little straight scars, often several close together, where a burden shifted. A good mender can recognize a person’s work by where their sleeves wear through.

The mender lives in one of the few upper rooms with light from two directions. She has three children, none of whom are permitted to touch her copper needle. They may use the bone ones. Her husband drowned before the town had an outer wall, and people still sometimes speak of her as though that had happened recently. She is tired of it. She has been a widow longer than she was a wife, and has strong opinions about other things: the quality of imported thread, the foolishness of stone roofing over sleeping rooms, the handsome shoulders of a man who comes with the grain boats.

She likes red. There is very little red to be had, so she saves every scrap.

The town does not feed itself. Much of what its people eat comes along the marsh channels in shallow boats: grain, dried peas, oil in stopped vessels, sometimes onions with their tops braided together. These are unloaded beyond the wall and brought inward on backs. Going out are baskets, reed mats, pots, dressed flakes of stone, and lengths of timber that have been split more carefully than they were delivered.

Fish are eaten often, though not usually the narrow fish beneath the gate. Those are too small to be worth the trouble, and their presence makes it easier to judge the water. Children feed them crumbs in secret, despite being told there are better mouths for crumbs.

One boy has given names to seven fish. There may only be four.

The marsh workers leave before the upper walls brighten. They know channels that cannot be seen from the town and patches of mud that bear weight until the third or fourth step. Their feet are broader and more battered than those of the indoor workers. They bring back reeds, eels, waterbirds when they can catch them, and news from the landings. They are not considered brave merely for going out. Someone who can clear a blocked conduit under a loaded wall is taking a risk everyone understands much better.

There is an old reed-cutter who refuses to enter covered passages if he can find another route. He helped build some of them. This does not make him more willing.

Within the walls, people know a great deal about one another without necessarily being intimate. A woman knows when the family behind her has visitors because the extra sleeping bench scrapes twice across the floor. A man knows that his neighbor is ill before anyone tells him: water is being carried at the wrong hours, and the little jar used for boiling herbs has been taken from its hook.

They can also choose not to know.

A cough heard through stone may go unmentioned for weeks. An argument can pass through three households without any of them admitting they heard it. Privacy is often maintained by the listener, who lifts a pot lid, speaks to a child, or begins some small noisy task until the private sound has stopped.

This courtesy has limits. Everyone hears a dropped flour basket.

Their quarrels concern water, weight, smoke, and access, but are rarely only about those things. A trough shifted a finger’s width may become the occasion for discussing a marriage that should never have happened. A brace laid across a passage may remain there because two sisters have not spoken since their mother’s death. The scratches beside the doors record agreements, but somebody must remember which marks belong to which agreement, and whether the witness was sober.

One woman keeps these matters in her head. She is not the oldest person or the head of the meeting house. She has simply been present for a great many arguments and remembers the embarrassing parts accurately. People invite her when dividing a room or changing the course of a drain. They feed her first.

She has a son whose own gutter has been leaking onto the same neighbor for six years.

The children spend much of their lives where adults cannot comfortably follow. There are spaces behind incorporated slabs, dry shelves above cistern mouths, short ways beneath stairs that cease to be usable as a child’s shoulders widen. A favorite hiding place may be lost between one summer and the next without changing at all.

They measure growing up by these losses before anyone measures them against a doorpost.

They make toys from things that maintenance produces: stone rings broken out of drains, hard white curls, chips with a groove along one side. They float reed slivers in the gutters and argue about whose arrived first. They know which channels join, which vanish, and which apparently run uphill. Sometimes a toy emerges days later in another house and is returned with a complaint.

They are repeatedly warned about the rising water. They lick the stone anyway, at least once. Each new set of children believes the warnings conceal a flavor adults wish to keep for themselves.

During a dry spell, the town’s attention becomes dispersed and exact. Someone touches a familiar wall on passing. Someone lifts a hanging mat to look behind it. Conversations continue while eyes move toward a darkening seam.

There are inhabitants who feel the change in their joints and say so well in advance. There are others who say the same thing every week. Both kinds are remembered when they happen to be right.

The skilled workers can cut the new crust without driving a crack into the old slab beneath. They work by sound as much as sight, tapping lightly, changing the angle of a wedge, waiting while someone upstairs takes weight off a shelf. Their apprentices first learn to carry rubble without shedding it and to distinguish a piece that has come free from one that only appears loose.

An apprentice of fifteen has become very good at shaping the little spillways that fit below weeping walls. He gives them a unnecessary curve at the lip. Water falls cleanly from them, but a plain notch would do as well. His master complains about the time wasted, then places one where it can be seen from the doorway.

The apprentice has begun making another for the mender’s eldest daughter. He has not yet found a reason she should need it.

They do not admire every trace the water leaves. A crust on a storage jar is a nuisance; a crust on a dead mouse is an unpleasant discovery. Yet someone has kept the stone casing of a reed flower. Someone else has allowed a fringe to build beneath a window, patiently clearing the opening around it. Small household preferences become visible this way. One family cuts everything flush. Another leaves rounded knobs wherever they do no harm.

The town contains good housekeepers, careless ones, people too tired to keep up, and people whose rooms are immaculate because they cannot bear to sit still.

It also contains the snails, which flourish behind all four sorts.

At night they come a little farther out of the seams. Their translucent bodies move over stone that looks smooth to a human eye but offers them ledges, hollows, and sheltered routes. A drop falling from a stair can sweep one away. By morning there are others, or the same ones returned, clustered beneath the damp lip.

The townspeople seldom trouble them. They brush them off a vessel before drinking and lift them from the cutting line with a wet fingertip. Killing something so small would not clear a drain.

There are people who intend to leave. One young woman keeps asking the boatmen about places with trees large enough to shade a whole house. Another has already gone and sends back things her family does not quite know where to put: a broad wooden platter, a stool with spreading legs, a length of cloth too fine for daily damp.

There are people who arrived intending to stay only a season and have grown old while borrowing the same room.

The oldest woman keeps a little patch of earth in a pot beside her bed. It came from the place she lived before the clay rose. Nothing has grown in it for years. Occasionally one of her grandchildren offers to fetch better soil from the marsh, and she says no, not that kind.

When she is well enough, she takes the pot upstairs herself. She sits beside it in the light while laundry moves overhead and someone, invisible below, calls for a smaller wedge.

She does not spend all that time remembering. Sometimes she sleeps. Sometimes she watches the young man on the opposite roof and wonders why he is making such an elaborate trough.
