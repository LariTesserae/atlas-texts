Under the lowest surviving step, something has packed three broken shells into a gap too wide for them. They are held there by a piece of tile laid at an angle. Behind the tile is darkness, and in the darkness a pale disc occasionally appears, adheres to the stone, and releases.

The arrangement changes. Sometimes the shells are outside, concave sides upward. Sometimes the tile has been drawn so close beneath the step that only a crab could enter. One morning the tile is gone and a bottle neck occupies its place, the green glass furred on the outside but clean around the broken rim. Nothing else nearby is clean in quite that way.

The occupant is an octopus, large enough that its body must narrow to pass under the step. It does this without appearing to squeeze. A thick part becomes a thin part; what was gathered behind the eyes travels forward; an arm remains outside until the rest of the animal has vanished, then draws itself in, suckers releasing in sequence.

Within the cavity, it cannot extend all its arms at once. They lie against one another, against the roof, beneath the mantle. Several tips pass into smaller faults in the masonry. A shrimp can live a short distance away, beyond a constriction through which even the smallest arm tip cannot usefully reach. The octopus touches that constriction often. The shrimp continues to clear its entrance.

Its skin is usually the color of the underside of the step: a dirty cream interrupted by gray-brown patches. Along its back, little elevations give it the broken surface of encrusted mortar. But this is not a fixed appearance. When it moves onto the dark paving, the pale areas contract. Beside a shell bank, scattered light spots appear. Crossing a bare strip, it becomes smoother, flatter, less evidently composed of separate parts.

It does not always match what is immediately beneath it. Sometimes it remains mottled over an empty block, looking like a piece of rubble where no rubble should be. Sometimes an arm lies pale across black stone. The chamber is dim enough that these failures may not matter. More important is the long interval in which it does not move.

An eye stands a little clear of the rest of the body. The pupil is a narrow horizontal opening. Fine grains settle on the skin around it and are shed when the surface changes.

It has seven long arms and one short one.

The short arm ends in a taper rather than a wound. Its suckers are small and closely spaced, and it joins in movements for which it is not yet long enough. When the animal spreads over a cluster of mussels, this arm reaches for the stone and fails to touch it. The neighboring arm takes hold farther around. There is no pause in the movement, only a slightly uneven distribution of the body.

It hunts chiefly along edges. An arm goes beneath a lip of paving while the eyes remain directed into the room. Another follows the underside of a cable. The suckers close against rust, shell, slime, the rough mouths of worm tubes. They taste what they touch. A crack that appears empty from outside may hold the trace of a crab recently withdrawn into it, and the arm stays there, bending farther than sight would make useful.

Sometimes the octopus covers a whole patch of wall growth. Its arms spread and the membrane between them settles over the uneven surface, enclosing a small volume of water. There are brief movements beneath it. Then one arm curls inward, carrying something toward the mouth.

A crab shell may emerge much later, still hinged together. It is passed from sucker to sucker and dropped beyond the step. This is how some of the shell bank has accumulated. The current removes the lighter fragments. The heavier ones remain available for the entrance.

The octopus has no interest in preserving the arrangement as an arrangement. A useful shell can become an obstruction. A stone placed yesterday may be pushed away today. It works from inside, pulling with one arm while another braces against the roof, until the gap admits water and excludes whatever pressure it has just encountered.

The conger sometimes investigates.

First there is the head, low over the paving, then more of the length behind it than is usually visible. The octopus draws back. Its exposed skin darkens, and an arm holds the tile firmly across part of the opening. The eel’s jaw continues its slow movement. Its snout touches the stone, withdraws, approaches from another angle.

For several minutes there may be almost nothing to see: the eel at the step, a few suckers against the tile, water passing through the remaining gap. Eventually the conger leaves, or the octopus moves deeper into a fault behind the stair. The cavity is not a fortress. There is another way out, too narrow for the eel, which opens among the tube growth farther up the slope.

The pumps affect nearly everything it does.

At the first strengthening of the draw, a loose arm tip lifts from the paving. The octopus secures it. Its mantle lowers, and the arms that were reaching into open water return to something solid. If it is crossing the floor, it may continue, but the shape of the crossing changes: less height, more points of attachment, the body passing close behind a column instead of taking the shorter line.

It is not troubled in the same way by every sound. The deep note goes on while it feeds. The familiar rattle may bring no visible response. A valve striking hard can stop it in the middle of opening a mussel. The skin turns pale, then strongly blotched; the arms draw close. For a while afterward, only the mantle moves.

When the draw eases, food is sometimes left in places where there was none before.

It examines the scour hollow beside the wrench. One arm follows the buried handle; another turns over a shell caught against the corroded head. There is a small crab underneath. The crab runs into the open, and the octopus extends after it with unexpected speed, briefly becoming a broad, thin shape laid across several blocks. Once the crab is enclosed, the animal gathers itself again. An arm remains hooked around the wrench until the others have found purchase.

At times it goes up rather than out.

The wall offers innumerable holds. Suckers adhere to the smooth faces between colonies, release from crumbling patches, find the sides of brackets. Near the barred opening, daylight brings out colors almost absent below: reddish brown along the arms, a faint violet in the shadows of the web. Here its skin becomes coarse among the mussels. It reaches between the bars into water that moves more freely than the water inside.

It could leave that way. On some nights it does.

It returns with no visible burden, or with a crab held close beneath the body. Its route home is not always the same. It may descend behind the pipe and approach the step along the wall, touching cavities as it passes. It may come directly across the floor, tall on several arms, the others curled loosely beneath its head.

After heavy rain, it stays lower. Silt gathers on the step and around the entrance. The animal sends a short jet of water across the threshold. A brown puff rolls out, carrying shell splinters and a little curled husk. Another jet follows. From farther away, the disturbance resembles a small failure in the masonry.

The tile shifts outward. Two arms emerge and fasten on opposite sides of the opening. The head follows, then the mantle, its surface taking on the pale, irregular pattern of the stair.

For a while it rests there. One arm reaches back into the cavity. Another extends down the slope, its tip moving from joint to joint. Behind the tile, the place where its body lay is already filling with suspended grains.
