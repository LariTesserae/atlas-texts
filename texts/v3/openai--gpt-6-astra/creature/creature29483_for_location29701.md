The station keeper owns three pairs of boots, but only one pair is fit to leave in.

The others stand beneath her bed. Their soles have gone hard and slightly convex, so that they rock when put on a flat surface. She wears them for work, changing pairs at noon. Inside each she has sewn a loose pad of canvas stuffed with the pale hair that catches beneath the mineral shelves. This keeps the worst of the heat from her feet. It also means that, wherever she goes, the little dust-colored mammals stop what they are doing and smell her.

Her name is Nera. On the freight labels she is N. Venn, Upper Receiving. Letters addressed this way reach her more reliably than letters bearing the name of the basin, which has changed twice since she came.

She is not old, although at a distance she can look it. She walks with her knees a little bent, putting each foot down as though testing a stair in darkness. On the road below the clouds she forgets to stop doing this. People offer her an arm.

Her face is broad, with a small mouth and eyes that water in direct sunlight. The backs of her hands are dark; between her fingers the skin is almost pink. There is a notch in one thumbnail that grows out, splits, and grows out again. She has tried varnish, glue, and a little metal cap sent up by someone who misunderstood the problem.

For the first hour after waking she is difficult to speak to. Even when the relief man is there, she takes her breakfast into the lee of the turbine house and sits facing away from him. Breakfast is flatbread softened over a vent, with something sour from a jar. She drinks her tea lukewarm. There is no pleasure for her in holding another hot object.

The birds land quite close during this hour. They have learned the limits of her reach, including the extra reach provided by whatever she happens to be holding.

She does not think of them as tame.

“There’s less of me moving,” she says.

***

Her work begins with the water, not the engine.

She checks the receiving tank, the condenser gutters, the pump seals, and the narrow overflow that passes beneath her sleeping room. The overflow is supposed to remain dry. When it runs, something else has stopped running, and she must find what before opening the cable line.

She carries a strip of white enamel to dip into the tanks. Against it she can see grit, larvae, rust, and the faint cloudiness that comes before one particular kind of mineral deposit. Two of the tanks contain water that looks identical and cannot be used for the same things. She once let a visiting inspector wash his socks in the wrong one after he told her the labels were unnecessarily elaborate.

She regretted this. The socks were ruined, and he had only brought two pairs.

Under her supervision the works is neither especially clean nor allowed to become dirty in its own way. She sweeps insects away from electrical contacts but leaves the crowded seams under the walls alone. The felted hair beneath the pipe racks stays until it begins to obstruct drainage. A nesting bird can occupy a disused sleeve; it cannot occupy a switch box, however many times it returns with grass.

There is a lizard living behind the pressure gauge in the pump house. Nera has twice dismantled the gauge’s wooden backing to get it out. Both times she found eggs there and put everything back.

She has since mounted a second gauge.

***

She was sent up for a season to repair the condenser banks. Before that she worked in a valley laundry, keeping the boilers and wringers alive. She came with a toolbox, a green dress, and the expectation of returning before the autumn weddings.

The dress is still good. Wrapped in paper inside a flour tin, it has survived better than almost anything she brought. Once a year she opens the tin to check it. She no longer puts it on.

At first the basin disgusted her. There was nowhere to set a spoon without first wiping the place where it would rest. A dead moth became several occupied objects before she had finished looking at it. Things she had mistaken for dust unfolded legs.

She spent her first wages on fine wire mesh and fitted it over every opening in her room.

Some of it remains. Some she removed when she discovered what accumulated on the inside.

She stayed initially because the pay increased. Later her mother died, the laundry was sold, and the man who had been keeping a room for her let his married daughter have it. None of these events decided the matter by itself. She still describes the station as a place she is working.

She has particular plans for leaving. She wants a room above a street where deliveries begin early. She wants a deep bath and a window that opens onto a tree. She has saved enough money for both, though probably not in the district she favors.

What delays her now is partly the difficulty of teaching another person the works, and partly her suspicion that she will be unpleasant to live near. The relief man eats apples with his mouth open. Down below, there will be whole streets of people eating apples.

***

She knows fewer names for the basin’s animals than a visiting naturalist would like.

The pale creatures under the crust are carriers. The shrimp are shrimp, although apparently some are not. There are three kinds of bird, distinguished by where they nest, and a fourth she calls the thief because it takes the soft lining from other birds’ holes.

Her distinctions become much finer around things that break machinery.

She recognizes six sorts of growth inside a warm pipe. One can be scraped out dry. One must be kept wet until it is removed. One releases a smell that makes her leave the pit immediately, without collecting her tools. She marks that one in the ledger with a square rather than a word.

Of the grazers, she recognizes individuals. A female with an injured lip has been descending for seven years. The lip exposes a tooth and gives the animal an expression of fixed annoyance. Nera dislikes her: she rubs her horns against the insulated posts.

Nevertheless, when the female failed to appear for several evenings, Nera took a field glass to the upper benches and searched until the light went.

She found her with a new calf standing beneath a shelf so low that the mother had to kneel to reach it. Nera watched the calf back out, try to nurse, get pushed aside, and try again. Then she returned to find that a sack of flour had traveled all the way around the cable circuit because she had not been there to unload it.

The lower station never let her forget this.

***

She is not exceptionally patient. She can wait a long time for something that will happen, but cannot bear being told to wait for something that might.

The line telephone makes this distinction a frequent source of trouble.

When a shipment is late, she takes the receiver outside on its long cord and argues while watching the empty buckets pass. The person below can hear the clack of the support wheel. Sometimes there are birds audible at their end, loud birds from trees, and this distracts her enough that she loses her place in the argument.

She likes news about strangers: a warehouse marriage, a dispute over a boundary wall, an escaped pig that entered the magistrate’s office. News about people she knows makes her feel obliged to do something.

Her letters are full of practical questions followed, on the last page, by questions that are not practical at all.

*Does the little bakery still have the colored glass?*

*When you say he looks well, do you mean he has put on weight?*

***

At night she occupies only a small part of the largest building.

The chair by the turbine-house door is hers. Its front legs stand in shallow drilled sockets so that it will not migrate while she reads. Above it hangs a shelf of books, mostly novels missing their first or last sections. Complete books go into a cupboard. She has strong feelings about endings and will sometimes write a replacement on the blank side of a freight manifest.

The cup leaves a crescent because she never quite succeeds in wiping up beneath its handle.

Before bed she goes outside with a lamp. She checks the cable brake, then shines the light under the lowest pipe rack, where the mammals stand among their overlapping footprints. Several remain still. One always runs.

She empties her cup into a particular hollow, a little way from the pump foundations. She began doing this to draw them away from a seal they were chewing. Now they gather before she pours.

On some nights the hollow is already wet. On others it takes the whole cup, and the water disappears before any animal approaches.

Nera waits, holding the lamp low. Behind her the chair is lit through the open door; above her a bucket gives its small knock against the wheel. When the first muzzle touches the stone, she turns back inside, careful not to tread on the carriers moving across the threshold.
