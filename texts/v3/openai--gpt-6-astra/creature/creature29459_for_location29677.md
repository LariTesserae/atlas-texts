It lives behind the cut faces, where the peat is still wet enough to yield.

Most of the time, only an edge of it is exposed. This looks like a hanging strip of the bank: black, fibrous, fringed with pale stems. Frost gathers on it as it does on the roots nearby. A person passing along the terrace might knock it with a shoulder and feel nothing except the resistance of something partly frozen.

Afterward, very slowly, the strip draws back.

It does not pull against the bank. The bank seems to take it in. Fibres that had been dangling become taut, a shallow crease closes, and a few grains of ice fall onto the ledge below.

There are several places where this can be seen, though not usually on the same day.

Its body is broad and exceptionally thin. Folded into a seam in the peat, it occupies less space than a slab of the material removed by the excavator. Unfolded, it could cover the maintenance shed’s door. But it seldom unfolds completely. The edges are delicate, and once they freeze they break as easily as wet paper left overnight on a step.

The central part is tougher: a dark, flexible thickness with the faint cross-grain of something woven. Water stands on it in small beads. Under a light, the beads show a skin that is neither smooth nor hairy, but crowded with short, translucent loops. These hold water close against its surface. Peat dust lodges between them. So do fragments of moss, birch seeds, and the empty cases of insects.

Some of the moss continues growing.

There is no obvious head. One end divides into several blunt folds, which can separate and press independently into the ground. When it travels, these go first. They explore joints in the terraces, enter holes left by roots, and rest against ice for a long time before withdrawing. Along their undersides are patches of pale tissue, ordinarily concealed. They are the only parts of it that look readily alive.

It cannot cross the basin as a hare does, or even as a beetle might. It advances by making room for itself.

Water gathers beneath the forward folds. The fibres around them loosen. The rest of the body follows into the softened space, leaving the surface almost as it was. In summer, this movement might cover a terrace in a night. Now a crossing between two root mats can occupy most of the daylight.

Open ground is dangerous. The wind dries its upper surface, while the frost fastens the lower one to whatever it touches. It prefers the undersides of overhangs and the interiors of broken banks. A stack of peat blocks offers many suitable seams, but there are gaps between them where light and dry air enter. It has to investigate each passage separately.

Occasionally it makes a mistake. A grey scrap caught between two blocks, too regular to be a root and too soft to be bark, may be a piece it left behind.

The pumping station has altered its habits.

Before the excavation, pressure came through the peat in broad, faint changes: rain accumulating, frost deepening, the weight of an animal passing overhead. Now the whole lower basin trembles. The pulses travel differently through frozen ground and saturated ground, and it can distinguish these differences across the spread of its body.

It knows the pipe supports especially well.

Each carries a strong vibration down into a small area. Around the buried ends of the timbers, it presses itself into branching hollows and stays there, sometimes for days. When the pumps fall briefly out of rhythm, its forward folds lift. When the ordinary pounding resumes, they settle again.

Whether this is attention or merely an involuntary response would be difficult to decide. The maintenance worker has noticed something similar with the wet material beside the wall. During a shutdown, the dark green patch under the vent seems to tighten. Its edges curl away from the concrete.

He has put this down to the cold.

That patch is where it feeds most often in winter. Warm condensation carries dust, minute scraps of plant matter, and whatever has collected inside the ventilation housing. Beneath the moss, it spreads a pale, almost colourless underside against the softened peat. It takes very little at a time. By morning, a crushed grass stem may have become an empty sleeve; a patch of loose brown fibres may have disappeared into a shallow depression.

It does not eat the healthy green tips of the moss. They grow through its loops, anchored in the dirt held there. Where it rests for a long time, these small plants form a cover thick enough to keep its skin wet after the drips stop.

This makes leaving difficult.

The moss joins it to the wall, to the peat, to other moss. It must ease itself free a fraction at a time. Sometimes it takes a little of the ground with it. Sometimes part of its own edge remains attached.

There may once have been many separate bodies here. Or the cuts may have divided a larger one. Its torn edges close without forming a visible scar, and pieces separated for long enough begin to move independently. Yet when two pieces meet in a wet seam, they remain pressed together until there is no clear boundary between them.

The excavation has therefore made it both less and more numerous.

One portion lives beneath the fuel tank, in a hollow warmed slightly by the return line. Another occupies the damp core of a stack that has acquired three birch seedlings. A smaller piece has worked its way into the culvert mouth. There it remains behind the rim of white crystals, opening its folds whenever the black water rises.

The piece nearest the station has encountered the maintenance worker repeatedly.

It feels his approach through the concrete before his boots enter the floodlight. His weight is irregular and widely spaced, unlike the machinery. It draws its exposed folds beneath the moss. He breaks the mound of ice with a bar, clears the vent, and leaves.

Once, the bar caught an edge of it.

The worker lifted what he thought was a mat of roots. Water ran from it. For several seconds it hung heavily from the steel, its lower margin almost touching his boot. Then the pale folds opened against the bar.

He put it down.

Nothing followed him. The mat lay where it had fallen until he went inside. By the time he returned with a shovel, there was only a wet impression among the broken ice.

Now he clears that side of the vent with his boot.

Farther from the building, the largest portion is withdrawing from the fresh wall. Air has entered behind the sedge roots. The water films between the fibres are thinning, and the peat no longer closes properly after it passes.

It has found the crack behind the hanging mat.

The opening is narrow, but the dark continues inward. It raises one folded edge into it, then another. Snow rests on the part still outside. A few grains melt; the rest remain.

Through the short afternoon, it draws itself into the bank. The roots lift slightly from the wall, held from behind by its passing thickness. Then they fall back.

One pale fold stays at the mouth of the crack. It is pressed against the wet underside of a sedge stem, in contact with the tremor coming through the ground.
