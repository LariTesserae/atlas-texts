The largest hunter in the enclosure can lie across a screw head without concealing its slots.

It is brown, though the green indicator makes it look black along one side. Its abdomen is softly divided, widening behind a narrow front, and its two pincers extend ahead of it on jointed arms. There is no tail. Seen from above, it resembles a scorpion from which everything extravagant has been removed.

Dust adheres to the hairs on its limbs. One pincer is cleaner than the other because it passes, several times each day, through a constriction beneath the ceramic block. The other catches on the edge. To get through, the animal must fold that arm back and turn its body slightly sideways.

It has made this movement hundreds of times.

It is a female pseudoscorpion. She arrived nowhere. She hatched inside the instrument, in a silk chamber built against a bead of hardened sealant. The animal that brought her lineage here is several generations dead, and most of its substance has passed through mites.

Her world is larger than that of almost anything she eats. She has crossed the gorge on the insulated wire. She has hunted under two circuit boards and once reached the folded lip of the inspection plate. There she remained motionless long enough for the cold to slow the joints of her legs. Her return took much longer than her outward journey.

She has not gone there again.

Her eyes, small dark points near the front of her body, distinguish little. The sharply bounded sunspot is not a circle to her. It is a change encountered across a few steps. Much more of the enclosure reaches her through contact: the yielding surface of insulation, the brittle purchase of stone, the faint movements that travel along a wire when another animal mounts it.

Fine hairs on her pincers register what happens close by.

A mite passing beneath an overhanging piece of solder touches one of these hairs and stops.

The hunter also stops.

For several seconds there are only the minute adjustments of the mite’s front legs. Then it turns. One pincer closes across it, not quite centrally. The mite twists, catches the solder with two feet, and is held between the fixed landscape and the moving claw.

The hunter draws back. A little dust comes away with the prey.

She eats slowly. Her mouthparts work against a joint in the armor, where the body can be opened. What she takes becomes liquid before it enters her. The hard portions remain outside, changing their arrangement as she turns the mite.

When she abandons it, it is still recognizably a mite. It will remain recognizable through several more feedings by smaller animals.

Under her abdomen she carries a pale cluster.

The cluster changes the way she walks. She holds herself higher on clear ground, but lowers and spreads her legs when passing beneath a wire. A route that formerly admitted her now brushes the brood against its roof. She enters it, retreats, and feels along the opening with both pincers before going around.

The eggs are held together close beneath her body. Their water has come from hers. So will some of their nourishment. In this enclosure, where a dead insect may be dry all the way through, she is carrying a concentration of moisture large enough to sustain several lives.

She spends more time near the warm washer now.

Other animals approach her there. The pale mites flow around her planted feet, sometimes touching them, sometimes climbing a little way before turning back. They feed on things too small or too firmly attached for her to use. At intervals she catches one, but most pass unharmed. She cannot remain poised to strike in every direction, and a pincer occupied with food leaves much of her body undefended.

A dark mite comes to the emptied shell beside her and begins working at it.

It is almost spherical, with legs that can withdraw beneath its body. Its armor bears a pale scrape left by an earlier encounter with a hunter. It advances until one foot touches her mouthparts, retracts that foot, waits, and then begins feeding from the other side.

They remain like this until the pump starts.

The initial knock reaches the hunter through all eight legs.

She lets the shell go. Her pincers open and press against the floor; her body sinks over the brood. A narrow gap beside the washer admits her front half, and she drives into it until the eggs almost touch the metal.

The dark mite folds itself down.

Air moves. A fragment of insulation strikes her rear leg and lodges there. The hanging fiber sweeps past outside, touching the same exposed stone repeatedly. Fine powder enters the shelter in a low stream and gathers against her feet.

She does not count twelve seconds. When the vibration ceases, she continues to hold. Only after the fiber has fallen still does she back out.

The shell is gone. So is the dark mite.

Caught against her leg, the insulation fragment moves with her for several steps. She stops and pulls at it with a pincer. It breaks. One piece stays among the hairs.

She begins to feed again.

Elsewhere in the enclosure, survival takes less mobile forms.

Inside a crack in the mounting cement, a thread of fungus has entered the remains of an insect egg. Most of the shell has collapsed, but one curved portion encloses a space that retains moisture a little longer than the open passage. The fungus grows there by amounts too small to alter its appearance from one pump cycle to the next.

A mite returns repeatedly to the crack. Its mouthparts cannot reach the deepest thread. It grazes what emerges.

Behind the cable bracket, an immature scavenger is partway out of its old skin. The discarded covering holds the shape of its antennae. The living antennae are still drawn close, tender and incompletely extended. When the pump knocks, the animal can do very little. The bracket shelters it; the loose skin trembles.

There are bodies sealed inside dust that never become food because no feeding part can reach them. There are eggs that have outlasted both the animal that laid them and the warmth that made their ledge suitable. In one cold recess, a mite is alive so slowly that nothing in its posture has changed since the previous day.

The hunter passes these places without discovering most of them.

She is seeking a chamber.

Under a folded edge of aluminum, she finds a hollow with a low entrance and a deeper pocket beyond. She enters, turns, and comes out again. A second visit takes longer. Her pincers sweep the floor. She catches a loose particle, carries it to the entrance, and leaves it there.

The pocket is not empty. Three pale mites withdraw into a seam at its back. One remains with its front legs projecting.

The hunter starts laying silk.

It issues from the small mouthparts between her larger claws. She touches it to one surface, then another, moving her front end in short, repeated arcs. At first the strands are separate and almost invisible. Later they hold a little dust. The opening acquires a dullness, as though the aluminum has tarnished across a space where there is no aluminum.

She works, rests, leaves to feed, returns.

Above the route outside, the loose nut turns.

This time its movement is enough to dislodge a bright grain from the thread. The grain drops onto the hunter’s silk and hangs there. Under its weight, two strands draw together.

She approaches it from inside. Her pincers feel along the new obstruction. She pulls once, releases, then fastens another strand nearby.

The nut remains overhead.

By the next pump cycle, the chamber has a roof. By the next, its entrance has narrowed. Warmth reaches it through the folded metal, unevenly, with the changing load of the instrument.

Within it, the female turns until her brood is clear of the wall. There is room for her body and very little else. A pincer rests beside her face. One rear leg remains extended into the entrance, its hairs touching the silk.

Outside, a pale mite investigates the grain suspended above the opening. It finds nothing to eat and descends.

The pump knocks.

The silk bows inward. Every strand attached to the aluminum tightens at once. For twelve seconds the little chamber changes shape around its occupant, and she holds the eggs beneath her, clear of the moving walls.
