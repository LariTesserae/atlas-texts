The first animals onto the ridge are low enough to shelter behind a boot.

They come up through the old drainage cracks, pushing caps of dust ahead of them. For a while each remains at its opening with only its forelimbs exposed. These are pale, broad at the ends, and fringed with short stiff hairs. The animals spread them on the stone.

They are not feeling for warmth alone. A warm stone may have nothing beneath it but another warm stone. They wait for the small irregularities in the current: the interruptions and resumptions made by brine moving through a passage that is not yet fully open.

When they begin to emerge, they do so almost together.

Their bodies are soft between overlapping plates. There is no obvious head until one end lengthens and unfolds a ring of mouthparts. They scrape at the dry black material in the cracks, swallowing some and packing the rest beneath their bodies. The first moisture to reach the surface will soak this material. By then their eggs will be inside it.

Many of these arrivals will die without encountering open water. Their eggs are less hurried. A pocket of them can wait through several failures of the ridge, swelling at a little dampness, contracting again, losing only a few of its number each time.

By the third night, larger tracks have appeared among their scrapings.

These tracks occur in groups of six. Four are broad and shallow. Two, set close together, have pressed through the dust to the stone.

The animal that makes them is about as long as a resting grazer, but much lower. It carries its weight on four bent limbs, with two smaller limbs folded beneath the front of its body. Its back is covered in dull overlapping shields. Dust settles in the joins and is held there by a sparse growth of hooked hairs, so that an old individual carries a thin sample of many districts on its back: red grains from one crossing, black scales from another, pale fragments from a dead condenser slope.

The belly is almost white.

It is seldom exposed. Even in sleep the animal keeps its underside against the ground, or against another of its kind.

Its head has a broad, blunt upper surface and a soft lower face capable of considerable movement. The eyes are small and deeply recessed. They distinguish nearby outlines, the opening of a pale mouth, the passage of a wing against the copper sky. Most of what matters arrives through other organs.

There are pits along the lower jaw that compare temperatures across a surface. Inside the mouth, two combs remain moist even when the tongue is dry. Drawn close to the ground, they register the electrical differences between one patch of stone and the next.

The two small forelimbs are more sensitive still. Each ends in three thick digits surrounding a dark central pad. The animal places these pads apart, waits, shifts one a little farther away, and waits again.

From a distance it seems reluctant to choose a place to lie down.

In fact, it is measuring the ridge.

Others arrive over the next several nights, usually alone or with a young one riding beneath the body. They do not greet at once. Each makes its own passage across the exposed rock. Only afterward do they approach one another.

They touch the insides of their wrists together.

The contact may last a moment or several minutes. Small contractions pass up their forelimbs. Sometimes one withdraws, returns to a place it has already examined, and tests it again.

These are the people of the upland, though there is little in their appearance to separate them from its other large animals. They do not stand upright to look into the distance. They have no reason to light their settlements. A traveler could pass within a few yards of a gathering and notice only that the gravel was unusually thick with rounded stones.

For convenience, they might be called the keepers. Much of their work consists of keeping something open, moist, alive, or in the right place. The name would tell very little about what they call one another. Their names cannot be shouted.

A name is a brief pattern made against the wrist, with pressure and timing both important. It changes as its bearer grows. An adult retains something of the rhythm used for it when it was carried under its parent, but injuries, migrations, and long associations add other movements. Very old names take time.

An individual in a hurry can give only the beginning.

Their first work on the ridge is to find somewhere to put the children.

The youngest cannot regulate their temperature well. Their undersides are thin and almost transparent, crossed by vessels that darken when they have been feeding. They thrive in warmth, but a hot patch that merely makes an adult shift its weight can injure one before it wakes.

The adults search for a slab with an even temperature. They clear its edges, work flat pieces of stone beneath it, and raise it no higher than a hand. Under this roof they arrange dry leaves, then damp leaves, then a layer of porous fragments carried from their previous stopping place.

The fragments are important. Their pits contain living films.

Every traveling group carries such material. Some pieces are wrapped in shed belly skin. Others are held in pouches inside the cheeks, where a little moisture can be spared. The films feed on substances brought up in the deep water. Different kinds tolerate different brines; a flourishing black skin at one vent may turn gray and inert at another.

The keepers have learned which to bring together and which to separate.

They taste the ridge’s first seep and choose among their burdens. A fragment is placed at the wet edge. Another is set slightly higher, where it will receive less salt. Over the following nights they move them by increments, scrape away failed growth, and return a little of the healthy material to their carrying pouches.

Only part of this tending is for food.

Some films bind loose grains into a skin that slows evaporation. Some remove substances that make the water dangerous. A thick rust-colored growth produces a soft, nourishing layer beneath its mineral shell; another is scarcely edible but leaves cleaner water below it.

A good settlement has these growths arranged along the course of a seep. Nothing resembles a field. There may be three dark stones, a wet groove no wider than a finger, and a shallow depression covered with a slab.

The distinction between an occupied place and an unoccupied one is often the placement of that slab.

They eat the soft layers of their cultivated films, roots, burrowers, eggs, and whatever else the local cycle permits. Their broad back teeth can grind brittle stems, but they prefer to let those stems soften first in warm, inoculated pits. A meal may have been buried by another group long before the eaters arrived.

They replace what they take when they can.

This is not a rule observed with equal generosity everywhere. There are pits that have been stripped and left dry. There are groups known for leaving rich ones, and others whose arrival leads residents to conceal their best fragments under apparently undisturbed stone.

Scarcity has not made them uniformly gentle. It has made waste conspicuous.

The grazers come after them.

At first the keepers let the herds pass over most of the ridge. They watch where the animals settle and compare those places with their own measurements. An old grazer lying down can confirm what a keeper has spent several nights trying to establish.

When the current strengthens, they begin placing obstacles.

A line of knee-high stones is enough to divert a dispersed herd, provided the line does not lie across a strong route to water. The keepers leave openings above the best conductive seams. They clear grit from resting places there and remove sharp flakes that could damage the grazers’ throat pads.

In return, the herds bring dung, seeds, parasites, and food.

The keepers gather dung into the warm pits. They pick certain swollen parasites from the animals’ folds and eat them. A tolerant grazer may allow its entire underside to be examined. Others rise and move away at the first touch.

No keeper owns a herd. The animals leave when the ground tells them to, including when every prepared resting place seems suitable and every visible sign says they should remain.

The keepers pay particular attention to such departures.

Their settlements contain few tall structures. Anything raised into the air loses warmth and moisture, and can accumulate a dangerous charge. Most dwellings are widened spaces beneath stone plates, with several exits and a smooth floor. The occupants sleep loosely arranged around the children, wrist or flank touching wrist or flank.

A sleeper near an entrance periodically wakes and tests the air.

Predators can find them by smell. The most dangerous are narrow-bodied animals that hunt along the underside of slabs, able to flatten their ribs and work through gaps that appear much too small. Their approach makes almost no ground vibration.

Against these, the keepers use doors.

A door is a heavy tapered stone pulled into place from inside. Good ones are laborious to shape. They are left with the dwelling when a group moves on, and sometimes receive more care than the roof.

Outside, the adults defend themselves with short stone points gripped in their smaller hands. They do not throw accurately. They brace low, protect the soft underside, and strike at whatever comes within reach.

They will abandon food to a predator more readily than they will abandon a sleeping slab.

Their young spend much of their early life learning surfaces.

An adult places a child’s wrist on a stone, then its own wrist against the child’s. The lesson passes partly through the rock and partly through the teacher: this pulse; this difference; wait longer.

The children often attend to the wrong thing. A burrower moving below the slab is more interesting than a slow change in conductivity. The distant steps of grazers are more distinct than seepage through a deep bed. They have to learn to keep their pads still.

They also have games that require being anything but still. Young keepers race across plates that ring underfoot, try to cross a sleeping hollow without waking an adult, or make a companion mistake a tapped pattern for the approach of something large.

Adults sometimes join them. A successful deception is answered with a rapid series of belly contractions, visible along the flanks.

Their equivalent of laughter is easiest to feel while leaning against them.

They have no calendar that serves the whole upland.

They know the stages of familiar places: the dry warmth before a seep, the brief abundance of exposed pools, the salt coming back to the surface, the long interval when only deep-rooted things remain alive. But one place can linger at a stage for the lifetime of a keeper, while another passes through it during a single visit.

They reckon journeys partly by what a child will be able to do upon arrival.

There are routes suitable for young that still ride beneath an adult, and routes that should not be attempted until every traveler can carry water. There are crossings the old decline, not because they cannot walk far enough, but because their mouth combs have become less sensitive and there will be no landmarks they can trust.

An old keeper may remain in a dwindling grove with two companions, tending a seep too small to support the traveling group. If the grove persists, later travelers find them there. If it fails, there may be carefully sealed culture stones beneath the sleeping slab.

Information survives in worked stone as well.

At shelters and route junctions, the keepers cut grooves along the undersides of ledges. These can be read by hand without exposure to the open ground. Some grooves record directions and intervals. Others indicate the character of a water source or warn of a slab under which the air once became poisonous.

The marks require interpretation. A warning can outlive the condition that caused it by many generations. A route to a dependable seep can become a route to a dry hole.

Travelers add corrections rather than erase what is there.

Under certain ledges the stone is so crowded with old and newer grooves that a reader must spend hours sorting their intersections. These places draw visitors even when there is little food nearby. Several groups may wait their turn beneath a ridge, exchanging wrist-contact accounts of what they believe the marks mean.

Disagreement can occupy a whole cooling cycle.

On the newly warming ridge, there are no grooves yet.

The arrivals have found an old shelter, but it predates anyone’s surviving account of the place. Its door is buried. Inside lies a scatter of porous stones whose films have dried to almost nothing.

One stone, moistened and held for several nights at the edge of the new seep, develops a dark spot.

The keeper tending it moves it closer to the water. Later it brings another adult to examine it. They sit with their wrists joined, their free hands resting beside the small growth.

Above them, the first grazers are lowering themselves onto the conductive seam. A broad-winged animal makes a low circuit of the ridge and continues into the dark. In the nursery, a child has discovered that tapping the roof brings an answering tap from the adult outside.

The seep advances through the dust by less than the width of a finger.

Before resting, its keeper checks the dark spot once more, then fits a thin shard over it so that the water will not pass too quickly.
