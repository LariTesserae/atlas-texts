What lives there is most easily mistaken for something used in the building.

At the back of a loosened facing slab, between the stone and the clay it once held in place, there is sometimes a pale strip. It looks like a remnant of packing: flax beaten flat, perhaps, or the inner bark of a tree. It has no clean edge. Fine strands run from it into the bedding, and grains of sand adhere so closely that they seem part of its substance.

Where the slab still presses against it, the strip is thinner than a leaf. Where the stone has drawn away, it has thickened into a soft, uneven welt.

The exposed part turns brown. Most of it remains the color of wet flour.

It is alive, although nothing about a single inspection would establish this. Lift the stone and it does not contract. Touch it with a pole and the mark remains. Only after several days would the edges of that mark become rounded, and only after considerably longer would fine new strands cross the depression.

Lifting the stone would probably kill that portion of it. It requires the narrowness.

Its body occupies the places where one piece of the old work almost meets another. It spreads behind facings, under paving, through the imperfect contact between successive courses of brick. Some of these spaces were left by the builders. Others have opened since. A grain of clay washing out can make room for a thread. A slab settling can close a passage through which part of the body has lived for years.

There is no single thickness or proper outline. Beneath a broad paving stone, it may be a sheet with ragged margins. In a bed of broken pottery, it divides into flattened cords that turn around the sherds and join again beyond them. Against old wicker it takes the imprint of the weave.

Occasionally, when the mud falls away, a piece is exposed with such a precise basket pattern on its surface that it seems to be the basket itself, softened and bleached.

The larger portions lie farther inside the work.

A terrace may contain, behind its outer courses, a cavity no bigger than a closed hand. There the pale material folds inward on itself. Thin pleats surround a darker central space, and along each pleat runs a line of small, blunt lobes. These are firmer than the rest of the body. Pressed between finger and thumb, they would feel like soaked seeds.

Water passes through the folds.

It enters through one set of seams and leaves through another, moved by the slight differences in pressure along the terrace face. The creature has no need to draw a visible breath. The same current that inclines the pondweed passes through its interior, much reduced, carrying the dissolved remains of leaves and the finest particles of their decay.

Not every cavity contains these folds. Much of the pale material is only a passage between them, or a surface laid against something from which nourishment can still be taken. Around a buried length of timber it forms a close sleeve. At the end of a wooden peg, it may thicken into a little cup.

The timber remains recognizable because the feeding is so slow.

There are portions of the body that have spent a succession of winters against the same cord mark in a brick. Their substance has entered the shallow groove without quite filling it.

It is difficult to decide how much of this constitutes one animal.

Two sheets behind neighboring slabs may be connected deep in the wall. Two others, apparently continuous, may only overlap. When one thread meets another, they sometimes join; sometimes each develops a faintly thickened edge, and a line of water remains between them.

Those lines can last longer than the masonry joints around them.

A small individual might occupy the underside of a single tile. A large one might extend through most of an embankment, though there would be dead sections within it, closed passages, places supplied by only one hair-thin connection. It would have no boundary corresponding to the visible wall. One of its extremities might follow a buried drain beneath a courtyard while another reached upward through three generations of repairs.

The repairs suit it especially well. Different materials settle differently. Brick shrinks from stone. Clay loosens beneath a cap laid in haste. Each imperfect answer to the rising water has left another habitable interval.

There are no eyes, and no exposed organs reaching into the channels.

What sensation it possesses is distributed along its surfaces. It responds to cold, to the passage of dissolved food, and above all to pressure. A well-supported slab gives a steady compression over a broad area. A failing one presses first along an edge, then at a corner. Well before the stone moves far enough to be seen moving, the pale tissue beneath it has begun to change.

Under increasing weight it becomes dense and slick. Its more delicate passages close. The folds in the nearest cavity narrow, and new ones develop somewhere less constricted.

On the side from which the weight is lifting, it grows.

This can give the impression that it is keeping the stone in place. Sometimes it does delay a small displacement. A pad swells into a hollow, or tough strands remain stretched across a crack. But it cannot support much. When a facing fails, the creature is torn with it.

For a while, pale tatters hang behind the fallen slabs.

Then the brown film covers them.

The pieces still caught beneath stone may live. A severed sheet, provided it retains one folded chamber and a path for water, can close its torn margins. In time it extends into the new rubble. What was one body becomes several without anything departing, without a young creature crossing the open water.

This is one way it multiplies.

There is another, visible only along the margins of healthy folds. Here a few of the seedlike lobes become hard and dark. Each encloses a tightly pleated fragment of living tissue. They remain attached for a long time, ripening without any outward alteration except a slight increase in weight.

Eventually a change in the current loosens one.

It drops a short distance, or travels through a seam, or waits on the floor of its chamber until the wall opens. Most never find a suitable place. Buried in deep mud, they remain closed. On exposed paving they acquire the same coating as everything else.

One lodged beneath a fresh splinter of brick may soften. A white edge appears around it, then a thread follows the underside of the splinter toward the nearest patch of darkness.

It needs shelter before it needs much food.

The living root mats overhead are less suitable than they appear. Their undersides offer shelter, and their decay supplies nourishment, but they move too freely. A root can lift several finger-widths in a season. For this creature, that is an unstable foundation.

It will follow roots where they enter masonry. It can lie between a root and a stone for years, receiving small amounts of what leaks from the living plant. As the root thickens, the tissue is pressed into a long shallow channel. When the root dies, the creature occupies the channel it leaves.

Thus some of its highest branches lie close beneath the sedge fields, while its lower parts descend through the terrace into work older than any surviving plant above.

Winter reaches those branches first.

The tissue stiffens. Its passages become narrower. Farther down, in water whose temperature changes little, the folded chambers continue to receive their scant current. An individual extending through several levels can be almost inactive above and still feeding below.

The reports of contracting ice pass through it as pressure.

For a moment, thousands of soft surfaces are pressed against their stone ceilings. The firmer lobes flatten slightly. Water is displaced from one fold into the next. Then the pressure passes, and the chambers resume their previous shapes.

Nothing turns toward the sound.

On the deepest plain, where the old work has largely subsided into a continuous bed, it is rarer. There is more dead material there, but fewer open seams through which water can pass. A body can be buried intact and starve with food against every surface. Some of the pale layers beneath the silt are such remains, preserved between courses that have finally settled together.

Others still live around a single passage no wider than a grass stem.

The gradual leveling of the place is therefore a loss to it. A wall that becomes a bank provides fewer sheltered surfaces. A channel filled with sediment no longer supplies the chambers along its sides. Each collapse opens new spaces, but fewer than it closes.

It persists most thickly where the old arrangement is still legible: beneath curbs, behind the last raised courses, around narrow cuts left for water.

There, under a slab whose outer face carries the marks of closely spaced blows, one of its folds may be opening into a recently emptied pocket. A little clay has escaped. The water now has a way through.

The new tissue is almost transparent. It lies against the underside of the stone, taking the shape of an unevenness left by the tool. Over the next season it will thicken there. Its edge will reach the adjoining brick.

From the channel, neither change will be visible. A loose leaf will remain on the ledge above it, darkening, while water passes beneath the stone.
