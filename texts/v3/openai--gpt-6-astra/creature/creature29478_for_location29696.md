What lives here is easiest to find while the gutters are emptying.

Not in the gutters themselves. Look beneath the raised lip where the ceramic deck meets the base of a service ridge. For most of the cycle, a band of black material fills that joint. It resembles the flexible packing still visible around certain pipes. It has the same dull surface, the same shallow wrinkles, the same tendency to collect a white crust along its lower edge.

As the water draws away, part of the band withdraws.

The movement is slight. A strip of clean ceramic appears, no broader than a fingernail. Then several short, pale structures emerge into the space. They touch the exposed surface separately, remain there while a valve impact passes through the ridge, and withdraw in a different order.

Farther along, something eases itself out.

At first it seems to be a length of packing pulled loose by suction. It is flat and dark, with a thickened central portion and a margin that drags in the remaining water. It advances without any obvious stroke. One end becomes narrower; the rest follows. When it reaches a rough patch in the paving, it gathers itself into folds and lifts over it, revealing a pale underside crowded with small gripping pads.

Fully extended, it is about as long as a human forearm. There are larger ones, but they seldom leave the joints.

Its body is not equally soft everywhere. Beneath the skin are overlapping pieces of tough cartilage, small enough to let it turn through a narrow opening, broad enough to keep a pressure change from flattening the organs between them. The back is almost black. The underside has the color of something kept beneath a dressing. Along its margins, thin lobes can spread into a skirt or close tightly against the body.

Out on the deck, the lobes stay closed. It moves with the economical haste of an animal crossing an exposed place.

The broad end carries a transverse fold. Nothing about it immediately suggests a face. But when the animal pauses at a seam, the fold opens, and from within it comes a cluster of fine, jointed palps. Some explore the seam. Two remain planted on the paving. Another turns backward and touches the animal’s own flank.

There are no visible eyes.

It can distinguish the maintenance lamps, but only as differences in brightness passing through patches of thin skin. Light tells it whether it is covered, whether a familiar opening has widened, whether the grinding unit has come close enough for its undercarriage illumination to enter the joint. It does not use light to judge distance.

For that, it presses itself against the place.

The pads on its underside receive vibration through the deck. Each can be lifted independently. By breaking and restoring contact, the animal compares what reaches it through different parts of the structure. A loose ceramic plate, a flooded cavity, a loaded support, and a length of metal with a crack beginning inside it all return different patterns.

The tremendous noise does not resolve for it into silence. Some events overwhelm it. During the hardest valve closures, it contracts and lifts all but two of its pads, holding to the surface by the edges of its skirt until the impact has passed.

Between those blows, however, the plain is full of distinctions.

An open route has one texture. A route about to fill has another. The change in load before a reversal arrives through the foundations long before the shallow water begins to move.

The animal crosses the gutter now because there is time.

It knows precisely how much.

***

There are many of them, though an observer might spend weeks on the pumping plain without seeing two.

They occupy the narrow spaces left between systems: expansion joints beside heated conduits, abandoned fastening sockets, the undersides of inspection covers whose seals have failed without allowing the covers to cool completely. A cavity large enough to admit a gloved hand may hold several adults, their bodies folded together around a trickle of warm water.

Inside such a cavity, they look different.

The dark outer skin relaxes. Pale side lobes unfold and stand in the moving water. The gripping pads release in succession, leaving only a few anchored. What seemed compact and almost rubbery on the deck becomes thin, layered, and delicate. Water passes between the exposed folds.

They feed partly from the films that grow in warm discharge paths. The inner palps scrape these loose and convey them to a mouth hidden well behind the front margin. They also cultivate a fine, colorless growth on their own folded surfaces. It thrives where deep water carrying dissolved chemicals meets the more aerated seepage from above.

Their dwellings are arranged to bring those waters together.

A little mineral paste placed on one side of an opening will divert the warmer trickle. A scrap of gasket can keep a passage from closing under pressure. A flake of iron, wedged upright, separates two currents until they reach the chamber.

These arrangements are small and easily mistaken for deposits left by flow. Some are accidental. Others are adjusted after every reversal.

The animal crossing the gutter carries a fragment of ceramic beneath its body. It is holding the fragment with four of its pads. At the far side, it enters a slit beneath an old route marker and does not emerge again.

Later, the slit releases a fine stream of warmer water.

***

They are not solitary in the way their infrequent appearances suggest.

Within the joints, they keep prolonged contact. A resting animal usually has one palp against another’s body. Young shelter beneath the margins of adults, moving from one to another as the water changes temperature. Several dwellings may be connected by a passage too shallow for the adults to traverse but wide enough for their extended palps to meet.

Their signals are made by pressure, by the order in which contact is established, by a small ridge stiffening beneath the skin.

The signals must be delivered directly. Tapping on a wall carries poorly through the machinery’s much greater movements.

A newcomer waits at the mouth of an occupied chamber with its front margin open. An animal inside touches the exposed folds. The exchange may last several minutes. Sometimes the newcomer enters. Sometimes it backs away and tries another opening.

They recognize particular individuals. A familiar one can pass into a dwelling without presenting its folds, and sleeping animals rearrange themselves to admit it. After a long absence, the examination is more deliberate.

They also exchange things.

A useful wedge may travel through several chambers. So may a strand of tough fiber, a piece of mesh with its sharp ends worn smooth, or a bead of hardened sealant small enough to plug an unwanted seep. Objects are carried beneath the body, out of the current. In older dwellings, spare pieces are pressed into soft deposits above the normal waterline.

Their palps can turn a threaded fitting if it is small and already loose.

They cannot make a new one.

***

The maintenance machines destroy them regularly.

A grinding head passes over a seam and removes the warm upper chamber, its inhabitants, and a century’s accumulation of careful diversions. A cutter opens a more efficient drainage route through a network of dwellings. A pressure plate seats properly for the first time in many years.

The survivors do not abandon the district. Beyond the machinery’s warmth, there are few places where they can remain active.

They learn its intervals instead.

Along a settling rim, the chambers nearest the track empty well before the maintenance unit arrives. Their occupants withdraw into deeper cracks, carrying the young and whatever objects can be removed. After the unit has passed, they return to a cleaner surface with fewer places to hold.

Occasionally, one fails to leave.

An old animal may remain attached to a warm patch even as the light beneath the chassis reaches it. Its neighbors pull at its margins. If it releases, they can drag it into shelter. If it does not, they eventually let go.

There are no bones to collect in the channels afterward. The supporting cartilage softens quickly in this water. Most of the body is consumed in the recesses where it comes to rest. A piece of dark skin may survive longer, traveling downhill among the strips of gasket.

***

They grow throughout their lives, but their ability to travel does not grow with them.

The largest become inhabitants of a single junction. Their bodies extend around corners and through openings that no longer admit the thickened central organs. Some have several separate regions of exposed skin along the same service ridge. What looks like a series of black seals may belong to one animal.

Others settle beneath a cover and slowly take the shape of its underside.

They can still shift their margins, tend their feeding folds, and manipulate objects. They cannot leave. The younger animals bring them food and clear the passages they can no longer reach.

These old ones are useful in ways that become apparent only across many cycles.

An animal confined to one foundation receives the same set of vibrations for years. It knows the ordinary variations with extraordinary precision. A change too small for a traveler to notice may cause it to close a water passage or begin moving its outer body toward a cooler recess.

Others respond to those movements.

Sometimes an entire cluster evacuates a ridge during an otherwise unremarkable outward draw. They cross the open deck singly and in pairs, carrying pale young beneath their skirts. Several reversals later, the ridge begins to settle.

Not every evacuation is justified. Some chambers remain abandoned and warm for years.

Eventually, someone returns.

***

They approach the central basin, but they do not feed there.

The clean overlapping plates offer little purchase. The water between them is unsuitable for the growth on their bodies, and the deep pressure obscures much of what they ordinarily perceive. Even a small animal keeps most of its pads lifted when it moves along the rim.

Nevertheless, several routes end there.

Beside one white lamp, sheltered under the fastening that holds its cable, is a pocket of dark skin. It belongs to an old animal whose body follows the cable duct for several meters before widening into a cavity beneath the rim.

Visitors stop beside it. They establish contact beneath the lamp and remain while the basin draws water through its floor.

The old animal has palps extended into the gap under the nearest plate.

When the joint begins to open, it withdraws them before the movement is visible. Along the rim, the visitors flatten. One presses against another so firmly that their margins overlap.

The plate settles. The pale line appears.

For a short time, warm water issues from the cable pocket instead of entering it. The old animal opens folds that have been held shut through many reversals. They are nearly transparent. Small shapes move within them.

The visitors receive the young one at a time.

Each is scarcely larger than a loosened fleck of paint, pale except for a dark central spot. It adheres to the underside of the animal carrying it and spreads its first few gripping pads.

By the time the joint closes, the folds beneath the lamp are empty.

The adults wait through the next valve sequence. Then they leave along different seams, keeping low, taking the young toward chambers where two kinds of water meet.

Behind them, the old animal draws its skin back into the cable pocket. A little mineral paste falls from the edge. The fastening is left clean for a moment before the water covers it again.
