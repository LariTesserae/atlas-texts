The people who live here keep their coats on indoors until the middle of the morning. At breakfast they sit with their ankles tucked under their chairs, holding cups in both hands. Children dress beside the stove. In the workshops, the first person to arrive puts the grease tin in a basin of hot water; otherwise it will scarcely spread.

They are human, and the distinction matters most in winter. Their houses are alive but give very little warmth. A wall can close a small wound, draw moisture out of a cupboard, or thicken beneath a loaded beam. It cannot make a bed comfortable. For that there are quilts, hot-water bottles, and the custom of leaving a brick beside the kitchen stove for whoever comes home last.

Their clothes are mostly brought in from elsewhere. The cloth made in town is too coarse for much besides aprons, overshoes, and the stiff capes worn while washing down the reservoir. They favor wool with shiny elbows, cotton shirts, knitted cuffs that can be replaced without discarding the sleeve. A good many have a pale band across the thighs where they habitually wipe their hands.

You can tell something of a person’s work from what catches on them. Drawing-room workers carry almost invisible fibers in their eyebrows. Those who tend the roof leaves come down with green stains along the inner wrist. The people who work below the streets are cleaner than you expect, except behind the ears, where the fine brown powder from dry channels settles and is difficult to wash away.

There are a little over a hundred residents. The number changes with the season, with contracts at the workshops, with marriages and departures. A few houses contain one elderly person and a great deal of carefully unused space. In another, three children sleep in a room originally grown as a drying chamber. Its narrow ventilation slits make it hard to darken in summer. Their father has fitted shutters, ordinary shutters on hinges, and is tired of being advised to train the wall closed.

Not everyone wants the town to do everything.

A kitchen shelf that grows is still a shelf that must be checked for level. A self-sealing window frame can seize around the glass. One woman has spent eleven years preventing a rounded projection beside her stove from becoming a useful cupboard, as everyone assures her it wishes to do. She likes that corner empty. She keeps a chair there.

The residents speak readily of what a building wants, though they do not necessarily mean the same thing by it. A house wants light along an upper landing. A loading bay wants to sag. The reservoir wants a second outlet on its cold side. In the workshops, such statements are usually followed by measurements.

There are people who lay a palm against the wall before answering a question about it. Others consider this theatrical and use a probe.

The best wall-tender in town does both. She is a broad woman with close-cut gray hair and one thumbnail permanently split down the middle. She carries her instruments in a narrow leather roll, but often begins by leaning her shoulder against the surface under examination. This tells her less about the wall than about its attachments: whether a pipe is knocking, whether a bearing in the next room has begun to labor, whether water is moving where it should not be.

She has been wrong often enough to keep records.

At home, her notebooks occupy a metal cabinet standing clear of every wall. Its drawers contain dates, sketches, samples wrapped in paper, and several angry letters from a former reservoir keeper. She retains these because his observations were good, even when his conclusions about her character were not.

Much of the town’s knowledge is held this way: by people who disagree, in cupboards that must be kept dry.

Children learn some of it simply by being difficult to supervise. They know which seams lift enough to admit a coin, where a roof will bear weight, which membranes can be pressed without leaving a mark. In the oldest quarter, they find cavities that the adults have forgotten. They come home smelling of cork dust, with fragments of household things: the ceramic foot of a basin, a bent spoon, a piece of blue tile with half a flower on it.

They are forbidden to peg anything into the ground without asking.

This rule is broken more often than any other. A peg can turn a shoot, divert a trickle, begin a swelling that someone will have to pare back for years. Behind one house stands a low, awkward arch made by two boys who are now middle-aged. It serves no purpose and obstructs the passage of a wheelbarrow. Neither man will consent to its removal. Each claims the other is sentimental about it.

The children themselves are not especially quiet. Their voices carry across the broad courts, and a dropped tray can be heard from one end of town to the other. When they play chasing games in the square, their feet make a dry, hollow drumming over the chambers below.

Adults sometimes stop talking to listen to that sound.

An unusual note under a running child may be the first sign of a void enlarging. It may also be nothing. The child is called back and asked to jump in the same place, which is an interruption everyone finds pleasing except the child.

There are other inhabitants, mostly small and concealed.

Pale, many-legged creatures live in the permitted rot beneath the northern terraces. They withdraw when a board is lifted, not quickly but with a steady sorting of their legs. Tiny beetles occupy dry pockets in old roof fleece. A soft gray mold appears in unused rooms if circulation is reduced too far. Its arrival is treated with more concern than that of mice, which occasionally come with deliveries and generally leave disappointed. Very little in the fabric of the town is easy to gnaw, and household food is kept in glazed crocks or metal bins.

One household maintains a cat at considerable expense. It spends most of its time on a folded coat above the power-house motor. The operator denies feeding it, despite keeping a saucer in a drawer labeled WASHERS.

Birds visit the roofs, but do not nest there often. The pruning rollers disturb them. Swallows once built under the weighing-platform hood, and for two summers the platform was moved by hand so that the drive belt could remain still. People still mention this when discussing the man who was then in charge, as evidence either of his kindness or his poor management.

The town has room for both opinions.

It also has rents, debts, bad knees, visiting cousins, and arguments about whose work counts as maintenance. Water allocation is a recurring source of resentment. So is the order in which roofs are cut back to admit winter light. A family can lose an hour of sun because their neighbor’s upper room has thickened. Correcting that may require opening the neighbor’s bedroom wall.

Such disputes are conducted with diagrams.

The meetings take place in a room beside the square, where one corner is always damp despite several generations of intervention. Someone brings a kettle. Someone else opens a window before the room is warm. The workshop foreman wants more circulation through the southern yard; the residents of the adjoining houses want their floors to stop cooling at supper. Both have reasonable cases. The pump cannot satisfy both at once.

They remain dependent on the cable crossing the bank, on iron cast elsewhere, on salt, grain, medicine, and people willing to arrive in a place that can look unfinished even after centuries. Young residents sometimes leave because every alteration requires consultation with something older than themselves, even if that something is only a drain.

Those who return do not always return to their old houses.

An empty house is kept dormant for a time. Its leaves are reduced, its water ration lowered, vulnerable openings covered. After enough years, the decision must be made to restore it or let it settle. The decision is harder when the absent owner continues to write.

There is a woman in the northern row who sleeps on the ground floor of a house built for nine. Above her, rooms are being slowly taken out of use. Each autumn she brings down another piece of furniture. A wardrobe had to be dismantled to get it through a doorway that had narrowed since it was carried up.

She was furious about the wardrobe. She is not reconciled to everything simply because it happens slowly.

On fine days she sits outside repairing clothes for payment. The wall behind her has a shallow recess at shoulder height, worn smooth where she rests between jobs. Her customers stand in the court while she examines a cuff or measures a child. She knows which families are short of money and which merely dislike parting with it. For the former she invents small errands. For the latter she charges for thread.

By evening, people gather around sources of heat rather than around the square. A workshop bench acquires mugs and a plate of something wrapped in a towel. In kitchens, damp stockings hang from rails that are deliberately metal. A boy practices a wind instrument with three unreliable notes. Two houses away, someone shuts a window.

The late-shift worker comes home with cold hands. Before washing, he puts them against his daughter’s neck, and she shrieks and tries to bite his wrist. His partner tells them both to stop. There is soup on the stove and a notice on the table concerning a drainage inspection.

Beneath their feet, water passes slowly through a chamber none of them has ever entered. The floor gives a little as the night cools it. One leg of the table begins to rock.

Without looking down, the worker reaches into his pocket for a folded scrap of packing cloth. He has been carrying it all day.
