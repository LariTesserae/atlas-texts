The first sign of the inhabitant is a place where you can put your foot.

It appears beyond a curtain of rootlets that otherwise seems impassable: a flat, dry surface, about the length of a shoe, with a shallow depression in its center. When you put your weight on it, another becomes visible farther ahead. The rootlets have not drawn aside. Something behind them has turned, taking their attachments with it.

There are four such footholds. The last deposits you beside the old rail bed. Looking back, you cannot find the first.

For some time afterward, the inhabitant is simply whatever is keeping pace with you on the other side of the stems.

It makes no separate footfall. Its weight arrives as a tightening underfoot, a slight tilt of the polished roots. Once, through a gap, you see a surface carrying a little collection of things: a white stone, a piece of bottle glass, three snail shells nested together. They travel past at the height of your waist. Then a heavy fold closes over them.

You see it properly near the brick building, where it has come to attend to the gauge.

It is lower than you expected and much longer. Its front is propped against the wall while the rest of it occupies a bend in the passage. Several parts that seemed to belong to the buttress behind it prove, as it advances, to belong to it instead. There is no neat moment at which it comes entirely into view.

Its surface varies with what that part of it does. The weight-bearing portions have thick, dull skins, scored across the direction of travel. Between them lie folds as thin as the membrane inside a broad bean. Here and there a rounded plate has the glazed appearance of an old leaf kept pressed in a book. Wet grit adheres to the underside. Along its upper length, in small sheltered depressions, seedlings have established themselves.

It does not resemble a person until it begins working.

Two narrow appendages unfold beside the gauge. One braces the pipe where it enters the wall. The other passes over the glass, stops at the clear center, and remains there through several knocks from underground. Its tip is divided into many short pads, crowded together like the end of a brush worn down to the binding.

A gray snail is crossing the rim. The appendage waits.

When the snail has passed, the inhabitant presses one pad to the gauge and another to the green socket surrounding a brick. A ripple runs backward along its body. Farther down the wall, something gives a small, hard click.

It repeats the action twice. On the third attempt, the hollow rattle behind the building loses a beat.

The inhabitant withdraws its appendages and rests.

This takes long enough for you to think the encounter is over. Then its back opens, very slightly, along a seam you had mistaken for damage. Warm air escapes with the smell of bruised stalks. The little collection of stones and shells rises into view.

There are other objects in the fold: a short pin, a washer worn almost through on one side, a wedge of amber growth, the stem of a clay pipe. They sit in separate dimples. The white stone is smooth enough to reflect the window.

It selects the wedge.

You do not see how it chooses. The compartments contract in succession, passing the piece forward until an appendage takes it. The wedge goes into a gap beneath the copper fitting. The inhabitant leans against the wall with much of its weight.

The next underground knock comes cleanly.

Only then does it turn toward you.

There is no face on the part that turns. There is a shallow, moist recess bordered by short, stiff filaments. The recess tightens when you speak. When you stop, it loosens again.

If you keep talking, it eventually lays an appendage against your boot.

The contact is light but exact. The pads find the stitching, the edge of the sole, the hollow over the laces. One rests on the ground beside your foot. For a moment you are included in whatever comparison it is making.

Then it moves away.

It has little use for the passages at their full human height. Most of its traveling is done through the low spaces beside and beneath them, where the larger roots lift from one another during the pressure cycle. Its body can flatten remarkably, but not everywhere. The thick plates have to be brought through one at a time. At a narrow point it may spend ten minutes transferring its weight, leaving its forward appendages free to do something else.

Waiting is not inactivity for it.

While one part is caught between roots, another cleans the mouth of a small bore. While it rests beside the central growth, the pads of an extended limb move over a strip of worn material, locating a split by touch. An appendage left against a trunk may remain there so long that new root hairs begin adhering to it. When the inhabitant departs, these break with tiny audible snaps.

It is attached more firmly at other times.

In the falling half of the cycle, it settles into one of several long depressions beneath the supporting lobes. The hollow closes around it. Soft edges along its flanks meet corresponding edges in the depression, and a row of little pale openings becomes hidden between them.

Once it has settled, its separate outlines are hard to recover. Water moves beneath its skin. The seedlings on its back stand straighter.

This is where it feeds, or drinks, or accomplishes something for which the distinction is insufficient. It grows firmer as the paths grow soft. Small injuries on its underside fill with clear matter. If it has lost a piece of itself during the day’s work, a pale swelling forms near the loss.

It can leave such a resting place prematurely, but doing so tears it.

One afternoon a length of hanging growth slips across the rail bed while you are still inside. The inhabitant comes out before the basin has filled. You hear the wet separation under the lobe, and afterward see a bright raw strip along its side.

It does not pull the obstruction away. It works beneath it, spreading its body until the two rails take its weight. Then it raises a narrow section of its back.

There is room to crawl through.

Its skin is warm against your shoulder. Up close, the seedlings growing there are not all seedlings. Some are branching tufts rooted in old scars. Others have the folded, compact appearance of parts still being formed.

After you pass, it lowers itself. It remains across the rails for a while, with the injured side turned upward. A bead gathers there, holds its shape, and is drawn back in.

It does not accompany you to the entrance.

There are traces of its choices throughout the hollow, though most are too small to notice on a first visit.

Some bores are kept circular by rings of hard growth carried from elsewhere. Others have been deliberately plugged. A troublesome strip of iron has been wrapped in layers of old leaf until it no longer scrapes the root beside it. The upright flywheel turns less than a quarter of a revolution, but its exposed edge is clean. The inhabitant gives it that movement whenever it passes.

Behind the building is a place where useless things accumulate.

Here lie flakes of rust, exhausted wedges, lengths of fiber that have become brittle, and pieces of ceramic too small to brace anything. The inhabitant does not simply drop them among the leaves. It works them into a shallow shelf above the damp, packing the smaller pieces between the larger ones.

Mixed into this store are objects with no apparent use: the bottle glass, when it is not being carried; a dark pebble crossed by one white line; a small, hollow fruit that has dried without collapsing.

Sometimes it rearranges these.

It turns the glass until the green edge faces the light. It puts the hollow fruit into a cup of bark, then takes it out again. There may be several pressure cycles between one arrangement and the next.

The gray snails gather on this shelf. The inhabitant moves them when necessary, lifting each with a pad held under the shell until the foot releases. It places them on the same moist stretch of wall. They return slowly, over the following days.

Beneath a loose plate near its rear is a smaller inhabitant.

At first it is no more than a row of folded pads and a translucent pouch. It remains connected by several broad strips of tissue. Water can be seen passing through them when the adult settles under the central growth.

As it develops, it begins reaching for surfaces the larger body has already passed. Its pads miss, close on nothing, withdraw. Later they catch hold and have to be detached one at a time.

The adult occasionally stops for this.

When the smaller one finally separates, it fits between the rails. It has no hard plates, no objects in its carrying fold, and only one serviceable bracing limb. For several afternoons it attends to a joint that does not seem to need attention. It presses, releases, listens through its pads. The joint beads water. It presses again.

The older inhabitant goes around it.

There is enough room, though only just.

Near evening, when the discharge gate begins its long scrape, both become still. The smaller one flattens against the rail. The larger lays its working appendages along the floor, widely spaced.

The first current runs beneath them. A second follows, stronger. Somewhere in the crowded growth, a bore clears with a sound like a mouth opening.

The larger inhabitant lifts one appendage. It holds it above the smaller one without touching, waiting through the last uneven knocks.

Then it sets the pads down beside the young creature’s pads.

They remain there until the water has passed.
