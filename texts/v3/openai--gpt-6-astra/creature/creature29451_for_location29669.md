The first part of one you are likely to see is the underside of a hand.

It appears in a seam that has darkened during the afternoon: a pale, soft surface pressed against the opening from below. At first it resembles clay squeezed between bricks. Then the fingers separate. There are six, short and almost equal in length, with deep webs that unfold as the hand spreads. It rests there until the evening air has cooled sufficiently. Sometimes it withdraws without anything more emerging.

Their sensitivity to dryness is greatest at the joints. The broad surfaces of their bodies can form a protective skin, but wherever they must bend, they remain vulnerable. A hand exposed too early will stiffen at the knuckles. To loosen it, its owner must hold it for a long time against the damp underside of a bank.

When one does come out, it takes several minutes.

The arm follows the hand, then a flattened shoulder, then a head so low between the shoulders that it is difficult to distinguish. Much of the creature’s width proves to be folded flesh. It draws itself through an opening no wider than your wrist and spreads on the cooling ground, pressing first one side and then the other against it. Its back rises and falls in several places independently.

An adult, fully extended, occupies about as much ground as a person lying down with their arms spread. It is considerably lighter than a person. Beneath the thick shoulders, the body becomes thin and loosely pleated, with four further limbs along its margins. These end in smaller hands. The rear pair usually remain beneath it, feeling the surface as it travels.

It can lift its front high enough to look over a low ridge. Doing so is costly. The folds pull taut, moisture brightens the skin between them, and after a short while it lowers itself again.

Most of its life is conducted close enough to the ground to feel individual grains.

Its upper skin takes on the color of the material in which it has been resting. Lime leaves it nearly white. Red clay lodges in its fine creases and persists through several immersions. An old individual may have pale shoulders, an ocher back, and a dark band along one side where it habitually lies against a particular seam. Underneath, all of them are a translucent gray-pink. You can sometimes see water moving through them, slowly filling one broad chamber while another contracts.

The face is easiest to find when it drinks.

Two short folds part beneath the forward edge of the head. The opening between them is wide but very shallow. It touches a wet surface, and the surrounding clay gradually pales. There is no sucking sound. On either side of the mouth, small dark pits remain directed toward the ground.

Above these are the eyes: four dull points under a single clear membrane. They register shadows well. Fine detail appears to interest them less than changes in brightness, especially the passing of clouds. An individual working in the open may stop with both hands embedded in clay when a cloud’s edge passes over it. It waits, spread flat, until it knows whether the shade will last.

They eat the living stains on the banks.

At night, they move their mouths across the green crust in short, overlapping passes. By morning the grazed patch is paler and faintly lustrous. They leave narrow strips untouched, especially where the crust enters a fissure. The following rain spreads growth outward from these remnants.

They also feed beneath the surface, where a nearly colorless growth inhabits the boundary between sand and earth. There is much more life at these boundaries than the plain reveals. Fine threads coat the sand grains. Soft films occupy spaces too narrow to admit daylight. The creatures turn these layers with their smaller hands, bringing fresh damp surfaces together.

Their excretions are a fine, dark paste. Worked into clay, it gives the next green stain somewhere to begin.

***

They live between the laid surfaces.

What looks like a solid bank contains shallow accommodations: broad spaces with low roofs, connected by passages that close gently around a moving body. Some are barely thicker than a hand. Others allow several inhabitants to lie over one another, separated by thin cushions of water.

The roofs bear weight because the creatures maintain them. A palm pressed upward can support a soft place until it firms. Several bodies, spread side by side, can hold a newly laid floor throughout a night.

This accounts for some of the settling heard after rain. An inhabitant has moved from beneath a surface that seemed ready to stand without it.

They do not always judge correctly.

A collapsed chamber can trap one against a dry layer. Its neighbors reach it by wetting the fall from below, passing moisture from body to body until the broken earth softens. The rescue may take days. At the surface there is only a damp patch, perhaps a slight swelling, and a few beads of water that vanish before noon.

Their own bodies provide the implements the country was made with. The large hands knead and spread. The smaller ones sort inclusions, pull out a hard lump, press sand into a seam. Along the underside of the torso are two muscular ridges that can close around a mass of clay and compress it into a broad, irregular brick.

One creature can make a brick. Laying it is often work for several.

They carry it beneath them, supported on interleaved hands, their bodies touching along the shoulders. Their pace is so slow that a distant observer would see only a slightly raised patch of ground. At its destination, they lower the brick and remain around it while its edges are joined to the surrounding work.

There are no heaps of building material because they bring up only what can be placed before it dries. Fresh earth passes through the occupied layers from depths that are difficult to determine. Those nearest the surface receive it in small portions, already damp and worked.

Occasionally a darker individual comes up with the clay. Its skin has no lime on it. It pauses beneath the open seam, extends one hand into the night air, and goes down again.

***

Their knowledge of a place is largely knowledge of how it receives pressure.

They recognize one another through the hands. Two meeting in a passage press their palms together, then change the pressure in a sequence too slight to see. An injured or stiffened hand makes recognition slower. Old companions will stay joined for a considerable time, feeling around the altered parts.

Information travels through a floor in much the same way. A creature places both front hands flat and presses, waits, then presses again. Farther along the bank, another answers.

The pauses can last several minutes. A message sent across a large basin may still be passing through it when dawn forces the outer workers under cover. Those already sheltered continue. From above, the country is quiet.

They know where rain has fallen before its water arrives. They know which roof has begun to sag and which patch of green has thickened enough to feed several bodies. They know the familiar burden of a neighbor crossing overhead.

Your footsteps are something else.

At first, the inhabitants beneath your route flatten themselves and hold still. If you stop, they withdraw from under the pressure. This can make the ground yield more deeply beneath you. Walking on allows them to close the space behind each step.

After several crossings, they may strengthen the route. You return to find that the treacherous plates have been bedded into firmer clay, and that the most troublesome seam now has a broad, low lip.

They will have learned your weight without having seen you.

***

The young are carried under the body, in folds beside the water chambers.

At first each is little more than a small mouth surrounded by hands. It remains attached while the adult works. Later, it reaches outward to touch what the adult is touching. A parent smoothing a floor may leave a second set of marks inside its own: very fine, close together, easily lost in the next shower.

A young one’s first independent task is usually a depression small enough to cover with its body.

It must make the floor hold water without letting the rim soften and fall. It brings clay from a nearby seam, works it, waits, and tries again. The adult remains within reach but may be occupied with another surface.

Many of the finger-deep puddles are such places.

Some have been abandoned. Some are being maintained by an inhabitant still too small to travel beyond the basin where it was carried. After rain, these young creatures lie around their puddles with their mouths just touching the edges. They feel each loss through the floor. A leak sends them beneath it at once.

Eventually they begin to make outlets.

This is difficult work. Water escaping too quickly tears at the soft lip. Released too slowly, it evaporates before reaching the next hollow. For several nights a young builder may alter a slope by amounts you could scarcely detect with a fingertip.

Then a thread of water crosses the intervening ground.

The youngster follows beneath it, one hand against the underside of the channel. At the receiving hollow, another waits. Their hands meet through the wet seam before either comes up.

Much of the country has been joined in this way.

At dawn they shelter beneath the place they have connected. The new channel lies empty above them, already paling. But deeper in the bank there is still water on its way, and the two remain close together until they can feel it arrive.
