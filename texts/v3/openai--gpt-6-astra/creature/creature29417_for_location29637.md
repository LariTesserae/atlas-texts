The woman who replaces the water cups has a little finger that will not straighten. When she lifts a vessel, it rests against the clay before the rest of her hand closes. Her cups have a shallow hollow just below the lip, made with that finger while she smooths the rim. It is not a signature. She puts it there because it helps her hold them.

She lives on the second gallery, though her mother still calls it the bottom floor. Their doorway has been raised twice. Inside, the ceiling is low enough that the woman dresses sitting down, and a shelf above her bed holds six cups she will not put on the wheel. They are sound but too heavy. For years she has meant to grind down their bases.

Her name is Sefa. People shout it from below without coming to her door.

She knows which cup has struck the header jar by the sound, but she cannot always hear someone speaking beside her when the kiln is open. Then she turns her better ear toward them and looks impatient, even when she is not. Her mother does the same thing. Her daughter has begun to do it in imitation.

The daughter is eight and can pass through a gap behind the drying racks that Sefa has not fitted through since she was eleven. Adults send her in for fallen pegs, coins, the occasional unbroken bowl. She charges her uncle for this service and nobody else. Once, reaching farther than she could see, she touched something warm that withdrew from her fingers. She has refused that particular gap ever since, though she gives different reasons.

She knows the court by where she can be unseen.

There is a place beneath the three-plank bridge where she can sit with her knees against her mouth and watch ankles pass. She recognizes the grain seller by the flour in the stitching of his sandals, a kiln worker by his careful left foot, her grandmother by the pause before each step. The cats know she sits there. One will put a paw through the boards and touch her hair, apparently to discover whether she has brought something edible.

That cat is a heavy female with a white chin usually stained grey. She belongs, when there is something to be paid for, to nobody. When she catches a rat, three households claim to have fed her. She sleeps nearest the kiln in winter and on the water stair in summer, where people curse her without moving her very far. Her last surviving kitten went upstairs in a basket of laundry and now lives somewhere in the district above. Occasionally it returns, sleek and suspicious, and is driven away.

Sefa dislikes the cat because it broke one of the six cups on her shelf. The daughter says this means there are only five to finish now.

There are forty-three people who sleep in the court most nights, and several whose presence depends on work, quarrels, or the state of a roof elsewhere. Nobody agrees on the count. One old man is included by his sister although he has lived with his son for two years. A baby is left out whenever the grain seller calculates how much each household should contribute.

The grain seller is not generally considered a bad man. He lends his scales, helps carry anyone who cannot manage the upper stairs, and is merciless about grain. At sweeping time he stands with a stick while his apprentice gathers the spilled kernels. The rats wait beyond its reach.

One of them has learned to approach while he is looking upward to answer a neighbour. It does not hurry until it has to. Its coat is rubbed thin along both flanks by some narrow passage no person has found. Sefa sometimes sees it from above, carrying a kernel held crosswise in its mouth like a tiny pale bone.

She has tried to kill it twice.

The people here are skilful about particular things. A boy who cannot yet tie a secure knot can tell which roof tiles will crack in the firing. The woman with the fig knows how to open the catchment shutters in an order that keeps the first dirty water out of the jars. Two men can pass each other carrying full baskets at the narrow turn, but only if the taller one goes first. Visitors watch this happen and then get stuck trying to make room.

Skill does not make everyone agreeable. There is an enduring dispute over a shelf that projects a hand’s breadth into the passage. Washing is sometimes hung across a light shaft. Someone takes more water than their turn allows, and someone else remembers it through three changes of season. Sefa’s mother can name every household that failed to help when the lower stair collapsed. She can also name what they were doing instead.

Yet when Sefa burns her palm, a neighbour she has not spoken to for weeks comes to pedal the wheel. He sets his sandals neatly beside the treadle and works barefoot. Neither mentions the quarrel. The next morning it resumes over the same disputed basket.

By evening, Sefa has clay dried into the creases at her wrists. She washes over a bowl, rubbing slowly to use little water, and takes the cloudy remainder to the mint. Her daughter follows with the day’s findings: a bent nail, a wasp’s nest no bigger than a knuckle, a blue bead recovered from beneath a stair.

They sit on the threshold while the mother inspects them. The bead goes into a jar. The nail may be useful. The nest must stay outside.

Above their heads a pigeon turns in its cavity, scratching brick with its claws. Something small falls onto Sefa’s shoulder. She brushes it away before looking up, then shifts her daughter a little farther under the lintel.

They continue talking there until someone needs to get past.
