The woman at the foot of the signal tower is called Sela. She has a broad face, a damaged thumbnail, and a habit of putting a little of her weight on anything before trusting it with the rest.

She does this to chairs as well as stairs. Visiting someone, she will pause with one hand on the table and test the chair with the back of her knee. Her friends no longer notice. Once, in a newly opened café, the proprietor took offense.

“I fitted those feet myself,” he said.

“Then you’ll understand.”

She is not especially cautious otherwise. She crosses freight lanes between the warning rectangles, eats fruit without washing it, and lets the long-haired cats in the maintenance sheds bite her fingers when they play. But a surface that appears to be resting on another surface deserves a moment’s attention.

Her coat has a carpenter’s pocket sewn inside it for wedges. Most are wood. Two are a dense, dull red material taken from an obsolete electrical housing. She has carried those for eleven years. They will support an extraordinary weight without splitting, and she will not lend them to anyone who is likely to mistake them for rubbish.

At the end of a shift she empties her ordinary pockets onto the kitchen table: wire ends, felt, a nut, three seed husks, a receipt, a small white shell that may belong to an insect. Her husband moves these things to a saucer before setting out supper. Neither of them considers this tidying. Tidying is when someone has to decide where they belong.

***

They live in the fourth section of a building that was once straight.

The sections have drifted into a shallow hook around a moisture garden. From the kitchen, Sela can look into the bedroom of a neighbor who used to live beyond the lift shaft. They have arranged their curtains accordingly. The neighbor keeps pigeons, illegally, and Sela’s husband sometimes signals him with a saucepan when one gets into their ventilation hood.

Her husband, Dav, repairs optical instruments. He is patient with tiny screws and impatient with almost everything larger. There are six unfinished repairs in the apartment, each occupying its own cloth. Sela walks around them with a tolerance he does not extend to the contents of her pockets.

Their daughter, who is nineteen, has left home twice.

The first time she moved to a nursery hostel so far east that visiting required two trams and a walk beside a hot-water main. The second time she moved into a room over a glove shop. That room is now closer to her parents’ apartment than the communal washhouse is. She finds this embarrassing.

She still has her own key. She comes to borrow pans and leaves with food, lamp batteries, or something Dav has repaired without being asked.

There is no height chart on her childhood doorframe. The frame was replaced when she was seven, and again when she was twelve. Her heights are marked on a strip of wood that lives behind the coats. It also carries the heights of a cousin, a former lodger’s son, and a cat, measured upright under protest.

***

Sela works for the district, not for the tower.

Her route includes signal equipment, public stairs, two retractable footbridges, and a lavatory whose drain coupling is a persistent source of humiliation. She knows which grilles have been painted shut. She knows where a gauge gives a false reading in hard frost. She knows a particular flight of steps that remains safe until the third tread begins to squeak; after that, it must be closed even if every measurement is within tolerance.

The apprentice assigned to her wants to know why.

“Something underneath is touching something it shouldn’t.”

“What?”

“We’ll find out when we open it.”

He is disappointed by answers like this. He has been trained to distinguish faults, and Sela frequently treats a thing as faulty before she can say which fault it has.

His name is Fen. He carries an expensive level in a padded case and wears gloves too clean to grip wet iron. He is very good at drawing replacement parts. Sela has begun giving him work that depends on this, although she still makes him carry the barriers.

In the quiet streets, people recognize the clatter of those barriers before they see either of them. A shopkeeper will come out holding a mug. Someone upstairs will ask whether they are there about the smell. Children sometimes follow until given something dull to carry.

Sela does not mind an audience provided it stays out of the working space. She dislikes people who lean over a grille and say they cannot see anything moving.

“You wouldn’t want to,” she tells them.

***

Not everyone who lives here is good at living here.

Some people never acquire the foot’s small hesitation on an unfamiliar landing. Some cannot sleep while their building is being adjusted, though they can sleep beside a badly balanced fan. There are tenants who tape threads across wall joints and wake repeatedly to see whether they have broken.

Dav will not use the west footbridge. He knows Sela maintains it. He knows its permitted load and the date of its last inspection. He walks twelve minutes out of his way.

Sela stopped arguing with him after she found him halfway across one morning, holding the rail so tightly that he could not let go to take her hand.

Other residents are careless to the point of ingenuity. They block inspection hatches with cupboards. They lash separate balconies together to make room for a party. They wedge adjustable windows open with cutlery, then complain when the frames buckle.

In the old wards, a rigidly tiled bathroom is a luxury. In the cheap wards, it is usually an alteration made by someone who has since moved away.

Sela has seen beautiful rooms ruined in an afternoon. She has also seen a family continue eating around a table tilted so steeply that each plate had a folded towel beneath it. They had reported the problem three times. The youngest child thought the towels were part of laying the table.

For this reason she keeps a list separate from the district’s list.

Fen has noticed, but has not yet asked about it.

***

The birds beneath the houses know the foundation crews by their boots.

When unfamiliar feet approach, the birds withdraw into the steelwork, becoming little more than eyes and dusty feathers. For workers they recognize, they descend almost to arm’s reach. Their bodies are heavier than their short flights suggest. Landing on a loose panel, one makes a surprising knock.

They watch hands rather than faces.

A lifted plate may disclose larvae. A loosened brush seal may disclose an entire pantry of them. The birds wait through long stretches of unprofitable work, grooming the frost from their toes. When food appears, their patience ends completely.

Sela likes them until nesting season, when they steal felt from her repairs.

She has pulled out seals that looked sound from the street and found the hidden side picked into lace. The birds use the felt around a cup of dry leaf fibers, with finer material at the center: hair, insulation threads, the pale fuzz from the undersides of certain leaves.

One pair beneath the tower has lined its nest with red survey cord.

She leaves it alone. The cord was not hers.

The brown animals that run from disturbed hedges are less trusting. Seen closely, they have tiny ears folded almost flush with the head and a row of stiff hairs along each cheek. They test a gap with those hairs before entering it. Their young follow by pressing their noses beneath the tail ahead.

They do not always escape the rollers.

The gardeners keep a shallow box on the back of their equipment for the ones they find alive. By convention, nobody counts how much working time is spent placing that box beside a suitable thicket and waiting for it to empty.

***

Sela’s daughter works with roots.

Her name is Rill, and she dislikes the way people look interested when she tells them this. They want the roots that arrive inside the ground. They want something found alive in a sealed stone. Mostly she spends her day separating nursery mats with a blade, checking for rot, and washing trays.

Her hands smell of crushed stems even after she bathes.

Occasionally a tagged specimen comes through her department. These are kept apart from ordinary stock, in shallow beds with separate tools. Some are indistinguishable from familiar plants. Others remain lengths of pale tissue, neither decaying nor doing anything recognizable as growth.

Rill is particularly attached to one that has produced the same short shoot four times.

Each shoot divides into two leaves, darkens, and drops off. After an interval, another appears a little farther along.

Her supervisor considers it a declining specimen. Rill thinks they are giving it the wrong conditions. She has tried different shade, different condensation schedules, a warmer bed.

Sela asks whether it might simply be what the plant does.

“Yes,” Rill says sharply. “That’s one of the things we’re trying to find out.”

Later she apologizes. She has been asked this before.

She does not bring specimens home. But once, when Sela visits the room above the glove shop, she finds three ordinary nursery plants arranged around a saucer of greenish stone. There is a handwritten temperature record tucked beneath the saucer.

Neither mentions it.

***

That night, after the tower stair has been made safe, Sela is called to a row of dwellings beside the freight siding.

The problem is not dramatic. A service passage has narrowed by less than the width of her hand. Unfortunately, the passage contains the only door through which an elderly resident’s wheeled chair can leave the building.

The gauges are all within tolerance.

The landlord has sent this information twice.

Sela finds the resident awake, dressed, and waiting beside the door with a bag on her lap. Her name is Mrs. Ves. She is supposed to stay with her sister while the passage is worked on, and she is angry because her sister keeps the rooms too hot.

Fen measures the clearance. Sela checks the hinges.

From somewhere behind the wall comes a brief scraping, followed by silence. Mrs. Ves asks if that is the ground.

“Probably a branch,” Sela says.

“Probably?”

“I haven’t looked.”

They remove the door. Then the stop. It is still not enough.

Mrs. Ves watches them confer. Eventually she says that she can walk six steps if someone puts a chair at the other end.

“You should have said,” Fen tells her.

“You should have asked.”

Sela puts a chair in the passage and tests it with the back of her knee. Mrs. Ves stands, one hand on Sela’s forearm, the other gripping the dismantled doorway. She is lighter than Sela expects and much stronger through the fingers.

Behind them, Fen folds the wheeled chair and turns it sideways through the opening.

It takes a little while. Mrs. Ves rests after the fourth step. Nobody urges her on.

Outside, the waiting vehicle casts its rectangle of light across the hedge. Small leaves glitter in it, each holding a bead of water. Mrs. Ves stops to adjust the bag on her lap before they lift her aboard.

There is a plant inside, wrapped in a towel, its stiff leaves projecting beside a pair of slippers.

“My sister forgets to turn it,” she says.

Sela tucks the towel around the pot so it cannot rock.
