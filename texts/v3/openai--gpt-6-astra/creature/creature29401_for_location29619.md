One of the yellow boundaries encloses a ridge with a handrail still attached to its crest. The handrail has tilted almost horizontal. From a distance, it looks like something left on its side.

There is an animal beneath it.

Not the whole animal. What you can see through the opening is a length of pale, finely pleated skin, pressed against the warmer underside of the flooring. Water passes over it in a sheet. At intervals, a contraction travels through the pleats and the sheet breaks into separate streams, exposing small dark pores that close before the water joins again.

The animal is not responsible for all the ridges. It has chosen this one, and made adjustments.

At the lower end of the hollow it has packed a drain with felt, paint flakes, and the flexible black lips of discarded seals. A little water still escapes. Enough remains to cover its underside when the rest of the basin empties.

At the upper end, where the flooring has torn away from a row of fasteners, something projects into the open air.

It could be a bundle of stripped cable. There are six lengths, dull white, with darkened tips. They rest on the floor in a loose fan. Each tip touches a different plate.

When a distant pressure door closes, the tips lift in sequence.

Then they settle again.

***

Its body is difficult to measure because it seldom occupies a single space.

There is a thick central portion, flattened and slightly asymmetrical, which fits beneath the ridge. From this extend narrower parts that enter service gaps, pass behind insulation, or lie along the heat-exchange channels. Some are limbs. Others are folds of the body pulled so long and thin that they serve as breathing surfaces. A few end blindly in swollen pads.

It can withdraw them, but not quickly. Leaving a shelter is an undertaking. Before it moves, it spends several cycles emptying its outer tissues of water. It draws them inward a little at a time, peeling them from surfaces to which they have adhered. For a while afterward, its former resting place bears an exact wet impression of it: branching lines, a broad central oval, the marks of hundreds of tiny gripping cups.

Out in the open it is smaller than its dwelling suggests. About the length of a maintenance vehicle, but very low, with most of its weight spread through a skirt of muscular lobes. Its movements have the patient unevenness of something negotiating a narrow opening even when there is nothing around it.

The pale many-legged animals sometimes seen beneath the walkways are its young.

They have not yet lost the proportions that make them easy to recognize as animals. They have legs, a front, a quick retreat. They can turn around without first deciding which parts of themselves must be pulled out of a wall.

***

It feeds where water slows.

Along its underside are short combs that sweep deposits into shallow grooves. The grooves lead toward several mouths, each scarcely larger than a bolt hole. It takes in the brown growth from wet rails, the soft matter caught in filter weave, the nearly invisible skins that form inside warm pipes. Mineral grit passes through it and accumulates beneath the rear of the body in brittle, porous cakes.

A mature animal cannot live on what drifts into one hollow. It keeps feeding surfaces elsewhere.

These are the strips of filter felt tucked into sheltered seams, the loose mats beneath a leaking collector, the small obstructions that make water linger without quite stopping it. Some were already there. Some have been dragged into place.

The young carry pieces farther than they need to. Often they abandon them. A shred catches on a fastener, stays warm, acquires a film of life. Weeks later, another young animal finds it.

Around an occupied ridge, such accidents become frequent enough to look like an arrangement.

The stiff-leaved plants are neither food nor decoration. Their roots hold the felt open. Without them, the fibers flatten under the repeated tide and become difficult to comb. The animal grazes between the roots, working slowly enough that it seldom tears them.

Sometimes it does tear them. A hungry animal leaves a ragged margin. Around a failing shelter, the neat dark lines of growth become patches, then isolated tufts.

***

It has eyes, though you would have to be below it to see them properly.

They lie under the front edge of the body, sheltered by clear folds. Their view is mostly horizontal: the bright undersides of handrails, the low lamps, the interruption a moving wheel makes in a reflection. They are useful during a crossing. In its hollow, it rarely uncovers them.

Most of what it knows arrives through contact.

The six pale feelers outside the ridge listen to the separate plates. Further in, pads press against pipes and register changes in warmth, vibration, and the slight expansion that precedes a discharge. The animal can distinguish water moving through a channel from water gathering above it. It can tell when a pump’s effort has increased without an answering increase in flow.

A stopped pump is not silence to it. It is a missing support in a familiar structure.

When that happens, it draws its exposed parts inward and begins opening routes out of its shelter.

It does this before the warning lamps change.

***

The rising of the floor is more complicated.

Beneath some plates are flexible pressure membranes, installed to accommodate changes in the channels below. The animals seek these out. A small one can live beside a membrane without altering anything. A larger one may close one bypass and open another, pressing a soft, narrow appendage through a damaged inspection seal.

It cannot turn a wheel or understand a diagram. It can hold something shut. It can worry at an edge until it comes loose. It can repeat a movement that once brought warmth.

Over many cycles, it learns the particular responses of a particular junction.

Pressure does the heavy work. The animal maintains it, releases it, tries again. A low ceiling lifts. Water enters a newly opened space. The animal follows.

Sometimes the plate buckles much farther than it needs to. Sometimes it does not lift at all.

There are dead animals in the basin whose shelters are perfectly flat.

The vehicles recognize the pressure changes before the movement becomes visible. They stop, measure, and wait. If the deformation remains within certain limits, they paint around it and amend their routes.

The yellow line is not for the animal. It cannot see most of it from underneath.

Still, an old individual may spend its entire adult life inside one.

***

Every six hours, it becomes more active.

Before the collectors empty, the pale feelers withdraw. The breathing folds loosen from the warm flooring and descend into the water retained beneath the ridge. Small legs, concealed along the margins of the adult body, begin testing the ground.

When the separate puddles join, routes open.

The young emerge first. They cross the shallowest reaches at speed, carrying nothing now. Their feet make the quick tapping heard between changes in the machinery. Some go to neighboring shelters. Some climb the baffles to graze on the fresh wet film. A few reach the tower aprons and are still there when the water withdraws, stranded in exposed rings of light until the next cycle.

The adult ordinarily stays home. It extends long feeding lobes into the moving water, anchoring their tips behind seams. The current washes them clean and brings the taste of distant colonies.

Occasionally, another adult approaches.

The meeting takes most of the flood. Two low bodies settle edge to edge, and pale extensions travel over one another with extraordinary care. Each has hard grit lodged in its skin. Each can injure the other simply by tightening too fast.

Afterward, one returns beneath the ridge. The other continues across the basin, stopping frequently to place its listening tips against the floor.

***

The eggs are kept above the water, against warm metal.

They resemble beads of cloudy sealant laid in short, irregular rows. The parent covers them with a thin fold of its body during the coldest part of the cycle. When warmth returns, it uncovers them. Condensation forms on their surfaces and runs down between them.

For several days before they hatch, there is tapping inside.

The parent answers by moving one of its gripping pads against the plate.

The exchanges are very quiet. A vehicle passing nearby overwhelms them completely. So does the opening of a valve.

Between those events, they resume.

***

An old animal eventually becomes difficult to separate from its shelter.

Its skin grows into the filter weave. Mineral deposits stiffen its outer folds. Parts that once withdrew no longer do. It can still feed and regulate the water, but moving would require leaving too much of itself behind.

The ridge with the fallen handrail contains such an animal.

One of its six listening tips is missing. Another rests on a plate that has shifted away from its neighbors and no longer carries sound well. It keeps lifting that tip and setting it down a few centimeters farther along.

Beneath it live several generations of smaller creatures. They use openings its body holds apart. They graze surfaces it cannot reach. When it contracts, they retreat into the warm channels and return once it settles.

During the flood, the handrail disappears almost to its highest bend.

Water presses into the hollow. The old animal closes the drain with the broad pad of a limb, then eases it open as the level rises. Inside, the eggs remain dry.

Above them, a maintenance vehicle crosses the surviving walkway, its amber light turning slowly through the vapor.

The animal feels its weight pass from support to support.

For a moment, all five listening tips lie still.
