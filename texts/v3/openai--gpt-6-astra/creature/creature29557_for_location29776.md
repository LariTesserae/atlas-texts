The young have no plates.

For the first several years they live beneath the edge of an adult, in the narrow region where its weight does not quite touch the earth. They are pale, flexible things, each about the size of a closed hand. When the adult moves, they move with it. When it settles, they fit themselves into hollows beneath its flank. Their upper surfaces carry a fine nap that holds water so effectively that a drop spread there cannot easily be shaken free.

There may be three beneath a large animal, or twenty, or none. They do not all belong to it.

An adult passing over a recently hatched clutch acquires whichever young can attach themselves. They fasten to the soft seam behind its feeding ridges and remain there until the ground beneath them is moist enough to enter. Later they may change hosts where two adults graze close together. Some spend their whole unarmored lives beneath one body. Others cross a valley without once exposing themselves to the sky.

Their first food is what the adult has failed to extract.

This requires very little food to be wasted. The young possess, for a time, digestive organs that their elders have lost: delicate branching pouches able to take apart the last persistent compounds in the expelled grit. They leave behind them something finer still, a nearly weightless dust that cakes when wet. Along a well-traveled flank it forms a narrow ledge.

There are small jointed animals that live only on these ledges. There are worms that eat only what falls from them.

An old grazer carries a succession of such dependents, some sheltered inside the perimeter of others. To lift it from the ground would be to uncover several habitats at once. Most of their occupants would not survive the exposure.

***

The adult’s body is less solid than its outline suggests.

Beneath the armored back lies a shallow vault crossed by muscular partitions. The organs hang within it in repeated sets. A wound that destroys one set need not destroy its neighbors; circulation can be closed around the damaged region, and the animal continues with a little less capacity. Very old individuals are often incomplete beneath their shells. Their undersides contain sealed compartments, dry and empty, beside others still passing dark fluid from chamber to chamber.

The largest are not necessarily the strongest. Some have grown too broad to cross broken ground. A shallow step in the bedrock can turn them aside for the remainder of their lives.

Their movement comes from hundreds of short muscular pads arranged around the belly. No single pad lifts the animal. They swell and draw back in overlapping sequences, transferring the weight across small distances. On fine gravel the motion is smooth enough to escape the eye. On bare stone there is an occasional tremor, followed by a pause while the body finds purchase.

An animal approaching a crack examines it with its forward edge. The soft tissue extends, touches the far side, withdraws. This may happen repeatedly over several days.

If the crack is too wide, the animal begins to turn.

The turn can take longer than the examination.

***

There is no obvious head, though each animal has a front.

The plates there are smaller and overlap more freely. Beneath them runs a band of tissue the color of wet ash, crowded with pits too fine to see without magnification. These open into separate sensory chambers. Some register salts; others respond to the products of damaged roots, the presence of living membranes, or the particular changes that occur when ice begins to release liquid water.

The animal receives the ground in pieces.

A damp seam detected at the left edge may be beyond the reach of anything the right edge can sense. Signals move slowly across the body, and the two sides can begin different adjustments before one response prevails. For a while, the animal becomes slightly crooked. Its flexible lower surface gathers into folds.

Then the whole body takes up the new direction.

Above it, a cloud may have crossed the sun and gone. This registers only as a change in the slow loss of heat through the plates.

They do have light-sensitive cells. These lie deep in the seams of the armor and are most responsive when a plate has lifted or broken. Light entering the wrong place causes the surrounding muscles to contract. It is useful information about the state of a covering. The sky itself offers them little they can use.

Pressure travels better.

An adult can detect another feeding nearby through the faint compression of wet sediment. It pauses when that pressure becomes rhythmic. Two animals approaching the same patch will often stop before touching, then begin to shift until their working edges lie alongside one another.

From above, the arrangement resembles indifference.

Below, their margins are sampling the same water.

***

They distinguish one another chemically.

A body releases minute quantities of compounds produced by its internal colonies. The mixture changes with diet, age, illness, and the history of the organisms living inside it. A neighboring animal responds to some mixtures by drawing away. With others it remains in contact, sometimes for a season.

During that contact, material passes between them.

Along the flanks are small pockets lined with tissue thinner than the rest of the skin. When matching pockets meet, each releases a bead of thick fluid. The beads mingle and are taken back in. What moves through them includes reproductive cells, but also living cultures needed by the digestive chambers.

An isolated animal may possess a complete body and still be unable to live on the ground beneath it. The missing part is microscopic: an organism capable of making one stage of extraction possible.

After an exchange, a grazer sometimes begins to feed on a mineral band it previously crossed without stopping.

The effect can spread through a valley over centuries. Animals that have never met acquire descendants of the same internal colony. Their feeding routes alter. A formerly dark slope grows paler as one fraction of its soil is removed.

Nothing about the change requires them to gather.

***

Eggs are laid rarely, in places that already have a history of shelter.

The hollow underside of a dead adult is particularly favorable. Its plates blunt the temperature changes, and the ground beneath has been enriched by the long withdrawal of its tissues. An animal ready to lay will examine several openings along such a shell. It may return after a thaw, or after a decade.

The eggs are small and dull, embedded in a paste of mineral grains. They resemble a repair to the old armor.

Each contains a young animal and an initial supply of the organisms that will allow it to feed. Most never hatch. Some are eaten. Some dry beyond recovery. Others remain viable through conditions that give no outward indication of their survival.

Moisture alone does not wake them. Neither does temperature. Hatching requires a sequence: water entering, draining, returning; particular dissolved salts; the pressure of a living adult settling close enough to provide shelter.

Even then, only part of a clutch emerges.

Within one patch of eggs, siblings may begin their lives many decades apart.

***

The pale hunters search for the young.

Their long feet spread over the crust without breaking it. Under each toe lies a row of soft pressure pads, and the feet remain in contact with the earth while the narrow body leans forward to test a scent. The hunter’s face ends in a muscular mouth surrounded by short, stiff bristles. It can press this mouth into a gap and turn the rim outward against the stone, making a seal.

Then it draws.

A young grazer caught outside shelter has little strength with which to resist. Beneath an adult, it can brace against the roof above it. The struggle is small and largely invisible: a tightening in the hunter’s neck, a few grains shifting beneath the armored flank.

Sometimes the adult lowers that flank.

The movement may trap the hunter’s mouth. An experienced hunter releases its grip before the plates settle; younger ones can be injured badly enough to starve. Along certain old grazers are shallow scallops where their own armor has broken against struggling teeth.

The adult’s response also occurs when cold air enters beneath its edge, or when a stone presses against exposed tissue. The young survive within the reach of that response. They cluster most densely where the margin is still sensitive.

As an adult ages and sections of its body close down, those sections lose their dependents.

***

The first plate forms near the center of a young animal’s back.

It is a translucent disc containing a sparse arrangement of mineral crystals. For several seasons it remains small enough that the body can fold around it. Later, additional plates appear at its edges. The upper skin thickens. The nap that once held moisture disappears beneath a surface that sheds it.

The young animal becomes harder to accommodate.

It catches against the adult’s underside. It requires more food than the sheltered margin supplies. It begins to spend longer outside, first during thaws, then through stretches of cold, returning with frost caught in the soft joints of its new armor.

Departure usually occurs without a distinct separation. The adult advances. The juvenile stays over a patch it has begun to feed on. By the time it needs shelter, the dark opening beneath the familiar flank is several body lengths away.

Some follow and regain it.

Others settle lower, close the small valves around their breathing pores, and endure their first night alone.

Their plates are thin. The moisture beneath them freezes inward from the edges. By morning a surviving juvenile has drawn itself into the smallest workable shape, with only a narrow central patch of belly still active against the soil.

There it begins to sift.

For years afterward it may remain within the distance its former host covers in a day. Its pale new growth slowly takes on the local minerals. Grit enters the seams. One edge becomes blunter where it habitually meets the weather.

Eventually there is enough of a hollow beneath its flank to shelter something smaller.

A transparent jointed creature finds it first. Later, during a brief wet interval, a newly hatched grazer presses into the gap and fastens to the seam behind the feeding ridges.

The host shifts its weight. The small body withdraws a little, then settles where the ground is damp.

Above them, the frost continues forming on the plates.
