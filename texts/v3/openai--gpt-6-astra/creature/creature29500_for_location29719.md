What lives here is not quite divided into twenty.

The separate growths are separate in the way fingertips are separate when a hand is laid against the underside of a table. There is no buried hand in this case. Beneath the ceramic are contacts, deposited traces, layers of insulation, all individually specified in the drawings. But the living tissue has incorporated their distances into itself. A change at the hooked plant is available to the flat one without having to cross the intervening floor.

This does not happen quickly. Speed was among the things omitted from its requirements.

Its body includes the green blades and charcoal bases, but not everything connecting them. It uses the conductors without keeping them alive. Some of the ceramic belongs to its sense of position, though none belongs to its flesh. The boundaries of the organism would therefore be difficult to mark on a plan. A technician could circle every living part and still leave out much of what it experiences as injury.

It is especially sensitive at the unfinished bridge.

The blade there has a slightly thickened edge, visible under magnification as a row of clear, closed chambers. Those on the side nearest the conductor are smaller. The difference is not a defect. It was by making progressively smaller chambers that the blade approached its final length, each increment a fraction of the preceding one, until further growth would have cost more to initiate than the local field could supply.

It did not stop because it had arrived at the conductor. It stopped because it had arrived at the distance.

That distance remains active inside it.

There is almost no outward sign of the work. Along the translucent margins, charges are moved between structures that would collapse if allowed to equalize. Damaged molecular bonds are opened and remade. A few constituents pass through a sequence of temporary compartments before returning to where they started. Nothing has to be shed. A portion of tissue can be dismantled internally while its outer wall remains in place, so that even a very old blade has no worn patch by which to show its age.

The organism is not dormant. Dormancy would be cheaper.

It spends continuously to remain this exact size.

The light has little to do with feeding it. The green substance in the blades was useful during commissioning, when the lighting and electrical supply were brought up in stages. Now the lamps mostly give its surfaces a second, redundant account of their position. Each blade has a lit face and a less-lit face. Each base occupies its own short shadow. The hooked growth receives a narrow reflection from the fastener it nearly embraces.

There is no day, but there are differences.

It lives among those.

The machinery is more intimate. Sound crosses the blade walls and enters their dry interior as pressure, too weak at that scale to cause visible motion, sufficient to alter the ease with which certain reactions proceed. The low rotations reach every growth. Higher frequencies are admitted or excluded according to the shape of each blade and its position between the ribs.

Consequently, the organism does not receive the chamber’s enormous chord as one uniform condition. Each of its parts has a different share of it.

The flat growth lies in a relative hollow of one frequency. The tallest stands where two others reinforce. At the far wall, a pair of narrow blades receives a persistent difference between its left and right faces.

These relations have become part of its metabolism. To silence the machinery would not bring it peace. It would remove several of the conditions by which its separate tissues keep agreement.

Whether there is an experience accompanying that agreement is less easy to determine.

It has no organ to which everything is reported. Yet some states of the whole cannot be produced by any one growth, and when one contact is disturbed, undisturbed parts alter their internal exchanges. During testing, this was entered as distributed compensation. The phrase describes the measurements accurately.

It does not establish how much the organism knows.

What it can distinguish is very narrow. There is no provision for detecting a moving animal, no useful response to an approaching hand. Humidity would register as damage in several places at once, not as the presence of a visitor. A shadow would be an electrical inconvenience. Speech would pass into an acoustic environment already occupying nearly all the available range.

Someone could crouch beside it and remain, in every sense available to it, an obscure local fault.

The room’s height is equally inaccessible. The organism has never had a lower place to compare with this one. It knows a direction along which its own small weight acts, but the suspension has made that information remarkably uneventful. The thousands of tonnes below it enter its existence only through the steadiness they help maintain.

Nevertheless, it has a past.

Near the base of each blade, its internal partitions are larger and less regular. These were made when the chamber was new, while supply voltages were still being adjusted and the tissue had not yet found the shape that would end its growth. The older compartments retain materials in proportions no longer produced. Maintenance preserves them because replacement would require a brief alteration of local conductivity.

Thus the organism continues to accommodate the circumstances of its making.

There was an interval when the lamps were on and the compressors were not. There was a later interval when the basin had reached its operating temperature but two contacts were still unpowered. For a short time, the hooked plant grew toward the fastener as though the fastener were something it needed.

Those events persist as internal asymmetries. They do not replay. But the present must still pass through them.

The fastener remains important to the hooked growth for a reason the rest of the body no longer shares.

This is the nearest thing it has to an individual attachment.

There are no offspring. The scattered forms are not a colony waiting to spread. Their widely spaced arrangement was determined by the work they had to do, and no unoccupied hollow offers them an opportunity. Bare ceramic is not deficient soil. It is simply somewhere the organism has no means of becoming.

Nor does it wait for release. Beyond the panel are moving air, moisture, interrupted supplies, temperatures that vary. Expansion into those conditions would mean the loss of the precise differences on which its life depends.

Its continuance takes place inwardly: another exchange completed, another boundary restored, another small discrepancy absorbed by the whole.

Under the ordinary white lamps, the unfinished blade holds its clear edge below the conductor. Across the basin, the flat growth maintains a subtly different balance. The hooked one keeps the shape of an obsolete necessity.

Nothing travels between them that an eye could follow.

Still, each persists partly because the others are there.
