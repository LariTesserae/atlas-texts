The animal has eyes, but they are not on its head.

They lie along the undersides of its forelegs: four cloudy beads on each, almost indistinguishable from the joints. To look ahead, it must lift a foot. To look closely, it lays the leg against the floor.

What these eyes receive is not a picture of the hollow. They distinguish the light held in living tissue from the light that has begun to leave it. A healthy ridge is dark to them. A place about to split shines.

This is why the animal sometimes stops before an apparently unbroken surface and waits with one foreleg raised. It may wait through several bouts of thunder. Then the floor opens exactly where it has been looking, and the animal lowers its foot onto the new edge.

Its head is small and blunt, mostly mouth. Beneath the mouth are two soft pads that fit together into a tube. When it drinks, these unfold without a sound. The tapping comes from farther back, where the last pair of legs touches the underside of its abdomen.

The abdomen is divided into five pale plates. Each overlaps the next imperfectly. Under certain lights, something amber can be seen moving beneath them, passing forward when the animal walks backward and backward when it advances. Its organs do not seem securely fastened inside it.

Nor is the body quite opaque. Pressed against the wall, it nearly disappears. Crossing a shadow, it acquires a thin white outline, and the little hooks at the ends of its feet become visible.

There are six feet altogether. It keeps at least three attached.

The splinter is longer than its body and dark only at one end, where repeated wetting has softened the fiber. The animal carries it beneath itself, gripped by the middle legs. This leaves its forelegs free to examine the way and its hindlegs free to hold on.

There are many loose fibers it might have chosen. It chose this one before the hollow had three pools, before the raised knot was raised, when the floor and ceiling were still close enough for it to brace its back against one while walking on the other.

At first the splinter was too long. It caught on folds. Once it lodged crosswise in the lower opening, and the animal spent a long interval working it free, retreating, turning, pushing from the other side. One end broke during this effort.

The broken end is the end it now drinks from.

Near its resting seam, tucked beneath a lip of dry tissue, lie other pieces: two short fibers, a curved husk, and a translucent crescent with the impression of tiny joints. The crescent is an old covering of its own foreleg. The husk was once most of its back.

It has eaten the softer parts.

The remaining pieces are not arranged, exactly, but neither are they loose. Each is wedged where the wall will press it more firmly into place as it grows. Between them is a narrow recess that stays open even when the animal is absent.

Without these pieces, the root would close over its bed.

It sleeps folded around the splinter. The forelegs cross above its head, their eyes turned outward against the wall. Its mouth rests on the worn end, although there is nothing there to drink.

During sleep the amber thing beneath its plates moves very slowly. Sometimes it gathers at the rear of the body, and the last plate lifts. A clear pellet appears, clings for a moment, and drops.

The animal wakes immediately.

It turns and eats the pellet before the floor can take it.

Almost nothing it gives up is allowed to remain outside it for long.

Yet there is one thing beneath the lip that it does not eat. Farther in than the fragments of its old body, held between two ridges, is an oval no larger than one of its eyes.

The oval was soft when it was placed there. Now its surface has the faint grain of the root. Light passes through it without revealing much, although a dark comma sometimes lies against the lower side.

The animal does not sit over it. It cannot afford to keep still that long. On returning from water, however, it goes past its sleeping place and touches the oval with the wet end of the splinter.

Usually nothing happens.

Sometimes the comma moves.

The hollow’s weather is especially troublesome here. Brightness swells the ridges around the oval; amber light makes them slacken. When the two disagree, one end of the oval is held while the other is released. The animal has already moved it twice.

To move it, it must let go of the splinter.

This takes time. It puts the fiber down, touches it, turns toward the oval, then returns and shifts the fiber to a narrower ledge. Only when it has found somewhere the floor cannot easily swallow it does the animal attend to the other thing.

The oval has no convenient edges. The animal clasps it with all four front legs and walks backward on the remaining two, its abdomen scraping the wall.

It never carries it through the center.

There was another oval once.

It loosened during a contraction and rolled no farther than the width of the animal’s head. It stopped in plain sight on a smooth part of the floor.

The animal approached along the margin. It extended one foreleg, then both. The oval remained just beyond them. Moving closer did not bring it within reach.

For a while, its dark comma was visible.

Then the wall brightened. A film spread over the oval, so transparent that the animal continued reaching toward it after it had been covered. By the next dimming, the floor there was smooth.

The animal still tests that place when its route takes it nearby.

Now a new seam has opened behind the knot, and warmth moves through the hollow strongly enough to lift the fine hairs beneath its body.

In its refuge, the animal unfolds.

The eyes on its forelegs shine milky green. It touches the floor, withdraws, touches again. The worn end of the splinter rests beside its mouth.

Beyond the knot, droplets tremble on the new faces of the root. One runs sideways and disappears. The animal follows its movement with a lifted leg.

For once there is more water than the floor can immediately reclaim.

It leaves the seam without the splinter.

The journey to the oval is short. It makes it slowly, stopping whenever the root cracks. At the recess, it presses its mouth to the oval’s surface and works the two soft pads along the place where the grain is finest.

A clear line appears.

Inside, something straightens.

The emerging animal is smaller than a broken connection between floor and ceiling. Its legs are folded lengthwise along its body. As they come free, they fasten to the nearest surface—which is the larger animal’s head.

For a moment the two remain attached mouth to mouth, one standing on the other.

Then the larger animal turns.

It carries the new one down to the sleeping seam and retrieves the splinter. There is some difficulty arranging all the legs. Twice the smaller animal reaches toward the fiber; twice its foot closes on air.

At last it catches hold.

Together they proceed toward the water, keeping so close to the margin that their pale bodies brush the wall. The younger one rides beneath the splinter, where the eyes along its folded forelegs receive, for the first time, the changing light of the floor.

Behind them, the empty oval softens.

The root begins to drink it.
