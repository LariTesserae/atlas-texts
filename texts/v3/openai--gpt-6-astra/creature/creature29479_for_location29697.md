At first, its presence is measurable only as a discrepancy.

A row of snails disappears from beneath a walkway, although the herons cannot reach there. A filter basket comes up with its accumulated weed pressed into a smooth hollow. Beneath the disconnected pump, where the concrete is reliably dry, someone finds a wet mark wider than a boot and without the pattern of a sole.

The mark ends before the doorway. There is no corresponding mark outside.

The workers know about it, but not all of them know the same things. The morning attendant has seen a broad head resting on the lip of a drain. An electrician has watched something withdraw behind a cable tray that appeared to have no space behind it. The woman who cleans the pump strainers knows which basket will be unexpectedly heavy, and eases that one out rather than pulling it sharply.

On the maintenance sheets they call it an obstruction. In conversation they call it the sump animal, though it moves through several compartments and has no particular attachment to the sump.

Much of it is usually hidden. Even when it lies in shallow water, its outline is difficult to follow. The back is the color of wet concrete, with paler areas that resemble deposits rather than markings. A loose fold runs along either side. In still water these folds spread onto the bottom, concealing the short limbs underneath. In a current they draw close to the body.

It is longer than a filter basket, shorter than the electric cart. These are the comparisons available to people who have seen it.

The head is broad and slightly flattened, with a mouth set underneath. Its eyes are small, dark, and placed far enough apart that a person leaning over it may see only one. Behind each eye is a soft-edged opening that closes when the animal leaves the water. The skin around these openings moves even when the rest of it is motionless.

There are four feet. The rear pair rarely become visible. The front feet have thick, separated digits, blunt at the ends, which can close around the edge of a grating. They do not look capable of delicate work until one watches the animal remove a snail from a bolt.

It feels around the shell, braces against the concrete, and turns it.

An empty shell is released a little later. The current carries it toward the reeds.

It eats other things as well: insect larvae in the drain channels, softened weed caught against screens, the pale organisms growing inside a pipe that has been out of service for several days. It will take a dead fish, but has never been seen chasing a live one. Fish shelter beside it. Sometimes they hold themselves in the small patch of slower water behind its head.

There are narrow, almost colorless filaments beneath its jaw. Under a lamp they can be mistaken for loose fibers. Each rests separately against the surface the animal occupies. On concrete they spread into cracks and around grains of grit; in water they lift and bend.

Through these, or through the feet, or through the whole soft underside of its body, it attends to the pumps.

The large, continuous noise that fills the district does not seem to obscure anything for it. It can be feeding beneath the far walkway when a motor changes its load, and stop with one foot still raised. A worker approaching along the paving produces another kind of interruption. The animal generally moves before the worker comes into view, but it moves without haste. It knows the interval between the first footfall and the opening of a particular door.

It is less comfortable with someone standing still.

People who stop halfway across a walkway may eventually see its head emerge below them. It looks first along one side of the concrete, then the other. If they shift their weight, it withdraws. If they remain motionless, it may climb high enough to put two feet on the black line left by the water.

The workers have learned not to reach down.

It does not bite, as far as anyone knows. When startled at close quarters, it flattens and forces water out through the openings behind its eyes. The sound is brief and surprisingly loud. Once, an apprentice dropped an entire box of washers into the basin after hearing it.

For weeks afterward, washers appeared on the lowest step of the pump house.

Some had been carried there by the current. Others were too large to have passed through the intervening slots. No one established how many belonged to which category.

Its regular resting place is behind the covered machine. There is a gap between the plinth and the wall, shaded from the doorway and warmed by the neighboring motor. The animal reaches it through a drainage recess beneath the floor. Here its skin becomes lighter and faintly pebbled. The folds at its sides gather into thick wrinkles.

It sleeps with its front feet extended.

Occasionally a worker lifting the fitted cover finds that one foot has hooked over the bottom hem. The cover must then be lowered and approached from the other side.

There are other places it uses for shorter periods. It rests beneath the falling water when the basin is especially warm. It wedges itself between two submerged braces while shedding thin scraps of skin. On nights when insects gather heavily around the lamps, it climbs onto the lowest crossbar of a railing and waits with its mouth just above the water.

The herons watch it then.

Their relationship is neither peaceful nor openly hostile. The animal will feed beneath a heron’s favored perch, but not while the bird is there. A heron will walk along a ledge recently occupied by the animal, pausing to inspect the places where its feet have disturbed the algae. Both seem aware of distances that the workers cannot judge.

Once a heron struck at something beside the animal’s head. The animal turned so quickly that water slapped against the underside of the walkway. For several days afterward, the bird used a different compartment.

There were small ones that year.

They were discovered during the cleaning of a return channel: five pale bodies pressed together inside a coupling that had been left in place after its pipe was removed. Each was about the length of a finger. Their feet were already broad. When exposed to the light, they did not scatter but pushed farther into the coupling, climbing over one another until only a close arrangement of backs remained.

The woman cleaning the channel replaced the inspection plate.

She came back later and painted a small blue stroke beside its number. The stroke is not an official marking. It is enough to make the other regular workers ask before opening it.

The young appeared over the following weeks in shallow places where the current was weak. They grazed on the submerged edge of a step and vanished when a shadow passed over them. One occupied the hollow behind a loose tile. Another could sometimes be seen beneath the lip of the slotted plate, holding on with all four feet while water streamed over its back.

By the end of the season, there were no small ones to be found.

The channels extend beyond the retaining wall. Their routes are shown on a drawing in the pump house, though several later alterations have been added by hand. No one knows which openings the young could have passed through.

The large animal remains.

During the last pump changeover, it came out into the lane. For several minutes the usual vibration had stopped, leaving the fans, the falling water, and an unfamiliar abundance of small sounds. The animal crossed the wet paving in low, uneven movements. Without water supporting it, the body looked heavier than expected.

It went past the workers and the open toolbox, entered the pump house, and put its front feet against the plinth of the machine being started.

The motor turned. A tremor passed through the loose skin along its sides.

It stayed there while the pressure settled, its jaw filaments laid against the concrete. Then it lowered itself, turned with some difficulty in the narrow space, and went back toward the drain.
