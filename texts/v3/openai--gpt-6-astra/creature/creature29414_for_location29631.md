Under a slab beside the tarn, there is a nest made mostly of things that blew here.

Grass stems from lower slopes. A feather worn down to its shaft and a few loose barbs. Seed fluff caught while it was still dry. These have been worked into a shallow cup, then pushed to one side of the cavity, away from the warmest part of the stone. The floor beneath them is dry. A finger laid there would find it scarcely warmer than skin.

The animal is not in the cup. It is pressed into the narrow space behind it, where the slab meets the ground.

At first there seems to be too much of it to fit. Then it moves, and what looked like a compact body becomes a succession of parts: a pointed muzzle, a low head, shoulders that pass almost without widening the opening. Its ribs follow. Its hindquarters hesitate, then slip through. Last comes a short, thick tail with a naked underside.

It is about the length of a hand, though few of its movements allow a good estimate. In the open it gathers itself into a dark, quick shape. In a crack it becomes surprisingly long.

The fur is charcoal at the tips and nearly white beneath. Droplets remain on the outer coat, so that an animal coming out of mist looks lightly silvered until it shakes. Around the muzzle the hairs are sparse. Long whiskers project forward and downward, some of them bent or broken by stone. Its ears are small folds that can be closed against the head. Its eyes are black and perfectly ordinary, but it seldom looks up.

Most of what matters is close enough to touch.

It pauses at the mouth of the cavity with one forefoot raised. The exposed ground is freezing. Beneath its chest, warm air leaks through the grit. It lowers its muzzle, touches three separate places along the slab’s edge, and goes left.

There is no visible path.

For the first few paces it keeps a shoulder against the rock. Then it crosses an open patch in a series of low bounds, stopping beneath an overhang no bigger than a boot heel. Here it puts its nose into a hole, withdraws, and begins digging beside it.

Its forefeet are broad, with short claws rather than the long tools of a deep burrower. They are useful for moving loose ash, peeling moss back from a stone, and bracing the body while the head works underneath something. They cannot do much with fresh lava. Where the crust has no opening, the animal must go around.

It finds a midge larva in wet grit. The meal takes a moment. Then it searches again.

Its hunger keeps it moving through much of the day and night. It sleeps in short bouts, tucked into warmth, and emerges leaner than it went in. Springtails are worth taking only where they gather densely. A thawed margin full of larvae may occupy it for an hour. It eats eggs, stranded insects, the soft remains beneath a dead bird’s wing. During a hatch it stands at the water’s edge and catches what settles within reach, its jaws moving rapidly while mist collects on its back.

Around such abundance, it becomes almost careless.

That is when the birds on the ridge sometimes drop lower.

Usually, however, the animal travels under cover: beneath overlapping plates, through the space left by a melted tongue of ice, along a fissure just wide enough for its skull. Its whiskers test the passage ahead. The underside of its tail drags briefly over the floor when it stops.

Warmth is useful only within limits. At one opening it retreats before its muzzle reaches the steam. At another it enters, then comes backing out with wet fur flattened along its flanks. A passage it used yesterday has filled with hot water.

It waits outside for a little while. Then it takes another route.

Within its small circuit are several sleeping places. One is only a dry hollow under a stone. Another holds a little bedding. The best has an entrance sheltered from the prevailing wind and a second exit through gravel. It carries nest material between them in awkward mouthfuls, sometimes losing half of what it has collected during a crossing.

These shelters do not remain equally good. Snow can improve one by sealing the draught around its roof. Ice can ruin another by blocking its escape hole. A change underground may heat a familiar chamber until the bedding smells scorched.

After that, the animal may approach the entrance repeatedly without going in.

Others of its kind live along the tarn and beneath the warmer outcrops. They encounter one another chiefly at narrow feeding margins. A few sharp squeaks, a rush over wet stone, and one withdraws. Where a hatch is dense enough, two may feed less than a body-length apart, each occasionally turning to show its teeth.

The bare distances between these places are harder to cross than they appear. A stretch of cold grit may contain no food and no hole deep enough to hide in. The animal stops at its edge, advances, returns to cover. Sometimes it waits until cloud has erased the ridge and the hunting birds with it.

Then it goes.

In the nest behind the slab, there are three young.

They are blunt-faced, with sealed eyes and pale feet. Their fur has begun to darken along the spine. They lie tangled together in the displaced bedding, making small movements that are more like kneading than crawling.

The adult returns with its coat wet and stands over them before settling. One finds its way beneath her. The others follow.

For a while the cavity is quiet.

Later in the season, they will be moved. The water beneath the slab is already taking longer to drain, and a damp patch has appeared under the outermost grass stems. Their mother will carry them separately, gripping each by the loose skin behind the head. Between journeys, the ones left behind will press into the cup and wait.

The new shelter is several dozen paces away. Getting there requires a crossing of open crust, a turn beneath a yellow-stained lip, and a climb through loose fragments whose edges shift underfoot.

She has already begun leaving dry grass there.

Above the nest, evening snow whitens the slab. It remains white at the center and dark around one corner, where a little heat escapes. The difference is too small to distinguish from the ridge. Even close by, there is nothing to suggest a home until the dark corner stirs and a pointed muzzle appears, whiskers carrying beads of water.

The animal waits, testing the air.

Then it comes out to feed.
