The living part is not all green.

Beneath each crown, where the thick foot enters the clay, there is a collar of gray material that looks like an unfinished repair. It has no regular edge. In some courts it spreads flat against the floor; in others it climbs a little way up the wall, filling the old finger marks without smoothing them away. Dust sticks to it. A stone chip pressed against it will, over many heat cycles, become difficult to remove.

This is the part that grows first.

A new individual may spend a long time as no more than a discoloration inside a joint. It takes what the warm air brings and incorporates a little of the surface beneath it. Its earliest folds lie within the crack, too small to lift free. Only when one side of the growth can remain warmer than the other does an edge begin to stand out. The first green blade is not pushed upward from below. It separates from the collar along a prepared seam, lifting like a shaving that has not been cut all the way through.

For a while it is almost transparent. The dust on it is more visible than the blade itself.

The older crowns have no comparable delicacy. Their apparent softness comes from the number of edges, not from the substance of any one edge. A blade touched with a needle gives a hard little click. Press farther and it yields in steps, one internal fold slipping past another. It can bend a considerable distance before breaking, but it cannot bend in every direction.

What looks like lush growth is an accumulation of carefully permitted movements.

Each blade is divided into strips so narrow that the divisions are difficult to see. At the base, those strips remain joined. Farther out, they can move separately. Heat reaches the underside and the strips lengthen unequally, turning the blade open. As they cool, they settle back into one another. A healthy crown closes with its edges slightly out of alignment. There must always be room for the next opening.

Sometimes a grain catches between two strips. The blade fails to close by a fraction. During the next pulse, the neighboring strips bear more strain.

The crown cannot pick the grain out. It grows around the obstruction, slowly making the place where it is caught less important. A narrow section ceases to receive fresh material. Two seams deepen beside it. Eventually the damaged strip falls away, carrying the grain with it.

Old plants are full of these small omissions. Their fans have slots, shortened fingers, missing corners. The largest are often the most open. Heat passes through them readily, and their numerous separate surfaces move without requiring the whole crown to move at once.

A perfectly unbroken crown would be a young one, or an unhealthy one.

They do not turn toward sounds. Nevertheless, the noises of the city enter their lives.

The late knock beneath the central court passes up through the feet of the plants. Loose material shifts in their collars. Dead layers separate. Grains caught in the lower folds are thrown outward, sometimes into a neighboring trough. Near the buried slab, crowns remain low and broad, with roots that divide repeatedly before entering the mortar. Farther away, where the knock arrives weakened, the plants can grow tall enough to brush the underside of a roof.

There are courts where a roof has begun to rest on a crown.

At first the contact is slight: one pale patch on the uppermost blade, polished by repeated opening. Later the blade thickens below that patch. Other blades change their angle around it. The plant continues to shed pieces, but not from the part bearing the load.

This does not make it a pillar. It cannot support a roof indefinitely, and the next strong expansion may crush it. Yet such a crown can persist for years beneath a flake that would otherwise have fallen. When it finally breaks, living pieces may remain along both sides of the collapse. Warm air finds a different route. The surviving edges begin to grow into that.

Their shape is therefore partly a record of things that have happened to the building.

A blocked return channel produces thick, dark blades with little space between them. A new crack overhead produces thin blades held almost level, presenting as much surface as possible to the cooler tile. Where a wall has lost a useful mineral, the collar travels along its base as a dull streak, leaving the crown connected to its feeding place by a brittle, hollow strand.

These strands sometimes meet.

There is no immediate merging. Two collars may remain pressed together through hundreds of pulses, each growing more slowly at the contact. Or one may die back. Occasionally the boundary becomes impossible to find. Material laid down on one side continues into the other, and a root that entered the wall beneath one crown begins feeding a blade beneath its neighbor.

Afterward, either crown may die without killing the joined growth.

This makes them difficult to count. A court with three crowns may contain three individuals, or the exposed portions of one old individual whose middle has disappeared. A green fringe at the entrance may belong to something rooted several passages away. The connection need not be thick. It need only remain unbroken at the few places where new material is still being added.

Most of what appears to be a plant is no longer active.

The thick central foot, the coppery underside of a mature fan, the stacked layers beneath a hungry crown: these are retained structures. The living lattice occupies margins, thin internal boundaries, certain channels within the collar. An old specimen can be mostly its own remains and still open at every warming.

There are no sleeping places because closing is not sleep. There is no single chamber in which an individual keeps itself.

During a poor cycle, little happens. The blades move through a small angle. The warm currents carry insufficient material. Some edges lose more than they gain, and the crown becomes lighter without visibly shrinking.

A succession of poor cycles leaves a particular kind of damage. The green retreats from the tips in fine parallel lines. A blade held against the narrow entrance light shows alternating living and dead bands. When stronger heat returns, growth resumes along whichever bands have endured. The new margin is uneven, and remains uneven thereafter.

The city contains very old deprivations, still visible in the fans.

It also contains offspring.

Along a well-fed blade, the outermost layers sometimes thicken into red-edged scales. They are small enough to pass for the dust that coats them. Most will break loose and enter the lanes. They do not unfold while being carried. Their active surfaces are turned inward, protected between two dry faces.

Many end in the central drift. Some are buried too deeply to grow. Some lodge against bare rock and persist without changing. A few reach a clay seam that opens and closes without grinding them apart.

There they begin the gray collar.

The young plant on the black pedestal is one such arrival. Its three blades are widely spaced because nothing has yet struck them, shaded them, or forced them to divide. The red grains at their margins include ordinary mineral dust and a few scales that have not detached. It is already shedding the means of making more of itself, though its foot scarcely spans the split.

Below it, the pedestal remains cooler than the floor for part of each cycle. The crack admits a brief current of hotter air. For now, the difference is enough.

The missing builders may have placed something quite different on those pedestals. Whatever purpose the enclosures had, the young growth does not preserve it. Nor does it erase it all at once. It takes a little from the joint, leaves a little beneath its foot, and extends an edge into the available space.

At the raised corner, the pale fringe withdraws as the heat strengthens. Its retreat exposes a line of older blades behind it, dark and chipped, fitting closely into the passage. Behind those are the gray collar, the hollow roots, a run of mortar loosened almost to powder.

The visible fringe is only the newest part.

When it opens again, one tip will reach slightly farther than before. Another will be gone.
