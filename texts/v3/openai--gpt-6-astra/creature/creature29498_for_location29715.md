The inhabitants have no enclosed place to which they withdraw. Each lives with most of its sensitive tissue spread into the daylight.

The broad leaves are not armor over something softer. Their polished surfaces are living all the way through. Along the copper edges, the mineral mesh becomes so fine that the passage of a shadow changes its conductivity. A trunk can distinguish the shadow of its own crown from that of its neighbor: one crosses familiar paths through the lattice; the other arrives at a slightly different angle, with a different distribution of warmth. The distinction travels downward, through the bands of little plates, to the roots.

There it meets other information. Pressure against a root’s flattened underside. A change in the potential between two buried crystal faces. The minute widening of a contact deep below a terrace.

For these organisms, the sun and the interior of the plateau are equally immediate. A disturbance far beneath the stone can reach a crown before a passing shadow has left it.

Their individuality is easiest to see near the outer edge of a grove. There, a small plant may stand by itself at the end of a branching seam, with a bronze stem and four or five leaves. Its roots have not yet joined those of its neighbors. All the charge it gathers passes through its own tissues. At certain hours a faint flush appears beneath its youngest leaf, a cloudy amber that clears when the sun moves farther west.

Among the mature trunks, the boundaries are less simple.

The joined roots exchange material as well as charge. Transparent compounds move through them in thin films, carrying carbon, dissolved metals, and the substances that keep the mesh flexible. The flow does not maintain a single direction. One crown, brightly lit, supplies the shaded stems beside it; later, when its own root contact becomes less favorable, it receives from them. There are narrow constrictions in the common strip where each plant can limit that exchange.

They do not merge completely. Even after a very long association, neighboring trunks retain different responses to the same small change. One admits current readily and holds its leaves a little warmer. Another passes a disturbance downward through its roots before allowing it into the crown. At their boundary, those differences sustain a faint oscillation.

Much of the grove’s low tone comes from such boundaries.

It is not a call. No distant listener is required. The little plates along the stems flex by amounts too small to see as charge moves between dissimilar tissues. Hundreds of plates, then thousands, reinforce one another through the fitted stone. Between two trunks, a visitor’s skull becomes briefly another resonant object in the circuit.

The plants register that presence.

A body standing in the shade intercepts reflected light and returns heat at an unfamiliar spectrum. Feet make two patches of pressure on the slab. None of this resembles an injury, and there is no dramatic response. The nearest copper margin grows fractionally duller. A few plates change their spacing. The tone acquires a wavering too slight to be sure of.

After the visitor leaves, the grove does not immediately return to its previous condition. Charge remains caught at particular junctions. For a while, the next change in light takes a different route through the trunk.

That is one way an event persists here.

A mature plant contains many such differences. They are not written in growth rings; the trunk has scarcely increased in width. They remain in the preferred paths through its mesh, in junctions that open more readily than others, in an asymmetry between two apparently identical leaves. A crown that looks perfectly poised has arrived at that poise through a long succession of adjustments.

Its youngest leaf is particularly active.

The pale tissue at the tip is loosely organized, with room between the conductive fibers. Material is continually offered into those spaces and withdrawn from them. A little more support forms along one edge, then is reclaimed elsewhere. The leaf remains at the threshold of expansion because the conditions that would favor expansion are almost, but never quite, present.

It is not waiting without doing anything. Maintaining that tender margin is a substantial part of the plant’s work.

Older leaves are maintained differently. Within their deep green surfaces, damaged molecules are separated and brought back into circulation. A fault in the mineral mesh is bridged from within; the faulty portion is dismantled after the new path can bear its load. Nothing needs to detach. There is no season in which a crown must abandon what it cannot afford to keep.

A copper edge may therefore be extremely old without being composed entirely of old material. Its shape endures through countless local replacements. The faint irregularity beside one vein survives because each repair takes guidance from the tissue around it.

The fronds in the hollow follow another way of living.

They do not reach widely across differences in the buried seams. Their short roots occupy a shallow band where the mineral composition changes gradually, and their dense ranks hold a close, even temperature. Each frond receives light on one face and releases a little heat from the other. Between them are narrow passages of warm air, almost motionless but never wholly still.

Their ceramic-looking edges are where most exchange takes place. Under magnification, an edge would resolve into tightly folded tissue, with sheltered surfaces far greater than its smooth appearance suggests. The blue-green color deepens toward these folds. Their chemistry is slower than that of the broad crowns, less able to accommodate a sudden change, but extraordinarily economical.

At the border of a patch, the smallest fronds are no larger than fingernails. Some belong to the established network. Others have roots of their own.

New individuals arise when a buried root forms two working contacts and gradually separates the tissues serving them. For a long time the prospective offspring is only a slightly independent portion of its parent. It begins to regulate its own current. It develops a small reserve of the transparent compound. Eventually a pale ridge appears above the stone.

There need be no decisive instant of birth. Even after its first frond has opened, the new organism may remain connected by a thread that alternately supplies and receives. Separation occurs only when both sides can sustain it. In some places, that condition has not yet been reached.

So there are young inhabitants here, although a person could return throughout a lifetime and never see one grow taller.

There are differences in vigor, too. One trunk holds an ample reserve; another depends on its neighbors through the cooler hours. Some roots have excellent contacts. Others occupy fissures whose two faces have slowly become less favorable. Beneath the apparent uniformity, resources move, connections strengthen, and particular tissues become costly to maintain.

The equilibrium belongs to the country as a whole. Each living thing still has its own work.

In the afternoon, the projecting leaf receives the sun along its copper margin. Current passes inward through the green, divides at the main lattice, and descends through the shaded stem. Deep in the seam, a constriction relaxes. A neighboring plant receives a little more than it did a moment before.

Nothing falls. The pale tip stays pale.

Under the leaf, its sharply bounded shadow rests on the slab while the tissues that hold it there continue their minute, unequal labor.
