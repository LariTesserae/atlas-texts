One of the abandoned houses has a clean threshold.

There is no smoke above it, no drying fiber laid out on the roof. The pale stones leading to its entrance have tipped into the leaves. But something has bitten back the runners around the reed collar, and the cut ends are fresh enough to bead with moisture. Just inside, where a person would put a hand to steady themselves, the doorframe is polished.

The animal that lives there is asleep in the former sleeping place.

She is longer than the doorway is wide, although very little of that length is leg. Her belly rests on the dry stalks. Her forefeet are turned inward beneath her chest, and her hindquarters lie rolled slightly to one side, exposing a strip of sparse, pale fur. The rest of her coat is brown at the roots and gray at the tips. Root dust collects in it. When she gets up, some of the house comes with her.

Her head is broad behind the eyes and narrows abruptly to a soft, divided nose. A fold of skin conceals each ear. She can close these folds against falling debris, but usually they are open, small dark crescents in the fur. Along her cheeks grow stiff hairs that reach farther sideways than her shoulders.

People call her kind a houseback. The name is less accurate outside the settlements, where most of them occupy root galleries, but those are harder places to observe an animal.

She has taken the house’s driest corner for herself. Elsewhere she has made substantial changes.

The old floor bundles have been pulled apart and sorted. Short, broken stalks are heaped near the entrance, where they catch wet dirt from her feet. Longer ones have been dragged into the sleeping hollow. Beneath a peg that once held water gourds is a shallow pit containing beetle casings, pellets of matted wing, and several bright fragments of stone. The stones were not collected for their brightness. They came in attached to grubs, and she has no reason to carry them out.

There are three exits now. Only the largest belonged to the people.

One of the others opens into a narrow gallery behind the house. The third ends under an apparently undisturbed patch of leaves. It is an emergency exit, and she keeps its final covering thin enough to break with her shoulders. In warm weather, young insects gather on the underside. She eats them while doing the maintenance.

Most of her work is done with her mouth.

Behind the soft nose are two pairs of broad cutting teeth. When she closes them on a runner, the lower pair meet just behind the upper pair, slicing rather than crushing. Her jaw muscles give the back of her head its width. She can sever a stem thicker than a person’s thumb, but only after settling herself firmly and working it into the corner of her mouth.

Fine roots she removes with a sideways rake of the teeth. The useful ones are eaten. The woody ones are pushed beneath her belly and kicked backward.

This leaves a recognizable heap outside an occupied house: pale clippings, dark pellets, stalk fragments, all scattered in a low fan. During dry weeks the clippings curl. After rain some of them begin to root, obliging the houseback to clear her own rubbish again.

She does not keep the whole dwelling open. The far chamber, where the roof presses lowest, has grown nearly solid. She has left it alone except for a tunnel at floor level. A wooden bowl is caught somewhere in that wall. Each season a little less of its rim can be seen.

By noon she is awake, though she seldom goes outside immediately.

First she lies with her chin against the floor.

The bare patch beneath her jaw is densely supplied with nerves. So are the broad pads behind her wrists. Pressed against wood, they register movements too slight to lift a hair: the stuttering passage of beetles, a footfall on the ridge, the slow release of a root under strain.

The insect roar does not disappear for her. It is the background against which she feels interruptions. A walking animal briefly loads the roof, changing the vibration beneath its feet. A hunting animal that stops above a gallery may cause less noise than a grazer, but it still makes a small, persistent alteration in the chamber’s sound.

She lifts her head when the disturbance is interesting. If it is close and heavy, she draws her feet beneath her.

Her eyesight is adequate for the rooms in which she lives and poor at the distances offered by the plateau. In full daylight she squints. A person standing still thirty paces away is less distinct to her than the same person’s weight arriving through the floor.

This is why the people stamp before reaching an occupied doorway.

A houseback surprised at close range can open a calf to the bone. Warned, it generally withdraws. Around inhabited houses, a few consistent footfalls become familiar enough that the animal need not retreat at all.

This female knows the woman who cuts the neighboring roof.

The woman has a damaged knee and lowers one foot carefully before transferring her weight. Some afternoons she leaves a split stem outside the old house, its pith exposed and damp. The houseback eats these. She will also take a discarded larval husk or the soft lining of a seed pod.

She does not accept food from a hand. Once, years ago, she did, and took part of a fingertip with it. Neither participant has repeated the experiment.

Her ordinary food is belowground.

She travels with her belly nearly brushing the gallery floor, stopping at branches to lay her chin against a root. Where grazing larvae are active in the ceiling, she rises on her hind feet and braces her back against the opposite wall. Then she peels away a strip of dead fiber with her incisors.

The exposed larvae curl violently. She gathers them with a narrow tongue and eats the surrounding tender tissue as well. A feeding place is usually no wider than her head. If she opens too much roof at once, daylight enters and the remaining larvae move deeper.

Winged insects are caught differently. She pushes her nose among their resting bodies and exhales.

Those nearest her loosen their grip. She sweeps them from the ceiling with one forepaw.

She cannot do this indefinitely. After several breaths the colony becomes restless, and some insects bite. On the thin skin around her nose are dark pinpricks and old white scars.

At night, when dew has traveled down into the mat, she drinks from root junctions. Her tongue fits into the wet angles. She visits these places in a loose circuit, returning more often to some than others. In a drought, she begins her circuit earlier and spends less time feeding.

A houseback’s territory is not a patch of surface. It is a set of connected passages.

Two animals may sleep almost directly above one another and seldom meet. A third, living much farther away, may be a regular rival because the same gallery leads to their drinking places. Disputes happen at constrictions. Each animal turns sideways, presenting the dense fur of its shoulder and rump, and shoves.

There is much open-mouthed clicking. Serious bites are uncommon, but not absent. The old female is missing the end of one ear.

She has raised several litters in the house.

The young are born with closed eyes and disproportionately large forefeet. At first they hold her belly fur. Later they follow her through the passages, touching the hind foot ahead whenever the line stops. Their tails are short and nearly hairless; in a close procession, the mother can feel a youngster nosing beneath hers.

They learn to cut long before they learn what to leave uncut.

For a time, a family’s dwelling becomes untidy in a particular way. Door collars unravel. Sleeping stalks are chopped into useless lengths. Roots that should have been left to support a roof are bitten through, and the mother repeatedly moves the nest away from sagging patches.

Not all the young survive this education.

People sometimes find a small collapsed chamber with a few cut stems protruding from it. They mark the place if it is near a path. Farther out, the plants close over it without assistance.

The survivors leave by stages. First they sleep in side chambers. Then they cease returning every day. One young male remained through most of a cold season, using the old female’s rear exit and avoiding her feeding route. By the next rains he was gone.

She still keeps that exit open.

In the evening she comes aboveground. Her body, so well fitted to a tunnel, looks exposed on the open mat. She moves in short stretches, lowering herself between them. Her feet spread under load, each toe finding a separate purchase among the stems.

She is not a reliable judge of crossings. Her senses tell her what is moving and what is straining now, not what will fail under an unfamiliar weight. Sometimes she reaches the middle of a span, feels it settle, and freezes there until it stops moving. People who know the animals do not follow them blindly.

On firm ground she grazes a little, selecting folded leaves before they harden. She scratches her flank against a pale path stone. Loose fur remains on its rough upper edge, to be taken later by birds.

When the evening calls swell beneath her, she turns toward home.

At the entrance she stops to bite through a new runner. It has grown across the lower part of the opening since yesterday. She holds it with one forefoot, cuts, chews the wet end, and leaves the rest outside.

Then she squeezes through the reed collar. Her shoulders rub the polished frame. For a moment her broad hindquarters fill the doorway, and then the house appears empty again.

Inside, she noses the sleeping stalks apart before lying down. A beetle runs from the disturbance. She catches it, eats it, and settles with her chin on the floor.

Above her, the roof continues to grow.
