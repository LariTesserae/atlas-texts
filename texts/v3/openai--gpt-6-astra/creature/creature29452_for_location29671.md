The people who live here have broad hands, damaged lips, and an exact sense of how much another person weighs.

They learn this early, carrying younger children over crossings. A child rides low against the carrier’s back, inside the outer coat, with its legs secured in a sling. Older children complain about the sling long before they are allowed to travel without it. They want to turn their heads. They want to see what the adults keep looking at.

At first, they are taught only a few things: close your mouth against the cold; put your hand where mine was; when I stop, do not ask why until I move again.

Their first journeys are short. Across a shelter. Around the sheltered side of a knot. Out to the waste pits in dead bark, holding a cord. They learn that a surface can be strong in one place and hollow a finger’s breadth away. By the time they travel between settlements, most can distinguish living wood from dead by the feel of a walking hook pushed into it.

This does not make them fearless. Some never become comfortable on a narrow reach. There are adults who cross on their bellies in weather when children walk upright, and nobody who has carried a body home laughs at them.

Their clothing is made close at the wrists and ankles. Loose hems catch on bark and fill with wind. The common coat has a tough outer face pieced from grazer hide and a lining of combed underfur, which must be taken out and dried whenever there is enough warmth to spare. Frost accumulates in the lining from the wearer’s own body. Left alone, it becomes a cold weight across the shoulders.

A person preparing for a long journey may spend half a morning picking ice from a coat.

They carry little that can roll. Cups have flat sides. Needles are tethered. Knife handles end in loops large enough to admit a mittened hand. A well-made object is often recognizable by the number of ways it can be secured while its owner is doing something else.

Ornaments are small and worn inside. A bead at the throat; a carved plate sewn beneath a cuff; a scrap of colored moth wing pressed between thin layers of resin. When people wish to admire one another’s finery, they sit close and open their clothes.

***

The grazers provide much of what a settlement cannot grow or gather, but the herds belong to no one.

Certain animals can be persuaded to tolerate a pack. They accept it for a season, sometimes longer, and then refuse with a stubbornness that defeats both hunger and beating. A laden grazer may lie down in the middle of a path and hold the bark with all six feet. Sensible travelers unpack it. Stories about less sensible travelers are told while dividing meat.

People recognize individuals by their antlers of worn horn, their gait, the patches where old injuries have changed the color of their fur. They give them names, usually unflattering ones. There are herds in which the names have outlasted the animals: a succession of Split Lips, White Knees, Bad Mothers. Hunters disagree over whether the animals recognize human families in return.

The oldest herd leaders are seldom killed. They know routes that have not been used within a human lifetime. During a bad season, a settlement may abandon its own carefully maintained path and follow a trembling, nearly toothless animal onto a spur everyone had believed ended in emptiness.

Sometimes it does end in emptiness.

The animal stands there for hours, pressing its chin against wood that no longer carries the vibration it remembers.

People wait with it as long as their water permits.

Predators are less easily known. Usually the first evidence of one is a grazer refusing to pass a perfectly sound crossing. Then someone must crawl forward with a mirror of polished resin or a small sheltered lamp and look underneath.

There are specialists for this work. They tend to be quiet people, though not necessarily brave ones. A frightened examiner who takes a long time is preferable to an impatient one.

The underside hunters have pale patches above their eyes, almost invisible in ordinary darkness. In lamplight those patches resemble two hands holding the edge.

***

A settlement survives through several kinds of work that cannot be hurried.

Someone tends its drinking hollow, clearing grit from the lip and watching the roots beneath the ice. Someone maintains the fire rind. Someone remembers which surfaces must not be cut, even when they look dry and expendable.

And someone goes below for the flowers.

In one settlement, built behind a knot that leans farther each year, this person is a woman called Senn. She has a pale scar running from the corner of her mouth into her hair. It was made by a broken climbing hook, not an animal, although visiting children prefer the animal explanation and she sometimes allows it.

Senn works in a harness with three independent lines. She moves one attachment at a time. In her shelter she is untidy: bowls left unwashed, bedding never properly rolled, old lashings kept because they might have another use. Below the branch, she handles every piece of cord in the same order.

The flowers she tends hang in a long fissure beneath the settlement. From above, their presence is betrayed by a warm seam in the bark and, for a few nights each season, a sweetness that makes people stop speaking.

They are large enough for Senn to put her head inside.

Within each cup, the air is wet and surprisingly warm. Fine hairs cover the inner folds. Moths sleep among them, their wings overlapping like pale scraps of cloth. There are smaller creatures too: blind, jointed things that lick the folds clean, and soft grubs that must be removed before they eat into the flower’s thick base.

Senn removes only some of them. Her predecessor taught her how many could be left. She has adjusted the number twice in thirty years.

She harvests the clear liquid held in grooves around the flower’s throat, never draining a cup completely. She gathers shed hairs, which make good dressings, and cuts small pieces from flowers that are already closing. Cooked slowly, the flesh loses most of its bitterness. Children like the tender inner layers. Adults pretend not to have a preference.

The settlement calls the flowers its garden, though Senn has never succeeded in making one grow where she wanted.

Her work is chiefly to keep access to them: easing roots apart, scraping dead tissue from the fissure, discouraging a burrowing colony from filling the warm chambers with dust. Some years the garden shifts farther underneath. Some years a flower opens beyond the reach of her lines and stays there, visible and useless, until it withers.

She is angry about these flowers in a way she is not angry about storms.

***

Water is kept under guard, but the guard is usually also the person who serves it.

Visitors announce how many vessels they carry before approaching a pond. They place the empties where they can be counted. Filling is slow, with a narrow ladle, and there is ample time for questions: where the party has come from, what has broken, whether the herd beyond the fork still has young.

A host is expected to give enough water for departure. This is a serious obligation, and people sometimes conceal the arrival of travelers from neighboring shelters to avoid sharing it.

There are generous settlements with poor ponds and wealthy ones where a guest’s cup is filled just below the customary mark. Their reputations travel farther than most of their inhabitants ever will.

Children play at water keeping. They use husks for cups and dust for water, and their invented travelers are outrageously thirsty. Arguments over the rules can occupy an entire sheltered afternoon.

Adults gamble on less imaginary things: when a leaf will turn, whether a distant drone will deepen before the next sap warming, how many animals will pass before the first calf. Gambling debts are not enforceable during a retreat. This exception has produced a considerable body of argument about what constitutes a retreat.

They have songs, especially for work done out of sight of one another. A bridge crew sings short verses with long responses so that a missing voice is noticed quickly. Good singers are valued, but a reliable singer who cannot hold a tune is still useful. Elaborate ornament is discouraged when someone is hanging below the work.

At home they sing differently.

***

Senn has a brother on a neighboring reach.

For much of their childhood, the two reaches lay close enough that people crossed between them on a woven bridge. Then a new growth lifted one side. The bridge tightened, narrowed, and finally had to be cut before it tore out its anchorage.

That was nineteen years ago.

The brother’s settlement is sometimes visible: a little smoke caught against a black slope. Reaching it now requires a journey trunkward and out again, through two uncertain crossings. Senn has made the journey three times. Her brother has made it once. Neither considers the other sufficiently willing.

In still weather, their settlements exchange signals by striking suspended plates of dry heartwood. The messages are limited. Safe. Trouble. Travelers coming. A death.

For anything complicated, they must wait for a person to walk around.

Senn has a niece she has never met. She knows the girl is tall and has trouble with one knee. Last season a traveler brought a length of cord the girl had braided, fine enough to pass through the eye of Senn’s smallest hook.

Senn uses it for nothing. It remains wrapped inside her coat.

***

Age is difficult here.

An old person may know more routes than anyone else and be unable to reach the doorway without help. Settlements keep the infirm near the most stable part of the shelter, where growth has slowed and the floor needs fewer adjustments. Beds are made low. Cups are tethered to the bedframe.

There is work for weak hands: separating fibers, checking knots by touch, listening to a child repeat the turns of a route. Still, there are bad seasons when care exhausts the living. People remember these seasons selectively. Family accounts do not always agree.

Senn has begun to lose strength in two fingers. On warm days they work well enough. In the cold they remain curled after she releases a rope.

She is teaching an apprentice, a young man who dislikes moths. He endures their feet on his face with a rigid expression that makes her laugh. He is better than she is at finding rot beneath an apparently sound attachment, and worse at leaving things alone.

For now they descend together.

Above them, the settlement is audible through the wood: a floor being wedged level, a child stamping grit from a shoe, the uneven blows of someone preparing fuel. Below, a flower is opening. Its outer folds have separated, and moisture shines along the curled lip.

They stop beside it while the apprentice tests his next hold.

A moth works its way out between the folds. It is larger than his spread hand. For a while it clings there, drawing its wings clear one slow section at a time.

Senn reaches past him and releases the strap he has caught beneath his knee. He checks the buckle, embarrassed, then looks back at the moth.

Neither feels the small change in the air that makes it let go.
