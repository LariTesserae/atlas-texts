The woman who painted around the fern has a bad opinion of that particular fern.

Its roots are entering the bolt socket. In another year someone will have to lift it out, scrape the cavity, check the collar for corrosion, and settle it somewhere else. She could have done that before painting. She had the tools with her. But the new frond was coming through, and the weather had been dry, and it is difficult to move something so small without taking more of its home than it can afford to lose.

Her name is Eda. She is fifty-six, and she maintains the exposed metalwork between the western screens and the third reservoir stair. This is a smaller territory than it sounds. It is also deeper than it sounds. There are pieces of it she reaches on her hands and knees, and others she visits in a harness, descending beneath paths where her neighbors are going to buy breakfast.

She has a broad face, closely cut gray hair, and one shoulder that hangs a little lower than the other. When standing still she rests her left hand in the crook of her right elbow. The nails on that hand are clean. Those on the other retain dark crescents of primer, however she scrubs them.

Her work clothes are pale because dark cloth becomes unbearable against the outer walls. The knees have been patched with a slick material cut from an old irrigation sleeve. Small things cling less readily to it: burrs, metal dust, the hooked feet of the beetles that drop from the lattice when she disturbs it. A brush rides in a closed tube at her hip. Loose brushes catch on everything.

She does not look like someone who spends her life above an abyss. She looks like someone considering whether a latch is going to be troublesome.

***

Eda lives in three rooms behind a bakery’s exhaust shaft. The arrangement makes the front room too warm in the afternoon and the back room pleasant for sleeping. Her father used to claim that the shaft warmed the apartment for free. Her mother pointed out that they paid for this warmth whenever they bought bread.

Both are dead now. Their argument survives in the family.

The apartment contains a table that cannot pass through the present doorway. It was brought in before the wall was rebuilt, and its ownership has therefore become largely theoretical. When Eda’s marriage ended, her husband took the chairs and left her the table. For several months she ate standing up. Then a neighbor gave her a low stool, and eventually she acquired three mismatched chairs. She now tells the story as though the chairs were the important part.

Her daughter lives six levels below, in rooms with no direct sun. They disagree about this. Her daughter likes the rent, the quiet, and the fact that the water arrives with enough pressure to wash her hair whenever she wants. Eda brings her cuttings that grow long and pale beside the window. The daughter keeps them alive out of affection, which both women find irritating.

At home, Eda grows very little. A pot of chives. A sour-leaved herb for soup. One flowering plant inherited from her mother, which occupies the best light and produces three enormous, disagreeable-smelling flowers each year.

Outside her bedroom, an entire vine belongs to somebody else.

This is a common arrangement. A plant may be rooted in one household’s box, trained along a public wire, fruit over a second household’s balcony, and shed its leaves into a gutter maintained by a third. Rights to its harvest may be perfectly clear. Responsibility for its blockage rarely is.

Eda knows which arguments can be settled with pruning shears and which have already outlived the plants that caused them.

***

Most of the people here have a second way of locating an address.

There is the official number, painted beside the door, and then there is the practical description: above the hot stair, behind the dead lift, where the evening water turns, under the pear tree that never fruits. A stranger can follow the numbers and arrive on the wrong side of a wall thick enough to contain a workshop.

Children learn routes before they learn the district’s shape. Their earliest maps are sequences of temperatures and sounds. Warm tiles, dark passage, bright bridge, the grille that smells of onions. They know where to hold an adult’s hand because the floor changes under their shoes.

By six or seven, most can distinguish a freight lift from a passenger lift by hearing it through a wall. They learn that a stopped fan requires attention even when the sudden quiet is pleasant. They learn never to put a finger into a drainage hole to retrieve something.

They still put their fingers in.

Eda has a small selection of hooked tools whose uses include retrieving buttons, toy wheels, and once a milk tooth wrapped in thread. The child had been told to throw the tooth somewhere a bird would find it. The family tradition had not anticipated drainage mesh.

She is good with children in short encounters. She speaks to them seriously, gives them things to carry, and sends them away before they become part of the work. Some mistake this for a great desire to supervise them all afternoon.

She corrects that mistake.

***

The blunt-faced animals behind the elevator door know her.

They are about the length of her forearm, with low bodies, deft forefeet, and tails that look naked until sunlight reveals their fine gray fur. Their ears fold backward when they push through a narrow gap. The older female has a pale scar across her nose. She sits just inside the broken door seal, where only her muzzle and front paws can be seen, and watches the traffic.

Locally they are called cupboard pigs, though they are neither pigs nor reliably confined to cupboards.

They eat fallen fruit, beetle larvae, soft roots, eggs when they can get them, and the paste used on certain older paper labels. They have a particular appetite for the glue on repair notices. This accounts for some, but not all, of the elevator’s administrative history.

Eda does not feed them. She says this often, especially to the person responsible for inspecting the fruit terraces.

She does sometimes open her lunch tin and discover that a piece of fruit has become too soft to keep.

The animals recognize the click of the tin. They also recognize the scrape of the long-handled catch cage, and vanish at the sound even when it is being carried somewhere else. Their young make a dry, impatient chatter behind the door. In the warm season the family sleeps separately. When cloud encloses the district, they heap together in a nest of stripped cable fibers and stolen laundry lint.

They are troublesome. They damage seedlings and squeeze through spaces intended to exclude them. Once, one chewed the soft casing around a moisture sensor, with consequences that occupied four households for most of a night.

Eda helped catch that one. She held the cage steady while it struck the mesh with its small hands, furious and frightened. She disliked the task without thinking it unnecessary.

There are people here who would close every unused cavity. There are others who regard any cavity already occupied as somebody’s home. Most change their position according to what has entered their kitchen.

***

Eda’s oldest friend repairs pump controls. They meet twice a week on a seat near a boiler chimney, where he complains about his work and she complains about hers.

He has begun to lose his hearing. At first he concealed it, guessing from context, laughing a little late. Now he wears a receiver against the bone behind his ear. Some frequencies reach him clearly; others arrive as an unpleasant blur. He says the district sounds cheaper than it used to.

When they sit together, he places two fingers on the bench’s metal bracket. Through it he can feel the pump cycle beneath them. Eda has learned to wait until the vibration eases before speaking.

They are not particularly wise. They repeat themselves. They hold grudges against people who have moved away. They have spent eleven years discussing whether to share an apartment, and the discussion has settled into the same comfortable category as replacing the reservoir clock.

On days when her shoulder hurts, he carries her paint.

She does not thank him every time. He would find that alarming.

***

There are wealthy people in the district. They have rooms where the sun can be admitted or excluded by choice, lifts that are rarely out of service, and enough growing depth for trees whose roots need not be trained flat.

There are households that move their bedding away from a sweating wall each morning. There are workers whose only sight of the cloud is during the walk between shifts. The gardens above them remain beautiful. Beauty does not distribute the light evenly.

Eda’s work takes her across these differences, sometimes through a drawing room to reach an outer balcony. She carries clean overshoes for such visits. She has been offered excellent coffee in rooms whose occupants would never consider her a guest.

She likes the coffee anyway.

What she dislikes is being told to remove her safety line because it is scratching the sill.

She can be embarrassingly loud about that.

***

Her fear of heights is specific.

She can hang beneath a terrace with the clouds moving under her boots, provided she knows the anchor and has checked the line. She cannot comfortably watch someone lean backward in a chair beside a parapet. A child standing on a planter will interrupt her speech halfway through a word.

In sleep, she sometimes dreams that a familiar path continues one step farther than it actually does. She wakes with her legs rigid. Then the bakery starts its first mixer, the shaft trembles faintly behind the wall, and she goes back to sleep.

She has never seen the foot of the structure. Neither has anyone she knows well. She has seen drawings, most of them concerned with loads and access routes, with large blank regions where no information was needed by the people who made them.

As a girl she wanted to go down and look.

She still would, if the journey were practical, if her shoulder were better, if someone she trusted could take her section for long enough. The wish has become one of the things she keeps without finding a use for it.

***

Late in the day she returns to the outer lookout to collect the little barriers set around her work.

The paint is dry. The fern has cast a fine, forked shadow across it.

She crouches with some difficulty and examines the socket. Then she takes a pencil from her breast pocket and notes the bolt number in a book swollen by years of damp. Beside it she writes that the collar needs inspection after the next wet cycle.

A green bird lands a short distance away. It tilts its head toward the open tool bag. Somewhere behind her, the freight carriage starts along the reservoir, each rail joint sounding in turn.

Eda puts away the book. She closes the bag before the bird can investigate it.

On her way home she will buy two small rolls, although she needs only one. She will pass the broken elevator door. Later, from her warm apartment, she will call her daughter to ask whether the pale cuttings have made any new leaves.

For now she presses a thumb against the paint at the edge of the repair. It comes away clean.

She lifts the barriers, hooks them together, and carries them back through the garden.
