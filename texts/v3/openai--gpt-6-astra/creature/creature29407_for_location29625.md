The people have broad, ordinary faces, and do not all resemble one another. Some are narrow through the shoulders, some heavy; some seem made almost entirely of elbows until they grow older. Their skin darkens where their clothes leave it exposed. Across the backs of their hands, pale scars remain visible from quarry chips, split wood, the sharp edges of certain grasses.

Their feet are especially expressive. A person seated on a porch will feel for a patch of sunlight with one foot while continuing a conversation. Children grip the edges of paving stones with their toes. Those who work in the saturated gardens have thickened soles but tender skin between the toes, and sometimes sit in the afternoon with their feet carefully separated over a warm slab. There is no dignity lost in doing this. There is considerable indignity in pretending a foot is sound and then dropping a load because it hurts.

They wear woven plant fiber, mostly in lengths that can be opened out to dry. A working garment is wrapped short or tucked up; in the square the same cloth falls below the knee. Some people favor the pale color of washed fiber. Others wear browns, dark reds, or a green that fades unevenly, leaving the folds darker than the exposed surfaces. A good garment lasts long enough to acquire a geography of repairs.

Hair is tied, cropped, braided, left loose. People who split roof boards cover theirs against the splinters. People who grind grain shake flour from it at the end of the day. A young man in one of the farther neighborhoods spends a great deal of time arranging his hair to look as though he has just pushed it out of his face. His sisters find this inexhaustibly funny.

They are not a silent people. The town is simply too large to be filled by their voices.

At the roofed working platforms, several conversations can proceed at once. Someone is stripping stalks against a stone edge; someone is teasing apart fibers; someone has brought work that could as easily have been done at home. News moves by these unnecessary journeys. A woman crosses two bridges to borrow a tool she knows perfectly well her neighbor owns, because she wants to hear whether the storehouse keeper’s son has returned.

They sing at repetitive work, though not all work accommodates singing. A drain must sometimes be listened to. A roof board must sometimes be struck and the sound considered. But fiber can be twisted to a song, and grain pounded to one, and the long walk back from a distant plot can carry a verse through several changes of singer.

There are loud households and quiet ones. From certain courts, people emerge already apologizing for the noise behind them. In others, visitors lower their voices without knowing why.

***

Their food is substantial, varied within limits that they do not experience as limits.

They eat steamed grain and crushed grain cooked thick enough to slice. They eat roots roasted in covered pits, green leaves folded around fillings, tender stalks peeled with a thumbnail. Sourness is valued. There are jars whose contents are kept alive by being fed, stirred, divided, and given away; a household may be known for a sour paste whose beginning nobody remembers.

Some fungi are cultivated in bundles of cut stalks beneath roofs. Others are scraped away with disgust. Children learn the distinction before they are trusted to fetch food alone.

Oil is precious enough to be noticed, not so precious that ordinary people never taste it. A little on hot food changes the mood of a meal. At gatherings there are seed cakes, crisp at the edges, which people praise while reaching for another.

Without flies, food may stand uncovered on a clean mat. Without livestock, no surplus can be walked elsewhere on its own legs. Everything gathered must be carried, stored, eaten, traded, planted again, or allowed to return to the ground. The town’s wealth is consequently heavy and particular: jars, baskets of seed, stacks of dry boards, lengths of sound cloth. A rich person has people checking the undersides of things.

Damp is the great thief, but it is not an enemy they expect to defeat. A wall is opened. A stack is shifted. The bottom layer is sacrificed before the loss reaches the rest.

There is an old storehouse worker who can tell from the smell at a doorway whether a bin needs emptying. She has trained three successors and trusts none of them. This is partly because they are careless and partly because she dislikes having become replaceable.

***

The waterworks are maintained by people, not by an undifferentiated civic wisdom.

There are apprentices who scrape too much from a channel because clean stone looks more finished. There are experienced workers who know precisely which film of growth can be left alone. There are arguments over whether a settling basin should be cleared today or after the next grain delivery, and whether a householder’s new doorstep has sent water into the adjoining court.

An inspection may begin with four people and end with twenty. Most of the additional people contribute opinions. One brings food. Someone eventually fetches the person who understands the problem, who arrives irritated at being interrupted and becomes interested as soon as she kneels.

The most respected water workers are not necessarily the people who speak in the hall. One man, whose adjustments are trusted across three neighborhoods, is almost incapable of explaining himself before an audience. Asked to defend a new branch channel, he turns red, repeats its width, and sits down. Later, beside the channel itself, he can show a child exactly what he intends.

Water rights are remembered through objects and places as much as through statements. A cut in the side of a stone marks an agreed height. A flat piece set crosswise belongs to one arrangement; turning it lengthwise belongs to another. Witnesses matter. So does the inconvenient survival of an old person who remembers what everyone actually agreed.

They can be selfish about water. A gardener may quietly deepen an inlet. A household may leave its own channel beautifully clear while pushing debris into the common drain. The offenses are small enough to be denied and consequential enough to produce feuds.

Their precision has not made them equitable. It has given them ways to say exactly what has been taken.

***

Children occupy the settlement differently from adults.

The great distances between neighborhoods are not empty to them. They contain a warm stone, a place where a channel vanishes, a stand of stems that can be bent without breaking, a bridge under which an adult cannot sit upright. Children know where voices carry strangely and where a person can lie below the level of a path without getting wet.

They float slivers of wood in the drains. They build little barriers and are made to remove them. They carry water from one pool to another for reasons too elaborate to explain when challenged.

A child’s first useful task may be watching: waiting beside a small opening until water reaches a particular joint, then calling out. Most children take the responsibility seriously for several minutes. Their elders have differing opinions about how much can safely be entrusted to the next several.

There are no animals to bring indoors, torment, tame, or mourn. Affection finds other attachments, not exact replacements. A child may own a plant that everyone else must avoid stepping on, or a collection of stones distinguished by qualities invisible to visitors. Children are fond of younger children until the younger ones become inconvenient. A cracked bowl can acquire a name and remain a member of a household long after it has ceased to hold anything.

Their invented creatures do not have to resemble things that exist. On scraps of board they draw people with branching arms, walking houses, faces underneath the ground. Sometimes a figure has too many legs simply because the drawing was enjoyable and they did not want to stop.

***

The adults also make things for which usefulness is an insufficient explanation.

A post is carved on the side facing a wall, where almost nobody will see it. A grinding stone is set into a border of alternating pale and dark chips. Someone has spent weeks fitting a lid to a box that holds three small objects of no public significance.

In the evenings, stories are told about journeys, disputed inheritances, people who returned too late, people who returned so unexpectedly that everyone had to put away what they had been saying about them. There are comic accounts of incompetent builders. There are stories about a person who could find dry ground in complete darkness, which sensible listeners understand differently at different ages.

They argue about the beginnings of the town.

Some families claim descent from the people who laid the first drains. Others say those drains were merely improvements to channels already there. The oldest houses do not settle the question. Their stones have been moved; a lintel can outlive several walls. When a foundation is opened, people come to look at what lies underneath, though the discovery is usually gravel, water, and another stone.

The sharp mountains are old enough to have been watched by everyone’s grandparents. Whether that makes them old is a question on which there is no agreement.

They have names for distant places, and not all those names refer to places anyone living has seen. A few travelers arrive along the basin’s margins carrying seed, stone, cloth, or information whose value takes time to establish. The town is spacious enough that a stranger can be visible for half an hour before anyone knows whether there is reason to be excited.

***

Age changes a person’s range before it changes their place in the household.

First the quarry road becomes an excursion rather than a shortcut. Then the square is visited only for something worth the walk. Eventually a bench beneath an eave becomes the point from which the wider settlement is known.

People bring things to these benches: work to be inspected, babies to be held, grievances that will receive a more favorable hearing there than at home. Not every elder becomes wise. Some become freer with an old malice. Some, relieved of tasks they never liked, grow unexpectedly cheerful.

The dead are carried beyond the seepage ground to dry fissures in the higher rock, wrapped in cloth that their families can afford to lose. The journey requires help. People who have quarreled may carry opposite ends of the same poles without speaking.

Afterward, practical problems arrive with indecent speed. Someone must finish the work left beneath the platform. Someone must know which seed was meant for which bed. A daughter discovers that she cannot reproduce a familiar taste because her mother measured an ingredient with a hand she no longer has.

The town goes on containing the dead in these incomplete skills.

Yet most days are not occupied by origins, deaths, or the careful distribution of water. Someone wants a different house. Someone wants to be admired. Someone has decided that the pale band around a neighbor’s doorway is exactly the wrong width, and cannot cross the court without looking at it.

On a mild afternoon, a woman carries a stool out of the shade and sets it against a warm wall. She has a shallow basket of stalks to split. Across the court, a boy is practicing the same few notes on a hollow stem.

She tolerates this for some time.

Then she tells him to learn the rest.

He says there is no rest; he made it himself.

She looks up at him, considers the claim, and moves her stool a little farther into the sun.
