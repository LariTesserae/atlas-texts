The woman watching the crossing has a sore between two toes that will not close.

She sits with that foot lifted into the dry air, turning a stone flake between her fingers. Her other foot rests on the black ledge. Every so often she stops turning the flake and presses her heel down hard, as though testing the firmness of a fruit. She is not listening for the deep knocks. Everyone hears those. She is feeling for the small, repeated movement that sometimes comes before them: a faint lifting, too regular to be an animal passing.

Her name is Eda. The people in the next shelter call her Eda of the Steps, although the steps broke away several years ago. Her youngest child has never seen them.

She is not old. Salt has whitened the hair at her temples and collected in the folds of her outer wrap, making her look older at a distance. Close to, her face is brown and rather narrow, with a thick lower lip split down the middle. She keeps touching the split with her tongue. When she laughs it opens again.

Beside her is a bowl of thin bone pins. She makes them while she watches.

Almost everything the people wear is pinned rather than tied. A wet knot dries stiff here; a knot repeatedly soaked in brine can become something that must be broken with a stone. Their wraps overlap loosely under the arms and fasten at the shoulders, where a person can reach them. A soaked garment can be stripped off without being pulled over the head. Children are taught to do this before they are allowed near the pools.

Eda’s pins have blunt points. Sharp ones work through a hide too quickly.

She exchanges six pins for a vessel of good water, or ten if the vessel is large. Sometimes she accepts food. Sometimes she refuses an exchange because the person offering it owes her already. She remembers these debts with an accuracy she does not bring to dates.

There are twenty-three people staying on her island, unless the two men at the western fissures have decided not to come back. Nobody considers that especially likely, but one has taken his best scraper.

The island is smaller than it was.

On its southern edge, the pale ground has risen almost level with the sleeping ledges. A child can now step from the stone onto what was, until recently, a steep bank. The plated grazers have discovered this. At night they come up in patient files and move through the space beneath the shelter, rasping at the mineral deposits on the supports. Their plates brush the hanging bedding.

The people could move. They discuss moving most evenings.

The objection is not simply that the shelter took work. Beneath the projecting ledge is a ceiling hollow that yields water with very little salt in it. Drops form slowly along its coolest edge. Eda’s grandmother enlarged the catch bowl, and her mother made the covered channel that keeps dust out. The water tastes of stone and old clay, but it does not leave the mouth thirstier than before.

No one wants to surrender that hollow.

So the bedding is raised another hand’s breadth. The lowest stores are carried up. A sloping slab is levered loose to make the approach less convenient for the herds. The people go on discussing a move.

They have many kinds of water and few reasons to confuse them. There is water for drinking, water for washing a wound, water for softening a hide, water in which food can be steeped, and water useful only for cooling the outside of a sealed pot. The distinctions cannot all be seen. A clear pool may be worse than a cloudy one.

Children learn the vessels first. A wide-mouthed shell with a notch in its rim is never a drinking vessel, even when empty. This is enforced more sharply than most rules. A child who breaks a bone pin may be given the pieces and told to find a use for them. A child who drinks from an unmarked bowl is seized and made to spit.

Their thirst has a domestic sound: lids lifted, lids replaced, a pebble rattling in a vessel too light to contain much.

They eat the plain’s animals, though not indiscriminately.

The plated grazers are caught at constrictions, where a low barrier will turn the crowded outer lane into a shallow pit. A pit must be emptied quickly. Those at the bottom can be crushed by the weight of arrivals, and then the little joint-feeders get among them. People sorting a catch work with their wrists wrapped. The parasites will explore a human sleeve as readily as the overlap of a shell.

The best flesh lies around the grazer’s leg roots. There is little of it. Much of the body is gut, and the gut must be lifted whole.

Eda is quick at this work. She opens the underside with two cuts, hooks a finger beneath the dark digestive sac, and sets it aside without spilling the cream-colored contents. Her eldest son breaks the plates and wastes meat. She can recognize his discarded shells without seeing him eat.

The translucent pool animals are richer. Their throat sacs are removed before cooking and turned inside out over smooth pegs. Small sacs become covers for small vessels. Large ones are carried folded in a pouch as emergency water bags, although they need care and seldom last long.

The people do not regard every useful thing as precious. Torn sacs accumulate behind shelters. Broken plates lie underfoot. A tool handle may be used for years and then thrown impatiently into a crack because its owner has pinched the same finger once too often.

They are untidy where they can afford to be.

Their most carefully kept possessions are usually soft: a piece of hide that has not stiffened, a wrap brought from above, a length of cord made from something that grew leaves. Such cord passes through many hands before it is finally cut. Eda has a short green strand sewn inside her bedding. The color is almost gone, but she remembers it brighter.

She has been to the surface twice.

She disliked the wind more than the open sky. It would not let her hear what was near her. She remembers holding a bowl with both hands because the air seemed capable of taking it.

Her brother stayed above. Occasionally an object arrives that he may have sent: worked wood, a little lump of dark fat, once a copper hook wrapped in a leaf that had become brittle. There is no reliable message with these things. The people who bring them have met other people, who met him somewhere else. Eda asks whether he still limps. The answer is generally yes, even from those who cannot describe his face.

She keeps the hook in a clay box. It is too small for most of her work and too valuable to risk losing.

Not everyone who lives below was born there. One of the men on the island came down as an adult and still looks upward when a distant bank collapses. He knows he does it. The others sometimes imitate him.

He is a good pot-mender. This matters more.

Children born on the plain grow into its difficulties unevenly. Some become good route-finders. Some are quick at catching fliers. Some never learn to judge a crust and must be shouted back from places their younger companions avoid. Familiarity does not make them proof against the ground.

Eda’s youngest, a girl called Seli, likes to peel the thin white edges from newly hardened blisters. Beneath them are chambers no bigger than fingertips. Sometimes they contain a soft scavenger curled around its eggs. Sometimes there is only warm moisture and the print of something that has left.

Seli carries the empty chambers home when she can detach them whole. She has arranged seven on a ledge above her mother’s bedding. Eda considers them dirt but has not thrown them away.

The girl is not permitted to gather them alone.

This morning she is beside the crossing, trying to make one of the warning shells hang properly. A shell must swing freely, but it must not touch its neighbor when only a grazer brushes the stake. She has shortened the cord too much. Now it hardly moves.

Eda watches her struggle for a while before calling her over.

There are lessons that can be given from a ledge, and lessons that require getting down. Eda puts her sore foot into its loose sandal and descends.

At the stakes she stands still first.

Seli stands still beside her.

They wait through the rattle of a herd passing beyond the first ridge. Pale fliers settle along the top of a nearby slab, their folded wings almost invisible. One crawls across Eda’s wrist. She blows it off rather than shake her hand.

“Now,” she says.

Seli presses her bare heel against the ground.

She feels nothing. She says so, reluctantly.

Eda moves her half a pace to the left. Here an older piece of crust lies beneath the powder. It conducts the tremor better. Seli’s face changes.

The movement is slight: a lift and a release, then another lift.

Eda takes the pin from the girl’s shoulder, loosens the shell cord with its blunt point, and gives the pin back. They retreat to the black stone. There is no cry of warning, because nobody is on the crossing.

For several breaths nothing happens. Seli begins to look disappointed.

Then two shells touch.

The stake nearest the pool leans toward its neighbor. Its base rises, still gripped by a piece of ground the size of a sleeping mat. A wet line opens underneath. One shell taps three times against another and hangs still.

Eda waits until the water stops trembling. She waits longer than Seli wants her to.

Then she sends the girl for a longer cord and a second bowl. The crossing will need to be moved, but for a little while there is good water coming out beneath the broken crust.
