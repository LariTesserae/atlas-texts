The animal comes out from beneath the stone backward, bringing a little of the pool with it.

First there are two hind feet, pale-soled, spreading against the clay. Then a flattened tail, blunt at the end and creased along its sides. The body follows reluctantly. For a moment it seems too broad to pass through the gap, but the ribs draw inward, the wet skin folds, and the animal eases free. Water runs from beneath its belly. It rests with its head still in the shelter, as though there were something left inside to attend to.

It is about the length of a person’s forearm, though most of that length is body. Its legs are short and set well apart. Above, it has the gray-brown color of submerged bark, interrupted by small cream patches that resemble places where the bark has rubbed away. The underside is nearly transparent at the throat. When it breathes, a pair of dark structures moves behind the skin.

There are many of its kind in the basin, but rarely many in sight.

They live at the edges of things that scarcely have edges: where a submerged slab rises enough to leave a finger’s width of air beneath it, where a mat of roots holds wet leaves above the mud, where the shallowest part of a channel remains damp after the water has withdrawn. Their broad bodies fit these low spaces. An adult can spend the bright part of a day under a stone that would not look large enough to conceal anything more substantial than a crab.

Its head is wedge-shaped, with a wide mouth underneath and two small eyes looking outward from the upper corners. Along the lower jaw hangs a row of soft, uneven fingers. These lie flat when it walks. In water they open.

The animal backs the rest of the way out and turns. One forefoot remains planted while the others step around it. It does not raise its belly clear of the ground.

At the pool’s margin, it puts its chin into the water.

The fingers beneath the jaw spread over the silt. Each bends separately. A worm withdrawing under a leaf touches one, and the head shifts a fraction to follow. Nothing happens for several seconds. Then the throat expands, the mouth opens, and a small cloud of sediment passes beneath the face.

It feeds this way for much of the evening. Worms, insect larvae, minute crustaceans, softened fragments from the undersides of leaves: whatever can be drawn in without taking too much clay. Occasionally it lifts its head and expels a compact gray pellet. The pellet falls almost where the mouth has been.

Larger prey often escapes it. A small fish can remain beside its cheek with little danger, provided it does not become trapped against a root. The animal is capable of a sudden thrust, but afterward it must rest, throat working, feet loose in the mud. In these warm shallows a meal can cost more breath than it is worth.

Its eyes are useful mainly when something moves against the sky. A passing dragonfly makes it pause. The shadow of a wading bird flattens it completely.

Under that pressure, it changes shape. The ribs widen, the limbs fold, and the tail presses sideways against the body until the outline becomes an irregular patch no thicker than a sodden leaf. It can remain like this for several minutes. A bird that has already seen it may still take it. The skin is slippery, but there are scars on many adults: a missing foot, two pale punctures across the back, the tail ending earlier than it should.

This one has a notch in its right forefoot. When it spreads the toes, water shows through the gap.

By dusk it has worked around to the brown rim of the pool. Here another animal lies with its head beneath a tangle of sedge roots. Only the hindquarters are visible. The first touches its tail with the fingers of its jaw.

Neither moves away.

For a while they remain in contact. Then the concealed animal pushes backward, forcing the newcomer to shift one foot. They settle beside each other, their flanks touching along a narrow seam.

They are not a pair in any lasting sense. They have shared this shelter before, and each carries the smell of its particular mud, its old leaves, the mineral seep beneath the slab. A stranger would be met differently. The resident would stiffen its forelegs and pulse its throat against the bottom, making a sound too low to travel far through air. In the water it can be felt several body lengths away.

Other throats would answer from beneath nearby stones.

Their gatherings are loose and unequal. The largest animals occupy the shelters that keep both water and air through the longest part of the year. Smaller ones use root mats, abandoned burrows, or shallow hollows they scrape for themselves with their hind feet. A good shelter may be occupied for many seasons. Its entrance becomes smooth. Pale feeding pellets gather downstream of it. Beneath the roof, repeated contact wears a greasy-looking patch in the fine deposit on the stone.

When the flood begins, they do not leave immediately.

Water enters their shelters by degrees, first wetting the upper back, then closing the little space above the head. They turn upward to breathe wherever a pocket remains. Sometimes several noses meet at the same trapped bubble.

Eventually they emerge.

Across the flooding clay they travel in short stages, swimming where the water permits and walking where it does not. The tail, unimpressive on land, opens into a low paddle. Their movements are slow enough that silt settles between strokes.

They do not always find higher ground. A familiar rise may already be submerged. An animal will circle it, touch the drowned shrubs, climb briefly among their lower branches, then descend again. During a long flood, some survive wedged in vegetation with only their nostrils above the surface. Some vanish into channels from which they do not return.

Breeding takes place as the water draws back.

The eggs are laid beneath stone, in a single layer rather than a heap. Each is fixed to the underside by a short, tough strand. Looking into an occupied shelter from the right angle, one would see a small ceiling of cloudy beads, trembling whenever the parent moves.

The adult stays beneath them. It does not guard with much force; it pushes intruders away with its head and eats the smaller ones. Its more important work is to move water. Through the hottest hours it slowly lifts and lowers its body, sending a thin exchange through the shelter. When the pool becomes too shallow for that, it presses its wet back against the eggs.

Some clutches dry anyway.

The young hatch narrow and almost clear, with feathery structures behind the head and tails much longer in proportion than those of the adults. For a few weeks they gather in the light. They hang beneath floating leaves and graze the green films on stone. A whole brood can occupy the shallow impression left by a large bird’s foot.

As they grow, they spread out. Their bodies broaden. The external feathers shrink. They begin putting their heads into cracks.

The animal with the notched foot has eggs beneath the slab it left earlier. After feeding, it returns across the same stretch of clay, stopping twice with its jaw lowered to the ground. At the entrance it turns and backs inside.

For a moment its face remains visible in the low opening. The throat rises and falls. A minute crustacean walks over one forefoot.

Then the body lifts beneath the stone, and a faint cloud of stale water comes out into the pool.
