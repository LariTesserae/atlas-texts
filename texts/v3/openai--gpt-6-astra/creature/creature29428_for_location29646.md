At close range, the inhabitants are not as slender as they appear on the balconies. Their clothes hang away from the body, suspended from stiff seams at the shoulders and hips. Inside this small private shade they are broad through the ribs, with soft abdomens and long, muscular forearms. The loose fabric hardly moves when they walk. A person crossing an avenue seems to carry a piece of the stillness with them.

Their feet are wide. Most wear shoes with separate soles under the heel and the front of the foot, joined by a flexible upper that leaves the arch unsupported. Indoors they take these off. The underside of a bare foot is dark and finely ridged, like the gripping surface of a tool.

They can feel the ground adjustments before the channels fall silent.

In a crowded room—not that rooms are often crowded—you would see the knowledge travel through them. Someone stops rubbing a mark on a cup. Someone lifts a child off the floor. A conversation continues, but both speakers have spread their toes inside their shoes. Then the water tips, and everyone has already found something to lean against.

This sensitivity is not equally developed. Children sometimes announce an adjustment when a heavy vehicle is only passing underneath. Older people complain that certain new floor coverings make them deaf.

Their faces are expressive in ways a visitor learns unevenly. The mouths are broad and mobile, the eyes protected by heavy upper lids. There is a little folded area of skin below each ear that opens when they are hot or distressed. A person may keep an entirely composed face while these small folds stand out on both sides of the neck.

It is rude to watch them.

Young people cover them with ornaments. Thin metal clips, colored membranes, little fringes that tremble conspicuously when the wearer is trying to appear unmoved. The fashion is periodically condemned as unhealthy. It returns in each district under a different name.

They have hair, though not much of it: dense strips at the back of the head, softer growth along the forearms, sometimes a dark down over the shoulders. The hair catches the drifting flower strands. Before entering a house, people brush one another with the backs of their hands. This is an ordinary courtesy, performed between neighbors who may have nothing else to say.

A close friend is allowed to touch the folds beneath the ear.

The city is quieter than its inhabitants prefer.

Inside homes there are little machines that click, wooden pieces hung where a sleeve will knock them together, bowls with loose rings around their bases. Kitchens are deliberately audible. A stirring implement scrapes a ribbed vessel; a lid settles in three successive notes. Some households keep a recorded murmur running through the meal, not quite speech and not quite water.

People disagree about these sounds. Neighbors complain. Someone who has chosen a distant apartment for its silence may discover that the household arriving two floors below has brought an old mechanical floor polisher, a device whose noise is apparently half its purpose.

The abundance of vacant buildings does not prevent these disputes. There are reasons to want a particular building. Good condensation. A reliable stairway. A garden with edible stalks rather than purely ornamental leaves. A bedroom whose windows cease holding yesterday’s weather by the time one wakes.

And neighbors.

They will put up with a great deal to remain near the right neighbors.

Most households occupy several rooms connected through a garden, rather than through an internal corridor. To go from a sleeping room to a kitchen one passes beneath wet leaves. There are sheltered routes for illness and heavy rain, but ordinarily the garden is part of the house’s circulation. A person returning late must ease past stems that have lowered across the path since morning.

The plants recognize repeated handling, though no one claims that this is affection. They leave a little more space where a particular shoulder passes. They turn collecting surfaces away from a habitual seat.

After a death, the household has to train them again.

This work is usually done by someone outside the family. It requires walking the old routes at slightly different heights, holding leaves aside, shifting a chair a finger’s breadth every few days. Relatives find it difficult not to restore everything. Sometimes they ask the gardener to leave one passage unchanged.

Food comes chiefly from the green envelopes of the buildings and from the roofed reservoirs. There are folded leaves with fatty white interiors, strings of dark fruit that must be heated until their skins loosen, cakes made from a fine underwater growth. A common street food is a length of jointed stalk split open and filled with a hot, sour paste. People eat it while walking and return the empty stalk to a rack outside the stall.

They like crisp things. Crispness is expensive in the warm air. Shops prepare little brittle sheets behind sealed counters and hand them over in packets to be opened immediately. Children put whole pieces in their mouths so that the first fracture can be heard through their cheeks.

The flat creatures beneath the water channels are not eaten.

Nor are they quite pets. Every building has people who tolerate them and people who remove them. A careful householder lifts one with a damp strip of cloth and carries it to a public channel; an impatient one scrapes it loose with a utensil. The animals remember disturbances well enough to become elusive.

Occasionally a child persuades one to live beneath the rim of a cup. It will remain there for months, feeding on a film the child cannot see, then vanish during a ground adjustment. Cups are left upside down near drains long afterward.

Children have more territory than they can use.

They play in the finished intersections of districts without buildings. They know which crossings will change for a thrown shoe, which service doors make a hollow sound, which handrails carry voices farthest. The space does not make them solitary. They gather stubbornly in the same few places, leaving kilometers of prepared pavement untouched.

Their favorite games involve arrivals.

One child sits beneath a station roof while the others approach from different directions, pretending to be strangers. There are elaborate rules about who may be recognized and when. A player who turns too soon must go back beyond the nearest crossing. Sometimes the waiting child closes their eyes and identifies the others by their footsteps.

Adults passing this game often slow down. They are not always aware that they have done so.

Actual arrivals are rarer and less orderly.

A transit car comes in with more passengers than it took out. A household registers in a district where no one remembers seeing its moving carts. People appear from the long roads connecting the faint settlements on the horizon, carrying luggage whose grips have been repaired many times.

They are met practically. There are offices, available rooms, medical examinations, arguments over credentials. New residents are taught to wait through an adjustment before stepping onto unfamiliar stone. They are given maps with certain passages printed in a different texture.

Questions about where they have come from are not forbidden. Answers can simply be difficult to use.

One newcomer describes a neighborhood whose name is familiar but whose layout is impossible. Another recognizes a tower and becomes angry when told how long it has stood there. Most soon learn which details interest their listeners and which bring the conversation to a halt.

At meals they are asked what they like to eat.

This, at least, usually leads somewhere.

The people who work at the edge of the city wear close-fitting clothes and carry very little in their pockets. Nothing must dangle into a seam. Their gloves have rigid backs and bare fingertips. Even in the warmth they cover their necks.

An edge crew includes surveyors, channel fitters, someone qualified to judge the stiffening of the stone, and usually a gardener. The gardener may spend an entire shift without doing anything. Then a pale root rises through a join, and everyone else has to wait.

They examine it without putting their faces directly above it. They note the branching, the moisture on its surface, whether it casts one shadow or several under the work lamps. A shelter is brought. The drain may have to be moved.

Some roots wither inside their shelters. Some remain unchanged for years. Some push up, slowly lifting roofs designed to come apart without resistance.

Workers develop attachments to particular ones. A person whose skill lies in fitting drainage seals may take a long detour home to inspect a growth no thicker than their wrist. They tell their household that it has developed another pore. Their household tries to remember how many it had before.

The trees are more troublesome.

Under an established red tree, the inhabitants do not seem to find counting any easier than a visitor does. They use marked table places, numbered cups, reservations entered before anyone arrives. Nevertheless, these are desirable meeting places. The shade is comfortable, and people who are tired often feel better after sitting there.

There are stories about an extra person at a table. Usually the extra person is a relative, or resembles one, or is remembered afterward by only half the company.

The municipal advice is to avoid carrying hot liquids around the trunk during an adjustment.

People repeat this advice as a joke until they have an occasion to need it.

An old channel keeper in the southern district eats her midday meal under one of the smallest trees. She brings two portions. This has attracted attention, which she resents. If asked, she says that she gets hungry later, or that the second portion is for an animal, or that the questioner should find useful employment.

She is a heavy woman with almost white hair and a damaged fold beneath one ear. Her hands are steady. When a building fails to draw water upward, she can often locate the fault by resting her wrist against its wall.

At the tree she unwraps both portions and waits until they have cooled. Sometimes she eats them both. Sometimes she wraps one again.

Before leaving, she brushes flower silk from the air above the opposite seat, at about the height of someone’s shoulder. Then she gathers her tools and goes to whichever building has called for her.
