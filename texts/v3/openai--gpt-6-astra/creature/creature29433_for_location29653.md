The people who live here are not especially quiet, but they know how to leave a sound unfinished.

A woman bargaining for fish stops halfway through a price when something knocks beneath the platform. The fish seller stops too. There is another knock, lighter, and the scrape of a chicken finding its footing on a brace. The woman resumes at a lower price, as if the interruption has strengthened her position.

They listen this way without turning their heads. A footstep on the water crossing is different from a footstep on the bridge. Water running freely beneath a house has a different pitch from water beginning to collect. Children acquire these distinctions before they can explain them. They also learn which sounds they may ignore, and occasionally make the wrong choice. Someone is always calling a name twice.

Most people have strong calves, small scars on their shins, and an habitual way of putting a hand out as they pass a post. Even carrying nothing, they touch things: a railing, a trunk, the corner of a stall. Older people remember when a familiar handhold was thinner. At one turn in the stair, a woman still reaches toward the place where a branch was cut away three years ago. Her grandchildren tease her for it. Then one of them, hurrying in the dark, reaches there too.

Their clothes are repaired most often at the shoulder, where carrying straps rub, and at the cuffs, which never quite dry. A good basket is fitted to its owner. Its load sits high enough to clear the steps and close enough not to pull a person backward. People recognize one another from below by the bottoms of these baskets, by a replaced rib or a dark patch of binding. A boy sent to find his father comes home saying he saw the basket, which is not the same thing.

The father is usually elsewhere.

He has a small terrace beyond the uppermost houses and a reputation for making excellent handles. He has also been trying to build himself a chair for six years. Whenever he finds suitable wood, someone needs it for something more pressing. This has become a joke he dislikes only when other people make it. Behind his sleeping mat he keeps two curved pieces nobody knows about.

There are private ambitions here, though there is very little private space.

One girl wants to go down to the river and stay long enough to see what it looks like at night. She has been there twice, both times on errands that required starting back before noon. She imagines a room where no one walking past can look in from above. Her brother, who has spent a season in the valley, tells her about heat, biting insects, and the expense of rice. She asks whether the river is loud. He says it depends where you sleep. This answer occupies her for days.

Another child wants the knife sharpener’s work, chiefly because he likes sparks. The sharpener gives him a blade and makes him remove the rust. After an hour the child goes away. He returns the next afternoon.

The sharpener himself is losing his hearing. He conceals it badly, answering questions about delivery with remarks about the weather. Customers have learned to stand where he can see their faces. His wife says his hearing is excellent when someone mentions payment.

He knows the birds above his bench individually. One lands on the crosspiece before entering the nest; the other goes straight in. He can feel their movements through the roof frame when his hand rests on it. In nesting season he will not let anyone lean a long tool against that corner, although he complains daily about the mess.

The woman who clears leaves from the drains is feared by almost every child and consulted by almost every adult.

She was not appointed to the work. She began doing it because the people who should have done it did it badly, and over time the distinction between her opinion and her authority disappeared. She knows what will lodge in each opening. Broad leaves must be caught whole; the little tough ones can pass unless they arrive with grass. A scrap of woven sack is worse than a branch.

She also keeps sweets in a tin that once held lamp oil. They taste faintly of the tin. Children accept them solemnly and complain out of earshot.

When her knees trouble her, she sits above the channel and directs a nephew with the hooked stick. They argue about every movement. He is faster than she was and less thorough. Both facts irritate her. Sometimes, after he has gone, she lowers herself painfully to check his work. Sometimes she finds nothing to correct.

No one here is self-sufficient. This does not make everyone generous.

There are disputes over diverted water, borrowed knives, the shade cast by a new roof. One household has been accused for years of extending its platform a finger’s width whenever it replaces the outer poles. The household denies this. Several people have measured. No two measurements agree.

A woman who will share her last dry kindling refuses to lend a particular cooking pot. A man who grumbles whenever the bridge needs work arrives first with the best rope. During heavy rain, people move a disliked neighbor’s sacks uphill alongside their own; afterward, the dislike remains.

They have long memories for both favors and embarrassment. A grandfather is still known among his contemporaries for falling through a latrine floor when he was seventeen. His present dignity has made the story more valuable, not less.

The animals know these people less by reputation than by sequence.

One dog follows the fish carrier as far as the first stair, then takes a shortcut and waits beneath the stall. Another sleeps through the entire market but rises when a certain basket is set down: its owner sells cooked food wrapped in leaves. The chickens gather around anyone scraping mud from a tool. The goats know which footsteps bring foliage and begin pulling at their tethers before the person appears.

Rats keep different hours in different houses. Where an old man wakes often and feeds the stove, they work in short visits. Beneath a family that sleeps heavily, they take their time. They nest in places people cannot reach without dismantling something useful.

There are smaller residents whose lives scarcely enter anyone’s notice until they become inconvenient. Ants travel inside a split bamboo rail, sheltered from nearly all the rain. A spider replaces the same section of web whenever a shutter opens. Pale larvae inhabit the rotten timber enclosed by the living bridge roots. The bridge is becoming stronger around their mouths.

Under a loose floor pole, a frog occupies a hollow barely larger than itself. For weeks a child leaves dead flies beside the opening, hoping to make it tame. The flies disappear. The frog never emerges while the child is watching. Eventually the child loses interest, but still lifts a foot over that particular pole.

By evening, the village’s inhabitants sort themselves into smaller companies.

People draw stools close to cooking fires. Someone works a thorn out of someone else’s heel. A mother smells a sleeve and sends its owner back to wash properly. The apprentice sharpener rubs a blister with his thumb, pleased by it until hot water touches his hand.

Outside, the dogs exchange places on the steps. Birds settle with brief, irritable movements beneath the roofs. The drain keeper’s nephew goes out once more with the hooked stick, taking care not to pass her window.

In one house the man unwraps the two curved pieces of his chair. He sets them upright, estimates the distance between them, and sits on a grain sack to test the height. For a little while he remains there, hands resting where the arms will be.

Then someone knocks and asks whether he has a length of dry wood.
