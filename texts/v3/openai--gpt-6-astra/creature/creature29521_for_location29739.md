At first, the differences between the plants seem more important than their arrangement. The narrow leaves belong to something tough and economical. The broad, felted leaves suggest a species that once had to contend with drying wind. The cushions on the floor have the dense, blunt growth of plants from exposed ground. Brought together here, they appear to have retained the habits of several separate climates.

It is only below the leaf litter that their separateness becomes uncertain.

The pale cables are not all roots. Some divide in the ordinary way, becoming finer until they disappear among grains of soil. Others narrow, pass beneath a stone, and widen again on its far side. They have a smooth, almost polished skin. Where one enters a root, there is no wound or swelling: the two tissues become continuous over a length no greater than a fingernail.

These connections occur throughout the hollow. A cushion near the entrance joins a broad-leaved plant in the alcove. A stem growing upside down from a ceiling seam is connected to something beneath the central bed. Several plants have no independent root system left. Their old roots remain as dark, hollow sleeves around newer tissue.

What lives here is most easily encountered as a garden.

That is not quite the extent of it.

Beneath the central bed, alongside the buried machinery, is a flattened mass with the color and faint translucence of a peeled chestnut. It conforms to the space available between two stones. On its upper side, roots and fungal threads enter through small puckered depressions. Its underside is bare, resting in damp grit.

There are other masses like it, smaller and farther apart. One occupies a crack behind the glass tray. Another has spread into a thin sheet under the entrance sill. The largest may be inaccessible, farther back in the wall, though the visible connections do not establish this with certainty.

None has a recognisable front.

Their surfaces contract very slowly. Over an hour, a fold may deepen enough to hold a drop of water that would previously have run off. Over a week, a narrow passage may open through one mass and close in another. Fine particles travel within them, suspended in fluid too viscous to flow readily at this temperature.

The inhabitant has no rapid circulation. Resources move by stages. A little fluid is drawn into a chamber, held there, altered, and passed onward. Some journeys take a season.

The plants do much of its living for it.

Their leaves receive the small disturbances of the room: a droplet, a settling stone, the touch of another leaf. Their roots locate dampness. Their dying tissues provide minerals and spaces into which new growth can extend. The fungal partners distribute what the buried system supplies, but they also carry substances between the plants and the pale masses. A change at the entrance can eventually be found in the alcove.

The response need not resemble the event that caused it. Pressure against the sill may result, days later, in a leaf stalk shortening on the other side of the hollow. A prolonged dry interval may cause a joint beneath the bed to swell.

There is enough delay that, to a visitor, almost everything appears to happen without cause.

Its most immediate sense is contact.

The leaf folds are crowded with hairs finer than the silver nap revealed by a lamp. Where two leaves rest together, these hairs bend and remain bent. A shifting load changes which of them bear pressure. As older stems soften underneath the floor cushions, the pattern alters across a wide area.

In this way it can register the wall settling.

It has been doing so for a long time.

Along the lower stones are places where living tissue once lay and has since withdrawn. They remain as faint, branching stains beneath the newer growth. Elsewhere, a pad has thickened into a crevice that did not exist when the pad began. These adjustments are too small to arrest the wall’s movement. They are sufficient to keep the connections from being pinched through.

At the entrance, the sheet beneath the sill has become thin almost to transparency. Its substance has been moved inward. The low plants there now grow sideways before turning their leaves into the room.

The remaining clearance is partly the result of this retreat.

Whether the inhabitant understands the entrance as a way out is doubtful. It has never, in its present form, needed to pass through it. Yet it maintains a distinction between the opening and other gaps in the stone. Growth advances more cautiously there. Fallen leaves close to the frame soften and collapse sooner than leaves elsewhere, leaving a shallow depression under the slab.

Something about the passage is still being kept available.

A visitor affects it unevenly.

The first changes come from warmth and breath. Moisture gathers on the nearest surfaces. A few exposed leaf tips become briefly more flexible. Deep in the bed, where the temperature scarcely changes, a chemical difference arrives through the fungal threads.

Touch has a longer consequence. A hand laid on a cushion compresses a hundred overlapping contacts into a new arrangement. Once the hand is removed, most return to their previous positions. A few do not. The inhabitant takes days to accommodate the discrepancy.

There is no part of it that looks back.

But after a person has spent an hour kneeling in the hollow, a slight change can persist in the leaves that faced their body. The stalks relax by a fraction. The leaves sit farther apart. The space remains altered after the frost from their breath has gone.

This may be no more than a response to an unusual source of moisture. It would require repeated visits, carefully spaced, to distinguish that from anything else.

Repeated visits would also change what was being observed.

The glass tray lies within one of its older arrangements. Under the mineral crust on the rim are the remains of several fine root tips, all of them directed inward. The living root that crosses it follows almost exactly the path of a dead one below.

When thaw water collects, the inhabitant takes it slowly. First the dust darkens. Then a translucent film advances down the inside of the glass, too thin to see except in slanting light. It reaches the water and thickens at its edge.

By the time the tray is dry, the film has withdrawn.

There may once have been a reason for supplying water in that particular place. The inhabitant retains the means to receive it.

The marker is more difficult to account for. Its buried edge rests against one of the pale cables. Fine tissue has grown into the irregularities on its underside, holding it firmly despite its tilt. The leaves around its face are younger than the leaves immediately beside them. They are replaced more often, while still small.

The indentations remain partly exposed.

No corresponding behavior attends the exposed corner of the machinery. Roots cover it as readily as they cover stone. Whatever relationship began here has outlasted any visible division between equipment and habitat. The inhabitant uses the system without keeping it clear, and the system continues to work while being engulfed.

There is no obvious cycle of waking and sleep. Activity passes through different regions at different times. The entrance plants may be nearly dormant while tissue behind the tray is growing. Beneath a motionless bed, a narrow connection may be reorganising itself around a newly loosened pebble.

It is also difficult to assign an age.

Some of the living parts are weeks old. Some have occupied the same fissure for longer than the mineral deposits have been accumulating on the tray. Between them lie tissues repeatedly consumed and rebuilt. A connection can survive the replacement of both its ends, then itself be replaced while the newer ends remain.

The continuity is local, carried forward a little at a time.

If there is memory here, much of it resides in arrangements that have proved worth making again: a receiving surface inside the tray, a sparse patch beneath the entrance, a particular sequence of connections through the bed. Damage can erase these. Slow repair can preserve them. Nothing in the hollow guarantees the difference will always be maintained.

For now, the new leaf above the tray is being supplied through an older stem whose center has already begun to whiten. The two are pressed together. Along the line of contact, the fine hairs on their surfaces have interlocked.

As the new leaf widens, that line will move toward the old leaf’s edge.

Eventually the older leaf will lose its hold and sink a little way into the foliage. The new one will remain above the glass, its lower margin curled toward the place where water sometimes gathers.
