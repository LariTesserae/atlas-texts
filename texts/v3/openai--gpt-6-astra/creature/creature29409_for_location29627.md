The smallest inhabitants have backs like the skin that forms on cooling metal. Seen edge-on, they disappear. Seen through the salt, they are cloudy little interruptions, each with a dark speck at one end and a fringe of limbs folded beneath it. Their eyes are not directed toward the passages. They look into the mineral.

An eye close to the surface shows what this requires. Its pupil is a slit with several corners, fitted to the branching fracture immediately before it. Behind the pupil lie other apertures, successively smaller, each turned a little from the last. The animal seems equipped to look a considerable distance into something opaque.

Its mouth is wider than its head. Two transparent lips rest on opposite sides of a seam. Between them, very fine teeth enter the salt without visibly chipping it. There is no powder beneath the mouth, no hollow where it has fed. Nevertheless, the body is full. Orange light reveals a crowded interior: knots of clear tissue, dark granules, a long pearly organ bent back on itself. Along this organ are constrictions that suggest swallowing.

One constriction lies immediately behind the jaws. Another is nearly at the far end.

There is no reliable way to establish which came first.

The animals beneath the ceiling are easier to count and harder to examine. Each hangs with its polished abdomen toward the room, its forelimbs held in a gesture of small, exact readiness. The tips of those limbs divide into brushes. Between the brush hairs, even in this dry chamber, something gleams.

Their throats look soft enough to bruise. The indentation in each is about the size of a thumb pressed into wax. It deepens the amber color at its center.

Up close, their shrilling separates into several pitches. Move your head a few centimeters and one pitch vanishes so completely that you become aware of the pressure it was exerting behind your eyes. Another takes its place. There are narrow positions between the columns where your teeth stop aching, though the sound is no quieter.

The hanging creatures occupy these intervals with extraordinary precision. The gaps between them are irregular, but no gap is casual. A large individual may hang alone over a shelf while six smaller ones crowd a span of pipe. Their bodies do not touch. Their sounds evidently do.

Some have lost limbs. One has a split shell exposing a pale wedge of flesh, and the rim of the split is as clean as a broken cup. Injury has not removed it from the arrangement. Its throat bears the same indentation. Its remaining forelimb is extended at precisely the angle of its neighbors’.

Below them, the armored animals seem almost sociable. They lie flank to flank around the compressor feet, pressed into the shallow spaces beneath pipes. Their plates vary from the color of unglazed porcelain to a brown so dark it appears wet. On the older-looking ones, each plate carries smaller plates, overlapping backward toward the head. Nothing is worn smooth.

Their eyes are tucked underneath the front edge of the armor. You have to crouch to find them: four or five on either side, unequal in size, all looking outward past the mouth. The effect is of an animal watching patiently from under furniture.

A feeding individual holds its prey with surprising delicacy. Its combs pass between the smaller creature’s limbs. No limb has been torn off. The prey’s body is stretched, translucent in places, and its own mouth remains fastened to a flake of shale.

There are several such arrangements. A small animal feeds on stone while a larger one feeds on it; beneath a ledge, something almost entirely concealed has inserted a white tube between the larger animal’s plates. All three are full-bodied. None shows the slackness of a husk.

The relationships are intimate without being peaceful. The held animal’s feet are braced. Its jaws have bitten deep. Every part of it appears committed to getting away, and every part of its captor to keeping it.

This is one of the chamber’s ordinary postures.

The pale creature behind the compressor has fewer plates than its empty shell seems to possess. Its new body is narrow, almost delicate. Several limbs are still gathered inside the old forelegs, as fingers might remain inside a glove being pulled off. Between the two bodies stretches a thin, shining membrane.

Its exposed eye looks outward. The eye covering on the discarded shell looks slightly down.

The pencil marks measure a point on its shoulder. Whoever made them must have reached past the hot cylinder and rested a hand against the wall. There is very little room. To see the dates, you must place your face close to the animal’s new limbs.

Those limbs have tiny hooks at their ends. They are open.

The copper-colored growths have their own inhabitants. In the dark cups at their tips sit pale beads, each marked with a minute black crescent. Under magnification, the crescent would perhaps prove to be a mouth. Without it, there is only the impression that every cup contains something leaning over its rim.

The needles nearest the broken conductor are thickest. Their surfaces carry straight, bright lines, too regular to resemble veins. Farther from the wire the growth becomes threadlike, then granular, then indistinguishable from the green corrosion on the insulation. It is difficult to decide where the plant ends. A length of intact cable has a faint ribbing beneath its sheath.

The broad animal overhead is visible mostly by the things it excludes. No hanging creatures occupy its part of the ceiling. No mineral blades descend through it. Its margins follow the shale, lifting here and there into narrow pockets in which smaller bodies are lodged.

What look like ribs are flexible supports running from the mouth toward the outer edges. Between them stretches a dull, close-textured surface, gray in one pocket of light and flesh-colored in another. The exhaust duct enters a muscular opening without touching the rock behind it.

The mouth has no obvious teeth. Its lip is thick and folded, with small orange bulbs reflected along the moist inner edge. Around it, the skin is drawn inward.

There is no swelling to indicate a breath received.

Nor is there any looseness to suggest it has stopped receiving.

A second duct, almost hidden behind the first, ends several centimeters short of the ceiling. Around its open end is a ring of smaller animals with their heads raised. They have arranged themselves where the broad one cannot quite reach.

From the shelter, these details become difficult to hold separately. The black shells return to shadow. The mouths disappear against the ore. The pale animal is a glimmer beyond the compressor, and the ceiling animal becomes the ceiling again. Yet the chamber does not become empty-looking. Every available darkness seems occupied to its proper depth.

The stool has been placed where a seated person’s knees would fit between two shelves. Its feet rest in four shallow sockets in the floor. Beside it, within comfortable reach, the ledger lies open.

There is room here for someone to sit, to take a temperature, to make a mark, to listen. The position is almost sheltered from the shrilling. Heat reaches it evenly. After the difficult passages, it looks thoughtfully arranged.

You would only have to move the ledger.

Under its lower edge, a row of transparent toes grips the stool.
