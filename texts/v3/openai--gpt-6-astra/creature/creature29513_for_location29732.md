The first part of the resident you are likely to see is a foot.

It appears on a threshold after the rain: narrow, pale, with four blunt toes and a fifth that turns backward into the doorway. The nails are translucent and worn almost to the quick. It looks less like something arriving than something uncovered by a receding tide.

There is no leg above it.

For several minutes the toes flex against the wood. Small animals approach, taste the skin, and withdraw. Then the backward toe tightens. The whole foot slides beneath the rounded lip of the doorway, into a gap that would not admit a leaf.

A little moisture remains. Before it dries, the threshold grows imperceptibly smoother.

There are many such feet in the town. They do not necessarily belong to many residents.

***

At first, it is reasonable to suppose that a large animal lives inside the walls.

You can follow signs of it. A pale tendon crosses the bottom of a gutter. Something like a shoulder presses against the inside of a translucent patch of bark. Deep in a dwelling, beyond several aligned openings, a flank rises and falls with the slow, distracted rhythm of sleep.

But the flank is on the wrong side of the room to connect with the shoulder. The tendon contracts several seconds before either moves. And when you find the place where the animal ought to be thickest, there is a clean, empty chamber with a little afternoon light on its floor.

The resident does not have a body that can be uncovered by taking the town apart.

Some of its parts are beside one another. Others are joined through the same accommodations that let a hunter enter an alcove and leave by a distant drain. A joint may include a passage. A length of gut may go around a corner without occupying the corner itself. What looks like an exposed ankle can be very far from its own knee.

The animal is continuous. The town is not obliged to show you the continuity.

Even so, it has habits, appetites, sore places. One of the feet favors its outer edge. A strip of skin beside a basin bears the white stippling of old bites. When the afternoon is particularly dry, the resident becomes irritable and moves too often, sending the little bark-colored processions scattering from the thresholds.

It is not the plant. It can hurt the plant, and the plant can crowd it.

They have lived together long enough to make most disagreements look like architecture.

***

Its skin is the color of the interior of a seed.

In wet weather it becomes almost clear. Beneath it are fine vessels, fat arranged in small overlapping cushions, and bundles of muscle that turn sideways and disappear without narrowing. It has sparse, stiff hairs, most often found around openings. Each grows from a little dimple that can rotate to aim it.

These hairs do not respond much to touch. A beetle can climb one without disturbing it.

When a call arrives from the far terrace, however, an entire row turns at once.

The resident listens with almost all of its surface. The tremendous sound of the town gives it something like a map, though not a map of streets. It hears which rooms are close today. It hears a passage becoming longer, a hollow closing around an old injury, the brief opening through which a mouth could reach water.

The loudest animals are useful to it, but they do not call for its benefit. They are advertising, threatening, finding mates. The resident attends to their quarrels as another animal might attend to light.

Occasionally it tests a route by tapping a nail against a threshold.

The answer may come from beneath its own skin.

***

There is a head, although searching for it is a poor way to meet the animal.

It keeps the head in cool places, often on the underside of a chamber that is warm above. You might find it by lying beside one of the pools and looking under the basin’s rim.

At first it resembles a folded piece of wet leather. Then one edge separates, showing a lip furnished with fine inward-pointing bristles. Above this are two shallow pits. A third, deeper pit lies farther back, where an ear might be.

The eyes are very small. They can look into different rooms.

It has no use for a commanding view of the square.

The mouth is broad, soft, and surprisingly particular. During the brief rain beneath the membrane, it extends a fringed tongue along the edge of a basin. The tongue sorts what the water has brought: pollen, sloughed tissue, eggs, exhausted flyers, the minute rich bodies that breed in the overhead veins. Most of the water passes through the fringe. Selected particles are carried inward by rows of tiny muscular nubs.

The resident also grazes. There are patches of tender plant tissue that it returns to, keeping them raw with careful bites. The plant thickens around these patches but does not seal them. In exchange—or simply as a consequence—the resident’s saliva prevents a certain pale fungus from spreading into the living grain.

It hunts when it needs to.

A foot placed carelessly in a lane may remain there all morning. Grazers gather in its shade. A white hunter arrives for the grazers.

The toes close.

For a while afterward, one of the distant throats beneath the windowsills makes a low swallowing sound.

***

The empty rooms are important to it.

Not because it needs room to turn around. It can turn around through a crack. But the arrangement of its body depends on certain hollows remaining open.

Watch a green shoot grow across an unused doorway. Until it reaches the middle, nothing happens. Then the resident begins to twitch in places all over the terrace. A heel withdraws. A listening hair turns flat. Far below the paving, something pulls with enough force to raise dust from the grain.

If the shoot continues, two portions of the animal that have rested comfortably beside one another may cease to be adjacent.

This is painful.

Usually the browsing animals eat the shoot first. Their young shelter in folds of the resident’s skin, and their droppings feed the plant. None of this requires an agreement. The relationship is maintained by appetite, irritation, and the survival of arrangements that do not tear themselves apart.

The square matters most.

There are no feet in its center, no visible flank, no mouth. Yet an obstruction placed there changes the resident’s posture throughout the town.

A fallen branch produces hours of uneasy adjustment. A large object left overnight may be found surrounded by moist, finger-wide impressions, although no one has seen anything approach it.

The resident cannot carry every obstruction away. Sometimes it must persuade the plant to grow around one. Sometimes it abandons a whole terrace.

The resulting houses still look habitable. Their steps remain, and their recesses shelter rain. But the thresholds roughen. The noise becomes thinner. Eventually leaves begin to project into the rooms.

***

For much of the year there is one mature resident.

Then there may be two, without any evident arrival.

The newcomer first appears as a difference in timing. A toe responds too quickly to a vibration. A basin is drunk from before the usual mouth reaches it. Two rows of listening hairs turn toward one another.

The animals can overlap extensively without touching. They can also touch in a room where neither is visible.

Courtship is an awkward business of making space.

One lowers a bridge. The other lifts a lip of skin from a distant wall. For a night, a familiar passage becomes easy to cross in one direction and nearly impossible in the other. Their calls are too low to distinguish from the town’s ordinary concussions, but loose water gathers into beads and rolls toward the same doorway.

Not every meeting is welcome.

After a fight, there may be bruising along three streets. A severed toe can lie in the square, solid and ordinary and much heavier than it looks. The victor eats it if the little hunters do not get there first.

***

The young are easier to understand.

They are born compact, usually in a wet recess behind the lowest terrace. Each is about the length of a hand, with a blunt head, a long soft belly, and too many closely crowded limbs. Folded skin lies in ridges down its sides.

For a few days it can be seen all at once.

It crawls badly. Its backward-pointing toes catch on everything. It eats the amber jelly deposited around it and listens with hairs that are still soft enough to bend under a droplet.

Then it begins to explore the recess.

A forefoot enters a seam and appears above its head. The young animal startles, bites the unfamiliar toes, and cries out. The cry comes from much farther away than its small body should permit.

Learning takes time.

Some passages will carry a foot but not a shoulder. Some are available only while the lane is flooded. Others look open and lead nowhere the animal can survive. The parent remains near the nursery in a way that is difficult to demonstrate: an adult mouth may be drinking on the upper terrace while the youngster sleeps against the warm underside of its jaw.

Gradually the folds along the young animal’s sides open. Its limbs cease to crowd one another. There comes a morning when you can see its head and hind feet but cannot find the middle.

Soon afterward, it leaves.

A few return. Most find hollows elsewhere, or fail to.

***

An old resident moves less.

The town continues growing, and routes that once came easily require effort. Parts of the animal remain in the lower streets after those streets have begun to compress. Its skin darkens where the wood holds it. A foot may spend a season working free.

This is when the plant grows the shallowest steps.

The resident rests its head near running water. Browsers clean the folds around its eyes. It still listens, but sometimes answers calls that have not been made for years.

By evening, when the high membrane becomes visible, it draws what it can of itself toward the central hollows. The terraces shift under the redistribution of its weight. Water inclines toward its mouths.

The membrane expands.

For a little while the old animal can hear every part of itself.

The brief rain follows. It drinks, and the young surface creatures come out to feed around its lips. A foot uncurls on a polished threshold. Somewhere beneath the square, a painful joint settles into a position it remembers.

There is still nothing standing in the open.

The resident is resting at home.
