At first, the animal is easiest to find by what does not settle on its back.

The floating mats collect everything: fallen leaves, fern dust, empty insect skins, the small brown flowers shed by the trees along the channels. But here and there is a patch that remains dark. Rain strikes it differently. A leaf resting on it gradually slips sideways, although the water beneath the mat seems still.

Then two nostrils open at one end.

They are set close together on a slight rise of flesh, barely higher than the surrounding roots. Each takes a separate, shallow breath. The dark patch swells. For a moment the mat is lifted enough to show pale root tips beneath its edge, and then the animal lets its breath out and sinks a little, without quite disappearing.

There are many of them in the basin, though a person could cross it and see none.

They are broad, soft-bodied amphibians, the largest about as long as a grazer’s foreleg. Much of that length is head and trunk. The tail, which is deep and muscular in the young, becomes a short, heavy rudder in adulthood. An old animal swimming in open water looks incomplete, as if something ought to follow it. Most of the time it does not swim in open water. It pulls itself through the underside of the marsh.

Its forefeet are large. Four long fingers spread from each wrist, with flexible tips that can curl around individual roots. The hind feet are shorter and broadly webbed. Under a mat, the forefeet do most of the work: take hold, draw the body forward, take hold again. The hind feet steer and occasionally push. This leaves very little disturbance at the surface.

On land, the same arrangement gives it an awkward, deliberate gait. It plants its hands widely, lifts its chest, and draws the rest of itself between them. The belly often remains in contact with the ground.

That is useful. The belly is one of the ways it knows where it is.

Its underside is pale gray, almost translucent between the folds. Across it run rows of small, densely sensitive patches. As the animal moves, these register warmth, pressure, and the faint movement of water through the turf. The hands investigate ahead. Before crossing a bare stone, it touches the surface with one finger, then another, sometimes withdrawing so slowly that the refusal is almost invisible.

It can tolerate warmth that would distress most of the plateau’s other animals. It cannot tolerate every spring. Individuals with shortened fingers are common.

The upper skin is thicker, dark brown flecked with greenish gold, and puckered into shallow ridges. On the shoulders these ridges stand apart. Along the flanks they collapse into loose pleats, which spread when the animal inflates its lungs. Out of water, it can look narrow and folded, then suddenly broad enough to fill the hollow in which it rests.

It breathes through both lungs and skin. Neither alone is sufficient for long in the warm, thin air. Even at rest there is work to be done: water must pass over the softer surfaces beneath the limbs; the nostrils must reach air; the body must not become so warm that its needs outrun what either route can supply.

For this reason, the richest feeding places and the safest resting places are seldom the same.

At night the animals enter the warm channels where small crustaceans, insect larvae, and translucent worms feed among the growth. They hunt mostly by touch. A hand spreads over gravel and remains there. Something moves against the palm. The fingers close loosely, making a little enclosure rather than a fist, and the head comes down between them.

Their mouths are wide but not especially strong. They suck prey out of weed and soft sediment, leaving pale, round clearings that the dark birds examine in the morning. Small fish are taken when trapped in a shrinking pool. More often the fish escape, and the animal continues feeling along the bottom without haste.

As dawn approaches, it retreats toward cooler runoff.

Each adult uses several resting hollows. One may be beneath a root bridge, another under a sedge island, a third in the submerged fork of a fallen tree. The useful feature is not shelter alone. Water must arrive from two directions: enough spring water to prevent a dangerous chill, enough surface water to carry oxygen and keep the hollow from overheating.

These mixtures shift after rain. A refuge that was good yesterday may be poor today.

The animals learn the changes in particular places. An old one approaching its usual hollow may pause upstream, lift its head, and turn away. Sometimes it tests two or three entrances before settling. Sometimes it spends the day in an exposed channel with only a curtain of roots over its eyes.

Their eyes are small, raised, and amber in clear light. In mist they show as two dull points above the mouth. They see movement well, but a still animal nearby may go unnoticed until a hand touches it.

Such meetings can be rough.

A feeding animal usually tolerates another passing beneath it. A resting animal is less accommodating. It expands its body, presses its hands against the sides of the hollow, and gives a low pulse through its throat. The sound carries poorly through the air. In water, and through a root touched by both animals, it travels farther.

The intruder may back away. If it does not, the resident pushes forward with its mouth shut, trying to lever the other loose. They brace and strain in silence. A struggle can last several minutes while scarcely disturbing the fern above them.

The winner often has to leave afterward to breathe.

They also make those low pulses when alone. Near the season of longest rains, the channels carry them after dusk: short tremors, separated by long intervals. A hand laid on a submerged branch can feel an animal calling somewhere beneath the opposite bank.

The calls are not precisely regular. A deep knock from the rock may interrupt one. The caller waits, its throat still, until the tremor has passed. Around the great pool that empties every few hours, the animals grow quiet well before the water begins to fall.

They feel the first acceleration in the narrow passages beneath its margins. One after another, they release the roots and move into side channels.

The young are less reliable. After a draining, some remain in the shallow mineral cups, gills spread in water that is warming rapidly. Birds know this. They walk down the exposed steps and stand very still beside the cups.

An adult occasionally eats a stranded youngster too.

Yet the adults spend much of their breeding season keeping their own young out of precisely such places.

Eggs are laid in cooler water, beneath ledges where the current is continuous but gentle. A clutch forms a thick, clear sheet fastened to roots. Within it, the embryos lie curled around pale yolks, each in its own round chamber.

One parent remains nearby. It draws water over the eggs with slow movements of its hind feet and clears sediment with its fingers. Most of this work happens after the afternoon rain, when fine soil enters even the well-filtered streams.

The parent does not defend the clutch fiercely. A grazer stepping into the ledge may destroy it. A sudden mineral-rich surge may kill it without leaving any visible injury. The animal can drive off a small fish, lift away a fallen leaf, or move a pebble that has begun to divert the flow. Against larger changes, it has few resources.

Sometimes, when the ledge starts to warm, it pulls at the roots holding the egg sheet. The sheet stretches. A portion tears free and is carried a little downstream before the parent catches it against its chest.

Fragments of one clutch may end up in several hollows. Not all survive the handling. Enough do.

The hatchlings have feathery external gills and narrow bodies marked with pale bars. They live in the loose outer edges of the mats, where rainwater passes through before mixing fully with the springs. Here they can disappear among roots too fine to support an adult’s weight.

For their first weeks, a drop of cold rain striking the water is a considerable event. They flinch, turn, and crowd deeper beneath the plants. Later they begin to follow the cooling water outward, feeding in the new spaces it makes available.

Growth changes their geography. The gills shrink. The forefeet widen. A channel once too large to cross becomes a route; a safe tangle of roots becomes something that catches the shoulders. The young must leave it before they quite possess the strength of the adults.

During these passages they are most visible. After rain, half-grown animals can be found lying across wet stones, heads raised, their slender tails still showing the pale bars of infancy. They stop often. They seem to be listening with their whole weight.

A few reach the basin’s outer streams and follow them toward the rim. There the water cools quickly, the ground becomes firm, and the protective roots grow sparse. Most turn back. Below the falls there may be other suitable water, but there is no gradual way to reach it.

Their world ends at a place where a hand can find nothing to hold.

Within the basin, some live long enough to become substantial features of their chosen channels. Roots thicken around the spaces they keep open. Their passage polishes the undersides of submerged branches. Silt collects in ridges beside their resting hollows, and seedlings establish themselves there.

An old female may have a pale scar across her head, clouding over one eye. She approaches a familiar junction from the same side each evening, using the good eye to watch the open water. One hand is missing its outer finger. When she braces against the current, that wrist turns inward.

Tonight, the water arriving at the junction is warmer than usual.

She rests her chin on the bottom. Behind her, the channel continues toward the feeding grounds. Ahead, a narrow thread of cooler water comes through a bank of moss.

For a long time she does nothing.

Then she sets both hands into the moss and pulls. A tuft comes loose, dark with sediment. Water enters the space it leaves. She pulls again, opening a passage just wide enough for her head, and presses into it until the cool flow runs beneath her throat.

Above her, the first wisps of evening steam rise through the sedges. She remains half in the old channel and half in the new opening, breathing. By morning, the water will have carried away the loosened soil.
