The animal beneath the leaf is smaller than the space it has kept for itself.

You see the space first: a patch of exposed felt, two fingerbreadths long, with the loose fibers pressed flat. Around it, roots turn aside. Their youngest ends have been bitten through. The leaf that covers it has a smooth notch in one edge, large enough to let something look out without lifting the whole blade.

For a while there is only a little movement in the notch. Then a forefoot appears and rests on the metal.

It has four toes. Three spread flat; the fourth bends back toward the wrist and grips the edge of a rust blister. The animal waits with its weight still concealed. Its foot is pale underneath, almost transparent at the margins, and divided into many small pads rather than one broad sole. Each pad settles separately.

When it comes out, it does not step so much as transfer its attachment. One foot loosens after another has taken hold. Its belly remains close to the wall, but never quite touches.

It is difficult at first to tell how much of it is fur. Along the back, short gray hairs lie in overlapping bands, greasy at their roots and powdered with rust at their tips. The flanks are nearly bare. When the animal stretches, they unfold into thin, wrinkled skin. Its head is broad behind the eyes and narrow at the mouth. There are no visible ears. Two dark slits close when a flake of insulation falls across its face.

Its tail is shorter than its body, flattened underneath, with a hard little hook at the end. The hook catches in the felt while it lifts both forefeet to clean them.

This is an elaborate business. Each toe is drawn through the mouth, turned, drawn through again. A grain of grit that would be nothing to a larger animal can hold a pad away from the wall. The animal scrapes at one such grain for nearly a minute. Then it lays the foot down and pulls against it, testing.

Only after this does it go to drink.

Its route takes it away from the nearest basin.

It travels upward along the edge of a plate, turns beneath a fern, and crosses a bare stretch of steel using old screw heads as stepping stones. It does not linger on the bare metal. At a folded seam it stops, puts its mouth against a bead of water, and drinks without disturbing the roots beneath it. The bead diminishes slowly. Before it is gone, the animal withdraws.

A clear swelling has appeared under its jaw.

Back beneath the leaf, something smaller moves.

The young are not gray yet. Their backs are dark and their feet disproportionately large, crowded together on the felt. There are three of them. One stays attached upside down while the other two press against the returning animal’s mouth. The swelling under its jaw subsides.

The third is late to reach it. It pushes between the others with its eyes closed and receives what remains.

Their shelter is not a nest in the birds’ sense. It has no gathered rim, no soft center. The adult has pared the felt down until a shallow hollow fits its body, leaving the tougher woven backing intact. A narrow strip overhead remains uncut. The young grip this strip while they sleep.

Below them, the adult has cleared a passage through the roots. Anything dropped from the shelter can fall straight out.

There are several such hollows along the ledge. Some have filled with moss. One is occupied by beetles. Another, farther up, is kept bare although nothing sleeps in it. The adult visits this empty place occasionally, removes a root tip, and presses its jaw against the backing.

It may once have lived there.

Its left hind foot is missing a toe. On broad surfaces the absence hardly matters, but a particular turn beneath the rail requires it to stop and bring its tail around first. The metal at that turn is polished. It has been taking the same extra moment there for a long time.

Other animals of its kind occupy the wall across the chamber. They are easier to locate by what happens around them than by their bodies: a fern lifted from below, a row of beetles abruptly separating, a leaf held still while all the leaves beside it tremble.

They do not call across the gulf.

Sometimes the adult braces its hind feet, places its lower jaw against a rail attachment, and works something in its throat. There is nothing you can hear. Across the chamber, another animal stops cleaning itself.

The exchange may last several minutes. A few movements of the throat, a wait, an adjustment of the jaw. The machinery continues without alteration. Whatever passes between them travels within that enormous, steady disturbance.

The young learn by putting their mouths beside the adult’s mouth. At first they also move their feet, their tails, their whole bodies. Later they become still.

Food arrives in several ways. The animal takes soft insects from beneath leaves and scrapes pale growth from the sheltered sides of bolts. It eats the translucent skins left after a molt. When a bird has finished feeding above, it searches the flashing for fragments.

It also waits at the lowest suspended veil.

Here its posture changes. The hind feet spread widely. The tail hooks into the rail. Its body extends over the empty chamber, farther than seems safe, and one forefoot reaches toward a dry scrap of fern held in the shimmering air.

The scrap shakes but does not come nearer.

The animal withdraws, shifts half a step, and reaches again. Its toes catch an edge. For a moment the fern remains where it is, stretched between the foot and something invisible. Then it comes free.

The animal backs onto the ledge with the scrap held beneath its chest. There are tiny insects caught in the curled leaf, and pale grains adhering to its underside. It eats these first. Some of the fern follows. The tough central thread is left beneath a rail post.

The young watch from well behind it.

One day the largest ventures onto the rail and stretches out in imitation. It has chosen an empty place. There is nothing to catch, but it extends farther, opening and closing its forefoot.

The adult takes it by the loose skin behind the head and pulls it back. Neither struggles. For some time afterward the young animal keeps all four feet on the felt.

During a rising storm, the family moves upward.

There is no visible signal. Long before rain reaches the opening, the adult begins cleaning the young with unusual thoroughness. It pays particular attention to their soles. Then it leads them out, one at a time, across the warm plates to the unused hollow.

The smallest rides beneath its body, gripping the flank folds. This makes the crossing awkward. At each screw head the adult pauses, its limbs spread around the young one’s clinging feet.

The upper hollow is drier, but less convenient. There is no dependable leak nearby. The adult must make a long journey for water, passing twice through a place exposed to the birds.

When the storm has gone, they stay there another day.

By then the old shelter is damp and its covering leaf hangs lower. The adult returns alone, noses through the hollow, and begins tearing out softened felt. It works in small mouthfuls, carrying each to the cleared passage before letting it fall.

A few fibers stop in the air below.

Most fall through the opening, turning slowly against the white cloud.

Later the young come down. They investigate every altered edge of the shelter. The smallest finds the overhead strip, grips it, and hangs there with the solemn stillness of something doing familiar work.

The adult settles beside the entrance. Its damaged hind foot rests in the shallow depression worn into the ledge. Its forefeet remain on the wall.

After a while its eyes close, but the pads of one foot continue to lift and settle, one after another, making small adjustments to the trembling metal.
