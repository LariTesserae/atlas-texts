In a house above the roofed market, a woman sits with a boot turned inside out across her knee.

The boot is not hers. Its sole is longer than her forearm, and the hide has stretched around a broad, irregular foot. She has packed the toe with felt and opened a seam where the stitching would otherwise rub. A bone needle rests between her finger and thumb. Her other hand is inside the boot, testing a ridge she cannot see.

She has a wide face, short hair, and a small white scar that interrupts one eyebrow. There is nothing priestly about her. Her apron has a pocket for scraps too small to be useful for anything except the particular repairs she knows how to make. Beside her stool stands a second stool, higher and sturdier, with a hollow in its seat. The person whose boot she holds sits there in thick stockings, one heel supported on the crossbar.

They are comfortable with each other, though not wholly in agreement. His mouth has the shape of an objection. Her expression gives the objection very little weight.

This, too, lives in the city: the exact amount of annoyance one person can feel toward another without withdrawing any care.

The inhabitants are human in all the small, uneven ways that make a body difficult to summarize. Some are broad through the chest and narrow at the hips. Some have little wrists protruding from heavy sleeves. There are soft bellies, crooked fingers, hair that grows thickly over the ears, knees enlarged beneath felt leggings. The mountain light finds the dry skin at the corners of their mouths. In sheltered faces it finds freckles; in exposed ones, the fine red lines of weather.

Their clothing does not quite match from person to person, or even from sleeve to sleeve. Wool takes dye unevenly. A russet cuff belongs to a brown coat. A pale patch under an arm has been sewn with dark thread in stitches so close that the patch seems outlined twice. Wealth, where it can be discerned, lies partly in how much fabric a person is wearing and partly in how little of its fastening is visible.

Yet a prosperous-looking woman at the cistern has repaired her own water strap badly. The repair is bulky, with a stubborn knot. On the arm of a child beside her, a plain mitten has been darned almost invisibly.

There are skills here that do not follow rank.

The city’s inhabitants are not all occupied with its maintenance. In the broad space behind a storehouse, three children have arranged flat chips of stone inside a scratched boundary. One chip stands on edge. A smaller child holds two more concealed against their stomach beneath a lifted hem.

An older child has noticed.

Nothing in the arrangement tells us whether the cheating will be exposed. The concealed stones, the suspicious glance, the pleasure of possessing an advantage all belong to the game as fully as its scratched lines. It is a game, not an emblem of play. Its rules matter intensely to the people within it.

Farther uphill, someone stands in the doorway of a house and looks across an unbuilt slope toward another doorway. The distance is too great for an ordinary voice. Their hand rests on the frame at shoulder height, without gripping it.

Whatever the regard contains, it is not made harmless by being still.

There is affection here, and resentment, and the embarrassment of being regarded too closely. There are people whose proximity is welcome and people around whom a room feels poorly proportioned. At the grain counter, the two pairs of hands do not quite agree about the shallow measure between them. One supports it from beneath. The other has two fingers laid across its rim.

The grain itself is pale and pointed. A single husk adheres to the back of a knuckle.

No one is imprisoned inside these attitudes. Imprisonment would require some other condition toward which the captive could turn. The woman with the boot is not enduring the interval before her needle moves. Her knowledge occupies her hand, the opened seam, the felt packed firmly into the toe. The repair is unfinished. Her care is not.

They have names. Nothing in the city makes those names available to an observer, but their possessions bear the marks of particular ownership: three cuts under a bowl’s lip, a blackened toggle, a small figure scratched where only the wearer could see it. Above one sleeping platform, five wooden hooks carry four belts. The empty hook is polished like the others.

In another house there are two cups of markedly different sizes, both set within reach of the same chair.

Such things resist being gathered into a single account of the people. They are not simply patient, or wise, or reconciled. A man near the upper wall has a face so tightly held that his composure seems costly, though nothing is being spent. Beneath an awning, another person’s smile is almost foolish in its openness. They are looking at a dog.

The dog lies on its back with one ear folded beneath its head. Its paws are heavy and loosely curled. A patch of skin shows through the fur of its belly, dark-speckled and vulnerable. There is no great stillness in the dog’s manner. There is only the particular extravagance with which it occupies a place where it is safe.

Other animals keep less agreeable company. Small creatures with blunt snouts inhabit the dry spaces beneath the storehouses. One has its teeth around a fallen grain, its whiskers spread against the underside of the floor. A lizard on a wall holds a beetle crosswise in its jaws. The beetle’s legs are extended, delicate as root hairs.

Life here has not been arranged to spare life.

Nor is every growing thing accommodated by the builders’ precision. A root has lifted one corner of a paving slab. Moss obscures the lower half of a carved mark. In a narrow window, a shrub’s outermost leaves occupy the view for which the opening was made. Their surfaces are glossy; their undersides are pale and thickly veined.

The people live among these encroachments without becoming their custodians. One person has placed a small board beneath a rocking table leg. Someone else has accepted the tilt. In one courtyard, a fern has the space between two storage jars. In the next, a clean-edged blade rests against a root at ground level.

There is room for both courtyards.

Above them, the flock occupies the air. From below, the birds look dark and much alike. Seen closely, one has a gap in its tail feathers. Another carries a thin strand of grass. One bird’s eye is turned toward the square, where a person with a basket has raised their face.

That person is neither young nor old enough for age to be the first thing noticed. Their basket contains folded cloth, a wooden lid, and a bunch of leaves bound at the stems. A handle presses a deep line into the palm.

Their eyes are narrowed against the light. Their lips are slightly parted.

There is no message passing between the person and the bird. The bird has its grass. The person has the weight of the basket, the brightness above the roofs, the dark shape distinct against the sky.

Nothing is missing from the look.
