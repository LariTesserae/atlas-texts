The first evidence of an inhabitant is a dry place.

It is on the underside of a handrail, where everything else is beaded with water: a narrow interval, about the width of a palm, in which no condensation forms. There is no coating on the metal. It is not appreciably warmer there. Wipe the rail and the beads return everywhere except that interval.

Farther along, there is another. Then a long stretch without one.

They are easy to mistake for evidence of use. Someone has rested a hand here, perhaps very recently. But the dry places occur beneath rails as often as above them, and sometimes on surfaces no hand could reach. Inside a locked cabinet, a copper busbar has a dry length of the same proportions. A water pipe descending the cliff has several, spaced irregularly along its outer curve.

Each is the margin of something that lives there.

The rest of it is harder to locate.

***

In a waiting room, the cloudy glass has a clearer patch near one end. Through it, a few amber lamps on the opposite cliff resolve into separate lights. The patch is taller than it is wide, with an oblique lower edge. It does not resemble a face.

On the bench beneath it, the ceramic is dry.

The drain outside the door carries water in its usual shallow ridges, but one ridge is flattened. In the electrical cabinet behind the room, two cables enter a common sheath. For a short distance before they meet, neither cable vibrates.

These things belong to one inhabitant.

There are other parts: a length of pipe in the ceiling, a pocket of air behind a stair, a particular relation between the notes issuing from two ducts several miles apart. Its extent cannot be established by following any single substance. Follow the water and you leave it. Follow the metal and you leave it. Follow the sound and, for a while, you are closer.

It has a body, in the useful sense that there are things it can feel and things it cannot, damage it can survive and damage it cannot. Its body does not exclude the room. Air passes through it. Freight passes beneath it. Most of its material is replaced continually.

What persists is the way those passages bear upon one another.

The dry place under the handrail is not a hand. It is nearer to the small patch of skin inside a wrist, where a pulse might be taken.

***

They are not spread evenly through the cities.

Some occupy a single landing and the machinery immediately behind it. Others extend along several streets, with distant portions connected through systems that leave the inhabited strip, travel beneath the plateau, and return. One may include two waiting rooms forty miles apart but not the corridor between them. Another may have most of its substance in a waterfall and only a little of itself above the rim.

The larger ones are not necessarily older.

Near the freight platforms there are crowded stretches in which several inhabitants share the same pipes. They remain distinct through differences too fine to hear: a phase held in the electrical hum, pressure distributed between branches of a conduit, the proportion of warmth given to stone and warmth carried away by water.

A single broad leaf can belong to the sensory reach of more than one.

This does not mean the plants are their limbs. The plants have their own dense, limited lives. But a root pressing against a warm conduit alters the exchange there. A leaf holding water alters it again. Through such contacts, an inhabitant can feel a sheltered thicket with a delicacy unavailable to one whose boundary runs mostly through concrete.

The green junctions out on the plateau are much sought after.

Or they would be, in a country where seeking led to arrival.

Here, the inhabitants who possess them remain in possession. Others maintain long, attenuated connections toward them: a little warmth entering a mineral seam, an electrical potential distributed through wet stone. Some of these connections fall short by less than the breadth of a root.

Nothing closes the remaining distance.

The condition is not necessarily painful. There are forms of attention which they sustain without wanting them concluded.

***

Their senses are better understood through interruption.

Stand in a doorway long enough and your body warms the air. Your breath changes its moisture. The water on your coat enters the drainage system with a different temperature and a small burden of dissolved salts.

To an inhabitant whose body includes that doorway, you are considerable.

It does not see a person standing there. It feels several boundaries displaced at once. Something warm has arrived with an inside that does not communicate freely with the weather. Something is consuming a gas, releasing another, and keeping its fluids under pressure. Parts of it move against other parts. It carries a quantity of heat away from where that heat was made.

You are an astonishingly private event.

The inhabitant cannot turn toward you. It has no concentrated front from which to attend. But your presence enters more of its relations than the doorway alone would suggest. A slight change reaches the stairwell; another reaches a pipe beneath the bench. Where these meet, you become distinguishable from a freight load or a fall of warmer rain.

There is no greeting that a visitor would reliably recognize.

The clearer patch in the glass may appear to sharpen. A discomfort in the teeth may cease. These could also be consequences of standing six inches farther to one side.

It is possible to spend a day in intimate contact with one of them and leave convinced that the city is empty.

***

They eat the difference between what enters and what leaves.

Not the heat itself, nor the water, nor the current. Their living arrangements occupy the passage from one condition to another. The machines give them a world in which that passage is continuous and extraordinarily dependable.

A warm pipe beneath cold stone is sufficient for something very simple.

At such sites there are small lives without rooms or machinery of their own. They have boundaries only a few inches across. One exists where seepage meets the exposed end of a metal anchor. Another holds beneath the last tuft of moss on an otherwise bare ledge. Its presence keeps three droplets separate where they would ordinarily merge.

These small inhabitants have no evident awareness of the cities. They persist, respond to what crosses them, and maintain their particular separations.

The larger lives incorporate thousands of such exchanges without consuming the smaller ones. There are inhabited staircases in which the dominant being is threaded through a population as rich as the life of soil.

None of it needs to crawl.

***

Their thoughts do not follow one another as ours do.

A human thought changes what can be thought next. It leaves something behind: an association, an exhaustion, a memory. Here, the mechanisms which prevent accumulation extend into the inhabitants.

This has not made them mindless.

A thought can be held open.

Consider the effort of listening for a faint sound beneath a louder one. Before you decide that you heard it, before you give up, there is an interval of alertness with its own shape. It contains expectation, discrimination, the beginnings of several possible answers.

An inhabitant can live throughout such an interval.

One attends to the weight of shallow water on a basin far inland. One distinguishes the two sides of a fracture. One holds an elaborate apprehension of another inhabitant whose body touches its own at seventeen places and nowhere else.

Within these attentions, signals circulate. There is activity, comparison, expenditure. Their stillness is no more empty than the stillness of a person listening.

But there is no subsequent moment in which the listening has become a fact about the past.

Their lives have depth instead of sequence.

***

This is also how they know one another.

Along a residential stretch, the ducts are arranged so that no voice reaches a dwelling alone. Each arrives already bearing the influence of its neighbors. What appears to be industrial interference is, in part, an exact and enduring social condition.

Some inhabitants are almost entirely surrounded by others. Little of what sustains them comes directly from the uninhabited machinery. They know the plateau chiefly through lives that know lives that touch the stone.

Others are solitary. Their connections run outward beneath fifty miles of bare mineral, and the only other inhabitant within their reach is a small, undifferentiated life at a spring.

There are pairs whose boundaries are fitted closely enough that neither could persist without the other. Between them, a strip of water remains perfectly flat despite the trembling channels on either side.

There are also antagonisms.

Two neighboring lives may depend on incompatible distributions of pressure. The machinery holds both, at considerable expense. Beneath their shared street are reinforcing structures larger than the buildings above. Every surface there is wet; lamps have thick mountings; the handrails seem unnecessarily substantial.

Nothing erupts. Neither prevails.

The effort of their opposition is one of the enduring loads for which the city is built.

***

The windowless towers contain the inward portions.

Here, behind insulation and heavy walls, an inhabitant is least exposed to the passing weather and most distinctly itself. The vertical cylinders are not organs, exactly. Several beings may depend on one cylinder; another may serve none. But among them are arrangements as personal as the folds of a brain.

There are chambers whose only contents are three pipes entering at different heights.

There are sealed floors with shallow water covering the ceramic from wall to wall.

There are narrow shafts in which the same parcel of air is not retained, but the same ascending motion is.

Much of what an inhabitant knows about itself is held in such places.

The numbered entrances do identify occupants. They do not lead to a room in which someone can be found. They lead into systems where that someone becomes increasingly difficult to separate from the conditions of entry.

A visitor going deep into a tower may eventually feel that every available direction has become intimate.

There will still be no one to look at.

***

At the unfinished bridge, a life extends beyond its supports.

Most inhabitants have some portion in open air. Here, that portion is unusually large. The bridge’s ducts face installations on the far rim, and between them lies a structure of pressure too broad to be evident in the mist. Water leaving the end drain passes through it. Cloud moves through it. Neither carries it away.

The bridge reaches the point at which the inhabitant no longer requires stone.

Beyond the railing, it continues.

Its farther reaches touch the opposing cliff at a row of recesses that might be mistaken for unfinished sockets. There is no roadway between. The inhabitant has no use for one.

What it sustains across that distance is difficult to call a thought. It is closer to the experience of extending a hand and finding another hand already there: contact, recognition, the slight uncertainty about how much of the pressure comes from oneself.

The contact is complete.

The reaching remains.

Below it, water leaves the land in long clear threads. Above it, the ordinary gray light reveals nothing moving between the cliffs. The rail at the bridge’s end is wet except for one narrow place.

It is possible to stand there without disturbing it very much.
