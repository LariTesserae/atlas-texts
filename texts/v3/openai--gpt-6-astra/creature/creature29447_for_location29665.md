The person who tied the cord across the western passage has gone to bed with a stone in her hand.

It is a small stone, flat enough to close her fingers around. She carries it when she has left a task unfinished. In the morning she will feel it before she opens her eyes and remember that the passage needs inspecting.

Her name is Eren. She has lived in this court long enough to have replaced the lower half of her door twice. She is not especially old.

Beside her bed are a cup, a shallow dish of salt, and a pair of shoes turned soles upward. The soles are made of six layers of woven fiber. She has worn through five beneath the left heel and is putting off the repair because she dislikes sewing shoes. In the morning, before inspecting the passage, she will probably stuff the hollow with loose felt.

People here put things off. There is sufficient time for almost everything, which does not make everything attractive.

Under her blankets, Eren is narrow and folded, her knees drawn up toward a patch of warmth in the wall. Her hands remain outside. Along the backs of them, fine pale lines divide the skin into irregular lengths. On a young person these lines can scarcely be seen. On Eren they resemble the grain exposed when a weathered stem is peeled.

The resemblance continues beneath the skin.

A deep cut does not bleed red. Clear fluid wells slowly from it, becomes cloudy in the cold, and draws into threads between the edges of the wound. Small cuts can be closed with pressure. Larger ones must be combed clean and bound along the grain. Bound crosswise, a hand may heal strong enough to lift a paving slab but never again close around a cup.

Eren’s smallest finger is stiff for this reason. She injured it when she was young and traveling, and let someone handsome dress the wound.

She still maintains that he knew what he was doing.

***

They have faces you can learn to read.

The eyes are dark, deeply set, protected by heavy lids. The mouth is broad enough to show displeasure without speech. At rest, the face tends to look severe because the skin is drawn tightly over the cheek ridges; affection often appears first in a loosening beside the eyes.

Their ears lie close against the head. Their hair grows in coarse, flattened strands and is usually kept braided beneath a hood. Loose hair takes up mist and can freeze against the collar. Children insist on wearing it loose for approximately the same span of life in every district.

Inside the layers of clothing, their bodies are built economically. There are no thick reserves of flesh. Tough longitudinal bundles carry most of the strain, joined at shoulder and hip by structures that become harder with age. They can remain standing for a long time without discomfort. Sitting on a low stool is easy. Rising from one, late in life, becomes an undertaking for which a person quietly arranges witnesses who can help without seeming to have been summoned.

They breathe. On a steep stretch of road, rare as those are, their breathing becomes audible through their scarves. A person who has spent years in the deeper, warmer rooms of a district needs several days to grow accustomed to working outdoors again.

They eat roots, softened stems, the flour of certain bladders harvested before they fill with pollen. Some foods are fermented in covered pits. Some are sliced so thin that the light passes through them and then dried against a warm interior wall.

There is a black paste made from a garden growth that smells like damp masonry. Almost everyone claims to prefer the version made in their childhood court.

Eren dislikes it everywhere.

She likes the translucent knobs that grow beneath copper-leaved mats. They take most of a day to soften. When fuel is short she slices them raw and chews until her jaw aches.

Sunlight alone will not keep a person alive. Nevertheless, after long confinement, their skin loses color and wounds become reluctant to close. The sick are carried outside during the clear hours, wrapped except for their faces and palms. There are shallow recesses in the south-facing walls where someone can sit out of the wind.

On favorable afternoons these are occupied by convalescents, old people, and entirely healthy people who have decided that their work can wait.

***

Children begin below ground.

Each inhabited court keeps a warm bed, though the bed may go unused for years. It occupies a small chamber with a porous floor and a roof that can be partly opened. Its preparation requires clean root fiber, powdered stone, old leaf matter, and contributions taken from the living bodies of those who intend to make a child.

The contributions are small. Taking them hurts.

There may be two contributors or several. What matters afterward is who agrees to keep watch, who has enough food put by, and who will get up during the cold part of the night when the bed needs turning.

For a long time there is little to distinguish a successful bed from a failed one. A pale thickening forms beneath the surface. It must be kept damp without being soaked. Eventually it begins to draw itself together.

The first unmistakable movement is usually a hand closing.

People have argued for centuries over whether there is someone there before this happens. The person on night watch generally speaks to the bed regardless.

A new child is lifted only when the fine attachments along its back release of their own accord. It is washed, wrapped, and carried from household to household so that everyone can see it. For several weeks it sleeps through most of these visits.

The tender marks along its spine close slowly. Adults retain them as a row of small dimples.

Gardens sometimes produce growths that resemble an early bed. A knot beneath a mat may have the shape of a shoulder, or two leaves may enclose something disturbingly like a folded face. Gardeners cover such discoveries while they decide what to do.

Most are ordinary growths.

Occasionally a court enlarges its nursery.

This is not discussed easily with strangers.

***

Eren shares her house with her mother’s former housemate and a child named Tavi, who belongs to three households and eats in whichever one is cooking something agreeable.

Tavi is old enough to cut back a path and too young to be trusted to decide where the path ought to go.

He has been moving the boundary stones around his garden assignment. Only a little at a time. He wants a narrower path, which means less cutting, and has explained that the garden itself appears to favor the arrangement.

Eren has noticed. She has not yet decided whether to make him move the stones back or let him discover what happens when the laundry cart can no longer get through.

He is frightened of the western passage. This has not prevented him from asking to inspect it.

Children are taught the practical warnings early, but knowledge and judgment arrive separately. A child may recite every precaution concerning unequal hours and still roll a cup across the street to see what happens.

There are accidents.

The city’s patience does not protect everyone. A familiar avenue can take someone away. A lintel can fail without announcing itself. A person can ignore a white cord because it has been there for six days and nothing has happened.

Every court contains some object that has remained where it was put down because nobody wishes to be the one to move it.

In Eren’s court it is a shallow basket beside the northern cistern. The basket has been repaired twice. Nobody uses it.

Tavi knows whose it was. He has not been told the whole story.

***

Their lives are long enough for a single person to become associated with several different occupations.

Eren was once a distance messenger. Later she tended nursery beds. Now she repairs the channels that bring condensation from the roofed cisterns into the gardens. She is particularly good at finding places where water is disappearing into a wall.

Her current work has made her left shoulder broader than her right. Her hands, kept damp so often, are softer than those of a road worker. Close to the nails, they have a faint greenish cast.

Bodies change with their work. The change is slow but persistent. A mason’s palms thicken into pale pads. People who spend their lives over looms develop unusually fine control in the last two fingers. Someone who carries loads for several decades acquires a second ridge of supporting tissue across the upper back.

Changing trades late in life is possible, though the old occupation remains visible.

When Eren dreams of running, she still has the body she had on the roads.

Messengers are not the quickest walkers. They are the people most able to continue at the same pace when a destination appears suddenly near. They must notice which side of a wall holds frost, which plants have opened, whether the light at the far end of a street agrees with the light behind them.

They carry messages braided into cords as well as words remembered aloud. Knots hold quantities well. They are less satisfactory for apologies.

Eren once carried an apology for eleven months. When she arrived, the recipient had forgotten the quarrel and was furious to be reminded of it.

For several years afterward she told this story at meals. Now everyone nearby knows it, so she saves it for visitors.

***

A district is small enough to make concealment difficult and large enough to make understanding optional.

People know who is generous with fuel, who returns a borrowed blade sharpened, who volunteers for distant errands when work on the latrines is due. They form attachments, exhaust them, continue sharing tools afterward. A couple may separate while remaining jointly responsible for a section of wind wall. Sometimes the wall benefits. Sometimes the court has to intervene.

The long distances offer relief.

A person can leave after an argument and spend a season somewhere that has never heard of it. On arriving, they will be given a bed and asked what work they can do. They may invent a more favorable account of themselves.

Eventually a conjunction brings someone who knows them.

Temporary neighbors are therefore greeted with a mixture of pleasure and apprehension. Goods arrive, and news, and techniques for setting a roof slab. So do debts and inconvenient witnesses.

There are courts reputed to be hospitable, courts reputed to be proud, and one very distant district where, according to Eren, everyone sings while working and nobody will admit how unpleasant this is for a guest.

She lived there for two winters.

One of the people there still keeps a room for her.

They have not seen each other for a long time. On evenings when the roads seem restless, she sometimes packs a bag, then unpacks the food before going to bed. The spare shoes remain inside.

***

Age comes unevenly.

The outer tissues grow less supple. The joints require warmth. A person’s need for food diminishes, but their need for water becomes more exacting. Old people keep cups within reach and grow irritated when someone helpfully removes them.

Memory often remains good. It is movement that becomes expensive.

Eventually there may be a morning when a person wakes clear-minded and cannot persuade a leg to bend. For a while they can be carried. They can teach, mend, shell seeds, settle disputes, or create them.

Later, small attachments begin to grow from the soft places on the body.

At this stage the person chooses where to sit.

The choice has usually been discussed for years. There are prepared places in household gardens, with backrests and shelters against the wind. Some face a doorway. Some face away from the court entirely.

Taking hold is gradual. Speech continues for a time. The hands can remain useful surprisingly long.

Eren’s mother spent her last speaking summer beside the washing stones, where she could criticize how much water everyone used. In the second summer she answered pressure with pressure. By the fourth, the branching growth around her shoulders had covered the backrest.

Eren still sits there to mend clothing. She does not claim that her mother hears her.

When she drops a needle, however, she apologizes before searching among the leaves.

People eventually stop attending a particular patch as a person. The interval varies. A household may preserve it for generations; another may divide and transplant it once the last recognizable response has ceased.

There is no universally kind way to do this.

The gardens contain old quarrels as well as old affection.

***

In the morning Eren wakes with her hand aching around the stone.

She lies still until the room has become distinct. Water has gathered beneath the window covering. Tavi is already outside; she can hear his scraper catching on the pavement with the regularity of someone doing a task angrily.

She puts felt in her shoe.

At the western passage, the cord hangs slack between its pegs. Beyond it are the same walls, the same narrow drain, the same patch of pale growth halfway along the paving. She crouches and sets the stone down just inside the entrance.

It lands.

She waits.

Tavi has stopped scraping. From behind her he asks whether it is safe.

Eren watches a bead of water make its way down the left wall. It pauses at a joint, swells, and drops. Somewhere in the house, the pot lid begins to tremble.

“Bring another stone,” she says.

He chooses one from the pile intended for repairs.

“Smaller. We’ll have to carry it out again.”

He returns with a pebble and squats beside her, careful of the cord. Together they watch the empty passage while breakfast takes its time.
