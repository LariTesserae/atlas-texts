The grazer does not look up when the other animal enters the path. It draws its hind feet closer together, making room.

At first you see only a hand on a root.

The fingers are long, with broad pads that flatten against the wood. Between them the skin is thin and faintly amber. They do not close around the root. The weight comes onto them a little at a time, until something that looked like a fold of wet bark beside the path lifts its head and steps forward.

Standing, it is shorter than you. Its body is deep through the chest and narrow behind, carried between four long limbs. A smaller pair of limbs rests beneath the throat. These end in hands too, fine ones, with pale nails and a dark seam of material pressed beneath each nail.

Its coat lies in overlapping locks along its back. Water follows them toward the hips and falls clear of the belly. On its sides the hair is shorter, almost silver where it parts. The face is small. There are nostrils that shut, a soft divided upper lip, and eyes set far enough apart that it turns its whole head to examine you.

It stands still for so long that you begin attending to the forest again.

Then one of the little hands beneath its throat opens.

There is a piece of fruit in it. The creature brings this to its mouth, bites, and continues looking at you while it chews.

***

They live in the dry enclosures, though not in all of them.

Some chambers are too warm now. Others have become islands surrounded by water that cannot bear a walking body. A few have stayed dry but developed a sound in their walls that the inhabitants dislike: an irregular ticking, almost inaudible, as fibers separate inside a supporting column.

They will sleep beside a deep, continuous groan. At the ticking, they take their young away.

Their bodies suit the raised rims. They fold the long limbs beneath them and lean sideways, resting the chest against the wall. The little hands remain free. Even asleep, an adult sometimes reaches out to touch the individual beside it.

Where generations have slept, the bark is polished at the shoulder, the hip, and the outside of one wrist.

The empty depressions are not all the same size.

A newborn can lie across two of the fine hands. It has almost no coat and feet so broad that they appear swollen. For its first months it lives against an adult’s breast, gripping a band of coarse hair that grows there in both sexes. Adults pass it between them without setting it down. The transfer requires all four small hands, and for a moment the infant hangs in the space between two chests, indignant, its long toes opening and closing.

They do not always move slowly.

A falling infant, a split in a wet branch, the first rush of a predator across a dry crossing: each brings out a speed that seems to belong to another body. The long limbs unfold. Pads adhere, release, adhere. An adult can travel along the underside of a branch with its back almost brushing the pool below, then swing upward through a gap barely wide enough for its shoulders.

Afterward it may sit for an hour, trembling through the flanks.

Their usual pace conserves more than strength. It gives the ground time to answer.

***

There are sensitive folds of bare skin inside their walking wrists. When resting, they press these against wood.

Through a single trunk they can distinguish climbing feet from the movements of water. In the connected flooring of a chamber, several adults listen together, each touching a different place. One changes position. Another lowers its breast to the floor. Eventually they rise and leave, or settle more comfortably, or begin moving objects away from one wall.

They are sometimes wrong.

A family may abandon a shelter that remains sound for another lifetime. A floor can open beneath sleepers without any warning they understand. Their oldest stories include chambers that fell whole into buried water, preserving their dry interiors long enough for voices to be heard inside.

They do not enter certain hollows.

Near these, they walk on the smallest roots available and keep their wrists lifted.

Their own messages use the wood sparingly. A heel laid down twice. A scrape followed by three light contacts of the fingers. The patterns say little at a distance, but they say where.

A reply may emerge behind the caller, although the answering creature is ahead.

For anything complicated, they come close. They have low, breathy voices and a range of movements around the lips too small for you to read. They touch wrists while speaking. Sometimes one holds the other’s fine hand and arranges its fingers, pauses, then begins again.

An argument can be nearly silent and leave both participants breathing hard.

***

They eat more than fruit.

In the warm places where wood softens, they turn back loose layers and gather pale, jointless animals from the wet grain. They harvest the swollen ends of certain roots. Some leaves are folded and carried home to soften in a wall niche; others are chewed immediately, with a brief tightening of the nostrils.

They open the floating leaves in pools and take the flat swimmers beneath them.

A swimmer’s skin is difficult to pierce. The little hands hold it against the breast while one nail finds the edge of its mouth. The killing is quick and practiced. Afterward the eater washes carefully. Blood caught in the chest hair draws small biting things that otherwise leave them alone.

Near an occupied shelter, the abundance has an arrangement.

Edible root tips emerge from the wall at reachable heights. Vines bear their fruit above dry ledges. A particular broad leaf grows over the entrance, its point directed toward a drainage groove.

None of this is accomplished quickly. A shoot is drawn through a loop of older fiber. Weeks later the loop is loosened. A branch is weighted with a bladder of water; as the bladder leaks, the branch rises a little, setting into a curve.

They have preferred foods that they never taste.

An adult may spend years training a fruiting stem toward the sleeping rim, then die while it is still producing only leaves. The stem passes into someone else’s attention. That person may cherish it, neglect it, or decide that the room needs light more than another fruit.

There are quarrels over inherited growth.

There are shelters with excellent roofs and bitter fruit everywhere.

***

The grazers know their smell.

At a crossing, one of the people can usually pass close to a grazer’s shoulder without disturbing it. Sometimes the grazer leans toward them. They part the stems in its coat and pick out hard, dark parasites fixed to the skin beneath.

The translucent creatures climb onto their fingers. They blow them gently back among the flowers.

A grazer with a painful place on its back may wait outside a shelter, shifting its weight until the threshold begins to slope. The inhabitants come out making sharp sounds. They relieve the itch, then push against the animal’s broad face until it withdraws.

They like the grazers individually. They also drive them away from young plantings, steal growth from their coats, and lead predators toward those that have become dangerous.

An old grazer sometimes loses its sense of footing. It enters water too deep to cross, or stands in a hollow until its weight begins drawing more water around it. Pulling it free may be impossible. The people bring branches and lay them beneath its body. They wait with it. Sometimes the branches spread the load enough.

Sometimes they return to take the meat.

***

The wide dry paths are still theirs in places.

They travel them with their bodies raised above the low obstructions, placing feet on either side of a crossing root. Where foliage closes the way, the fine hands work ahead of the face. Passage leaves little damage.

In other places the paths no longer lead anywhere they know.

An adult following a familiar route may find the entrance to a chamber half a body-length lower than it was on the last visit. Water stands inside. The sleeping rim is green. A plant it remembers eating from now hangs beyond reach, still bearing.

It may remain there until the light fades.

Their maps, where they keep them, are made of flexible strips laid across a frame. Knots mark shelters, crossings, places of food. The strips can be shifted. A route can be lengthened without breaking it. Some knots are old enough to have become indistinct, polished into the fibers by handling.

A young traveler learns the map partly through touch. Its teacher guides a finger from knot to knot, accompanying the movement with breath and small pressures of the hand.

There are pauses for things the map cannot hold: the smell before a particular ascent, the posture needed to pass beneath a branch, whose shelter once stood beside the falling water.

Several routes end in loose strands.

They do not cut these short.

***

A household is usually small enough to sleep along one rim, but its members need not remain together.

Some leave for a season of heavy dripping and return with different habits, a new food wrapped in leaves, or a young one whose other parent nobody there has met. Some travel between a few shelters for most of their lives. Others become attached to one chamber and refuse to leave even when their companions begin training a replacement roof farther upslope.

The reluctant are visited. Food is brought. Arguments are repeated.

Occasionally the new shelter is abandoned instead.

Their attachments can outweigh their knowledge of the ground.

They keep small possessions in the dry folds of the walls: pierced seed cases, a strip of unusually soft hide, translucent insect wings layered between bark. Hard objects are rare. A fragment of stone passes through many hands. They rub its surface with the thumb pads of their fine hands, feeling the coolness return after each touch.

Some have stone they will not allow anyone else to handle.

Some carry nothing.

A young one may keep an ordinary curled leaf for months, sleeping with it pressed beneath its chest until it has broken into pieces. An adult cleaning the rim will gather those pieces separately.

***

The individual on the path finishes its fruit.

Its coat has parted at one hip, revealing a patch of pale scar tissue. The rear foot on that side settles less fully than the other feet. You notice now that it has been standing with the injured limb on the firmer of two roots.

It looks past you.

From the enclosure you mistook for a bank of vegetation comes a soft series of breaths. Another face appears in the doorway, lower down. A young one pushes around it, sets both front feet outside, and is caught at the waist by a fine hand.

The adult beside the grazer answers.

Then it approaches you.

At arm’s length it stops and raises one of its walking hands, showing the broad pads. It waits until you hold out your own.

The touch is warm. Its fingers close loosely around your wrist. With the smaller hand it turns your palm downward and guides it toward the root beside the path.

For a while you feel nothing except the slick surface.

The creature adjusts your hand. Its face is close enough that you can see water caught in the short hairs around its mouth. It is watching your fingers rather than your eyes.

A faint movement passes through the wood.

You look up.

It keeps your hand where it is.

After a longer interval, the movement comes again: two uneven contacts, then the light, rapid disturbance of something finding a better hold. Somewhere beyond the wall of leaves, another member of the household is coming home.
