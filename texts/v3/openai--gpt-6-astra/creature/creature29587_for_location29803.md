The woman who lives there in summer has a way of setting things down that looks excessively careful until you have spent a day with her.

A knife goes into a crack, blade first. A cup goes beneath her knee. When she takes off her outer coat, she puts one sleeve through the other and passes a stone through both. Nothing light is left on the open floor. Even a handful of roots, put aside while she answers a question, receives a pebble.

Her name is Essa. She is broad through the shoulders, with narrow wrists and hands that are seldom entirely clean. The dirt remains beside her nails after washing, packed into the little splits that open there in cold weather. One thumbnail grows in two overlapping plates. She uses it to split seed capsules.

At first she seems old. Then she laughs, or lifts a loaded basket by herself, and the impression becomes less certain. Her hair has gone white above one ear and remains dark above the other. She cuts it with the knife she uses for roots, drawing a lock forward and sawing through it against her thumb. From behind, the result resembles something grazed.

She occupies the sleeping hollow nearer the door. Her brother takes the inner one.

He is older by nine years, although people who meet them usually assume the reverse. His name is Meret. He has a smooth, almost uncreased forehead and a beard that grows thickly under his jaw but scarcely at all on his cheeks. When he listens, his mouth hangs a little open. This gives him an air of trusting simplicity which has proved useful in bargaining and is not representative of his character.

They come up after the runnel begins to flow through the night without freezing. They leave when ice persists in the shade behind the shelter. Between those times they gather things too small, scattered, or inconvenient for most people to make a living from.

Not one thing, but a succession.

The first bitter shoots from a plant that grows under melting snow. A leathery lichen, taken in flakes no larger than a fingernail. Seeds from the flowering cushions, each kind kept separate. Certain roots, dug only from the loose rubble where a whole plant can be lifted without losing its branching tip. Later, the hard black fruits of the creeping shrubs.

Far below, people buy these things for physic, for dye, for gardens where the summers are too warm and the high plants usually die. Essa considers the gardeners her best customers. They pay promptly, are grateful for instructions, and return the following year needing more seeds.

Meret thinks she should be more sympathetic.

“I am sympathetic,” she says. “I tell them they need a mountain.”

Their baskets are shallow and divided into compartments. Inside are little folded packets, scraps of cloth, hollow lengths of reed plugged with wool. Seen in the shelter doorway, a morning’s work looks almost like nothing. There may be enough seed in a paper twist to sow a whole bed, but scarcely enough weight to register in the hand.

They do not harvest much from the green seam behind the shelter. Its plants are useful in another way: they can watch them closely. When a certain flower opens there, Essa begins visiting a slope beyond the western cleft. When its seed heads loosen, she knows she has perhaps two days to reach a patch farther down the cliff.

She has missed that interval before. Once, after three mornings of rain, she found every capsule empty.

For the rest of that summer Meret had only to say, “Perhaps tomorrow,” to make her angry.

The rope is theirs, though not the first rope to have worn the slab. They carry it away when they leave the hollow, and bring it back coiled beneath a hide cover. Even at night they draw it in. Rope is expensive; more importantly, its condition must be known all the way along.

Essa examines it by feeding it through a closed hand. Her fingers stop at places her eyes have not noticed. Meret repairs it, sitting with one foot braced against the opposite wall of the shelter, teasing apart the fibers with a polished piece of bone.

On the cliff he goes first.

This is not because he is braver. Essa can climb places he will not attempt, but his left knee sometimes locks when he lowers his weight onto it. Descending first allows him to set his pace without her beneath him. She follows carrying the baskets. Where the footholds disappear around the fold of rock, they exchange a few plain words about the rope, the water, the condition of the next step.

They have used those words for so many years that each can distinguish an ordinary answer from one spoken through clenched teeth.

Most of what they do together is less graceful.

They disagree about whether the bowl needs washing when it has held only dry food. Meret leaves his footwear where Essa wants to put her feet. She empties the grit from her bedding onto his. He eats while sorting seeds, despite repeated demonstrations of the difficulty of separating crumbs from the smaller kinds.

When visitors arrive, they sometimes stop quarreling so abruptly that the visitors imagine they have interrupted a private grief.

Neither is especially quiet. They are simply economical about competing with the mountain. When the hollow hums, they save most conversation for the shelter. Essa gets her brother’s attention by throwing something soft at him. He has learned not to turn until it lands; she is a poor thrower, and the object may be something he needs to catch.

They know the small animals individually, or believe they do.

There is one with a torn ear that steals drying stems from the gathering trays. Meret puts out rejected material for it. Essa objects, not to the gift but to where he puts it.

“You are teaching it to come to the door.”

“It already comes to the door.”

“It comes farther.”

This dispute has continued through several generations of torn-eared animals.

The brown hunter receives no gifts. They watch it with the close attention given to someone performing difficult work. Once it cornered a hay-gatherer beneath a root mat beside Essa’s hand. She felt the struggle through the soil before she saw anything move. Afterward she went on separating roots, but more slowly, testing the loosened mat with her fingertips before pressing it back against the wall.

Their presence alters the shelter’s smaller life. Moths settle in the folds of their coats. Spiders attach threads to the stored baskets and must rebuild after gathering days. Beneath the cooking stone, where a little heat survives the evening, pale insects collect in such numbers that the dust seems to stir.

Meret will lift that stone only in daylight. He dislikes putting his hand among things he cannot see.

Essa teases him about this. She herself will not look over the brink while chewing. He discovered the habit years ago and has never received a satisfactory explanation.

There are afternoons when they do no work.

Cloud fills the hollow, and moisture softens everything they have spent the morning drying. The packets must be opened, checked, closed again. Clothing hangs under the roof where it can acquire the smell of smoke without becoming appreciably less wet. Their knees almost touch across the cooking stone.

Meret sleeps easily in that cramped warmth. Essa does not. She sits with the upturned bowl in her lap and repairs its rim, pushing a cord through pairs of tiny holes bored on either side of a crack. Each year the crack lengthens. Each year she adds another stitch.

She would like a copper pot.

Not a holy object, not a family treasure: a copper pot with a close-fitting lid, such as she saw in a trader’s stall two winters ago. She discusses its likely weight, its resistance to splitting, and how quickly water might heat in it. Meret says copper spoils the taste. He has never owned a copper pot either.

They both have houses below. Essa’s is shared with a daughter, a son-in-law, and children who want to open every packet she brings home. Meret’s contains a loom too large for its room. In winter he weaves with the door open so that one part of the frame can project into the yard.

Neither calls the hollow home in quite the way they call those houses home. Yet each has worn a place into it. Meret’s shoulder has polished a patch of shelter wall. Essa can reach the knife crack from her bedding without lifting her head.

At dusk, when the cloud drains away, they sometimes bring their food to the doorway. Meret sits inside with his bad leg extended. Essa sits on the threshold, leaving just enough room for him to draw it back.

They watch the late light on the opposite peaks while they eat. They have names for those summits, but also for a pale scar, a leaning tooth of rock, a strip of snow that vanishes early in some years and remains all summer in others.

Occasionally one points out a change.

More often they notice the same thing and say nothing. Essa passes the bowl behind her without looking. Meret takes it, finishes what is left, and puts it upside down beside the cooking stone.

Before sleep, she goes out once more to check the drying trays. He calls after her to bring in his coat.

It is where he left it, secured beneath a stone.
