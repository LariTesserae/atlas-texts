The inhabitants are smaller than the work makes them seem.

At first, each moving leaf suggests an animal of that size: something broad-backed and green, picking its way across the earth. Then the leaf tilts, and beneath it is an ant, narrow at the waist, with a head bent toward its load and six legs finding separate footholds. A crumb rolls under one foot. The ant slips, catches itself, and continues. The fragment rocks above it, several times taller than the body carrying it.

Others arrive without leaves. These are easier to lose against the ground. They become visible when they turn, when a polished part of the body catches the sideways light, or when their antennae sweep beyond the edge of a mound.

They do not all have the same proportions. Some have substantial heads and jaws that close on a stem or grip an awkward burden. Others are slight enough to travel through the spaces beneath those burdens. On a fresh leaf fragment, a very small worker moves along a cut edge while the larger carrier walks below. For a moment, one ant is part of another ant’s terrain.

Near the entrance, two workers meet head to head. Their antennae touch rapidly. One continues into the opening; the other moves a short distance sideways and begins examining a grain of earth. There is no visible pause in the procession around them.

The hollow contains only the outskirts of their lives.

Below it are surfaces that never receive the narrow afternoon light: excavated walls, close passages, chambers whose air is kept moist by the surrounding ground. Workers enter these carrying pieces of the forest and reduce them further. A fragment that arrived stiff enough to cast a shadow is cut, cleaned, worked into material suitable for the garden.

The fungus grows through that material in pale, delicate masses. It has no green of its own. The leaves brought down to it cease to look like leaves; they become the bed through which it feeds and extends itself. Workers move over its uneven surface, tending new growth and removing what is spoiled or spent. Some of the smallest ants spend much of their lives at this close scale, among structures that a larger worker could step across.

The garden is another inhabitant, though it never comes to a doorway. Its needs send the ants into the forest. Its condition changes what must be carried in and what must be carried out.

There are young below ground as well: pale eggs gathered together, soft larvae unable to accompany the traffic, pupae in which the folded outlines of future workers are taking shape. An adult lifts one in its jaws with a care quite unlike the firm grip used on a load of spoil. The brood can be moved when a chamber becomes too wet or too dry. For now, it rests where the workers have placed it, being fed, cleaned, and touched.

Deeper within is the queen. She is larger than the workers, with a heavy abdomen and the old marks of wings she no longer bears. She does not inspect the widened entrance or direct the route around the water. Workers attend her where she remains, and carry away the eggs she lays.

Much of the colony’s order is made elsewhere, by ants encountering what is immediately before them. A foot loses purchase on wet ground. Antennae find a familiar chemical trace. An entrance resists the width of a burden. A worker turns, and another reaches the same difficulty after it. Along the higher ground, repeated journeys begin to hold a route.

Outside, the other inhabitants fit themselves around this activity.

The spider under the seed husk occupies a space the ants mostly pass beside. Its legs rest against the inner curve, where shade persists even when the lip of the clearing is bright. When a carrier goes past, the spider draws inward. The husk trembles faintly under a passing touch, then becomes still again.

At the moss, springtails feed among damp particles and minute growths. One appears as a pale fleck until it jumps and is suddenly nowhere near the place being watched. Their small gathering shifts as the wet edge retreats.

The snail stays pressed behind the fern stem. Through its translucent shell, the body is a dim, coiled density. It has reached a surface that remains cool after the exposed soil begins to dry. Later, when moisture spreads farther into the hollow, it may descend. Its passage will leave no ridge and move no heap.

Under the wood flake, mites go on among the softened fibers. Their shelter is gradually being consumed around them. The dry wood above sheds dust; underneath, decay makes room for bodies almost too small to distinguish from the particles they move between.

These lives overlap without sharing the same place in quite the same way. The wet corner is an obstacle to a laden ant and a refuge to a springtail. The fern is a rooted plant, a roof, and the shaded surface against which a snail waits. The seed husk is abandoned food to one creature and a lasting shelter to another.

By evening, the ants are still coming through the entrances. A worker climbs the new ridge with nothing in its jaws, stops near the crest, and cleans an antenna between its forelegs. Beneath it, loose grains settle into the marks left by earlier feet.

It lowers the antenna, turns toward the root, and crosses ground that was not there that morning.
