At first, the only evidence of an inhabitant is that certain things have been moved against the current.

A brass washer rests on the upper side of a sloping brick. Three broken pieces of glass have been carried out of the water and laid beneath the control booth, sharp edges facing the wall. Beside the loose drive belt, someone has wedged a folded strip of copper into a gap. It has not stopped the chatter, but it has prevented the belt from walking off its wheel.

None of this work is recent enough to identify a worker. The copper has gone green. The glass is furred with scale. But the washer is warm and clean, and if you knock it into the water, it will be back on the brick after the next rise.

What lives here can reach most of the district without crossing an open floor.

During the low part of the cycle, it occupies the backs of things: the space behind a shutter, the hollow beneath a sill, the wet seam where a pipe enters masonry. Its body is thin enough to lie between the leaves of a closed valve without preventing the seal. There it resembles a pale gasket that has swollen unevenly with age.

It is not equally thin everywhere. There are thicker portions, folded into recesses, with the color and translucency of boiled root. Narrow bands run through them. These bands contract slowly, drawing one fold through another, and minute grains of black sediment travel inside the tissue as though along a crowded passage.

A patch visible behind a grille might be no larger than a hand. Another, beneath the loading platform, hangs in overlapping folds nearly six feet wide. Whether these portions join somewhere behind the brickwork is difficult to determine. The district offers many routes that a human hand cannot follow.

At the approach of the blue sector, the patches begin to withdraw.

This happens before the shutters move. A pale seam closes along a doorframe. Something slips out of the inspection well with the patient effort of wet cloth being pulled through a ring. Under the platform, the broad folds gather upward until there is only a thick, puckered edge pressed against the boards.

Then the water rises, and the inhabitant comes out beneath it.

Its upper surface adheres to the underside of the lifted water. Much of the body remains spread there, almost invisible: a faintly clouded area through which you can see the reflection of the roof, slightly displaced. Along its margins are short, blunt projections. They find the rails under the bridges and close around them.

It moves by releasing these grips in sequence.

The motion is neither swimming nor crawling. A portion of its body loosens, hangs lower, reaches forward, and attaches. The rest follows in a long, shallow contraction. Water travels above it. Beneath it, grit lies on the exposed bricks, undisturbed.

When it passes over a drain, its outline narrows to match the channel. At an intersection it broadens. For several seconds it may be stretched through three gutters at once, with a folded mass held beneath the crossing. Its shape says more about the route it is taking than about anything you could draw and label as its anatomy.

Nevertheless, there are parts it uses consistently.

Along one edge lies a row of dense, cream-colored pads. These are sensitive to vibration. It presses them against the pump casing, the retaining wall, the copper housing of a switch. Against a surface that is working properly, they rest flat. Against a failing bearing, they lift and settle one after another.

Near the pads are finer structures, normally tucked into grooves. Extended, they look like the pale roots descending from the broken gutter, except that they branch and unbranch as they reach. Several can join into a thick cord. The cord can lift a latch. Separated again, its tips can enter the threads around a bolt.

It cannot turn a stiff bolt quickly. It may spend an entire high-water interval securing its grip, then leave the work when the lever drops. At the next rise it returns to precisely the same position.

Some repairs take it weeks.

The polished brass marks above the locks are places where these pads have rested repeatedly. On the oldest doors, the marks are deep enough to catch a fingernail.

There is no evident head. When it encounters something unfamiliar, it brings a thicker fold to the edge of its body and opens it.

Inside are closely packed ridges, soft and dark, with tiny clear spaces between them. The fold does not bite. It holds objects against those ridges, sometimes for a long time, then puts them down. A brick fragment is usually released where it was found. A piece of iron is taken to the pump house. Fresh wood troubles it; the fold closes and opens, and a whitish fluid appears along its edge.

If offered a hand, it first touches the nails.

The contact is hot, but not painfully so. Its finest extensions slide along the cuticles and stop at every small break in the skin. Then the heavier fold approaches. There is a gentle pressure around the fingers, an impression of many soft combs being drawn past one another.

It lets go if you pull away.

Afterward, your hand smells of chalk, with something green underneath. For an hour or two, you can feel the pulse in each fingertip unusually well.

It feeds in the strip of vegetation along the west wall. Here the thin portions of its body spread over fallen leaves, fitting themselves to the veins. The dark tissue of a leaf disappears over several cycles. The transparent mesh remains.

It does not take every leaf. Some are drawn into the roots and left there until they have become a soft brown paste. Others are carried to the little tree in the settling tank. The inhabitant works this material into cracks around the tree’s roots with the same fine extensions it uses on machinery.

When the yellow powder falls, narrow furrows open across its upper surface. Powder gathers in them. Later, pale tips emerge among the flowers of plants in other parts of the court.

There are no insects to do this work.

Sometimes a small piece of the inhabitant stays behind in a root mass. It continues to move after the larger body has withdrawn. Over the following days it develops its own dense pads, its own folded edge. It may eventually leave by a drain. It may remain beneath the plant indefinitely.

This makes counting the district’s inhabitants impossible.

Two broad bodies have been seen approaching the same grille from opposite sides. They touched, held still while the water thickened above them, then passed through as a single narrow band. Elsewhere, a body divided around a pier and did not join again. One half went toward the pumps. The other carried a loosened roof bolt to the dry loading bay.

Their purposes can diverge. Whether their attention does is less clear.

The inhabitant is not responsible for everything that happens here. Mineral ridges accumulate where it does not go. Deep impacts sometimes travel upward through the piers, interrupting the familiar grinding of the machinery. At those moments, every exposed pad lifts from its surface.

Then the body draws tight against the nearest substantial piece of masonry.

It holds there until the disturbance has passed.

The oldest visible part lives behind the pump house, in a brick chamber reached through an upper doorway. Its folds have hardened into thick, honey-colored plates. Repairs in the chamber wall have been built around it. One iron brace passes through an opening in its body, and the edges of the opening are smooth from years of movement.

It can still extend a single soft cord.

The cord rests on a handwheel that no longer connects to anything. Occasionally it turns the wheel partway, pauses, and turns it back. The movement coincides with no reading on the pressure clocks.

The more mobile portions visit this chamber. They press their pads to the old plates and remain through several cycles. When they leave, the old cord sometimes follows them as far as the doorway.

A visitor who stays long enough will eventually be accommodated.

A loose tread will be steadied from below. Glass will disappear from the route between the steps and the booth. If you habitually stop at one particular pier, a narrow clear space will begin to appear around its foot, the fallen foliage drawn elsewhere.

During the rise, something may take hold of the underside of your shoe.

It does not pull. It braces itself between the sole and a submerged rail, holding you where you stood while the water lifts around your ankles. Through the shoe you feel a succession of small adjustments, each made before the next unevenness reaches you.

When the heavy lever falls, the grip loosens.

The water comes down. By the time you can see the paving again, the pale folds are slipping under doors, entering grilles, flattening into the warm joints of the district. One fine extension remains long enough to remove a leaf caught against your heel.

Then it too withdraws, leaving the way to the steps clear.
