The red-jointed animal is not standing on the island so much as holding itself just clear of it.

Its feet touch down at six points. Between them, the body hangs low, a flattened oval with a soft fringe that almost meets the water. The shell is greenish brown when seen from above, but in the light passing through the leaf it becomes transparent at the edges. Beneath it are paired gills, a pale gut, and a little trembling organ that continues its own quick movement between the slower knocks of the root.

The red belongs to the flexible parts of its legs. When the animal crouches, the color disappears into folds. When it rises, six small marks show where the legs bend.

It is about the size of a fingernail. Its eyes are smaller than the green centers of the islands, set close to the shell rather than raised on stalks. They distinguish the entrance, a passing body, the withdrawal of the frog’s throat into shadow. Most of what it knows arrives through its feet.

Each foot ends in two hooks and a pad of short, blunt hairs. The hooks catch in tissue that looks smooth to us. The hairs rest against it. Through them the animal feels the first tightening of a vessel behind the wall, before the valve closes and the sound enters the chamber.

At that tightening it lowers itself.

The knock follows. Water lifts against the edge of the island. Its fringe becomes wet, then drains.

It rises again.

All six animals beneath the neighboring island make the same movement, though upside down it looks like a slight drawing-in of their legs. They are not responding to one another. Each feels the pressure arrive through the plant.

The scrap between the standing animal’s front limbs is a piece of old root lining. One side is translucent; the other carries a faint brown velvet of microorganisms. It holds the scrap by its clean edge and draws the velvet through a comb beside its mouth. After each stroke it turns the piece a little. There are no large bites. The feeding leaves a series of clear windows in the brown.

When nothing remains worth scraping, the animal lets go.

The fragment catches against one foreleg. It lifts that leg and waits for the current to take it.

For most of its life, this is the scale at which obstacles occur.

It has no burrow separate from the hollow. Its resting place is a shallow notch beneath the island, just high enough for its back. There it fits with two legs folded forward and four behind, antennae lying along grooves in the living floor. The notch deepens very slowly as the animal uses it. The plant grows around the occupied space, renewing the surface without quite closing it.

The other six have similar places. From above, there is no sign of them except an occasional antenna appearing beyond the island’s edge.

They meet where two feeding circuits overlap. One pauses; the other passes partly over its shell. Sometimes their front limbs remain hooked together for several pump strokes. Then one loosens its grip, and they separate. A newly shed animal is yielded to more readily. Its shell, not yet firm, bends under a neighbor’s foot.

The animal on the island has molted recently. That is why its joints are so red and the margin of its shell so clear. Older shells become clouded by mineral deposits and a fine growth that the mouth-combs cannot reach. Before molting, an animal looks almost like a moving flake of the floor.

For a day afterward, it stays beneath its island.

The frog can take one with a single movement of its tongue. Usually, however, it feeds at the entrance, where unfamiliar insects hesitate against the warm outward breath of the chamber. The crustaceans keep close to edges. Even the one in the light has only a short body-length to cross before it can fold itself out of sight.

Its most dangerous moment is not feeding but cleaning.

Fine particles lodge among its gills. To remove them, it braces on four legs, tilts its shell, and works the remaining limbs underneath. The exposed underside is almost white. For several seconds its feet receive the root’s vibrations unevenly, and its eyes are directed into the floor.

It cleans during the wet sigh.

The flushing noise fills the chamber; the water changes direction around the island; the frog presses its throat closer to its recess. In that interval the animal reaches under its shell and draws out a thread of trapped fibers. By the time the sigh ends, it is flat again.

Beneath the next island, the largest of the six carries young.

They are held against her underside in a pouch made by overlapping plates. Through the thin plates their bodies show as a close collection of pale commas. They have no red yet. Their legs move whenever she lifts herself into the current.

At first they will feed beneath her, taking particles loosened by her mouthparts. Later they will occupy the narrow clearance between her shell and the roof of her resting notch. There is room there for bodies too small to be noticed from the entrance.

Not all will stay.

As they grow, some will follow the flow toward the rear seam, past the white feeding mouths. They can flatten enough to enter passages closed to the adults. For a while they will live inside the root, grazing the outer surfaces of filters where water moves gently between flushes. Most will turn back at the first strong change in pressure. A few will continue through the low, branching spaces beneath the plain.

The inhabitants of the hollow are therefore not quite a family, though several are related. One arrived during a long rain, already carrying the dull shell of an adult. It emerged from the rear passage with one antenna missing and spent its first hours gripping a column below the water. By the following day it had found an unoccupied fold.

The oldest animal lives there still. Its missing antenna has grown back shorter and bends inward at the tip. It feeds more slowly than the others, but it is the first to move when the plant alters its working rhythm.

Now a valve catches imperfectly.

The sound is scarcely different: a knock with a brief second contact inside it.

The old animal comes out of its fold. The six beneath the island draw closer to the floor. In the light, the red-jointed one stops turning another scrap.

For two cycles, none of them feeds.

Behind the wall, a swelling of new tissue presses against the valve’s worn margin. The next closure is clean. The shallow current resumes its usual turn around the island.

The old animal retreats. Antennae extend again into the water.

The one in the light brings the scrap back to its mouth, but holds it there a moment longer before beginning to comb.
