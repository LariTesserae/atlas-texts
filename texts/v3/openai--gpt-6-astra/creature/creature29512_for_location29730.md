What remains of the inhabitants is most distinct at the thresholds.

On the sheltered side of certain doorposts, the tissue is folded into a short stack of blunt lips. They are easily mistaken for scars left by training the root upright. But the folds occur at the same height in houses of different ages, and each stack faces another across the entrance. When a vibration travels through the street, the two sides receive it at slightly different times. The difference passes down through the doorposts into the floor.

This is still how a house knows that something has approached it.

Nothing has approached for a very long time. The thresholds receive the bridge, the shell, the settling roofs. They distinguish these events with a precision that has survived the loss of any use for it. A crack in the next court produces one sequence of minute contractions; a crack beyond the terraces produces another. The contractions are too small to close a passage. Sometimes they are too small to complete themselves before the cold arrests them.

An entrance can spend an age halfway through recognizing a sound.

The inhabitants were once capable of gathering themselves into bodies. The hollows in the floors mark the places where this happened. Around each hollow, a ring of tissue supplied a different part: stiff fibers for support, folded membranes for joints, bundles of rootlets that could close around a splinter. No two bodies were quite alike. A narrow house produced a narrow inhabitant. In a district rich in old leaf tissue, the builders had broad, overlapping backs. Those raised near the shell were short and heavily braced, with several points of contact beneath them.

They did not carry the whole of themselves when they went out.

Much remained in the floor: reserves, unfinished repairs, the slow processes by which one interval could be compared with another. A builder crossing a court drew a fine connection behind it. These connections passed through grooves at the feet of walls, under raised crossings, around the rims of public stairs. Some of what now appears to be reinforcement was laid down to keep a person continuous with home.

Distance was consequently an expense. To go farther required more of oneself to be drawn into a thread.

The polished passages were the routes along which this expense had been reduced. Many inhabitants used them, letting their connections lie together in protected channels. At difficult corners, one would hold the gathered fibers away from an abrasive edge while the others passed. The shallow recesses beside such corners are worn smooth at several heights.

There were places where a body could stand for a long time doing only this.

When the wax ceased to move, the inhabitants returned to their hollows. They loosened their grips, lowered themselves into the feeding rings, and allowed the parts that had been held apart for walking to grow together again. An arm became a thickening in the floor. The folded support beneath a torso straightened into a root. What had been carried upright settled into the house.

For many stoppages, they emerged afterward.

Then there were fewer reasons to emerge, and less material with which to do it. A stair could be repaired from beneath. A wall could be drawn upright by tightening fibers already attached to it. The inhabitants learned to accomplish work without assembling all the organs of a worker.

Their last bodies were partial. A grip beside a tool. A joint raised far enough to shift a brace. A small, hard surface pushed out of a wall to scrape its neighbor.

The open rootlets in the houses are not quite abandoned hands. They are hands whose owners found a cheaper way to wait.

Now an inhabitant occupies an uneven region of connected tissue, sometimes smaller than a room, sometimes extending under several streets. Its boundaries are marked by old grafts. Across these seams, salts pass reluctantly. A tremor travels freely, but the response on one side differs from the response on the other.

This is enough to preserve a distinction.

One house thickens when the shell’s note deepens. The house beside it sheds a dry layer. Farther along the same wall, a third does nothing until the note rises again. These habits originated in bodies, in the different loads they carried and the ways they braced themselves. They persist in the rooms the bodies have become.

The inhabitants remember chiefly through what they are prepared to do.

A low wall retains the capacity to bend away from a burden that no longer exists. Beneath a public stair, a bundle of fibers is arranged to tighten in succession, lifting something from one level to the next. The steps have worn into a slope, but the succession remains. Given enough warmth and material, it would begin at the bottom.

There are also preparations involving more than one inhabitant. Whole courts are connected by them. One district still waits for a pull from a district that has become almost entirely dead tissue. The receiving fibers remain sound. They have been maintained through losses elsewhere, although the answering end has long since broken into grit.

This waiting takes substance. It is among the city’s continuing costs.

The bridge belongs to such an arrangement.

Its note reaches a set of roots beneath the neighboring houses, where narrow bands of tissue respond to the pitch. When the deck slackens, the bands contract. Their contraction opens the channels leading toward the bridge. Material moves into the supports until the pitch returns to the range at which the channels close.

The mechanism was established when the fissure was wider and the crossing served many streets.

Now the houses supply it with themselves.

They do not experience the bridge as an object competing with them for nourishment. Its tension runs through their oldest shared connections. To let it fail would require them to interrupt the process by which they still recognize that this part of the city holds together. Each house gives a little. Each becomes less able to give anything else.

There may once have been an inhabitant whose work was to judge such arrangements and undo them when they had outlasted their purpose. A recess beneath the bridge contains a hooked thorn laid across three severed fibers. The thorn’s inside edge is polished.

Nothing remains there capable of lifting it.

In the basin, life has become less particular.

Fragments arrive from every district. A grain worn from a threshold may retain several joined cells and the arrangement by which they responded to pressure. The plates close over it. Its resistant layers are softened; its salts enter the roots below. The arrangement comes apart.

Afterward, some small portion of a house may thicken a lintel at the other end of the hollow. It carries no knowledge of its former doorway. The basin is thorough in this respect. What it returns is usable.

The inhabitants nearest it have consequently changed the most. Their old boundaries have softened under repeated nourishment. A contraction begun beneath one court can finish beneath another without crossing a distinct seam. Several former households now share a single slow response to the falling dust.

They are well supplied, by the standards of the hollow. They make few preparations. When a particle lands, there is time to grow toward it.

Along the sheltered crease, the youngest tissue has never made a doorway.

Its thinness allows a tremor to travel some distance without being divided among roots and walls. A report from a roof passes through it as a broad disturbance. The sheet yields, recovers, and retains a minute difference in thickness along the path the disturbance took. Later sounds encounter that difference.

Over immense intervals, the sheet has acquired an internal grain.

One direction carries the bridge clearly. Another favors the deep shifts of the shell. Near the advancing edge, several such directions cross, and there the tissue is slightly thicker. It receives sounds from the city through a structure that those sounds have helped to make.

As it reaches the first foundations, it grows into their irregularities. It covers an old feeding hollow. It enters the groove where an inhabitant’s connection once lay. The tissue beneath is still capable of responding, and occasionally it does: a buried ring tightens; the sheet puckers around it.

For a while, there is the beginning of a shape.

A ridge rises, supplied from both sides. Its tip divides into several short lobes. One presses against the edge of a fallen splinter and remains there through many episodes of noise. The city still contains enough of its former bodies to suggest a grip.

But the sheet continues growing. It spreads the available material sideways, filling the hollow, laying a thin skin over the splinter. The lobes lose their separation.

Whatever is becoming distinct along the crease has no use yet for a hand.

It is sensitive to the streets before it reaches them. Their vibrations arrive through the floor, each altered by the tissues that have endured there. When the bridge sings, a long band draws taut within the sheet. When the houses beside the bridge settle, the band remains taut a little longer.

At its edge, one flattened cell encloses a particle rubbed loose from a vanished step. Nearby, an old threshold begins its small answering contraction.

The two motions share a root now.
