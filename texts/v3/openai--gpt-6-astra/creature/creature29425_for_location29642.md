The woman waiting for the wall to open has taken off one shoe.

She stands with her bare foot against the seam, her knee turned outward, holding a bowl of dinner in one hand. Her other shoe is a heavy thing with a split sole and a fastening that can be released under tension. There is a pale band around her ankle where it usually grips. Above it, her skin is brown, freckled, and marked with the small round scars common among people who work in filter galleries.

Her name is Sere. She is not listening for the wall to open. That will happen when the pressure on its other side falls sufficiently. She is feeling for a second vibration beneath the first, an irregular knocking that would mean someone has left a freight coupling loose inside.

She finds it. She closes her eyes, irritated.

Behind her, three other members of the crew continue eating.

They are recognizable as people before they are recognizable as anything else: tired, hungry, dressed differently for the same weather. One has shaved his head except for a stiff patch above each ear. One wears a necklace of polished valve teeth that keeps slipping into her bowl. The youngest has brought a dessert and is trying to eat it without being asked to share.

Their bodies are well suited to wet footing, though not so well suited that they trust it. Their toes spread widely under weight. The skin of their palms and soles furrows within moments of immersion, deepening the grip. A thin inner lid protects each eye when they put their faces into the water, but they cannot see far through suspended silt. They have no gills. They drown.

A grown person can close their nostrils, slow their breathing, and work for a little while beneath a hull. Some can do it for much longer. These people attract admiration, bets, and, eventually, employment offers that should be refused.

Sere used to be one of them.

Now she has a stiffness beneath her left ribs, and when the crew needs someone to go under, she takes charge of the line.

She puts her shoe back on.

“Coupling,” she says.

The youngest shuts his dessert box.

***

It would be easy to imagine that the inhabitants of this place are uniformly patient, sensitive to minute changes, reconciled to the movement of everything they own.

They are not.

They slam doors whose frames have altered. They paint lines across floors to prove that a neighbor’s partition is encroaching. They wedge furniture against a wall that has been formally instructed to thicken. A shopkeeper in Sere’s street has spent eleven years resisting a necessary buttress because it would obstruct the view of his sign.

There are people who cannot sleep without the foundation noise, and people who have spent half their earnings trying to exclude it from one room.

Their oldest common technology is probably the wedge.

They make wedges of ceramic, metal, dense shell, and a tough dark material grown in pressure molds. They use them to hold shutters, isolate a vibrating bed, correct the tilt of a stove, or delay a surface that is beginning to close. A good household keeps them sorted by angle. An untidy household keeps them in a bowl beside the door and argues about where they have gone.

Children steal the smallest ones to make whistles.

The sounds do not carry far. Few sounds made by an unaided person do. Intimacy has therefore acquired a practical posture: shoulder toward shoulder, mouth beside ear, one hand resting where it can feel the other person begin to answer.

Strangers adopt this posture when exchanging directions. Lovers sometimes stand farther apart so that everyone will know they are quarreling.

***

Sere lives with her father, her sister’s son, and a man who was once her husband and now rents the room above the kitchen.

This arrangement is neither exemplary nor particularly unusual. The man has a good hand with the household plumbing. Her father likes him. Moving would involve finding another room at the same elevation, transferring his entitlement to the local baths, and transporting a collection of fragile recording plates through six changes of vessel.

He and Sere are on reasonable terms except in hot weather.

Her father rarely goes down to the street now. His feet have lost much of their sensitivity, and the bells reach him as a vague discomfort without direction. He keeps a cup filled nearly to the brim on the arm of his chair. When the surface trembles, he looks at the wall indicator.

He once maintained the charged beds where the district’s food films grow. He can tell by smell whether a batch has been harvested too late. At dinner he offers this judgment even when nobody has asked.

The boy, Iven, is nine. He has a scar along his chin from being struck by a floating cupboard.

He is not afraid of water. He is afraid of cupboards.

He will swim under a landing to retrieve a dropped tool, but if a tall cabinet creaks in the night he goes upstairs to sleep beside his grandfather. No one mocks him for this. Every household contains an object that someone cannot bear to hear move.

Their rooms are kept as dry as they can afford.

That qualification matters. Warm water is pleasant against the skin for an hour; it is less pleasant in bedding for a week. The inhabitants suffer from swollen joints, infections between the fingers, persistent coughs, and patches of pale fungus beneath clothing. They dry their shoes over kitchen vents. They inspect children’s ears. They pay more for powder that does not immediately clump.

The richest residents are not necessarily the highest. Some live in old, well-balanced chambers below the water, where pumps and seals have independent supplies and the temperature barely changes. But everyone knows what height buys in an ordinary house.

A rise of one stair can improve a marriage.

***

Iven attends school in rooms above a freight basin. His class moves twice a year because the basin changes function.

The children learn reading, calculation, maintenance signs, and the distinction between a closure that will stop for a person and a closure that will not. They practice escaping from a tilted room in the public baths. They are taught to abandon a bag before it becomes heavy.

They also learn songs, arguments about ancient rulers, the names of animals they will probably never see, and a complicated game played with counters that has been forbidden at three neighboring schools.

Their teacher dislikes the assumption that living safely is a sufficient education.

On clear afternoons she takes them onto the roof and asks them to describe something far away. At first they report signs, laundry, people on bridges. She makes them look beyond those.

One child says a storm is dragging its feet.

Another says the weather tower resembles his aunt’s strainer.

Iven usually reports vessels. He wants to work on a long-route carrier. His grandfather disapproves because carriers are dangerous. Sere disapproves because Iven’s idea of the work consists entirely of standing at the bow while other people handle the cargo.

Nevertheless, she brings home obsolete route plates for him.

He traces journeys that cross months of rain patterns without leaving the built plain. On some routes, the people at the far end count age by a different flood cycle. Their everyday words are strange, though warning calls remain intelligible. Their clothes fasten differently. They cook the same broad-backed grazers with sour mineral sauces instead of pepper.

Iven wants to meet someone who has never heard of his district.

Sere, who has spent years trying to persuade distant offices that her district exists, finds this ambition difficult to understand.

***

There are animals in their rooms, though none that they consider pets.

Small six-legged inspectors pass through apertures near the floor. They are soft-bodied, with horny shields over their heads and a fringe of sensitive bristles along each side. The city breeds them—or maintains the conditions in which they breed; the distinction depends on whom one asks. They enter cavities too narrow for devices and return to feeding stations where the substances caught in their bristles can be sampled.

Sere’s father leaves scraps beside one aperture.

This is prohibited. It draws the inspectors away from their routes.

He insists that he feeds only a retired individual.

There is, admittedly, one with a cracked shield that spends much of the afternoon beneath his chair. When disturbed, it makes a dry clicking sound entirely out of proportion to its size.

The birds are less welcome indoors. They steal fats, tear screening, and carry bedding material out through open windows. A nesting pair can dismantle a cushion in minutes.

Yet people recognize particular birds by the damage to their feet or the shapes of their pale facial patches. Vendors throw scraps to favorites while loudly denying that they do so. A bird that has waited through several floods on the same railing becomes part of the directions to a house.

The wall-grazers inspire a different feeling.

They are large enough to injure a swimmer without noticing. Their mouths are broad muscular pads studded with hard scraping ridges. Up close, they smell of warm hide and the sour films they eat. When one surfaces beneath a walkway, its breathing slits open with an intimate, fleshy sound.

People lean down to touch them.

Some grazers tolerate it; some roll away. An old female near Sere’s landing will rest her muzzle against a submerged step while children scrub the parasites from the folds behind her jaw. She has carried a broken length of mooring ring embedded in one shoulder for longer than Sere has lived there.

When she disappears for several days, the adults check the outfall temperatures before they let the children swim.

***

The inhabitants disagree about what the city is.

There are districts where an opening wall is greeted aloud, and districts where speaking to a wall marks you as provincial. Engineers distinguish carefully between responsive material, colony behavior, and decision. Other people use the same word for all three.

Sere knows a mason who believes every foundation has a temperament. She has watched him diagnose faults more accurately than an instrument crew. She has also watched him lose a week trying to flatter a blocked return channel.

Her own position is practical. A wall may or may not know she is there. She marks her presence anyway.

Before entering a cavity, she presses a patterned plate against the threshold. Before leaving, she retrieves it. If the surface starts to close too soon, she does not plead. She inserts a wedge and calls for help.

Still, she sometimes thanks it.

Not in front of apprentices.

The city feeds them, but it does not reliably spare them. Its responses occur at different scales. A room can be crushed to preserve a block. A block can remain intact while water is sent through it to save a pressure trunk serving millions. Decisions made by people are often hidden among these physical necessities.

After a flood, residents ask which gates opened.

Then they ask who authorized them.

There are memorials built into the landings: names cut into inert plaques that cannot heal over. Some are surrounded by official seals. Some have been installed without permission, and the people living nearby prevent their removal.

Sere touches one each morning on the way to work. Two names. Her mother and a neighbor who went back with a rope.

On mornings when she is late, she touches it quickly.

***

Tonight the loose coupling is found before the wall finishes opening. The crew secures it, replaces a worn sleeve, and argues about whether the wear should have been detected on the last inspection.

Sere gets home with her dinner cold.

Her father is asleep. Her former husband is upstairs playing a recording of someone singing in a room so quiet that every intake of breath can be heard. Iven has left his route plates spread across the kitchen table.

She stacks them, washes, and sits down.

Through the open doorway she can see a few meters of canal, the opposite wall, and someone’s bare legs on a landing. A bowl is lowered from above on a cord. Someone below puts something into it. The bowl rises.

The handrail begins to tremble.

Sere lifts her feet from the floor to judge how much of the vibration is coming through the chair. Then she sets them down again. Routine diversion, somewhere beyond the next basin.

In his sleep, her father moves a hand toward the cup.

Upstairs, the singer stops to breathe.

Sere pulls Iven’s largest route plate back across the table. There is a place he has circled, very far away, where three weather regions meet. She has never been there. She studies the connections while she eats, trying to work out how long the journey would take.
