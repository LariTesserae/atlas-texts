The one beneath the awning has seen you.

This becomes apparent when you shift your head to spare your cheek from the cold. The tray remains level, but the face above it turns by precisely the same amount. You have been mistaken about where its eyes are. What looked like a dark fold in a hood is a band of very fine lashes, nearly meeting across the bridge of the nose. Beneath them, two gray eyes watch you without much visible white.

The tray holds six slices of something translucent, each no larger than a grass seed. The inhabitant has been carrying them into the light to dry.

For the moment, neither of you moves.

They are not quite people made small. Their bodies are long through the ribs and short through the legs, with shoulders that can draw inward remarkably far. Standing, they give an impression of being bundled against the weather; passing through a doorway, they flatten and lengthen, one shoulder preceding the other. Their low rooms allow them to sit upright but seldom to raise both arms. Most work is done in the lap.

Their hands are large. Four fingers lie together on one side, with a short opposing digit set well back toward the wrist. The palm has a pale, ridged pad. They use this to hold a vessel against the body while the fingers perform some other task: loosen a stopper, separate a thread, touch the wrist of someone passing.

The soles of their feet have the same pale surface. There is a slit in the bottom of each soft shoe through which a little of it touches the ground.

Even while seated, even with a foot tucked beneath them, they generally keep some part of themselves against the masonry.

You might take this for caution. It is also how they attend to what is happening elsewhere. Someone crossing the bridge can be recognized by the sequence of weight and release. A dropped utensil is known several houses away. When the person on the stair presses the heel of a hand against the wall, another inhabitant appears at an upper window and looks down.

The sounds of the lane are much less legible. They arrive immense and without useful detail. A wheel, a boot, a barrel put down hard: each can briefly overwhelm all the small information traveling through the town.

After the cart passes, the inhabitants do more than resume their work. Several first touch a neighboring wall or step. One waits with a palm resting against the bone-supported lintel.

Only when these contacts have been made does the basket rise again.

***

Their skin, where it shows, has the color of the inside of a hazelnut shell. At the knuckles and throat it becomes almost translucent. Older inhabitants have darker specks along their forearms, and sometimes a white, polished patch where a lifetime of carrying has thickened the skin.

They have hair, though little of it grows long. There is a close nap over the scalp, the backs of the shoulders, and the outer forearms. Children look furred until they put on their first proper clothes.

Clothing is pieced rather than woven whole. A good coat may contain felted plant down, strips of softened inner bark, and a membrane peeled from some larger seed. Its patches do not conceal one another. Each is cut to leave the sound material around the damage intact.

Fastenings sit toward the front, where they cannot catch against a passage wall. Nothing hangs loose. A person taking off a coat does so carefully enough to suggest that the coat has responsibilities beyond keeping its owner warm.

It may have a pocket for a damp wick. It may carry seed oil in a little sealed bladder. Inside the breast of one garment, a cutting is wrapped against the wearer’s skin.

They are warm, though not very warm. In this weather, a hand withdrawn from a sleeve soon becomes clumsy. Tasks requiring fine movements are shared between two workers, one keeping their hands tucked away while the other works. Without discussion, they exchange places.

The slow movements you first notice are partly chosen and partly imposed. Once, a shaving catches under the stone blade. The seated worker snatches a finger back so quickly that you lose sight of the hand.

Then they sit holding it.

Another inhabitant comes out, examines the finger, and returns indoors. The injured one waits with an expression you have no difficulty recognizing.

***

There are children here, but you initially count them as adults farther away.

One is carrying a length of hollow stem through a passage. It is too long to turn at the corner. The child tries, backs up, tries again, then stands considering the same arrangement as though the passage might eventually concede.

An adult watches from a doorway.

At last the child raises one end, feeds it through an open window, and brings the other around. The watching adult steps aside. The stem continues on its journey.

Children are given work whose failure can be recovered from. They sort flakes, gather trimmed leaves, and carry empty containers. They learn early which moss may be trodden on, which pale crust is salt, which seemingly dead twig must be left alone.

They also do things for which there is no apparent household purpose. Under a stair, three rounded stones have been balanced one above another. Someone has scratched a face on the bottom one. The mouth is enormous.

A nearby wall bears a row of marks, mostly rubbed away. Each new mark is slightly higher than the last.

***

The town cannot be enlarged, but households change.

A room becomes available when someone dies, or joins another household, or can no longer manage a stair. These alterations occupy them intensely. The broad room nearest the reflected daylight is desirable; so is a darker one with a wall that remains dry. A house with two entrances can occasion years of disagreement.

You can see one such disagreement preserved in the architecture. An upper walkway has been interrupted by a partition. Beside it, an exceedingly inconvenient new stair descends, turns, and climbs again. Both are well maintained.

They are capable of spending considerable labor to avoid one another.

Elsewhere, two houses share a sill worn smooth by the passage of bowls.

Possessions accumulate histories that are not always agreeable. Someone remembers giving the clay pot. Someone else remembers repairing it twice. The person who uses it now would prefer a lighter pot but cannot discard this one without reopening a conversation older than their children.

Their faces are mobile at close range. Annoyance draws the lashes down into a single dark line. Pleasure opens the mouth a little and lifts the skin beside the nose. Laughter exists here as a quick movement in the chest, often followed by a hand placed on somebody else.

They do speak. At first you cannot separate it from the minute friction of sleeves and leaves. Their voices are low, breathy, and used at distances of a few inches. A conversation across a room requires someone to get up.

Consequently, approaching another person carries meaning. So does remaining where you are.

***

The oldest inhabitant you see is sitting beside the door below the square.

Their coat is large at the shoulders and folded under their knees. One foot rests directly on the threshold. The other is wrapped, its shape obscured by layers of pale material.

The green shoot beside them has two mature leaves and a third still folded along its length.

From time to time, the old inhabitant turns the vessel slightly. The movement brings a different part of the leaf into the reflected light. They inspect the ties without adjusting them. Once, they scrape a trace of something from the rim with a fingernail.

People stop there on their way past. Some bend close to speak. One puts down a parcel and leaves without being acknowledged. Later, the old inhabitant opens it and eats.

The shoot might be rare. It might belong to someone absent. It might simply be difficult to establish a plant in that particular pot, against that particular wall.

Whatever its importance, the old inhabitant has become impatient with one of its leaves. They lift it, look beneath it, and let it fall. A few moments later they lift it again.

***

By the time you withdraw, the six translucent slices have been set out beneath the awning.

Their owner has not returned indoors. They stand with the empty tray held against their middle, watching the entrance. Behind them, someone else waits in the doorway, too far back for you to distinguish a face.

Your movement admits a little more light and a little more air.

The wren feather bends. Along the nearest roof, several leaves lift at their edges.

The inhabitant puts down the tray and approaches the feather. With both hands, they draw its separated barbs closer together. They pause to feel the air against one palm, then close another small interval.

It takes several adjustments.

They are still working when your shadow leaves the opening.
