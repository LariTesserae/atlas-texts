Sometimes a leaf remains bent after the thing that brushed it has gone.

Not for a moment, as leaves do, but through a whole change of light. The stem bears no injury. The water has resumed its ordinary course. Yet the leaf keeps the shallow impression of something resting against it, and smaller leaves nearby gradually turn to accommodate the same absence.

There is usually a second sign. Beneath the bent leaf, the stone is cleaner than it should be. Not scraped clean, as it is after the passage of the white grazers, but clean in a pattern: narrow lanes between untouched islands of film. The lanes meet. At their meeting lies a grain of black sand.

Wait there, without touching anything.

Eventually the grain moves uphill.

What moves it is difficult to separate from the water. At first there is only a slight doubling of the root fibers behind it, as though you were looking through a warmer current. Then the doubling gathers an edge. A broad, transparent fold advances over the stone, carrying the grain in a pocket along its underside. More folds follow. They pass around the roots without disturbing them, except for one leaf that bows and stays bowed.

The whole creature may take an hour to cross your hand. You will feel several cool lines, a mild pull at the nails, and once, briefly, something hard.

It is not flat everywhere. Most of its substance is gathered into low ridges that can be drawn together or spread far apart. Between them lie films almost indistinguishable from the sea. In a clearing it becomes a broad, irregular disk. Among closely packed stems it stretches into separate ribbons joined by threads too fine to see. It can occupy both sides of a leaf without going around its edge; one thin part passes through a breathing pore and widens again on the other surface.

This takes time. Much of its movement does.

Along the oldest ridges, an individual may extend through several acres of forest. Almost all of it will be folded against other surfaces. The same creature that touches a seed veil high in the canopy may have a portion beneath a loose stone, another lining a hollow, and a thick, faintly amber knot sheltered inside an empty shell.

That knot is the easiest part to recognize as alive. Cloudy material moves through it in slow pulses. Small mineral plates turn within the cloudiness, fitting together and coming apart. There are usually seven or eight, though injured creatures may have only two, and very old ones carry so many that the knot no longer closes completely.

The plates are not bones. The creature collected them.

They begin as ordinary grains from the barrens. It sorts thousands, keeping very few. Some are carried for a season and discarded. Others are worked against the stone until they acquire a particular curve, then brought inward through the folds. A creature deprived of its plates continues to feed and move, but repeatedly enters passages too narrow for it. It may spend a long time opening a route through roots, only to abandon the route before passing through.

Give the plates back, and it examines each one before accepting it.

Its sense of place resides partly in these worn objects, though not in any simple correspondence between a stone and a remembered ridge. A plate held near a warm seam turns in one direction. The same plate, held there after a storm, may turn in another. The creature presses several together, separates them, sends a tremor outward through its films. Far away, a fold lifts from a leaf.

It knows where it is by finding what still agrees.

This is useful in a country where a valley can move and a current can arrive before the water that carries it.

It feeds on the pale films of the warm hollows. Its lower surface opens into minute chambers, each taking in a little of the growth and closing around it. It also catches spores, but not indiscriminately. Some are digested. Some are carried to fresh stone. Others remain inside it for years, developing only as far as a root tip and a single folded leaf.

These arrested seedlings give older creatures their color. A young one is nearly clear. Later there are olive flecks, copper threads, patches the dull purple of a bruise. An ancient individual can be mistaken for a bank of vegetation loosened by a storm. Only when it lifts does it become apparent that the plants are growing inside a common skin.

They are not all useful passengers. Roots sometimes escape their chambers and begin to bind the creature from within. It responds by drawing warmth into the affected place until the growth dies, or by abandoning that part of itself. Thus a little grove may remain where an animal once rested. Beneath the grove are clear, empty channels in the shape of its former folds.

The white creatures under the forest are their young.

They are opaque because they carry food densely packed through their bodies, and compact because they have not yet learned to remain alive at a distance from themselves. Their opening and closing is both locomotion and practice. Each widening exposes a little more membrane to the water. Each contraction brings it safely back.

Many never spread farther than the width of a hand. They are eaten by the closed shells, or enter rain pools whose skins will admit them but not release them. Some settle on stone too cool to sustain the films they need. Their whiteness thins. Green threads colonize the places they have cleaned.

Those that survive begin to leave parts of themselves behind.

At first this seems accidental: a strip caught on a broken stem, a milky smear beneath a pebble. But the abandoned part remains connected. Food passes back along a thread. Sensation returns. The young creature moves farther away, then stops and spends several days drawing the thread thicker.

Its first territory is not much larger than a fallen leaf.

There is no particular age at which it becomes an adult. There is, instead, the first occasion on which two distant parts of its body require incompatible movements, and it manages to do both.

Near the old crossings, this becomes more complicated.

A creature entering a divided current may follow every route. Unlike a leaf, it does not always rejoin neatly. One returning portion may be starved, another heavy with food; a third may carry a mature seedling that the others have not yet encountered. The portions lie beside one another, touching along their edges. For a long time nothing seems to happen.

Then chambers begin to open between them.

They exchange dissolved food, living cells, and sometimes whole mineral plates. If the differences are small, they join. If the differences are too great, one portion moves away.

The two creatures will still respond to some of the same things. A pressure change may cause both to contract in the same unusual pattern. They may seek shelter in hollows of the same shape. But when they meet again, they pause at each other’s edges as strangers do.

There are regions near the crossings where almost every inhabitant was once part of the same traveler.

They do not gather there out of affection. Often they avoid one another. Their needs are too similar, and each recognizes suitable shelter at almost the same instant as the others. The stone in such places is covered with elaborate, competing arrangements of pebbles.

Most of these arrangements are temporary. A line directs a thread of cooler water toward a feeding surface. Three grains keep a leaf from sealing against the bottom. A ring protects a newly released seedling from the sweep of a migrating root mat.

But some works persist.

The low enclosures were made by creatures that had become too large to withdraw completely into shells. Over many dim seasons they brought stones together around the thickest parts of their bodies. The walls reduced the strain of passing currents. The sloping floors guided food-bearing water inward.

They did not need roofs. They needed exits everywhere.

What looks like a single widened doorway is usually the remainder of several openings whose intervening walls have worn away.

The dark basins inside are harder to explain. Some contain only water enriched by long residence. Others hold a membrane pressed so closely against the stone that it has acquired the stone’s fine irregularities. An inhabitant may still be there, too thin to lift, drawing nourishment from the plants rooted through it.

Such a creature cannot travel, but it can receive visitors.

A mobile one enters the enclosure, extends a fold into the basin, and waits. Sometimes it leaves a grain. Sometimes it takes one. Sometimes, after a long contact, it tears away a narrow strip of its own living surface and fits it into a crack beside the resting body.

This is not always enough. There are basins where the visitors continue to come after nothing remains capable of receiving them. New strips adhere to old ones. The stone grows a delicate lining. Eventually something stirs there.

Whether it is the former inhabitant, or its offspring, or several visitors continuing a habit together is not a distinction their bodies make easily.

They are capable of hostility.

A large individual can seal the channels through a thicket and keep another from feeding. It can concentrate the bitter residues of digestion along a shared boundary. Fights proceed as slow changes in the landscape: leaves paling along a front, roots exposed, a warm pool diverted until one combatant is forced to contract.

The loser may abandon years of growth.

There are also encounters in which two creatures maintain a still seam between them without closing it. Spores accumulate there. Young films feed along its margins. Each inhabitant continually adjusts its body to prevent the other’s current from overwhelming the seam.

These partnerships can outlast the forest around them.

They communicate through that adjustment. A widening here, a delayed contraction there. A small packet of mineral-rich water passed across a boundary and accepted without being broken. What they exchange is probably most precise concerning warmth, pressure, hunger, and the routes available through nearby stone. There is no reason to suppose it remains simple.

At the seven upright stones, an arriving creature places a fold in each interval. The little current that pauses from stone to stone passes through its body.

Afterward, it sometimes goes somewhere it has never been.

Near the outer rim, their bodies grow short and thick. There is too little forest to support broad extensions, and the bending water pulls differently at adjoining folds. They anchor themselves among the close leaves and keep their mineral plates packed tightly together.

They can sense the storms beneath the stone. Lightning changes something in the mineral, briefly and unevenly. Every point of contact brings a different part of the disturbance. The creatures spread against the bottom to receive it.

This is why the fine roots tighten around your wrists.

Some are roots. Others are clear living cords, lying among them.

The inhabitant beneath you has encountered your weight, your warmth, and the unfamiliar pulse traveling through your hands. It has opened small chambers against your skin and closed them again without feeding. Now, as the storm brightens below, it is securing everything that might shift.

A leaf turns over beside your face. Under it lies a polished black grain in a transparent pocket.

The pocket opens.

For a while the grain rests against your palm, held between your body and the warm stone. Then a clear fold reaches back beneath your fingers and takes it away. All along the rim, other folds are drawing inward. The leaves settle lower. The water begins to pull.

Where your wrist has sheltered it, the creature leaves one narrow passage open a little longer.
