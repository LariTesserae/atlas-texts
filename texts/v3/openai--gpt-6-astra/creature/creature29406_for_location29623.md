The inhabitant is usually on the other side of a small rise.

There are not many rises, and none is high enough to conceal something of its size. Nevertheless, you see a shoulder go out of sight, or the back of a narrow head, and when you walk around the swelling in the stone there is only a damp place. The grazers are moving through it. One has climbed onto a footprint and is scraping something from the rim.

It is not avoiding you. Later it will spend most of an hour beside you, absorbed in opening a blocked seam. But at first you are looking for it where it has been, and that is not quite where its body is.

Watch the rain instead.

Among the suspended drops there is sometimes a taller interval, an interruption roughly the shape of a person leaning forward. Drops move around that interval before there is anything visible inside it. Then two feet acquire edges against the ground. A long hand appears, palm upward, with a bead of rain resting in one of its creases.

The rest takes a little longer.

It stands no higher than your chest. Its torso is deep from front to back and very narrow across the shoulders, so that seen from the side it looks substantial, almost burdensome, while from behind it seems scarcely wider than its spine. The spine itself shows through a row of shallow hollows. Warm moisture collects in them.

Its skin is gray where it has been exposed for a long time. Under the arms, at the throat, and in the folds above the feet, it is the tender pink of the seams. Small plates lie embedded in the gray skin, not overlapping like the grazers’ armor but spaced apart, each with room to enlarge. On its left shoulder, two plates have grown together. It cannot lift that arm as high as the other.

You might take this for age. Then it turns, and you see that its right flank is almost transparent.

There are no recognizable organs there. Pale cords descend through a clear thickness, dividing around little chambers. A bright speck travels down one cord and stops. Farther in, something closes slowly. You are seeing a part of the body that has not yet become opaque.

It notices your attention and rests its hand against the place. Not to hide it. The pressure seems to relieve an ache.

Its face takes longest to become familiar. There are two dark eyes, close together and set low, beneath a broad forehead with no sharp boundary between head and neck. The eyes are quite small. What first looks like a mouth is a horizontal fold that opens and shuts with its breathing. Its actual mouth is beneath the chin, soft-edged and capable of extending a little way.

When it feeds, it kneels.

It presses that mouth to the cream-colored film on fresh stone and draws a clean oval into it. Then it moves one handspan and feeds again. There is nothing ceremonial about this. Sometimes it eats too quickly and has to sit back, the breathing fold working hard. Sometimes a grazer enters the patch it has chosen. It waits for the grazer to pass, or nudges it sideways with the back of a finger.

Its hands are better suited to nudging than gripping. There are three long fingers and a short, broad inner digit, all padded, all capable of bending farther backward than you expect. Along the inside of each forearm is a row of fine stiff bristles. It uses these to strain things from water.

At the western pool it lowers an arm and draws it slowly toward itself. The transparent hunters gather beneath the moving shadow. One rises far enough to put its mouth around a finger.

The inhabitant waits.

After a moment the hunter releases it. Caught among the forearm bristles are threads of cream film, two shed wings, and a little black mass that contracts when touched. It picks out the black mass and eats it. The rest it wipes onto a damp ledge.

By morning, something will be feeding there.

***

It does not wear the coat.

From outside the shelter, the coat resembles its back: narrow shoulders, a long central fold, two uneven hanging portions where arms ought to enter. In certain light it looks less like clothing than a skin left to dry. The inhabitant occasionally pauses outside the doorway and examines it.

Inside, beneath the bare hook, it rubs its shoulder against the wall.

There is a polished patch at that height. Below it are older polished patches, descending almost to the floor.

The dry square is where it sleeps, although sleep is not quite what happens there. It folds its legs beneath its torso, sets both hands flat, and remains with its eyes open. The visible edges of its body become unusually definite. Even the clear flank gains a faint, firm boundary.

For the rest of the day its outline is troublesome. A finger may seem longer before it touches something than afterward. You can look directly at one foot and be certain it has three toes, then find four impressions when it lifts. In the square there is no such uncertainty. You could draw it.

It rarely stays there long.

When it rises, it does so reluctantly, peeling its palms from the floor. There is a brief sucking sound, though the square remains dry. For several minutes afterward it limps. It flexes its fingers and presses them into wet cracks.

Under the bench it keeps objects too small or too ordinary to explain it: a grazer’s empty shell; a smooth length of root; five mineral nodules, each pierced by a hole; a strip of something dark and supple, folded over itself until it fits in the hand. It takes these out individually. Some it tastes. The pierced nodules it holds against the breathing fold.

Air passing through them produces a low, furry whistle.

Once, a whistle brings two blunt-headed creatures out from beneath the shelter in daylight. They emerge with their forefins braced, mouths empty, visibly agitated. The inhabitant immediately closes its hand around the nodule and waits until they have gone back.

It does not use that one again while you are there.

***

You may decide it tends the shelf.

Certainly it does things that look like tending. It clears mineral grit from beneath the hovering frames. It feels along a frame’s lower edge with the sensitive backs of its fingers, then puts its ear—or the place where an ear might be—against a newly formed terrace. Sometimes it removes a single grain from the rising stream.

But it also makes mistakes.

One afternoon it spends a long time pressing a soft nodule into a shallow hollow. The stone around the nodule begins to wrinkle. It holds the nodule down with both hands. A tremor passes under the pool; the hollow deepens abruptly; warm water fills it.

The inhabitant snatches back its hands.

For a while it stands over the new puddle with its shoulders drawn up. Then it goes away. The transparent hunters discover the puddle before dusk, although you do not see how they cross the intervening ground.

Nor does it control the grazers. They climb over its feet when it is still. The smaller ones enter the warm folds behind its knees, and it must remove them one by one before settling in the shelter. It does this with practiced patience until the last, which has wedged itself deep into a crease. Then it shakes its leg so sharply that the animal lands upside down.

It looks at the grazer.

It turns it over.

The most deliberate work it does is around the eggs.

At night, when the blunt-headed creatures are arranging their pebbles, it keeps away. In the morning it visits each new ridge. It lowers its forehead until it almost touches the porous surface. Its breathing fold opens. Air passes over the holes.

Sometimes that is all.

Sometimes it inserts the slender root from beneath the bench into a hole and very carefully lifts an egg out. The eggs are soft, smoky spheres with a white crescent folded inside. It carries one in the hollow between two fingers, supporting the fingers with its other hand, and takes it to a different ridge.

You cannot tell what distinguishes the eggs it moves. But the empty holes are always on the side toward newly growing ground.

As the days pass, it begins making a route between the ridges. Not a paved path: a few pebbles here, an old shell there, a pinch of softened mineral pressed into a crack. Small arrests join into a broken line. Along that line, the distance to the shelter changes less.

It uses the route when carrying eggs.

The grazers use it too, despite there being less food on it. Their feet make a lighter, quicker sound there.

***

The white post worries it.

It never touches the handle while the nest is occupied. Instead it crouches beside the post and peers upward into the hollow. The winged creatures settle on its forehead. Their folded legs catch in the fine grain of its skin.

Deep in the handle, behind the visible nest, are several older nests compressed into brown scales.

The inhabitant picks away the loose edges of these with one fingernail. It works only when the adults have descended to feed, and stops whenever they return. Once it draws out a dead winged creature larger than any of the living ones, with cloudy wings and a body the color of old honey.

It holds the body close to its eyes.

Then it places it beneath the western overhang, among the hanging roots. The tiny red animals approach along separate strands. It stays until the first has reached the body, and leaves before the others arrive.

Perhaps it has always lived here. The worn handle, the different heights of polish in the shelter, the dark tiles suggest a longer occupation than the newest stone can account for. But nothing proves that those traces belong to it.

On some evenings it stands beside the seven tiles and measures their spacing with its feet. Its own stride does not fit.

It tries again anyway.

***

Before a deep report from below, it becomes distracted.

Its head turns. Its fingers spread against whatever they are touching. Several seconds before the reeds straighten, the breathing fold closes tightly.

When the sound comes, the clear part of its flank fills with minute points of light.

Afterward it is hungry.

On days when the shelf grows very little, it eats and goes about its usual business. After a large growth, it may lie beside the fresh stone for hours. Its body feels unusually heavy then. You can see the pressure of its hip in the soft mineral. One hand remains on the ground, fingers spread, while the other rests over the unfixed portion of its side.

Once, as a new lobe rises at the northern edge, a swelling appears below its ribs.

It is no larger than a closed fist. The inhabitant touches it repeatedly, then stops touching it. Through its surface you can see a dark loop, a few threads, two small pale structures that separate and meet.

The swelling subsides before the lobe has turned gray.

For the next three mornings, the inhabitant brings food to that place.

It wipes film from productive patches with the dark folded strip and carries it north. It moistens the new stone. It clears away the larger grazers, gently at first, then with a sweep of its forearm when they keep returning.

On the fourth morning it does none of this.

It sits on the low rise nearby, feeding slowly from the underside of the strip.

Only much later do you notice that it has moved one of the pierced nodules out of the shelter. The nodule lies in a hollow at the northern edge, above the reach of the pools. A tiny quantity of warm water has collected inside its hole.

The inhabitant visits it whenever the shelf sounds.

It bends down, listens, and turns it a little, so that the opening faces away from the wind.
