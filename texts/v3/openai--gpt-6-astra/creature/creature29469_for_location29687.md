The animal beneath the broad leaves is clearest just after it has eaten.

Then a narrow brown shape appears inside it, suspended between the blunt head and the first bend of the tail. Sometimes there is a bright point in the brown: a grazer’s eye, still holding its shape. Over several hours the meal loses its edges. The animal becomes difficult to see again.

It is not invisible. Its body bends the straight lines behind it. A leaf vein viewed through its flank acquires a small kink. The yellow-white floor seems thicker beneath its head. When it moves through the lamp’s brightness, a succession of delicate surfaces catches the light—joints, membranes, the curved shield over its back—and for a moment it looks like something made from the same scratched glass as the ceiling.

Beneath the body are six short limbs. At rest they fold so closely against it that they resemble reinforcing struts. Their ends carry combs of hooks, each hook finer than a mineral hair on a leaf. On the open enamel they find purchase in scratches too shallow to hold grit. Among the plants they lock around old roots.

The tail does most of the moving. It folds beneath the belly and opens hard, throwing the animal backward. A lesser movement, almost a twitch, turns it in place. Walking is slow and rather laborious. It sets each foot down, tests, draws the body forward. Its speed belongs to emergencies and meals.

The two dark spots are eyes, though they do not give it a useful picture of the chamber. The suspended life would defeat better eyes. It sees brightness, the edge of a leaf close by, something passing between it and the lamp.

Most of what it knows arrives through the hairs along its jaw.

There are two rows, swept backward by the water. A swimming grazer disturbs them differently from a falling grain. A newly hatched animal, all tail and effort, sends out a repeated little pulse. The hunter can follow this pulse even when the hatchling is behind a leaf. It moves its head from side to side, waits, moves again.

Engine noise does not ordinarily confuse it. The growl travels through its whole body. Prey makes a difference between one side of its head and the other.

Sometimes the loose part in the machinery knocks at just the wrong moment. The hunter strikes at nothing. Its mouth opens into a short, jointed basket, closes on empty water, and folds away. It remains where it landed for a long time afterward, the jaw hairs spread.

It has no particular place to sleep. Beneath the broad-leaved colony are several suitable hollows, and it uses whichever the current leaves quietest. One lies against a root that has grown around an enamel flake. Another is under a dead leaf, now reduced to copper-coloured veins. Its usual resting place is a shallow scoop beside the broken seam, where the body can settle without being pushed against an edge.

There it attends to itself.

One limb passes repeatedly across an eye. Another draws a jaw hair through its hooked tip. The tail bends forward far enough for the animal to scrape its underside. This work occupies much of its life. Fine growth gathers at the joints; suspended metal settles on the gills. Left alone, the transparent body would acquire a felt coat and cease to move properly.

It cannot reach everywhere.

Behind the head is a narrow strip that remains faintly green. Tiny stalked organisms live there, opening their mouths into the passing water. When the hunter springs, they close. When it settles, they open again. Several have grown on top of older stalks, making a minute thicket that travels through the chamber without ever loosening its hold.

A young shrimp sometimes picks at this strip.

The hunter permits the shrimp to climb onto its back. Its limbs tighten against the floor, and its mouth stays folded. The shrimp works forward, taking particles from between the stalks. If it comes around the side of the head, the hunter turns abruptly and the shrimp flees into the leaves.

There is no lasting agreement. On another occasion, at another angle, the same shrimp would be food.

The hunter does not eat every day. After a large meal it can remain in the scoop through many passages of the shadow, moving only its gills. Hunger first shows itself in the feet. They release and fasten, release and fasten. Eventually it climbs to the outermost leaf and hangs beneath it with its head projecting beyond the edge.

From there it can reach the open middle.

The enormous-tailed young are easiest to catch just after the ceiling moss sheds them. They swim with great expenditure and little control. Some turn continuously in one direction. Others strike the floor and keep beating their tails against it. The hunter takes them individually, never pursuing far.

Not all the larvae are the same kind of animal. Some have forked tails; some carry a silver bead of air; some already possess the small folded basket beneath the face.

Those last are its own kind.

It catches them too.

Once, during a long interval without movement of the hinge, one survived beneath the inlet fringe and grew large enough to make deliberate, short jumps. It had a dark meal inside it and a greenish line beginning behind its head. For a while there were two hunters, separated by the open enamel.

The smaller one disappeared during a turning. Whether it entered the drain, lost its grip at the clogged opening, or became the brown shape inside the larger animal could not be told from what remained.

The larger animal’s preparations for a turning begin before the leaves bend.

A change comes through its feet. It lowers itself until its belly almost touches the floor. The limbs spread and the hooks seek purchase. Sometimes this warning amounts to nothing: a load shifts elsewhere, the strained note dies away, and the animal slowly rises again.

When the hinge does turn, it is already braced.

Water flattens the gills against its underside. Debris strikes its back. The broad leaf above it rolls over, and the copper surface makes the shelter suddenly bright. The animal’s mouth opens and closes, but it is not feeding. Water must still pass over the breathing surfaces, and the current is now arriving from the wrong direction.

Afterward it stays down while the shrimp emerge.

The new warmth enters it without delay. Its limbs begin to work faster. A smell in the incoming water—broken cells, perhaps, or the dissolved trace of another animal—draws it out before the pale grit has finished settling.

Once the water brought a creature longer than the hunter, thin as a root and alive along its whole length. It looped through the leaves, fastened briefly to the floor, then looped again. The hunter followed it from underneath. For several moments their bodies touched.

Nothing happened.

The thin creature eventually found the drain. Its front end vanished, its middle straightened, and the rest of it slipped out of sight.

The hunter approached the rim after it was gone. It lowered its head toward the clean enamel, felt the pull, and took three slow steps backward.

Its body is small enough to grow by fractions and still outgrow its covering.

When that happens, it wedges itself deep in the roots. A line opens behind the head. For a long time only the gills move. Then the animal draws itself forward out of itself, leaving the old eyes behind as two clear cups.

The new hooks cannot hold well. The new mouth cannot close hard. It stays concealed while water enters and enlarges it.

Its abandoned covering remains caught among the roots, complete enough to cast a hunter’s shadow. A grazer stops beneath it. Later a snail begins rasping one of the empty limbs.

Inside the seam, the living animal slowly hardens.

By the next passage of the lamp’s shadow, one jaw hair has lifted free of the roots. It bends in the current. A second follows.
