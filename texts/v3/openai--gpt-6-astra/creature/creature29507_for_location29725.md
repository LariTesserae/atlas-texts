When the lamp comes on, there is someone beneath the bench.

They have been there throughout the dark part of the cycle, fitted into the space between a cabinet and the pipe that warms the back wall. One knee is drawn up beside their head. The other leg extends behind the cabinet, where a gap in the boards allows it to enter the next room. In this position they occupy very little of either room. Fine roots have settled across their shoulder.

The first movement is a hand closing around the underside of the bench.

It is a large hand, though its fingers are slender. The thumb opens unusually far from the palm. Along the inside of each finger runs a pale, ridged pad, interrupted at the joints; these are what keep the rails clean. The nails are short and dark and worn smooth at their edges. One has a triangular split that has healed without quite closing.

They pull themselves out slowly enough that the roots lift and fall almost in place.

Unfolded, they are longer than the room is high. This presents no difficulty. They rarely extend all their limbs at once, and their habitual posture is bent at the hips, head forward, feet feeling behind them for something to hold. In open water their legs trail loosely. Indoors, a foot can remain hooked beneath a doorframe while both hands work in a room beyond it.

They wear a garment that might once have been a maintenance coat. Little remains of its original fabric. The shoulders have been patched with the fine mesh from a filter screen, the sleeves replaced by something pale and closely woven. There are no fastenings down the front. Strips pass around the body and tie at the back, leaving the chest unobstructed.

Here, on either side of the breastbone, the skin lies in soft overlapping folds.

A faint movement passes through them even when the rest of the body is still. At intervals the folds lift, revealing surfaces the dull red of the inside of a mussel. The water that leaves them carries minute specks of matter which settle on the coat. Much of the inhabitant’s grooming consists of brushing these away.

Their face is easier to mistake for a human face while it is turned aside.

There is a brow, a nose flattened close to the cheeks, a mouth whose corners reach a little too far back. The eyes are small, dark, and recessed beneath transparent lids. Looking straight ahead seems to require an effort. Usually the head is inclined, presenting one cheek toward whatever is being examined.

Around the jaw grows a fringe of fine, almost colorless threads. Some are scarcely longer than eyelashes; others reach the collar. They spread when the pumps change their pressure. In a narrow passage, these threads discover the movement of water around a corner before the inhabitant turns it.

There is very little hair on the head. What remains is tied with copper wire into a short tuft, less to keep it orderly than to prevent it entering valves.

For a while they stay beside the bench, holding its edge.

The amber lamp illuminates one side of their mouth and the broad knuckles. Behind the wall, the heavy component continues its turn. They feel this through their hand. Then they place their cheek against the pipe.

Something in the mechanism is arriving late.

The delay is small. The lamp has scarcely changed brightness when they lift the middle spanner, turn it once in their palm, and put it into a pocket sewn inside the coat. The smallest spanner they leave. The largest they move a little farther from the edge.

They do not take the replacement valve.

It has waited in its box for a long time. A sound valve is a considerable possession here. Installing it would mean opening a housing whose bolts may no longer withstand being disturbed. It would mean stopping a circulation upon which several rooms of roots have come to depend. So far, adjustment has been enough.

They pass through the root curtain with one forearm raised, carrying the strands over their head. At the doorway they pause to free a small forked root caught in the mesh of their shoulder.

Then they pull themselves down the stairwell.

There is none of the alternating motion of a swimmer. A hand grips, the body follows, a foot checks the movement against a wall. Every few yards they stop. Their route includes long intervals of stillness in places that seem inconvenient: beneath a landing, halfway through a hatch, with the upper body inside a cupboard and the legs suspended in a corridor.

During these pauses they breathe, listen through their bones, and sometimes forget the task for a little while.

The district offers many things worth attending to.

In the lamp bowl, the shrimp is working a particle between its forelegs. The inhabitant puts a fingertip against the glass and waits until it comes close. They have done this often enough that the shrimp no longer retreats, although it does not quite approach the finger either.

At a window they turn one of the rectangular leaves so its darker side faces the light. At the fountain they catch a flake of mineral matter and crush it between tongue and palate.

They eat in small quantities and at great intervals. Most of their food is a soft growth cultivated on removable ceramic plates in an upper kitchen. The plates stand vertically in a rack where the warm return water passes over them. Some carry a cream-colored film; others a brown fuzz that the inhabitant scrapes away and discards. They rotate the plates, clean the rack, and keep one cracked saucer separate because its growth tastes different.

There are things they will eat only when necessary. Root tips. The translucent eggs found beneath a tank flange. Once, the flesh of a fish that had become trapped behind an inspection cover.

The dark fish in the passage is left alone. When it appears, the inhabitant backs away enough to let it choose its hole.

Below the square, the walls press close to the pump shaft. Here the water feels heavier with warmth. They anchor both feet around a bracket and lay their tools in the shallow bowl of a stair tread, first clearing it with the side of a hand.

The work is mostly waiting.

A nut must be turned a fraction, then left through the next movement of the wheel. Too much correction would be worse than none. They sit with the spanner loosely enclosed in one hand and their other palm against the housing. Sediment settles over their thighs. A thin worm emerges from the mortar beside their elbow.

When the padded knock comes, it passes through their teeth.

They make another adjustment.

No one has taught them this repair within any span they clearly remember. There are other procedures they know in great detail and cannot perform because the equipment is gone. Their hands retain the sequence for dismantling a particular governor. They know how to wind a clock that would not survive here. Sometimes, finding a familiar shape beneath the growth, they clean it eagerly and discover that it belongs to something else.

Their memories have places attached to them more reliably than dates.

One turn in the staircase recalls an argument. A tiled recess contains the expectation of being handed a cup. Near the locked cage they experience a reluctance so persistent that they take the longer passage whenever possible.

The stool inside means nothing they can recover.

They are capable of opening the cage. Its lock is less complicated than several mechanisms they maintain. Nevertheless, they have not opened it.

Elsewhere, beyond the washhouse tree, they keep a room for things that have no use in the machinery.

A blue glass bead rests in an eggshell-thin porcelain dish. There is a brass label whose lettering has worn away, four white stones, and the handle of a cup. In the soft wall mortar they have pressed a row of small objects at the height their face occupies when they rest: a buckle, a piece of green bottle, a tooth, two differently shaped keys.

They rearrange these occasionally. The room receives no lamp light. Their fingers know the arrangement.

The washhouse tree has begun to enter through a crack above the door. They could stop it with very little effort. Instead they have moved the porcelain dish.

After the repair, they return by way of the central square.

They do not cross its open middle. Along the walls there are cornices to grip, recesses where the water carries a familiar temperature, roots strong enough to bear a gentle pull. Halfway around, they stop beneath one of the old air pockets.

Their forehead touches its mineral rim.

With a fingertip they disturb the silver surface. It trembles, divides around the finger, joins again. Beyond it is a space too small to admit their face. They remain there until the trembling stops.

Then they descend to the fountain.

The plume bends beside them. Its pale grains catch in the threads along their jaw. They turn their head, testing the restored circulation, and the folds of their chest open a little wider.

Above, the lamp in the workroom goes dark.

They will return the spanner before resting. They are particular about this, even now, even with no one else to use it. But for a while they remain beside the fountain, one foot tucked under the lip of its buried basin, letting the warm filtered water pass across their skin.

A leaf brushes their wrist repeatedly.

After the fourth or fifth time, they take hold of its stem and, without breaking it, guide it somewhere it will no longer trouble them.
