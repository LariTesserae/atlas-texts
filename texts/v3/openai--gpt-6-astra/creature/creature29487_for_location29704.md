On the underside of the lowest root-step, where the sheet of water breaks into drops, there is a patch of bark that does not quite belong to the root.

It is longer than a hand and dark enough to disappear against wet wood. Moss grows along its upper edge. Across its middle run several shallow seams, each packed with black grit. Nothing about it suggests a head. Even when water strikes it, it remains still.

But the root has grown around a bend, and the grain follows the bend. The grain of this patch runs across it.

Near dusk, one edge lifts. Beneath it is a narrow line of gray flesh. The line thickens, takes hold of the wood, and draws the rest after it so gradually that a falling drop lands almost where the previous one did. Two short feelers emerge from beneath the forward edge. One touches the running water and withdraws. The other remains extended against the bark.

This is one of the larger animals that lives entirely within the green knot.

Its back is covered by eight overlapping plates. In the young, these are thin, translucent brown, with pale margins that look unfinished. In an old animal they become nearly black. They bear scratches, embedded fragments of leaves, and sometimes a small standing crop of moss. The plates do not make a rigid shell. They slide over one another when the animal bends, allowing it to turn around a root or fold lengthwise into a narrow seam.

At rest it has the outline of a flattened oval. Moving, it becomes less regular. Its front broadens to explore a surface, while its rear may remain fastened several finger-widths behind. On uneven ground, portions of the body advance separately. A ripple passes along the exposed sole, and what seemed stuck is suddenly farther on.

Its underside is pale, almost blue. There is a mouth near the front, small and firmly puckered, and a broad muscular foot edged with short, soft fringes. These fringes find the wet places. On a wall that appears uniformly damp, the animal follows an exact, wavering route, passing over one patch and refusing another scarcely distinguishable beside it.

Dryness is more dangerous to it than cold.

It can remain for weeks beneath a rim of ice, drawn tight against the wood, its body reduced to a thin cushion under the plates. A sealed film holds moisture around its foot. Its feelers retract into pockets that cannot be seen once they close. In this condition, the animal contributes almost nothing to the movement of the place. Roots swell beneath it. Frost thickens at its edges. A leaf may fall across its back and remain there until the next thaw.

A few hours of liquid water are enough to bring it out.

It does not wait for the air to soften. Water reaches the fringes first, soaking into the seal. The foot loosens. Beneath the plates, small contractions begin. Long before the exposed buds have opened, the animal is feeding in a passage that still carries ice on its roof.

Its mouth contains a rasp, fine enough to leave most of a leaf intact. It eats the softened underside of old foliage, films from wet bark, the pale growth that spreads between tightly packed leaves. A leaf it has worked over may look untouched from above. Lift it, and it has become a delicate brown net.

The minute green blades along the chamber walls are bitten down in short stretches. Never a whole row. The animal feeds as it travels, and travels according to moisture rather than abundance. Sometimes it crosses good food without stopping, following a bead of water into a crack from which it will not emerge that season.

There are others.

They are not evenly distributed. Several chambers contain none, though they appear sheltered and well supplied. Then a low arch, unremarkable except for the way water gathers beneath it, may carry three adults and a number of young, each fixed to a different face of the same branch.

They do not gather for company. The branch offers something precise: bark that stays wet, air that moves a little, no direct path for falling snow. Yet their lives become entangled there. One feeds in a groove another has cleared. A small one shelters beneath the projecting rear plates of a larger one. When the larger animal moves, the small one is exposed and must either follow or draw itself shut.

Adults meeting head-on seldom turn aside at once. Their feelers touch. Each tests the other’s edge. Then one begins to climb over the other, spreading its foot across the ridged plates. The animal underneath holds fast. For a while there is a dark double thickness on the wall, fringed faintly with gray.

Afterward, a few grains of grit have changed owners.

The young are easier to see, if there is light. Their plates have not yet acquired the color of their surroundings, and their bodies are too small to bridge the roughness of bark. They go into every hollow. A journey across a root that an adult makes in an evening may take a young animal several thaws.

They hatch in the deepest leaf chambers, in clusters of clear eggs tucked beneath wood already softening at its edges. Each egg holds a folded speck and a little fluid. For much of the year there is no visible change. Then, during some brief interval when water moves freely through the floor, a cluster empties.

The hatchlings do not emerge together. A few remain in their eggs long after the others have dispersed. Some never emerge at all. Empty membranes, unopened eggs, and the first droppings of the young lie together in the same wet hollow.

Nothing tends them.

The gray bird takes them when it can. It knows how to turn a loose curl of bark and inspect the sheltered side. A young animal pulled free contracts into a bead, but its plates are not yet strong enough to matter. An adult is harder to remove. Its foot seals against the wood, and the flexible rim of its back leaves little purchase for a beak.

Sometimes the bird succeeds in raising one edge. Then the animal’s entire body tightens. Water squeezes out beneath it. If the seal breaks, it falls.

A fallen adult does not always land upright. On deep moss this scarcely matters: it bends, grips a stem, and slowly draws the shell over. On smooth ice, overturned, it may extend and retract its pale foot until the foot ceases to move.

The central pool holds several empty sets of plates. Most have come apart. They lie among submerged leaves, dark on one side and pearly underneath, light enough to shift when a descending root stirs the water.

The living avoid the pool’s open banks. They can endure immersion, but cannot swim. Where their routes cross water, they go beneath it along wood, holding to the thin roots that span the shallows. Bubbles lodge between their plates. Seen from above, one looks like a small, irregular thickening of the root, advancing with its own few points of silver.

The largest animals have worn paths that cannot be recognized as paths until one is seen using them. A clean band under a branch. A shallow trough through leaf felt. A smooth place behind a moss cushion, kept open by the repeated passage of a shell.

These routes may last longer than the chambers they connect.

When a crown collapses, daylight falls on backs that have spent years in darkness. The animals fasten themselves beneath whatever remains. Some are carried down with the broken wood; some persist on fragments that no longer lead anywhere. As new shoots crowd the opening, the survivors begin to travel through them. Their old circuits acquire a detour, a steep descent, an unfamiliar stretch of bark that still tastes sharply of resin.

The one beneath the root-step has a split across its fourth plate. New shell has formed under the damage, leaving the old edges slightly raised. A single red-stemmed plant has rooted in the grit there. Its two smallest leaves lie flat against the animal’s back.

Tonight it follows the water down the steps.

At the bottom, it pauses where the runoff enters moss. Its forward edge passes out of sight. The plates tilt one after another, slipping beneath the green surface, until only the rear remains exposed.

For a while, drops strike that last dark curve.

Then the moss rises behind it.
