The larger ones live where a grove meets open water.

There is usually a shelf beneath the bank there, held together by roots, with enough space between the water and the peat for a body to lie partly dry. In daylight, little shows. A pale fold rests against a root. Several dark tips protrude from the pool, easily mistaken for the ends of drowned twigs. Sometimes a patch of the bank rises, holds, and subsides without breaking its coat of small leaves.

An adult can be longer than the low trees are tall. It seldom extends to its full length.

Most of the body is loose enough to accommodate the place it occupies. It bends around roots and spreads into the shallow depressions between them. Along its sides are six pairs of short limbs, though a resting animal may conceal all but two. Each limb ends in a broad pad divided into narrow, independently moving fingers. Under water these open into fans. On stone they close together, pressing a continuous wet surface against the rock.

The back is dark, finely ridged, and almost bare. The folds along the flanks carry a short, dense growth that looks like fur until it opens in water. Each filament is hollow. Water passes through some; others contain blood. In cold air the animal draws them together beneath a skin secretion that sets into a flexible glaze. Submerged, they separate and tremble even when the pool is still.

The head is small for the body and difficult to locate among its folds. It has four eyes, arranged in two close pairs. The upper pair watches the bank. The lower pair looks through the water, protected by clear lids that accumulate a greenish stain in old animals.

Below the eyes is a blunt mouth surrounded by pale, mobile plates. These do not bite against one another. They fit around things.

A root, for example.

The animal takes a living root into its mouth without severing it. The plates close; a muscular ring makes a seal. For several minutes nothing appears to happen. Then a faint movement passes down the throat, followed by another. Water darkened with fine organic matter leaks from openings behind the head.

It is feeding, but not on the root itself. It draws off the films and sediments that collect where the current slows. The pale colonies inside the trees release cells into the water, and these are part of its food. So are mineral-binding organisms, dead root hairs, eggs, and the soft bodies of creatures that would otherwise lodge against the valves.

A feeding animal leaves the passage cleaner than it found it.

This does not make it harmless. In lean seasons it can hold a root open against the tree’s pressure and drink until the leaves above begin to curl. Young groves sometimes die this way. Healthy trees respond by tightening the collars farther upstream, and an animal that persists may find its mouth sealed around a root that will give it nothing for several days.

Most learn to move on before this happens.

***

They travel chiefly after sunset, when water is draining from the trunks.

First the head appears. It moves from side to side just above the surface. The dark tips that projected from the pool rise with it: a cluster of soft tubes behind the skull, each with a narrow opening at its end. They are breathing organs, but they also register pressure. Held against bark, they can distinguish the filling of one chamber from the emptying of another.

On land the tubes lie flat along the neck.

The forelimbs take hold. A considerable length of animal follows before the hindquarters leave the water. Its underside is pale and closely pleated, with a row of dark, oval pads down the middle. These grip in succession. On a steep slope, the animal seems to pour itself from one attachment to the next.

It can cross the underside of a leaning trunk without hanging free at any point.

The young are more hurried. They lift too much of themselves at once and sometimes come away from the rock with a sound like a wet leaf being peeled loose. They fall into sedges or shallow pools, remain motionless for a while, then climb again.

Adults rarely fall. They are heavy enough that a fall matters.

Their usual routes run below the canopy, along banks and through the sheltered seams of rock. A route may persist for generations without becoming a bare trail. The passage of their bellies presses the vegetation flat, but their waste supplies it with finely divided minerals and readily available nutrients. Plants on the route grow lower, darker, and more densely than those beside it.

Across an exposed ridge, such a path can be seen as a green line between two watersheds.

***

They hear the trees through their feet.

The central pads on the belly are sensitive too. A resting animal pressed against a trunk receives the working of the whole lower chamber: the slow increase in strain, the seating of a valve, the brief vibration when flow changes direction. Its own pulse slows to a simple relation with these movements.

This is why they may remain for hours beside a tree that offers little food.

A fault is easier to locate before it has become audible in air. A torn partition makes a flutter. Grit in a bearing produces a fine, irregular vibration. Where a chamber has begun to fill with gas, pressure rises too quickly and collapses too far.

The animal shifts, tests another part of the bark, and sometimes climbs.

Beneath the mouth plates are two narrow appendages ordinarily folded into grooves in the throat. They can extend farther than the head is long. Their tips bear stiff bristles and a single curved edge. The animal inserts them through a vent or an old wound and works inside the trunk.

It may pull out a rope of decayed tissue. It may scrape a mineral crust from a moving surface. It may spend an entire night enlarging a passage no wider than one of its fingers.

Often it eats what it removes.

Not every intervention helps. A tree may close the opening with fresh tissue, and the animal may return repeatedly to undo the repair. Some trunks bear thick, distorted growth around sites of these disagreements. Others retain an accessible opening for decades, its edges polished by the same visitor.

The small furred creatures that sleep against the bark wake when the work begins. Usually they climb a little higher. Bits of loosened growth fall past them. They catch some before it reaches the ground.

***

There are not many adults in any one basin.

Two meeting at a narrow passage approach slowly, their mouths closed and their breathing tubes raised. They touch along the jaw and neck. For a time each holds the other’s flank with one forelimb.

Water and dissolved substances pass between the open filaments.

Then one flattens itself against the bank and the other goes over it.

Who yields is not determined simply by size. An animal travelling toward a distant grove may be given room by several larger residents. One carrying young is almost never made to leave the water. But an individual that has recently forced too much from a root network may be held in place, surrounded by two or three others, their pads fastened to its skin.

These encounters can last through a night. They seldom draw blood. The restrained animal loses water steadily through its opened flank filaments, and by morning it is smaller, deeply folded, and eager to reach a pool.

Whether the others recognise an offender or merely respond to what its body contains is difficult to separate. The result is consistent: a depleted grove becomes an uncomfortable place for a heavy feeder to remain.

***

The young begin inside trees.

Their eggs are laid in the upper margin of a chamber that empties each night but never dries. There may be only three or four, translucent and firmly attached. The parent chooses a tree whose cycle is regular and whose roots reach more than one permanent pool.

For their first season, the young resemble the pale organisms around them. They have no dark back, no obvious limbs, and a broad fringe that stirs in the current. They feed on suspended matter. At night they cling to the chamber wall as the water falls.

Later, small eyes appear.

The limbs grow beneath the fringe. The mouth plates harden. A juvenile begins to move between chambers, squeezing through valves during the brief interval when they stand open.

It acquires its first route before it has seen the outside of a tree.

The parent visits the trunk repeatedly. Sometimes it touches the bark with the pads beneath its body and makes a sequence of contractions. These produce no more than a low tremor, but the young turn toward it. As they grow, they answer.

Their first emergence is usually during heavy rain. The vent is narrow; the new body comes through folded lengthwise, pale and shining. It rests on the bark while its breathing tubes open. Then it moves downward, following a line of moisture and the intermittent pressure of the parent’s body below.

For several years the young remain near the natal grove.

They learn which roots can be opened, which pools keep their water under ice, and where the rock carries vibration from a tree on the far side of a rise. An adult may take a long detour to a failing trunk with a juvenile following against its flank. At the trunk, it moves aside.

The juvenile tests the bark. The adult waits.

***

Old animals lose the even darkness of their backs. Pale areas develop around healed injuries, and the flank filaments become sparse. Their bodies remain large, but they spend less time on land.

One may occupy the same bank for twenty winters, travelling only between a deep pool and the roots of two or three trees. Vegetation establishes itself in the upper creases of its skin. Small flowering plants can survive there for a season, provided they root only in the accumulated debris.

The animal scrapes away anything deeper.

At dusk it still raises its head when a valve knocks in the grove. Sometimes it leaves the water. Sometimes the tubes settle again, and it remains where it is.

Under early ice, its movements are visible as slow displacements of the clear water. A forefoot closes around a root. The mouth finds an old opening. Fine dark material drifts from behind the head and settles among the stones.

Above, the tree finishes emptying one chamber and begins to empty the next.

The animal loosens its grip, waits through the change in pressure, and takes hold again.
