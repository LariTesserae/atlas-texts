Beneath the black plant there is a little clearance that none of its leaves quite touches. Something lives in that clearance. For most of the afternoon you would take it for the underside of the plant: a pale, folded thickness where the stems ought to come together.

Then one fold opens, and you see that it has been holding a foot off the floor.

The foot is broad, soft-edged, and divided along its sole by a single groove. It lowers onto the ceramic without a sound. Several minutes later, a second foot appears farther back than seems possible. The body between them draws out of the foliage.

It is about as long as the last joint of your thumb.

At first it looks white. Against the ivory floor, however, its color resolves into a very faint green, with darker material showing through wherever the body bends. It has something like a shell, but the shell covers it in overlapping saddles rather than enclosing it. Under each saddle is a narrow interval of exposed flesh. These intervals open and close as it moves, revealing the deep, polished red of something kept permanently hot.

Its front is blunt and slightly lower than its back. There are no visible eyes. Two small areas on its forward surface remain dull while the rest catches the light. They look unfinished, as though the glaze has failed there.

When its head passes into the rosette’s shadow, these patches turn toward the new leaf.

The animal stops.

One forefoot remains raised. Beneath the body, a soft ridge travels slowly from front to back. A second ridge follows it. The saddles lift a fraction, settle, and lift again in a different order. It is doing a considerable amount of something while appearing to wait.

***

Its skin is more flexible than its resemblance to the floor suggests. At the edges of the saddles it can be bent almost double. Pressure briefly turns it transparent. If you could hold it—and there is no good way to reach into the hollow without damaging what lives there—you might see, through the place your finger touched, a series of small chambers arranged around an empty passage.

Each chamber is a different temperature.

There is no central furnace. The hottest chamber is sometimes near the head, sometimes beneath the last saddle. Heat moves through the body in an order that changes with its posture. A foot cools when it bears weight. Another warms as it lifts. The pale tissues between the chambers tighten, and the animal advances.

It has six feet when crossing the open floor. Under the black plant, where it rests with its body gathered up, only four can be found. The remaining pair fold into recesses so closely fitted that their soles become part of its flanks.

On the underside, just behind the head, is a short transverse mouth.

It does not bite the leaves.

Along a seam in the ceramic it lays this mouth flat and waits for the mountain’s next compression. The new mineral rises against it. For a moment a hair-thin line of darker substance appears between the two sides of the seam. The animal takes some of this into itself.

Afterward there is no scraped place, no pit. The seam closes at the same level as before, though its blue cast has weakened.

This is the faint metallic discoloration across the floor: places where it has fed, still young enough to be visible.

A meal can occupy most of a day. Sometimes it leaves before the seam opens. Sometimes it waits through several strokes and takes nothing. Its dull facial patches move independently, one toward the floor, one toward the leaves, attending to differences too small to trouble the rest of the hollow.

***

The click comes from its back.

One of the saddles has a hooked inner edge. As the animal contracts, the hook passes a raised point on the saddle behind it. Usually the movement is silent. When the body has cooled sufficiently, the two surfaces meet.

There is the click.

The animal then stays still for a long time.

Beneath it, the roots of the black plant lose their electrical note. A little warmth travels outward through the floor. By the time the note returns, the animal has relaxed enough for the saddles to separate.

This happens most often after it has fed. It also happens, less predictably, when the sunlight reaches the far edge of its resting place.

It appears to like that edge.

Even on days when the seams nearby are active, it will leave them and position itself with its head in the light and its body under the foliage. It makes tiny adjustments as the afternoon passes. There is nothing efficient about these excursions. At times it travels farther to maintain the bright strip across its face than it traveled to obtain a meal.

The black leaves lift a little as it goes beneath them. Their central veins cool. Later, after it has settled, they lower again.

A shallow bed has begun to form there. The ceramic rises around the resting body, leaving its underside free. Each time the animal changes position, the growing edge becomes less exact. The result is an irregular little basin, with one lip too high and an unnecessary extension at the back.

It sleeps across the extension.

***

It is capable of moving quickly, though only a small part of it ever does.

Once, a red stem inclines during a magnetic interval and touches its flank. A ripple closes every exposed strip of red flesh. The feet disappear. What remains is an almost seamless pale object, slightly askew on the floor.

The stem recovers.

Much later, the head emerges at the opposite end.

For the rest of that afternoon the animal takes a longer route around the sprawling plant. On the following day it passes directly beneath the same stem and pauses there, one saddle brushing its underside.

There are no scars on the animal. Its oldest surfaces can be recognized only by their opacity. New growth is translucent and a little amber. It appears first under the saddles, pushing them apart. Eventually a fresh saddle forms in the interval.

At present there is room for one more.

The space is near the rear of the body, wider than the others and protected by a slight inward turn of the neighboring plates. When the animal crosses a hot seam, it keeps this part raised.

***

Occasionally it goes onto the leaf in the slit.

From the opening you can watch its forefeet reach the leaf’s edge. The leaf does not bend. The animal climbs, and for an instant its whole pale length is distinct against the dark surface.

Withdraw your face from the ledge and the veins become valleys.

Something pale is moving between two stands of black vegetation. You can no longer tell whether you are seeing its back or an exposed slope beyond it. Farther along the branching hollow, a brightness appears, disappears, and appears again. It could be another saddle catching the sun. It is much too far away.

When you lean close, the animal is on the little leaf, nearly at its center.

It spends a long time there.

On returning, it lowers each foot carefully to the ceramic. Its body is held higher than usual. Through the open intervals, the inner chambers show a dim olive color that slowly deepens to red.

It does not feed immediately. It goes first to the basin beneath the black plant and presses its face against the overgrown lip.

The lip is warmer than when it left.

For a while it rests with the blunt front of its head against that warmth. Then it turns, folds its last two feet into its sides, and fits itself as well as it can into the place that has continued growing in its absence.
