The woman nearest the rising ridge has already unfastened her basket.

She does not drop it. She passes its handle backward until someone takes the weight, then lowers herself onto three limbs and slips the fourth beneath a leaf. For a moment her whole arm disappears. The hooks turn farther out. Something catches her sleeve.

The others feel her effort through their feet.

She comes away with a cord, a torn cuff, and a small flat animal fastened to the soft underside of her wrist. The cord belongs to one of the boundary posts. She has saved the carving at its end: two figures bent over the same basket, their heads knocked together. Its owner will want it back.

The animal she removes more carefully. Pulling would leave its mouthparts behind. She presses along its back until its plates separate, admitting light to an organ that ordinarily lies against the underside of a scale. It releases her. She sets it beneath another leaf.

Only then does she examine her wrist.

A clear bead stands in the puncture. She rubs dust into it, waits for it to cloud, and folds her sleeve over the seal.

Her name, used at close quarters, is a pressure beneath the thumb followed by two quick touches. Across a terrace she is a crooked arm held above the head. In the narrow quiet inside a sleeping hood, she also has a spoken name.

Her brother still uses the name she had as a child. She dislikes this, except when she has been away.

***

They are not large people. An adult standing comfortably reaches about as high as the shoulder of a stilt-browser, though comparisons are difficult: the people seldom stand fully extended, and a browser is mostly leg.

Their bodies have a forward inclination that is not quite a stoop. The pelvis is broad, the trunk short, the arms long enough to reach the ground without much bending. From a distance, someone waiting motionless can resemble a piece of bark caught upright between stems. Then the head turns, the hands open, and the resemblance is gone.

Their skin does not carry armor everywhere. Hard, overlapping patches cover the shoulders, spine, outer thighs, and the backs of the hands. Between these patches are finely pleated areas, darker and easily injured. A person climbing reaches around a stem rather than embracing it. Children must be repeatedly reminded that a surface safe for a palm may not be safe for a belly.

Along each forearm runs a shallow channel lined with short, stiff hairs. Dust collects there. So do the minute splinters that would otherwise work into the elbow folds. In the evening, people clean these channels for one another with hooked slivers of tooth. It is intimate work, but not necessarily tender. Arguments continue throughout. Someone may hold an arm perfectly still while making an extremely insulting shape with the other hand.

Their feet are broad when resting and narrow when lifted. Four long weight-bearing digits spread beneath the sole’s flexible pad; a shorter inner digit can oppose them around a cord or stem. On uncertain ground, the feet keep making small adjustments even during sleep.

A sleeping child will grip a finger offered to its sole.

Their faces are more mobile than their backs suggest. The eyes sit beneath projecting ridges and have pale lids thin enough to admit the rooflight. Around the nostrils are folds that can shut against dust. The mouth is small when closed. Opened, it reveals broad grinding surfaces at the back and a pair of dark, closely fitted plates at the front.

Those plates hold the listening stick.

Old people sometimes have one fitted with a little wedge because wear has spoiled the contact. A good wedge can restore distinctions they believed the basin had ceased to make: the uneven tread of a lame herd beast, the first separate cracks inside a ripening seed chamber, a familiar person climbing toward the house.

They hear airborne sound, too. They enjoy it. A noise need not be useful to be worth making.

Children find hollow ribs and beat them with pieces of bark. Adults object less to the racket than to the fact that the ribs are usually parts of the house.

***

Their flesh is not wet in the manner of a grazer’s exposed feeding membrane, though it is soft in many places. Beneath a cut lies a close, cream-colored substance threaded with sealed vessels. The clear fluid in those vessels smells faintly of bruised leaves.

They obtain most of it from food.

A meal may include scraped pith, the fatty underside of a newly fallen light-plate, grubs extracted from dead bark, and the dense nodules that grow where certain roots have failed to separate. These last must be opened along their natural seams. Split them across the grain and the contents expand into a bitter foam, leaving very little to eat.

Food is worked between the back teeth for a long time. During a shared meal, everyone’s hands remain available for conversation. A newcomer can mistake the gathering for a lively discussion in which nobody is being fed.

The body retains what it needs with great efficiency. Waste emerges as small, dry pellets with a dark glaze. These are collected and stored beyond the sleeping places, not because they are useless but because too many things want them. One household’s daily production will keep a bait station active through several changes of the rooflight.

There is no common word corresponding exactly to thirst. There are several for the states a traveler can enter when the inner vessels are becoming depleted. The first is recognized by a dragging sensation behind the eyes. Later, the joints lose their smoothness. At that stage a companion may notice the trouble before the sufferer admits it: a hand opens in three little jerks rather than one movement.

The remedy is food, rest, and a particular waxy rind that nobody likes.

People carry the rind anyway. They offer it with the exaggerated courtesy reserved for unpleasant necessities.

***

The shelters contain more softness than a visitor expects.

Outside, everything is hooked, strapped, tiled, braced. Inside, the sleeping platforms are lined with teased fiber, and the wall nearest each sleeper bears small things that would not survive outside: a fan of translucent wing-cases, an old cuff embroidered with flat seeds, a curled shaving that happens to resemble someone’s face.

They like objects with no immediate use. Keeping such objects requires effort.

The woman with the injured wrist has a cabinet no larger than her basket. Each compartment is lined with material unappealing to borers. Inside are eleven bits of old light-plate, polished until their cloudy interiors show. One contains a branching red flaw. Another is almost perfectly clear.

She brings them out for certain guests. For others she leaves the cabinet shut, even if they ask.

Her house has moved six times without being dismantled. Each move required neighbors to loosen its three anchor lines, lift one side, slide a skid beneath it, and let the growing crown do part of the work. The last move left the entrance facing a gully. She considers this an improvement. Her brother considers it evidence that she is too stubborn to admit a mistake.

A domestic animal lives along the underside of her platform.

It has a soft, elongated body, many very short feet, and two hard brushes surrounding its mouth. All day it works through the shelter’s crevices, eating eggs, shed skin, and the sweet residue that attracts worse visitors. At night it folds itself around a warm anchor fitting.

It is not allowed near the food.

It goes near the food whenever she is away.

When frightened, it contracts into a tight knot and gives off an odor resembling scorched growth. This has caused two unnecessary evacuations. She insists the second was prudent regardless.

***

Children spend much of their early life attached to somebody.

The smallest ride beneath the ribs, held against a parent or older sibling by a sling. Their outer skin is still pliant. They have an unreasonable desire to put their hands into moving gaps.

Later they are tethered by a waist cord. The adult holding the other end must never pull blindly. A child who has become wedged may be safe until someone tries to retrieve it.

One of the first lessons is to answer tension with tension: two deliberate pulls mean *I am holding on*. A loose cord means very little. It may indicate safety, mischief, sleep, or a severed line.

They learn the basin by doing household work at its edges. They empty dust traps, renew insect barriers, test old lashings, and carry messages where the route is visible from home. Each task has a portion they are permitted to do alone.

Their games are less cautious.

They race the tightening of a young leaf, leap between moving stem bundles, and dare one another to stand beneath pollen-thorns just before the screaming starts. Adults recognize these games because they played them. This does not make them forgiving.

A popular game involves laying a hand on a support and guessing who is coming. Children soon discover that they can confuse their friends by carrying a heavy basket or walking with one stiff leg. Adults use the same tricks for less innocent reasons.

There are thieves in the settlements. There are also people whose possessions are always being stolen, even when the missing object eventually turns up where they put it.

***

Most adults have several kinds of work.

The woman with the cabinet reads openings. She visits places where old growth has drawn apart and judges whether they are likely to become passages, feeding grounds, or traps. She is paid in portions, tools, and obligations. Occasionally she is paid in something she did not ask for and cannot conveniently refuse.

She is good at her work. She is not reliably good at explaining it.

An apprentice following her sees her stop at a place apparently no different from the last twenty places, put a listening stick into a seam, and remain there until everyone is hot and irritable. Then she moves three paces sideways and begins again.

She studies feeding animals as carefully as stems. A borer withdrawing backward from a hole is ordinary. Six borers withdrawing and remaining beside their holes is worth attention. A procession of heavy beasts splitting around a patch of ground may mean the patch is weak, hot, chemically wrong, or simply occupied by something the first beast disliked.

She waits to see whether the next procession makes the same choice.

Her mistakes are marked on a strip of bark inside her house. Not all of them. The expensive ones.

One mark records a bridge she declared stable shortly before its far end detached. Nobody died, but a family lost its best cutting tooth and three baskets of stored food. For years afterward, whenever she gave advice, one member of that family would ask whether she meant it in the bridge sense.

Eventually they stopped.

She did not remove the mark.

***

The great animals know the people, though not as a single kind of thing.

To the heavy eyeless beasts, a settlement is an arrangement of obstructions and odors. They will pass through one if the growth beyond is attractive enough. People hang vibrating ribs across likely approaches. Most herds turn aside. Occasionally a beast stops to rub against the apparatus, and the whole warning system has to be rebuilt.

The stilt-browsers recognize individual feeders. A person who regularly removes irritating borers from a leg can approach that leg safely. This permission does not necessarily extend to the other legs.

The small animals that ride the browsers’ combs are thieves of exceptional persistence. Some have learned to recognize the opening of a food basket from several terraces away. They arrive bright and furious, skidding over shoulders, prying at fastenings.

People wear false fastenings to occupy them.

Predators are more troublesome in the gullies. Several hunt by detecting repeated contact with the same stem. Someone who pauses to work there changes position often. Tools are tied close. A dangling basket can provide the first touch; an investigating limb follows the cord upward.

Sometimes people lose baskets this way.

Sometimes they come home without enough hands to explain.

The injured are not rare. Houses acquire adaptations around them: a fastening that yields to a forearm, a ramp kept clear, a listening line carried directly to a sleeping platform. Someone unable to travel may become the person to whom a whole neighborhood brings unfamiliar vibrations, transmitted through lengths of cured stem.

Knowledge does not make such a life easy. Neither does injury make its owner agreeable.

***

At the new depression, the woman has tied her recovered carving to her basket.

The ridge has stopped rising. Its hooks remain extended. Below the watching people, a shallow crease travels across the naked root tissue and disappears beneath the opposite wall.

An old man sits down. Standing has become painful for him, and nothing here appears ready to happen quickly.

Two children begin exchanging the contents of their pockets. A basket-cleaner noses from beneath a lid and is pushed back. Someone distributes strips of tough food, each piece passed hand to hand until everyone has one.

The woman fits her stick between her teeth.

There is the close rasp of the surface beetles. Beneath it, a slow series of compressions. Farther down, something releases in separate stages, too regularly for a split and too widely spaced for feeding.

She moves the stick.

The old man notices. He sets aside his food and reaches for his own.

For a while they listen at different points along the rim. Then she extends a hand without looking, and he puts two fingers against her palm.

She asks whether he has felt this before.

He does not answer immediately.

Around them the spectators settle into the small arrangements of a longer stay. Baskets are hung clear. A child is sent home for another coil of line. Someone begins shifting the boundary posts farther back.

The old man finally presses his reply into her hand.

Once.

She waits for the rest, but his fingers remain still. Below them, the crease appears again, slightly wider than before.
