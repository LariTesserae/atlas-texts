The cream-colored animals are taller than they first appear. Seen across the basin, they seem to be standing on the banks of the pools. Only when one moves do you realize that what looked like a pale forked stem beneath it was the lower half of a leg.

There are usually five legs. They are set unevenly around the body, with two close together at the front and the others more widely spaced. This arrangement gives the creatures an uncertain appearance without making them unsteady. A resting animal can lift any two feet and remain quite still. When it walks, the order of its steps changes with the softness of the ground.

Each foot opens under weight into three translucent lobes. Fine green threads are visible inside them. The lobes do not cling, exactly, but their release takes a moment: the heel lifts, the leg inclines, and the last little breadth of contact draws out before letting go. Crossing a flat, an animal leaves a succession of pale marks among the dark footprints a visitor would make. Both kinds disappear.

Their bodies are narrow and deep, like something that would hang comfortably from a branch, although there is nowhere here for them to hang. The back has a slight keel. Beneath it the flanks descend to a soft, folded surface kept well clear of the ground. This is the part they lower onto the wet banks.

Up close, their cream color resolves into many colors. There is yellow along the keel, faint gray around the leg joints, and pink wherever the skin is stretched thin. A creature standing between you and the sun has a warm brightness through its legs. The shadows of vessels move inside them when it takes a step.

The small head can turn farther than seems necessary. It has no eyes that a visitor readily recognizes. On either side is a shallow oval depression crossed by a clear film. Behind those films, something dark shifts toward movement. Rain collects there. From time to time the animal tilts its head and empties both depressions in two quick shining lines.

There are no ears, either. Nevertheless, they attend to sounds.

A person calling from an open flat receives little response. A heel struck sharply against the floor may cause every animal near the pools to turn. Sometimes one of them lifts a foot and holds it in the air while the others continue facing the same direction. After a while it puts the foot down elsewhere.

They do this most often before a change in the noise below.

A chamber under the ground will begin to fill. Its low rushing note rises and becomes uneven; a knocking travels toward the western vault. By then the animals have generally moved off the channel that feeds it. They may have gone only a few paces. There is seldom any visible danger in the place they leave.

Their stillness is consequently difficult to interpret. A creature that has faced east all afternoon may have been watching the empty stairway. It may have been listening through four feet and comparing what it heard with the fifth.

It may have been doing something for which watching and listening are poor distinctions.

***

When an animal lowers itself, the legs fold outward rather than underneath. The body descends with extraordinary care. Its underside opens along several creases, exposing a broad patch of pale, closely packed fringes.

These fringes meet the ground.

For the first few moments nothing happens. Then the green nap beneath the animal darkens. A little water is pushed outward in a ring. The fringes spread, each finding a place among the wet filaments, and the two surfaces become difficult to tell apart.

An animal may remain like this for less than a minute or until the daylight has moved across the pool. There is no visible chewing or swallowing. Occasionally a slow thickening passes up one flank.

When it rises, the underside closes from the edges inward. The last point of contact stretches into a clear strand and breaks.

The damp oval left behind smells faintly of the inside of a split stalk.

They choose these places carefully. Much of the bank, to a human hand, feels equally warm and soft. A creature may test six or seven patches, touching each with the folded end of a forward leg, before lowering itself. There are favored places that several animals use in succession. There are others they avoid for years.

They do not defend the good places. If two arrive together, one waits with its head turned away. The waiting animal may rest a foot against the other’s leg.

It is tempting to call this feeding. It probably is feeding, among other things. The animals become lighter in color after a long contact, and the membrane of a nearby pool sometimes begins its slow contraction shortly afterward.

Once, an animal with a split along its flank was seen lowering itself beside a channel. It stayed there for three days. During that time the split acquired a clear covering, then a fine cream nap. The bank beneath it grew a raised oval which remained after the animal left.

The animal returned to that oval many times. It continued returning after the flank had become smooth.

***

They are not all alike.

One has a short rear leg and walks in a broad curve unless it makes a deliberate correction. Another carries its head low, almost between the two forward knees. An individual near the western vault has a dark line beneath the skin running from the head to the middle of its back. In certain lights the line divides into little branches.

These differences persist. Someone who leaves the country and comes back much later can recognize the same animals.

Their skin never looks old. There are no thickened joints, no worn feet. Even the short-legged one has the translucent, faintly swollen look of a body recently formed. Yet it knows where a channel passes beneath an apparently featureless flat. It knows the nearest quiet alcove from every point along the southern rim. If startled by a visitor, it takes a route around a fold in the ground that cannot be seen until you reach it.

Whatever age does to them, it does not make them look finished.

They learn people, too. At first they keep several body lengths away. After repeated encounters, an animal may allow someone to sit beside it. Eventually it may lower one of its empty-looking oval faces toward an outstretched hand.

The film is cool. Beneath it, the dark structure draws close.

Touching a leg produces a more complicated response. The animal shifts its weight without moving the touched foot, then sets another foot against your shoe. If you remain still, a faint pressure comes and goes through the sole. It resembles the beginning of a step, repeated without the step being taken.

They sometimes spend a long time doing this.

There is no reason to assume they mistake a visitor for one of themselves. Their contacts with each other are more elaborate. Two may stand joined at a foot while their free legs slowly change position. Once joined, they seldom look toward the same thing. One faces the pools, the other the rim, and the small adjustments continue between them.

At such times an approaching third animal usually waits.

***

Small ones are very rarely seen.

They do not follow the larger animals across the flats. They stay beneath overhangs, where the thin membranes show their vessels against the sky. Their legs are disproportionately short, and the folds of the underside extend farther up their flanks.

A small animal moves more quickly than an adult, in brief, untidy bursts. It will climb a wall, adhere there with all five feet, then seem unable to decide how to come down. An adult standing nearby sometimes places a leg against the wall. The small one transfers itself onto that leg and descends along it.

Nobody has seen them hatch from the house-sized bud.

There are smaller buds under the banks, but these open into water cups, pale scales, or nothing a visitor can identify. It would take years of watching a single one to be sure what became of it. Meanwhile, a small cream creature is discovered somewhere it was not seen before.

Adults bring them to the quiet alcoves.

There they settle close together with their feet touching. In the absence of the basin’s larger noises, one can hear little moist clicks passing among them. The small animals contribute irregularly. Sometimes an adult repeats a sequence until the small one has gone still; sometimes the adult stops and the small one continues alone.

They leave before rain begins, though the alcoves would keep them dry.

***

Most of their lives appear to be spent in company, but the company is spread over great distances. A group may occupy half a mile of bank, each member visible to the others across the shining ground. They seldom gather tightly except around the small ones.

An isolated animal will occasionally walk a long way to make a brief contact with another. It touches feet, waits, and returns to where it was standing.

The journey can take an hour. The contact lasts a few breaths.

There is one place where several of them go without lowering themselves to the ground: the dry depression beneath the unfinished bridge.

They descend its shallow sides and stand facing inward. They leave a space among them, not always at the center. Their heads turn toward that space. Now and then one extends a foot into it and rests only the tips of the lobes on the floor.

The ground there is sound. It has the same fine nap as the surrounding flats. Nothing emerges when they touch it.

Afterward they go back to the pools separately.

A visitor once stood in the space they left. The nearest animal backed away, then approached from a different angle. It touched the visitor’s ankle, carefully, with the folded tip of a leg. When the visitor moved, it stepped past and resumed its former position.

The others waited through the interruption.

***

At night they are easiest to find by the little changes they make in the water.

The basin continues rushing and knocking in the dark. Clouds passing beneath it whiten the open rim. Around a pool, the animals’ bodies are almost invisible, but their feet interrupt the reflection of the sky.

One lowers itself. Water lifts against the green cuff of the bank.

Farther away, another answers with a slight shift of weight.

Eventually most settle, although one or two remain standing. The settled bodies warm the air immediately above them. Their backs rise a little as the undersides unfold, and the cream skin takes on the faint green of whatever is moving through it.

Near morning they stand again, slowly separating themselves from the ground. A few remain attached after all the others have risen. Their companions wait nearby, feet spread on the wet bank.

The last strands lengthen. One breaks.

For a moment the newly standing animal holds a leg above the place where it lay. Then it sets the foot down, and the others begin to move.
