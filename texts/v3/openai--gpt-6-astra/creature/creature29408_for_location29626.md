The first sign of an inhabitant is usually something left in the way.

A pale weight rests across a threshold. It resembles a folded length of thick cloth, though there is no cloth in the city and nothing from which to make it. One end lies inside the room; the other extends a short distance into the street. Dust has gathered against it.

If you wait, the weight draws itself indoors.

This can take most of an hour. It is not an attempt to hide. The inhabitant has been resting with part of itself in the passage, and your arrival has reminded it of the inconvenience.

They are capable of much quicker movement.

You see this when the height comes through.

An inhabitant unfastens itself from the floor, rises on several narrow supports, and crosses to the sheltered side of its room in a succession of precise, almost impatient steps. For a moment it has the appearance of a tall person carrying too much bedding. Then it settles, and the resemblance is lost.

There are more of them than the empty streets suggest.

Some of what appear to be rounded benches are occupied benches. Some sleeping hollows contain bodies fitted so closely to their contours that only a dark line distinguishes flesh from stone. An inhabitant may rest this way through several passages of the traveling brightness. Its exposed surface grows cold. Dust comes to lie on it. The small, six-legged animals walk across it without alarm.

Look for the places where their hooked feet fail to find purchase.

The inhabitants’ skin is smooth and faintly yielding, cream-colored in its deeper folds, brown along surfaces habitually exposed to the air. Beneath it, long hard elements move independently. These can be brought together to make a limb, spread to support a great deal of weight, or folded out of use. There is no permanent distinction between an arm and a leg, though each individual has preferences. An old inhabitant may use the same three supports for walking for so long that it cannot easily remember how to walk otherwise.

Their bodies are substantial. They have weight, resistance, a mild animal warmth. A sudden pinch can hurt them. They are not made of dust, and they cannot pass through stone.

This last fact matters to them a great deal.

Along one edge of the body is a seam that opens into a narrow, moist recess. Inside are rows of short, translucent plates. They press this recess against the dark growth in cracks, remaining there until the plates have rasped the stone clean. Feeding leaves a delicate pattern of parallel scratches. The scratches disappear quickly under the passage of height, which is why the city seems to have no marks of use except those it makes itself.

The six-legged animals compete with them for food.

An inhabitant will gently push one aside with the end of a folded limb. Some are more patient than others. In a sheltered hall, you may find an individual allowing the little animals to feed along its own eating seam, removing mineral particles caught between the plates. It will remain utterly still while they work.

Elsewhere, an irritable feeder sweeps them off a promising crack again and again.

There is no face to help you decide which one you have met.

Their nearest equivalent to a face is a small area of skin that becomes intricately wrinkled when they attend to something. It can appear on any exposed part of the body. Several such areas sometimes form at once: one toward another inhabitant, one toward a doorway, one down against the floor.

They cannot see a distant district in the way you do.

They know it through the pressures it returns to the mountain. An open arcade, a court, a row of deep rooms each gives the passing air a different resistance. Those differences enter the stone. To an inhabitant lying with a broad surface against the ground, the city is full of distinct, slow impressions.

A neighboring house becoming shallower can be felt for days.

A familiar individual crossing a square can sometimes be recognized by the way it distributes its weight.

The inhabitants have no difficulty with a current that brings a remote balcony apparently close. They feel the gulf beneath the image. But a newly formed room can confuse them badly. It offers an unexpected depth where they remember resistance. Individuals approaching such a place will stop, flatten themselves, advance a little, and stop again.

Sometimes they gather to examine it.

A gathering looks, at first, like a heap of discarded skins.

Then you notice the small courtesies. One body lifts to admit another. A limb is withdrawn before it becomes trapped under a neighbor. A late arrival rests at the edge until space is made for it, or leaves after space has conspicuously failed to appear.

They communicate through contact, tightening and relaxing the hard elements inside their bodies. With a limb pressed against another limb, they can transmit movements too slight for you to feel. A conversation may continue across a communal bench, each participant touching the next.

What reaches the end is not always what began at the other.

They repeat things. They interrupt. An inhabitant wishing to make a point may lift part of its body and put it down with a deliberate firmness that causes every attentive surface in the room to wrinkle toward it.

Their most private exchanges take place with the soft inner folds pressed together.

Their quarrels are often conducted through the floor.

For a long time, a visitor might assume that all their concerns are practical: the condition of a route, the darkening of a wall, whether a hollow will remain safe through the next season. These matters do occupy them. But they also revisit passages no one needs to take. They arrange meetings in rooms with little food and poor shelter. An individual may cross two ridges to rest beside a particular other, arrive while that other is asleep, and wait without waking it.

Some maintain several sleeping places and move among them.

Others remain in the same house until leaving it has become dangerous.

They own very few objects. A loose piece of mineral is troublesome in a world where its height may cease to agree with the surface beneath it. Nevertheless, objects are kept.

In certain alcoves there are pale fragments wrapped in shed skin. Some are smooth, some angular, some so small that their preservation seems absurd. An inhabitant takes one out, turns it through several positions against an attentive patch, and puts it away again.

A fragment may have come from a room that no longer exists.

They do not show these things readily.

Their young begin as a thickening in an inner fold.

An adult carrying one moves with an unusual asymmetry, keeping that fold lifted clear of the ground. Other inhabitants make room for it even in narrow passages. Food may be left unscraped in the lowest part of a fissure, where the carrier can reach it without opening its body fully.

Eventually the young separates.

For some time it has no hard elements strong enough to support it. It lies in a shallow hollow, a warm, creased body scarcely larger than one of the little grazing animals. The adults rest around it in overlapping layers, leaving an opening toward the room.

A young inhabitant’s first independent movements are explorations of that opening.

It extends itself, touches the floor, recoils. Later it reaches the wall. Later still, it discovers that the wall can be followed out of sight of the hollow.

At this stage someone must keep bringing it back.

The young do not understand height merely because they are born here. They learn by being carried across moving boundaries, held with part of the adult’s body on either side. They learn the strain that precedes a rise and the brief relaxation that offers a safe foothold.

They make mistakes.

An adult may bear, deep inside a fold, the hard, pale scar of an early fall.

There are fewer young in the oldest districts. Those districts are safer, but their stone offers little fresh growth. In the newer quarters, where walls darken and split and passages change their width, food is plentiful. A household with a young one must eventually decide how much uncertainty it can endure.

Sometimes several move together.

They cannot dig a home or enlarge one. A niche remains a niche however badly they need a room. But bodies shelter stone from air, and repeated occupation can alter the conditions in which the architecture grows. An inhabited recess develops differently from an empty one. Its floor may become smoother; its entrance may persist while the neighboring entrances close.

The effects are slow and unreliable.

An inhabitant may spend much of its life encouraging a place that never becomes habitable.

Others will continue the attempt after it is gone.

The tall animal that walks the ridges troubles them long before it appears on the skyline.

Contact conversations stop. Bodies pull away from the walls. In a communal hall, everyone may suddenly gather in the center, leaving the benches bare.

Then comes the waiting.

They are reluctant to enter the street while it is nearby. They are equally reluctant to remain inside.

It does not seem interested in their flesh. Yet a room can become too shallow to contain an inhabitant while the doorway remains narrower than the inhabitant can make itself. The oldest houses sometimes contain pale, branching inclusions in the stone behind a shelf.

After the animal has passed, the survivors examine every room.

They work their limbs into sleeping hollows, pressing and withdrawing, testing the remaining depth. An individual may return repeatedly to a place it can no longer fit, trying a different arrangement of its body each time.

Eventually it stops.

Occasionally, however, a district needs the animal.

Rooms have deepened beyond their walls. Occupants resting beside one another begin to receive the distant pressures of different mountains. A limb laid down in a familiar place takes too long to encounter the floor.

In such a season the inhabitants leave certain approaches empty.

They gather elsewhere, keeping their bodies off the ground as much as possible. The unoccupied ridge offers its full exposure to the currents.

Whether this attracts the animal is uncertain. They do it nonetheless.

Their dead are difficult to distinguish from their sleepers at first.

Other inhabitants remain in contact with a dying body, adjusting their pressure as its responses become weaker. After the last response, they continue for a while. There is no obvious moment at which attendance becomes mourning.

The body gradually loses its ability to fold. Its hard elements settle into a single arrangement. Skin dries between them.

They carry it to an exposed place before it grows too brittle to move.

These places are usually shallow rooms opening directly onto the gulf. There the body wears away slowly, adding its powder to the suspended seams below. More than one inhabitant may visit. They press themselves against the remaining surface, which no longer yields, and stay until the moving height requires them to leave.

The warm square on the broad summit is not a burial place, nor does anyone live there permanently.

It is where inhabitants from distant ridges sometimes meet.

They arrive at different hours, by routes that look impossible from the paving. A body emerges over a low wall, unfolds from a passage, or comes slowly up the stair that returns from another mountain. Most have traveled for a long time.

At first they rest separately.

Then one extends a limb across the hollow. Another touches it. Others move close enough to join.

There are long pauses. Some arrivals are expected and some are not. Once in a while, an individual approaches the gathering and is recognized so suddenly that several bodies lift at once.

For the quiet hour, their warmth collects beneath them.

When the shallow line appears across the square, they feel it immediately. The conversation continues, but its shape changes. One withdraws a support from the forming edge. Another reaches across to preserve contact.

By morning there will be a step between them.

For now, they make room for it.
