The oldest of the white animals lives in a hollow in the wall that does not quite fit it anymore.

When it was smaller, it could withdraw all six feet and lie flush with the surrounding stone. Now one edge of its back projects. Water passing down the wall divides around it, leaving two faint tracks below. Its shell has grown unevenly: broad on the side facing the chamber, narrower where it presses against the hollow. Along the cramped edge, successive layers have folded into a thick, blunt lip.

It leaves this resting place each morning, though morning is not the arrival of light. Light has already been moving through the walls for some time. What wakes it is warmth reaching a particular depth in its shell.

First the feet appear.

They are darker than they look at a distance—not black, but a deep brown, with translucent joints. Each ends in two small hooks and a soft pad that spreads when pressed against stone. The front pair can fold inward almost to the mouth. The middle pair carry most of its weight. The hind pair remain attached to the wall until the rest of the body has lifted, then release together.

For a moment the animal stands high enough for light to pass beneath it.

Its underside is delicate. There are soft overlapping plates, short hairs worn away in places, a seam that opens and closes along either flank. The mouth lies well back between the front legs. Above it, almost hidden by the shell, two blunt feelers move independently. One touches the wall. The other sweeps the air.

There is no visible head to turn toward whatever interests it. Attention shows itself in the feet: the gradual release of one pad, the settling of another, a change in how its weight is held.

Today it goes toward the dry channel.

It carries no chip. Its usual one lies where it left it, beside a shallow blemish in the floor. The chip is dark green when wet, gray otherwise, and small enough to disappear beneath the animal’s body. One edge is thin. The opposite edge has a notch against which a front foot can brace.

Another animal has moved it during the night.

The old one finds it a short distance away and places both front feet on it. It rocks the chip once, then walks over it. For several minutes nothing happens. Eventually a foot reaches back beneath the shell, catches the notched edge, and draws it forward.

The animal continues toward the channel, carrying the chip at an angle that keeps its sharpened side clear of the floor.

***

The white back is not simply armor.

Under its outer surface lies a branching layer of very fine chambers. When the animal has spent a long time in brightness, these acquire a yellow color, beginning near the rim and spreading inward. The center of an old shell never becomes as yellow as the edges. It is too thick, crossed by opaque seams where damage has been repaired.

Whatever lives in those chambers is smaller than the eye can resolve. It passes into a new shell while that shell is still soft. It needs light, but also something the animal gets from the floor, and something else brought down in rainwater. An animal kept in the brightest part of the hollow would still have to travel.

The old one settles beside a mineral crust.

Its front feet hold the chip against the floor. The middle feet pull the body forward; the hind feet resist. The movement is scarcely a movement at all, only a tightening and release, but pale scrapings gather along the stone’s edge.

After a while it lifts the chip and feeds beneath it.

There is a small rhythm to this work. Scrape, rest, draw in the loosened material. Between bouts, the feelers touch the surface ahead. A deposit that looks identical to the last may be passed over. Another may occupy it until the brighter part of the day has almost gone.

Around the animal’s mouth are several smaller creatures, visible only when they cross the dark chip. They shelter among the hairs beneath its body and emerge to eat what it misses. Occasionally it combs them away with a foot. Most return.

One has lived beneath the thickened side of its shell for nearly an entire season.

***

The old animal does not go everywhere in the chamber.

There is a broad patch of floor it has crossed only when the wall beside it was too wet to climb. There is a crack it can no longer enter. It still pauses at that crack and puts a feeler inside.

Its journeys follow differences too slight to see from standing height: a roughness that gives purchase, a warm seam, a strip of floor where water has recently passed. When a flake falls from the ceiling, it may alter these journeys for weeks. The animal will approach the new edge, investigate it, withdraw, and eventually find a way around. Later, after the flake has been shifted or broken, the detour may remain.

Other animals use much the same routes.

When two meet, they often remain facing one another with their front feet lifted. The feet touch. Sometimes one climbs partly onto the other’s back, then steps down on the opposite side. Sometimes both turn.

The old one is slow to yield, but a very small animal can make it stop.

The smallest have shells so thin that their bodies show through as a faint gray shape. They move in short, uneven runs and settle abruptly, as if the effort of keeping their feet in order exhausts them. When one passes underneath an adult, the adult raises itself and waits.

This happens even when the adult is feeding.

***

After heavy rain, the old animal goes to the roots.

It arrives later than most. The young are already scattered around the place where the drops land, and several adults occupy the narrow wet line leading away from it. The old one takes a position farther down, where the water spreads into a film.

Its shell is dry and almost white. Its feet darken as they touch the moisture.

Above, a bead swells on a root tip. A pale thread has come down inside it, folded against the curved surface. The thread struggles occasionally. Each movement makes a small disturbance in the light held by the drop.

When the water falls, the old animal feels the impact through the floor.

All its feet tighten.

The young rush inward, then stop to drink. The thread lies uncoiled among them. One of the middle-sized animals catches it, holding it down with a front pad while its mouth reaches forward.

The old one drinks from the advancing edge of the wetness. Water collects against the hairs around its mouth. Its flank seams close.

For a long time it remains there.

A young animal climbs onto its back, perhaps following warmth. It searches the shell’s irregular center, tests a repaired seam, and settles in the shallow depression behind it. By the time the old animal starts home, there are three young riding on it.

Two step off along the way.

The third stays until the wall grows steep.

***

Its shell has broken twice.

The first break was small and has disappeared beneath later growth. The second left a pale ridge across the back and cost it the last joint of one hind foot. That foot still reaches and presses, though on smooth stone it sometimes slips.

Because of this, the animal returns to its hollow from below. It approaches the projecting lip, finds the rough place beneath it, and lifts itself in stages. The damaged foot searches longer than the others.

Once settled, it works the dark chip out from beneath its body and holds it against the wall. There is just enough space for it beside the folded front legs.

Light remains in the ceiling. The old animal’s back takes on a muted yellow, except along the repaired ridge.

Later, when the chamber has darkened, the branching layer inside the shell is briefly visible. It gives the animal a depth it does not have by day: something faintly illuminated beneath a thick, cloudy cover. The glow weakens slowly. A feeler emerges, touches the chip, withdraws.

In the night, a drop falls somewhere it cannot reach.

The animal wakes enough to loosen its feet, then settles again. Warmth is still coming from the stone behind it. Beneath the cramped edge of its shell, the little creature among its hairs moves closer to the body.
