In the hollow beneath the oldest floor there is a soft animal. Most of it has never been in the court.

One part can be seen through the deep fracture near the wall, though it is easily mistaken for the inner surface of a jar. It is cream-colored, faintly glossy, and crossed by several shallow creases. Lime has collected along the creases. If watched through a full rise and fall of water, the surface changes very little. A speck embedded in it may move the width of a grain of sand.

The animal is not asleep. That is simply how slowly this part of it moves.

Elsewhere it is quicker.

Its body passes through the buried pottery. In a broad vessel it thickens into a lobe; at a narrow neck it draws out into a cord. Some cords have grown around obstructions and joined again on the other side. Others terminate in soft, sealed ends against mortar. There are places where a jar has cracked under the animal’s gradual pressure, and places where the animal has narrowed itself so completely that water can still pass beside it.

It is difficult to decide how large it is. The pottery gives it a succession of shapes, none of which would be possible if it were taken out. There may be a great deal of it beneath the wall. There is certainly some beneath the basin: when the basin empties, a pale fold occasionally rises into one of the worn holes along its lip.

The fold withdraws if touched.

It withdraws more quickly from living skin than from wood.

Its exposed surfaces carry a fine nap, like the skin inside a small mushroom. Under water these hairs lie in one direction. A little current passes along them, independent of the current in the drains. Fine matter collects there: the dust from a crab’s meal, a loosened patch of wall growth, the nearly invisible remains of an insect after its wings have come away. All of this travels slowly toward deeper folds.

Much of the animal’s feeding is unremarkable. It cleans water.

The rest accounts for the behavior of the floor.

During the falling water, thin edges emerge between the bricks. They are transparent and have no strength against a direct current. Each lies down in the wetness and spreads. A large one might cover most of a brick, but it is so thin that the brick’s scratches remain plainly visible through it. Only at the margin is there a difference: water beads there instead of flattening.

The edges grow still thinner as they extend. They enter the shallow depressions left by fingers centuries earlier. They fit themselves around grains of lime. On a worn brick, one can sometimes make out a pattern of delicate branches, each branch ending in a minute cup.

There are hundreds of these cups on a single edge.

A walking insect presses several of them closed.

In the quiet, that pressure can be followed. The nearest cups turn toward it; a crease travels through the transparent sheet; something beneath the brick contracts. The sheet gathers around the insect from below, wetting its legs with a fluid through which the joints become unusually distinct.

The whole event takes less time than a drop takes to gather on a roof peg. Afterward the sheet lies flat again. A wing may remain upright for a while, apparently caught in an ordinary film of water.

An insect that does not move can stand on the same brick unharmed.

The animal senses weight badly and changes in weight extremely well. The steady pressure of a crab is almost indistinguishable to it from a clay fragment. A foot lifting, however, produces a clean sequence of signals. So does a fish striking the shallow water with its tail. The animal follows these sequences along the floor.

When the pier calls, every cup is pressed at once.

The membranes tremble against the water; the bricks transmit the enlarged voices; the little transparent margins shiver without direction. They begin to fold inward. During a full chorus the sheets vanish into the joints, and loose debris rushes into the spaces they occupied.

Then the crabs cross.

They do not hurry as much as one might expect. A crab burdened with pottery is incapable of much speed, and the crossing has to be completed without abandoning its shelter. It proceeds at its usual sideways pace, occasionally pausing to adjust the fragment over its back. Around it, feet and claws and dragged clay make a confusion that the animal below cannot resolve.

The youngest crabs are less exact. They start late or continue after the last call. There are small pieces of fired clay in the joints that no crab now carries.

The animals in the pier have their own contact with what lives beneath them.

At the bottom of several chambers, behind the dried membranes, a pale cushion fills a little of the available space. The amphibians sleep with their bellies against it. They scrape it with their hind feet before laying eggs. When the water rises, the cushions swell and become cool enough to draw moisture from the chamber walls.

Occasionally a cushion takes an egg. More often it consumes only the jelly left after hatching.

An adult driven from its chamber will go to considerable trouble to return to the same one. It squeezes past its neighbors, rejects empty holes, and pushes against a repaired membrane until it breaks. Some chambers have been occupied so continuously that the animals’ bodies have worn smooth hollows in the clay beside the pale tissue.

The contact tells them things the open air does not.

Long before a bubble reaches the basin, pressure changes in the buried jars. The soft body tightens in some vessels and slackens in others. A cushion at the base of the pier lifts an amphibian slightly against the roof of its hole. Its throat begins to work.

Whether the first call is a warning, discomfort, or simply the consequence of being squeezed is not apparent. The others answer it. Their voices travel down the pier as readily as they travel out.

Below, the animal draws back from the floor.

It has scars. A collapsed jar has pinched one of its thicker cords almost through. The tissue beyond the constriction is dull and loose, though still alive. Near a broken clay flap there is a row of regular injuries, opening and closing with the water. These heal incompletely. The flap has been striking the same place for a very long time.

In other places, the animal has altered the fit of the machinery. A lobe seals a passage that would otherwise leak. A toughened pad receives a falling clay weight. These surfaces have become gray and firm, unlike the tender feeding edges. Water runs over them with the same polished appearance it has over the pottery.

The court would still fill without the animal. It would fill differently.

The animal would presumably live without the court, but no one seeing its body could say in what shape, or where.

There are smaller ones in a few of the pierced lumps beside the basin. Each occupies a single hole. They have translucent bodies, a pair of thick lips, and a short fringe that can be extended from either end. When disturbed, they draw into the middle and hold fast. The clay is their shelter, not their shell; they can leave it.

Most do not.

A crab sometimes carries one into the wall. Water occasionally rolls an occupied lump toward a drain. What happens beyond the drains cannot be seen from inside the court.

The large animal produces these small ones irregularly. For several weeks there will be none, and then the underside of the basin’s rim will be crowded with pale knots. The fish eat many. Others detach and move through the water by turning themselves inside out, slowly and repeatedly, until they encounter a cavity narrow enough to grip.

This is the only time they have a whole, visible shape.

The one beneath the floor has long since exceeded such a shape. Its living surfaces are separated by brick, joined through darkness, pressed into vessels bearing the fingerprints of people whose bones may no longer exist. In one jar it is feeding. In another it is healing. Beneath the pier it holds a clutch of eggs against a damp wall.

During the quiet interval, a transparent edge appears beside a crab’s raised foot.

It spreads until it touches the foot, then stops. The crab does not lower its leg.

Below them both, water enters a jar already almost full. The animal feels the remaining air compress along a length of its body. Its tissue shifts against the sleepers in the pier.

One wakes with its throat inflated.
