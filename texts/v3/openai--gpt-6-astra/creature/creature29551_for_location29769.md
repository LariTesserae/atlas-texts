It lives beneath the lowest shelf of the seep, where the space between two stones is deeper than it looks. Most of what can be seen from outside is its back: a low, uneven oval, chalk-colored at the center and grayer around the edges. A few grains of limestone adhere to it. When dry, it resembles the upper face of a stone that has almost worked free of the wall.

It is about the length of a hand, though this is difficult to judge until it unfolds. The back is not quite a shell. It consists of overlapping plates, thick in the middle and thin where one passes beneath another. Along their joins, the pale surface gives way to a soft gray skin. The plates can shift enough to let the animal fit itself into the shallow irregularities under the ledge. At rest, it leaves very little space around its body.

Its underside is softer, the color of soaked bark. There are six short limbs, held so close that they first appear to be folds. Each ends in a broad pad with a firmer rim. It can hold to wet limestone without claws, and when it moves across the wall there is a slight delay between lifting one pad and setting down the next, as though it must make certain of each patch of stone.

The head is small and ordinarily withdrawn. Two dark eyes sit low on either side, protected by pale brows continuous with the first plate of the back. They do not catch much light. Below them is a wide, delicate mouth, almost invisible when closed.

It feeds along the wet seams. What looks like clean stone carries enough for it: a film of algae, softened organic matter caught among mineral grains, the minute growth in a groove that never quite dries. It places its mouth against the surface and stays there. Much later, it advances a little. The places it has grazed are not conspicuously cleaner, but the wet stone takes the light differently in narrow, interrupted bands.

The basin is too open for it. It approaches along the wall and drinks at the dark margin, lowering the underside of its head into the water. Its body remains above the ordinary waterline. It has no reason to cross the silt, and the pale gravel is uncomfortable beneath its pads. Almost all its journeys follow the same few lengths of rock.

There is another shelter behind the retaining wall. To reach it, the animal must pass beneath a fallen block, cross the foot of the wiry grass, and climb through a joint between two fitted stones. The joint is narrow enough to brush both sides of its back. Inside, the earth is dry and fine, and the stone above keeps some warmth after the open ledges have cooled.

It uses this shelter infrequently. Perhaps the seep sometimes becomes too cold; perhaps a change in the water, too slight to alter its appearance, makes the animal leave. Once within the wall, it turns with considerable effort until its head faces outward. The maneuver has made a small hollow in the earth. A few of its shed plates lie there, thinner and more translucent than the ones it wears.

It is not always wholly still. Even in repose, something happens beneath the edge of its body: a slow widening, a pause, a settling back. Moisture darkens the stone immediately below it. Occasionally one pad releases and takes hold again without moving the animal forward.

If you sat on the dry slab, it might remain hidden for the whole of your visit. If you returned often enough, you would begin to distinguish it from the rock by an awkwardness in the fit—a rounded edge where the limestone ought to continue inward, or a seam that had not been there before.

Your first clear sight of its face would probably come while you were looking elsewhere. It extends its head cautiously but not with any evident alarm. The eyes turn very little. A pair of short feelers emerges beside the mouth and touches the wet stone, one and then the other.

It knows the hollow through such contacts. The underside of a shelf. A groove broad enough to rest in. The rough crossing beside the grass. At one point on its route, it reaches for a hold before its head has cleared the corner.

There may be others beyond the entrance, in pockets of limestone where a little water persists. Nothing in its behavior tells you whether it has met them. Its back carries an old injury near one edge: two plates have grown together in a slight ridge, and it must lower that side to pass beneath its usual shelf.

Once through, it settles against the damp wall. A bead of water enlarges above it. Before the bead falls, the animal has raised its mouth.
