The person who uses the shelter has to take off her pack before she can get inside. She pushes it through the doorway first, then lowers herself onto one knee. The other knee does not bend far enough. There is a smooth place on the threshold where she puts her left hand.

She comes up several times in the short season, staying two nights, sometimes five. A change in the weather can keep her longer. From below, there are weeks when the shelf cannot be distinguished from the ice above it, and nobody attempts to reach her.

She is smaller than the shelter makes her seem. Standing outside, she barely reaches the lowest scar in the wall. Her face is broad across the cheekbones, brown except for the paler creases beside her eyes. She has lost a little of the outer edge of one ear. When she listens, she turns the other toward the sound, an unconscious courtesy she extends to people, birds, and the rock.

Her clothes have been repaired with material of several different weights. The oldest patches are the softest. Inside her coat, against her ribs, she carries a flat packet of dry tinder; outside it hangs a leather purse containing things that can bear getting wet. A needle in a bone case. A lump of resin. Two spare thongs. A piece of dark glass wrapped in wool, through which she looks at the snow before choosing a way across it.

Her pack is mostly fuel on the way up.

The sticks are cut to fit the hearth. She also brings dense cakes that burn with little flame and an odor that seems, in this air, almost unbearably domestic. For a few minutes after she lights them, the shelf smells of the settlements below: animals under roofs, damp courtyards, cooking pots. Then a shift of wind takes the smell away.

She has been coming here since before there was room for the shelter.

At first there was only a strip between the wall and the ice, wide enough to stand sideways. Water ran beneath it with a muffled, continuous force. She remembers putting her palm against the ice and finding grit embedded there, each grain held a little way out from its own dark hollow. She remembers a stone appearing over three visits: first its tip, then a flat shoulder, then the whole thing balanced on a narrow foot.

It is lying on its side now. She keeps her food behind it.

People who travel through the mountains ask her about the crossings. Where the old snow has opened. Whether the loose slope above the eastern saddle can still be passed before sunrise. Which water can be reached without descending all the way into the valley. She answers with particulars, and grows impatient when particulars are repeated back to her as assurances.

“You crossed it,” they say.

“On the fourth day of that month,” she says. “Before the sun.”

The shelf is not on the quickest route anywhere. She first returned because she wanted to see what the retreating ice would uncover. Later she built the shelter. Now she sometimes finds herself thinking about one of its stones while doing an unrelated task far below: whether it has fallen, whether the narrow black opening behind it has widened.

She would not say that this is a sufficient reason to climb so high. Nevertheless, she comes.

***

In the mornings she waits for light to reach the opposite wall before leaving her bedding.

During the night the little mammals pass behind her head. There is a passage between the shelter’s rear slab and the recess, too narrow for her hand, and they have retained it despite her occupation. Their feet make a faint brushing sound. Once, a stem dragged through there catches on the fibers of her cap and moves it several inches across the floor.

Her provisions hang from a roof stone in a lidded basket. The animals investigate the cord. They have eaten the grease from one of her spare boot bindings and cut a small, neat opening in the corner of her sleeping roll. She has repaired the opening twice.

She knows several of them, although not as reliably as she thinks. One has a pale notch above its nose. Another carries too much at once, backing into its doorway with a spray of leaves that catches on both sides. It stops, comes out, turns the bundle, tries again. She watches this while chewing her breakfast. Sometimes she laughs, and the animal abandons the whole load until she looks away.

Their stores are not all deep underground. Under certain overhanging stones lie clipped stems laid out where the moving air can reach them. On warm days the animals turn and retrieve these. A sudden cloud sends them hurrying between the little drying places and their holes.

She does not put her tools there.

This is partly consideration and partly experience. A paddle left across a busy entrance was gnawed along its handle. A mitten disappeared beneath a willow mat and came back, after much undignified searching, without its thumb.

***

Once the sun is on the basin, she makes a slow circuit.

She carries a length of knotted cord and a slender piece of antler. At some cracks she kneels and inserts the antler until a mark on its side disappears. At others she stretches the cord between two points. The procedure is not exact enough to satisfy anyone accustomed to straight walls and level floors. Here, where even the reference stones move, she supplements it with memory.

This edge was flush with that pale vein.

That shard used to shade the small red plant at noon.

Here, last time, she could put down her whole foot.

She checks the stones above the shelter before checking anything else.

The incisions inside are her record, though they would be difficult for another person to read. A short stroke may mean a visit; a pair of strokes, a fracture that has opened in two places. Certain marks are grouped beneath a shallow hooked line representing the lip. She can no longer confidently interpret all the older ones. Once she made a mark beside the wrong group, noticed only after cutting it, and sat for some time with the knife in her lap.

Since then she has carried a small board on which to scratch things first.

Most days there is little to record. This does not make the circuit feel unnecessary. She can spend a long time beside an unchanged crack, enjoying the unusual ease of believing what she sees.

The split green bank troubles her. She has considered propping its lower half with stones. But to get stones beneath it she would have to lift the hanging turf, and when she touches it the whole mass gives slightly, roots tightening in the dark.

For now she moves her stepping route farther away.

***

The birds permit her within a few yards. At that distance their apparent whiteness resolves into ash, cream, and a faint warm color along the folded wings. Their black eyes follow her without a turn of the head.

She collects feathers caught among the stones, choosing those with intact shafts. Some are useful for drawing resin into a seam or brushing grit from a cut. Others accumulate in the bundle inside. When she cannot sleep, she sometimes holds one against the narrow opening of the doorway. It tells her whether the air is entering or leaving.

One of the birds has learned to visit after her meal. It does not approach the hearth directly. It lands on the lip, waits, moves to a nearer stone, waits again. Eventually it takes a crumb scarcely larger than the gravel around it.

She has tried to imitate its single dry call. The bird has never answered.

There are other residents she knows chiefly through mistakes. The beetle beneath a warmed slab, exposed when she wants a level place for her cup. The moth that looks exactly like a blemish until her fingers nearly close over it. The small dark bodies on the snow which she once took for windblown dust.

She now examines a scoop of snow before melting it.

Even so, insects sometimes turn up in the water. She lifts them out with the tip of the antler and leaves them on a dry stone. Some walk away. Some remain exactly as she placed them.

***

In bad weather she lies inside with her boots against her belly to keep the leather from stiffening. There is not enough height to sit comfortably everywhere. She knows where she can raise her head and where she cannot.

Cloud enters through the joints. The bedding becomes damp along its outer fold. She eats food she would ordinarily soften over the fire and thinks, without pleasure, about the fuel she has saved.

At such times the shelter has none of the charm it possesses when seen from outside in sunlight. It is a small stone cavity containing a person who needs to urinate and does not want to put on her boots. Her shoulder aches. A drop gathers repeatedly at the same place on the roof and falls beside her face.

She shifts the bowl beneath it.

When the weather clears, she crawls out stiffly and stands for a while holding the wall. The animals are often working already. This irritates her a little, as though they have been granted an earlier morning.

Then she warms her hands around the cup, and the irritation passes.

***

Before leaving, she cleans the hearth and covers the ash. She checks the basket for crumbs, folds the bedding into its hide wrapper, and puts the paddle where water will not collect beneath it.

There is always something she is reluctant to leave unfinished: a crack she would like to see through one more thaw, a flower still closed, a stone giving occasional small sounds without visibly changing position.

She goes anyway. The descent is safer while the upper gravel remains frozen.

For the first few yards she walks backward, testing each step with her heel. Then the route turns under the lip and she disappears.

Above her, an animal comes out beside the shelter. It sits with its forefeet close together, smelling the air. Presently it crosses to the threshold and picks up a length of fiber loosened from her pack.

It has to pull hard to free one end from beneath the stone where she rests her hand.
