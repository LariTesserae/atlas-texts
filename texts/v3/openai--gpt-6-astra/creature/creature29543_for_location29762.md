The resident feels your arrival through the handrail. By the sixth step it has stopped what it is doing. By the twelfth it has put its tool down somewhere above the water.

You may have difficulty finding it even when it is close. Its back, presented between two benches, looks like something covered for storage: a sack over a motor, perhaps, or the collapsed hood of an old pram. There is a seam down the middle and a series of broad folds toward the bottom. Water darkens these folds unevenly. A pale stalk lies across one shoulder without appearing to rest on anyone.

Then it turns enough to look at you, and the stalk slides off.

It is about the size of a small adult when it stands, though standing is an awkward and infrequent arrangement of its body. Its usual height is that of the benches. The legs fold deeply beside the belly; the long arms can work ahead of it while it moves. It passes under a cross-brace by lowering one shoulder and drawing the rest of itself through, slowly, without the little adjustments you would expect from something squeezing into a space. When it has passed, the foliage closes almost where it was.

Its skin is gray-brown, with lighter areas inside the elbows and under the jaw. In the pump-room light those areas have a faint pinkness. Silt adheres to its shins. Along the spine there are small overlapping plates, soft-edged and darker than the surrounding skin, which lift a little when it bends. Fern spores sometimes germinate in the material collected between them. It removes the seedlings when they become troublesome, reaching back with considerable patience.

The hands are the easiest part to watch. They are narrow, capable hands, with blunt nails worn thinner on one side. A thumb closes against two fingers while the others brace against brick or bench mesh. It can strip a strand of root from a grate without breaking it. It can hold a washer upright on one fingertip while reaching for the bolt that belongs with it. The skin of its palms is almost white and densely creased.

It dislikes letting visitors see these hands idle. If you remain nearby, it generally finds something to carry.

There is no uniform. What first appears to be a garment is partly skin and partly a piece of heavy rubber sheeting, cut to fit over the shoulders. The sheeting has been repaired with wire at one corner. A loose pocket hangs inside it, kept against the body, containing a short knife, a square-ended scraper, and a brass key on a length of cord. The knife has scarcely any point left. Its handle is wrapped in green insulating tape that has gone black in the grooves.

It keeps the central aisle open. This takes less cutting than you might think. Much of the work consists of turning a new shoot while it is still tender, lifting a vine over a bracket, or moving a pot a finger’s breadth before its roots fix it in place. Where a stem must be cut, the resident supports it below the blade. Severed growth is carried beneath the benches and tucked into hollows in the leaf mold.

It makes different allowances for different plants. A begonia with brittle stems receives a generous margin. A vine that repeatedly crosses the stopcock opening is cut back almost to its source. Once a broad leaf has been allowed to occupy a certain place, however, the resident seems reluctant to damage it. There is one near the bottom of the stairs that obliges it to duck every time it passes. The leaf is clean, healthy, and much older than the leaves around it.

The sump is attended to without such accommodations.

At the first change in the pumps’ morning cycle, the resident clears the bars. It works upstream, gathering duckweed in the cup of one hand, laying it aside in a shallow broken pot where the water cannot take it straight back. Fibers are pulled free. Grit is scraped from the corners. Anything that catches twice is investigated.

A visitor might mistake this for an occupation. There are tools, regular tasks, even a place where completed work is evident. But when the pumps sound wrong, the resident’s whole body changes. The folds of its back tighten. Its lower jaw presses against its throat. It sets one palm flat on the floor and remains there until the vibration resolves.

It sleeps below the raised potting platform, with one wrist resting on the pipe that enters through the wall.

The hollow there is lined with coir teased out of the old bales. It is fairly dry. Two pieces of slate keep the bedding off the concrete, and a sloping fragment of plastic catches the drops from overhead and conducts them beyond the entrance. Inside are a shallow bowl, a folded remnant of cloth, and several objects brought down from the stairs: a blue bottle cap, a smooth white fragment of tile, a transparent ruler broken at eleven centimeters. They have no evident use in maintaining the garden. The ruler is kept clean.

It eats in this hollow, mostly the softened bases of fallen stems and the thick, pale growth found beneath rotting timber. It sorts through what it gathers. Some things are rejected after being held briefly against the mouth; others are laid aside until later. Occasionally a cut piece of fruit appears on the potting bench, left by someone from the buildings above. The resident takes it after the donor has gone.

It washes the bowl afterward. The water is poured onto a particular fern.

People who service the machinery know it is there. Their accommodations are small and rarely discussed. Tools are not left on the floor. The pump-room door is propped securely rather than allowed to swing. Before a pump is shut down, someone strikes the pipe three times with a spanner.

During these visits the resident stays beyond the doorway, usually concealed by the foliage around the stopcock. If a small part rolls out into the garden, a pale hand eventually returns it to the threshold. It does not enter the chamber while the workers are inside. Once they leave, it checks the floor for spilled oil, using the folded cloth from its sleeping place.

It is cautious with strangers but not entirely retiring. A person who stands too long in the aisle will feel two fingers against the back of a wrist, requesting passage. Someone reaching toward a loose electrical fitting may find the rubber-covered shoulder quietly occupying the space between. If you sit on the potting platform, the resident will wait nearby until you move. Part of the platform sounds different under weight, and it will not let anyone sit on that part.

Whether it remembers faces is uncertain. It certainly remembers footsteps. Some arrivals bring it into the aisle before the visitor has reached the bottom. Others cause it to put away the knife.

There are afternoons when all the necessary work is finished. The channel is running, the doors are clear, nothing has fallen against the hot pipes. Then it goes to the far wall and eases itself down beside the fern that covers the enamel sign.

A drip forms above this spot, at the edge of a split pipe jacket. It falls at irregular intervals. The resident holds one hand beneath it, palm up, while the other rests loosely on its knee.

It could move a little closer and catch every drop. Instead it watches them strike the ends of its fingers, occasionally turning its hand so that the next one lands somewhere else.
