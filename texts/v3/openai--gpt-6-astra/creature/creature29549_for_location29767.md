At first you might take one for something left outside a shelter: a bundle of harvested fans, tied upright and covered with dust.

Then a hand comes out from under the folds and turns a bowl a quarter of the way around.

This is not a furtive movement. The person has been sitting there openly, working. Across their lap is a strip of leaf with its soft material scraped away, leaving the ribs connected by a translucent skin. They are testing it against the rim of the bowl. Each time the strip bends, they press two fingers to its middle and wait.

Their patience makes them difficult to notice. So does the color of their clothing. They wear several short, overlapping mantles, pale on the outside and almost black beneath, with the edges weighted by chips of stone. These do not flutter. Nothing about a seated person suggests that it ought to move.

When the work is finished, they stand in stages.

First the hands find the ground. Then the body lifts between the arms, and the legs unfold underneath it. For a moment the figure is very broad and low. Only after both feet have settled does it straighten into something like an ordinary human height.

The joints have more freedom than yours, but this is not immediately apparent. Their movements are so deliberate that an elbow can turn the wrong way without looking wrong. You notice instead how thoroughly they arrive at each position. They never seem to be halfway through a step.

Their feet are bare.

Each has a wide, divided sole, with a deep crease running back from the space between the two largest toes. On a level slab the halves lie together. On uncertain stone they spread and settle independently. The sole is dark, slightly glossy, and thick enough that sharp mineral needles break beneath it without drawing anything from the skin.

They carry sandals for entering shelters. These are soft pads of stripped leaf, fastened around the ankle. Inside, where someone may be sleeping, even the small vibration of a foot on stone is an intrusion.

Their faces are less concealed than their bodies. A mantle rises behind the head, but leaves the cheeks and brow exposed. The eyes are large without being round, narrowed by two sets of lids. One pair closes sideways and is thin enough to see through. When a fresh split brightens nearby, those inner lids draw across the eyes and give them a milky appearance.

There is no visible opening to the ear.

Along each side of the neck lies a shallow fold. The skin within it is very fine, closely pleated, and never dusty. Sometimes, particularly near a garden, the fold opens a little.

It would be easy to suppose that they cannot hear the mountains. They can. A faulty arch will make one turn before any stone has visibly shifted. A person asleep beneath three layers of weighted fans may wake because a ledge half a mile away has acquired an underside.

But hearing does not occupy them as it would occupy you. There is no flinch, no exhausted bracing against the next instant of noise. Under the skin of the chest are broad, overlapping plates of something softer than bone. The plates move against one another with each breath. Their teeth are seated separately in dark, yielding sockets. Even the small bones of the fingers are divided by thick pads.

A hard continuous structure would carry the mountains straight through them.

They have very few.

Their bodies are dry, though not withered. The skin gives under a fingertip and slowly returns. A cut does not bleed. Its edges curl inward around a dark, tacky substance, which becomes opaque when exposed to air. They dress such injuries with a folded piece of fresh leaf and bind it tightly. A wounded hand may remain bound for a season.

They eat the nodules from the gardens, opening each with a thumbnail and putting the warm center against the roof of the mouth. The rind is saved. A string of empty rinds, flattened and pierced, is useful for taking the tension off a narrow binding.

They do not eat much at once. Most carry a little food under the inner mantle, where it stays warm. To offer a visitor an entire bowl is not evidence of plenty. It may be the food intended for the next several journeys.

If the visitor reaches for a second nodule before finishing the first, the bowl is quietly moved farther away.

They are hospitable, but they watch what people do with things.

---

There are more of them than the shelters suggest.

A shelter does not belong to a household. It belongs to a route, and whoever uses the route is expected to understand its needs. Food may be left in a bowl, but a blade is usually placed beneath it, so that taking the food also uncovers the work.

A damaged lining. A loose floor slab. A bundle that needs turning.

Some repairs are marked with a palm laid flat in green dust. Others have a line drawn through the palm. The distinction matters. A visitor who replaces a deliberately loosened stone can make a shelter dangerous.

Their own possessions are few enough to fit beneath the mantles, but not so few that they cease to be particular about them. One person favors a blade too short for anyone else to grip comfortably. Another carries a bowl whose broken rim has been polished smooth through long use. The leaf strips used to bind their clothing are worked into patterns on the hidden side. When two people sit close together, one will occasionally turn back an edge to show the other a newly finished piece.

They can be vain about this.

They also become irritated, impatient, possessive. A badly divided harvest can keep two people from sharing a sleeping niche for years. Someone who leaves a tool at an uncertain edge may find it waiting at every shelter ahead of them, carried there by others and set conspicuously in the entrance.

They travel alone often, but solitude is not their preference in all things.

At a meeting, they first stand within sight of one another. Both spread their hands, turn them, and show the soles of their feet one at a time. This establishes something that cannot safely be assumed merely from recognizing a face.

Then they approach.

Their most exact speech is made with the hands in contact. One palm rests over the back of the other person’s hand; fingers press, shift, hold. The exchange is not a rapid tapping code. A meaning may depend on the pressure that remains after a movement, or on whether the receiving hand yields.

A long conversation looks like two people helping each other hold something invisible.

At a distance they use larger gestures. Those are sufficient for *come*, *stay*, *the step is new*, *I have food*, and a considerable variety of warnings. They are not sufficient for an apology. Someone who wants to apologize may have to walk for days.

Young people sometimes try to settle such matters across a gap anyway. Their gestures become larger and less useful. An older companion will eventually put a hand over the moving wrist.

---

Children are carried against the lower back, beneath the mantles, where the adult’s broad clothing hides them completely.

You may watch a person for several hours before a second, much smaller hand appears beside their hip.

The young are soft-jointed and extraordinarily adhesive. They hold with the whole palm, not just the fingers. Set on a sloping slab, a small child spreads all four limbs and remains where it was put. It takes considerable effort to persuade it to lift a hand.

They are not born knowing which surfaces can be trusted.

Their first journeys consist of being placed on one safe stone after another while an adult waits close by. They touch every edge. They put their faces against the ground. Sometimes they refuse a slab the adult has crossed many times.

The refusal is respected until the slab has been tested again.

This does not mean the child is always right. Children also refuse surfaces because they are cold, or unusually pale, or because being carried is preferable. Adults know this. There are familiar, unmistakable arguments conducted with one small hand firmly attached to a parent’s cheek.

No one allows a child to play with a loose stone near an interval.

Their toys are joined things: rings cut from leaf bases, nests of flexible ribs, flat pieces threaded onto a cord that cannot be pulled free. The first object a child makes without help is usually a short tether. It is examined seriously, tested, and used.

Only much later does the child receive something small enough to lose.

Where the children come from is harder to establish. There are stretches of time during which a person ceases traveling and stays deep within a garden. Others bring food and fresh lining. The leaves are bent together to make a chamber, but not cut; their living roots remain fastened to the rock.

Afterward, there may be two people where there was one.

The adult is thinner and has difficulty straightening. For a long time they will not walk close to an edge.

---

Age is not chiefly visible in the face.

It appears first in the reach.

An old person may rest a hand on a wall that seems a little too far away. You look again and find no obvious error: the arm is there, the hand is there, and the distance between them seems ordinary. Yet when someone younger takes the same place, they have to lean.

Later, doorways become troublesome.

The person fits through easily enough, but something about their passage leaves the interior farther from the entrance. A sleeping niche used by an elder may deepen over several decades without any cutting. A tool set beside them can be difficult for another person to retrieve, although it remains plainly visible.

They dislike being helped before help is necessary.

They will still carry slabs, harvest leaves, and correct a younger worker’s bindings with an abruptness that needs no spoken language. But they stop carrying children. When they converse, they let the other person initiate the touch.

The handprints beside certain shelters are theirs.

They stand at the wall, place a palm against it, and leave a little leaf dust to mark the contact. Later they do it again, a measured distance farther along. The early prints show the ridges of the skin. The later ones record something deeper.

Those are not attempts to push through.

They are tests of whether the person can still let go.

Eventually an elder travels with a companion, and then with two. They choose broad routes. They avoid cliffs that correspond too closely across a gap. At the shelters, someone sits beside them while they sleep, with a hand resting lightly on their wrist.

There are places inside the ridges where these journeys end.

The openings are not always shaped like hands. Some are low doorways. Some resemble a gap beneath a displaced slab. Through one you may see a pale slope, a garden, even a shelter whose entrance is turned away.

Those who accompany an elder do not always return by the path they took.

When they do return, they bring back the bowl and the inner mantle. For a while they speak very little, even with their hands.

---

The person who tends the roofless enclosure is neither especially old nor especially important.

Their scoop has been repaired twice. They have a habit of resting one knee on a folded pad while they work. Under the dark leaves they loosen the fine dust in small quantities, taking care not to expose too much of it at once.

They do not make the mountains.

Mostly, they persuade a little of what is coming into existence to arrive somewhere useful.

The dust is carried to a stair that no longer quite joins its landing, or to the underside of a slab beginning to lose its claim on the ground. The work is difficult because the material must not be given the wrong shape too early. A bowl holds it too firmly. A bare hand holds it much too firmly.

The wooden scoop is shallow, flexible, and soon worn out.

If you lift it without understanding, the worker does not snatch it from you. A sudden pull would be worse.

They put one hand beneath yours.

With the other, they press two fingers against the inside of your wrist: a sustained pressure, then a slight release. They wait until your grip follows theirs.

Together you lower the scoop toward a space beneath the enclosure wall. What is in it has already become too heavy for so little dust. For a moment your arm seems longer than it should be.

Then the worker tips the handle.

The dust leaves reluctantly, preserving the hollow of the scoop until it touches the stone. A faint violet line appears beneath it.

The worker keeps hold of your wrist until your hand is empty and feels empty.

Only then do they let go.
