The animal beneath the seed-husk roof has a head at the end nearest the opening, though the distinction is slight. Its body is the color of peeled barley. Six short limbs lie close against its sides, each ending in a broad, divided pad. The pads fit small irregularities in the floor so exactly that it is difficult to tell which parts of their outline belong to the animal.

Along its back runs a row of thin plates. They overlap without concealing one another: through each plate the edge of the next is visible, and beneath them all is a narrow, milky organ, forked at both ends. There is no pulse in it. Its fullness is continuous.

The roof is not quite a shelter. Light passes through the husks and issues from the ground beneath them. Nothing could approach the animal without already occupying the place where it rests. Yet the inward-curving husks agree with the curve of its back, leaving a small, even interval above it. This interval belongs to the creature as surely as the space inside its mouth.

Its mouth holds the edge of a seed.

The seed coat is split, exposing a smooth surface of food. Two minute teeth meet that surface. Neither has penetrated it, but the contact is more substantial than a bite: the food and the mouth are fitted together down to details too fine to distinguish. The animal is fed there. No portion is missing from the seed.

Under the cliff, the three pale bodies are less alike than they appear. One has eight legs and a broad seam across its middle. One has no legs, only a fringe of transparent hooks caught among the root hair’s smaller branches. The third is almost entirely head. Its body is a short, soft attachment tucked into the angle between two grains of sand.

They share a boundary so intricate that, from above, they resemble one animal with a disorderly supply of limbs. Seen from the side, each is distinct. The legless one has a fine brown line within it. The eight-legged one carries a bead on its flank, neither an egg nor a parasite but a clear, stalked part of itself. The large-headed creature has two eyes set far apart and a third deep in the tissue between them.

Its third eye faces the root.

There is much to see at that distance. The root hair is not a simple thread. Its skin is laid in long, close strips, and between the strips are seams containing smaller presences. Some are round; some have the proportions of animals stretched along a passage. One has a pair of dark points and, behind them, a succession of pale chambers. There is no visible room for its feet. Nevertheless, at the limit of sight, the feet are there.

The inhabitants have senses suited to this closeness. A springtail’s antenna rests against a crystal face, every fine hair on one side touching mineral, every hair on the other touching the thin interval of air. A mite has set one foot in water and the other seven on dry ground. The difference occupies its whole small body. Beneath the moss, an animal shaped like a folded scrap of membrane has spread its outer edge against the underside of a leaf. Each white cup there contains one of its blunt projections.

This may be feeding. It may be an intimacy for which feeding is too narrow a name.

The roar passes through all of them, although nothing shakes. In the membrane animal it has a soft, crowded thickness. In the springtail’s folded fork it is almost painfully fine. The snail’s shell contains a deeper part of the sound, tucked into the excessive spiral with the snail’s translucent organs.

The snail itself is broad-footed and grave-looking, with two short horns. One horn touches the shelf above its head. The other ends freely in the light. Inside its shell, successive turns are visible through the outer wall, but following them inward does not lead toward a smaller and smaller center. There are broad portions deep inside, ample hollows behind narrow ones. A pale lobe occupies a chamber that could not fit behind the snail’s back.

It is still a small snail. It has no grandeur apart from the thoroughness with which it inhabits this difficulty.

The ring of legs stands near the upright flake. Each leg has four joints and a delicate claw, and each joins its neighbors through a little arch of white tissue. There is no body above the ring or concealed beneath it. Through its center the ground is plainly visible: three mineral facets and a tiny oval inclusion.

A fringe faces inward from the arches. Each filament ends just short of another filament across the opening. The empty center is thus almost furnished.

At one point the ring meets the hair that braces the flake. Two claws close around it. There is no reason to assign the construction to this animal rather than to the narrow, double-mouthed creature pressed against the flake’s other side. The arrangement accommodates both. The hair passes between the ring’s joints; the flake provides a surface against which the other creature can lay its whole flank. Ownership would be difficult to locate, but their separate preferences are visible.

Even the mite within the egg has its own particular fit. One of its forelegs lies across its face. The egg’s inner wall bears a shallow indentation above the knee, while the outer wall remains smoothly curved beneath the parent’s grasp. Parent and offspring are equally occupied, but not with the same things.

In the central oval, the inhabitants are harder to separate from the country.

Along the shallow crease is a row of structures resembling closed jaws. Each consists of two pearly margins with a clear seam between them. Fine bristles emerge from some margins. Others pass directly into the grain of the ground. At one end of the row, a pair of jointed appendages rests on a little enclosed flat. At the other, there is only a change in texture.

These creatures are not incomplete versions of the more recognizable animals around them. Their few distinctions are sufficient. One has a mouth, a narrow surface that feels, and a firm attachment to the fold. Another has two feet planted on opposite sides of a ridge and nothing that can confidently be called a body between them.

The smallest occupies the end of a bristle.

It is a slight thickening, clear around a white center, with a notch fitted against the bristle’s tip. On its exposed side are two exceedingly small lobes. The mineral light shows their undersides. Between them lies a cleft, and within the cleft is something finer still, held against both walls.

There, too, the contact is exact.
