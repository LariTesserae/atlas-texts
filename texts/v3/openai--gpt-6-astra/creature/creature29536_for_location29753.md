The woman in the doorway is shelling dried beans. The bowl rests against her stomach, held there by the pressure of her forearms, and she drops the empty pods into a basket at her feet. She is broad across the shoulders and wears a faded blue garment with a new strip sewn around its hem. One of her shoes has been cut open to accommodate a swollen joint.

She does not look up at every footstep. She knows several of them.

When a boy comes down the stair, she lifts the bowl out of his reach before he has quite reached her. He stops, offended by the accuracy of this, then takes a pod from the basket and begins pulling its two halves apart. They speak without facing each other. After a while she gives him a handful of beans to carry to the house above. He goes with both hands cupped, walking carefully now.

Most of the people here have work that can be put down, though not necessarily left. A blade can be laid across a board; a half-filled measure can wait under a cloth. There are interruptions all day: someone needs a second pair of hands beneath a roof slab, someone has brought news from the path, someone cannot remember whether the last jar of oil was paid for. The town is small enough that an errand often acquires another purpose before it is finished.

Its inhabitants are not all quiet. A man in one of the upper houses talks so loudly that people in his court can follow both sides of his disputes, although his wife’s replies seldom reach the door. Two sisters who share a storage vault have stopped speaking except about the vault. A young woman laughs with her mouth open and her head thrown back, which makes the older people look at her even when they have heard the joke already.

Their reserve, where they have it, is partly the economy of people who expect to see one another tomorrow.

They wear wool over linen, with leather at the places that wear through quickest. Clothes are altered repeatedly. A good cloak may fit badly because it belonged first to someone larger, and its present owner will tolerate that rather than cut away useful cloth. At the neck and wrists, where the fabric can be seen without being exposed to much damage, there is sometimes very fine stitching. One man has red thread worked around his collar in a pattern of small hooked shapes. He is otherwise the dustiest person in the square.

Hands are more various than faces at first suggest. The woman at the weighing bench has blunt fingers and a pale scar across her thumb. The man who repairs harness has long nails on two fingers and keeps the others close-cut. Children have dark crescents beneath every nail. An old man who no longer carries loads still reaches to steady them as they pass, touching a basket lightly after its weight has already gone by.

Walking here has taught them small habits. They shorten their stride before a turn in the stair. They leave the inside of a passage to whoever is carrying something. A person resting on a landing draws their feet back without interrupting a story. These courtesies are so habitual that a deliberate failure to make room can serve as an insult.

Few of them spend their whole lives within the gates. The mule path leads to grazing, cultivated ground lower down, other households, other weather. At certain times of year several doors stay barred for weeks while their owners work away from the ledge. The rooms are not empty in the sense of being relinquished. A cousin opens the shutters; someone checks a roof after rain. Messages return with the animals.

Grain comes uphill in sacks. So do oil, timber when timber is needed, and iron that will be worked until almost nothing remains of it. Wool goes down, and hard cheese, and bundles of dried plants whose value depends on their having been gathered at the right height and season. Some households earn more by carrying other people’s goods than by selling their own.

On an ordinary afternoon, none of this looks like commerce. A woman sits beneath the market roof mending the edge of a sack. A covered basket stands beside her. The weights are put away. But she knows what is in every load expected before nightfall, and which animal has begun to favor a foot.

The mules are substantial inhabitants of the town. They know where they can turn and which doorways conceal a hand with something edible in it. One gray mule halts at the trough whether thirsty or not. Its owner waits, complaining to anyone nearby but making no attempt to hurry it. Another dislikes the outward gate and must be led past it on the mountain side, its ears laid back at the brightness beyond the wall.

There are fewer dogs than a visitor might expect. The oldest sleeps near the oven, where people step over him until baking begins and he is expelled with a wooden shovel held flat against his ribs. He gets up slowly, then moves quite briskly once beyond the reach of the shovel. A small yellow dog follows the children but belongs, in all matters of food and shelter, to the loud man’s wife.

The children know the town less as a plan than as a collection of useful irregularities. They know which coping can be straddled, which slab rocks, which hole returns a pebble and which keeps it. They have names for places too small to need names in adult conversation. A shallow hollow behind the upper stair is a house, a prison, or a market according to who arrives first.

Their games sometimes interfere with drainage. This is one of the few offenses that can earn a reprimand from almost any adult.

Water is handled without ceremony. A full vessel is covered. What spills is noticed. The animal basin is cleaned with a piece of cloth kept for that purpose, not with whichever rag happens to be nearest. During a dry spell the rules grow more explicit, and arguments arise over exceptions that were harmless a month before.

The cistern keeper is not particularly solemn about his responsibility. He is a narrow man who likes sweet things and exaggerates the difficulty of climbing the upper stair. He also notices a change in the rope’s resistance before anyone else does. When he becomes concerned, he stops complaining. People who know him listen for this.

Age is conspicuous here because its accommodations remain in view. A second handhold has been fixed beside a door. A bench is just high enough that one particular woman can rise from it unaided. Someone’s daily journey has contracted from the lower gate to the square, then from the square to a patch of afternoon sun outside his room.

Help can be affectionate and impatient at once. The boy who carries an old neighbor’s water resents being called back to move the jar three finger-widths farther from the door. He moves it, nevertheless. Later he imitates her voice for his friends.

Nor does everyone find the town sufficient. A girl working at the grinding stone pauses whenever unfamiliar voices enter the court. She wants to know how long the road takes beyond the place she has seen, whether the houses elsewhere have wooden floors, whether it is true that in some towns a person can buy cooked food after dark. Her brother has been away two years. His letters are kept folded inside a cloth that once wrapped a knife.

Other people have returned. A man who spent much of his life below has brought back a taste for pepper and an inability to sleep through the early silence. He rises before the oven is lit and sits at the outward gate with his cloak around his knees. He insists that he does not miss the noise. He describes it frequently.

By evening, more of the inhabitants become visible. Work comes toward the doors to use the remaining light. A child is washed standing in a shallow bowl and objects whenever the cloth reaches his ears. Someone shakes crumbs from a board, and the yellow dog arrives before they touch the paving. Above the roofs, the bird returns to its hole.

The woman with the beans has gone inside. Her basket remains beside the threshold, the empty pods fitted loosely together. From the room comes her voice, sharper now: she is asking where the boy put the beans.

He answers from farther up the court.

No one can quite hear what he says, and she makes him come back.
