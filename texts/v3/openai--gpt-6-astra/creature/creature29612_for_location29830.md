The first evidence of the larger inhabitants is a clean place on a wall.

Not a feeding mark. The mineral is still violet, still opaque. But the small blades that grow on shaded surfaces are missing from a narrow band just above the floor. The band continues around a corner, passes behind a hanging window membrane, and ends at a crack too fine to admit a fingernail.

There are other such marks. Once you know their height, you find them throughout the district. In some chambers they form a nearly continuous border. In others there is only a short, polished crescent beneath a doorway that has never opened completely.

Something has been passing through.

You are most likely to see one during contraction, when the paving separates and the old streets briefly acquire more space. Sit beside a wall with a fresh vertical fracture. Do not put a light into it. Watch the lower end, where the darkness meets the ground.

What comes out resembles the edge of a pale leaf.

It advances a little, rests, then advances again. Behind it appears a second edge, folded against the first. For some time this is all: a white crease working itself free of a white broken surface. Then the crease opens, and a portion of the animal settles onto the paving.

It is much larger than the crack suggested.

Its body is a broad, thin sheet gathered into several long folds. Fully extended, an adult might cover the floor of a small room, although no one animal is often seen fully extended. Most keep some part of themselves inside a wall. The exposed portion has the color of the mineral’s fresh interior, with a faint yellow cast where the folds overlap. It is neither moist nor quite dry. Light catches on it as it catches on the skin inside a seed.

There is no shell. Along the margins, however, the body thickens into short, hard segments. These can brace against stone. A movement begins at one of them, travels inward as a low wrinkle, and draws the rest of the animal forward.

On an open floor this is a laborious way to move. In a narrow space it is remarkably effective. The creature sets one edge against each wall and passes itself between them, fold after fold. A street reduced to a finger’s width is still a street to it.

The broad steps do not help. It crosses them diagonally, hauling its loose portions after it.

At intervals along its underside are clusters of dark pits. When the body is stretched thin, they can be mistaken for holes. They are sensitive surfaces, recessed to protect them from abrasion. An animal emerging into a chamber pauses until several clusters lie flat against the floor. Then its folds begin to rearrange.

You can sometimes feel what it has detected before you see any corresponding change: a slight pressure through the soles of your feet, a tension in the paving. Hours later, a seam rises there.

They are good at finding rooms before the rooms exist.

They do not appear to direct the growth. A wall will lift beneath one, slowly forcing it to withdraw. A roof can close across the middle of an animal whose outer parts remain in different chambers. This causes no obvious injury. It thins itself at the point of enclosure and continues its activities on both sides.

Some become so extensively threaded through a building that it is difficult to decide where they could go if they wished to leave.

Yet they do leave. During the dim interval, a chamber that had seemed lined with pale stone may lose that lining. It gathers at the corners, pulls free in long soft pleats, and slips beneath a lintel. The room left behind is rough violet, with clean bands low on its walls.

The inhabitants feed where the mineral is still forming.

At the base of a young partition, an animal presses its underside against the yielding ridge. Small areas of its body become translucent. Beneath them, fine dark branches fill and empty with a slowness that makes the process hard to follow. It may remain in this position through several changes of light.

Afterward the ridge continues upward. The place where the animal lay is usually thinner than the surrounding wall.

This may account for some of the doorways. Not all. There are openings too high for any known feeding posture, and whole empty buildings whose walls show no trace of contact. The district has its own tendencies. Its inhabitants make use of them, and sometimes alter them by staying in one place.

Where ribbon-plants hold a courtyard open, the large animals gather at the edges. The roots discourage the mineral from rising; the animals prefer the young growth that accumulates just beyond them. Over many cycles, a low shelf forms around the occupied paving. Beneath this shelf there is often a continuous inhabited hollow.

A courtyard can therefore be empty in its center and populous around its entire circumference.

They do not crowd as separate, compact animals would. Their margins overlap. One passes a fold beneath another and rests it there. An individual withdrawing into a fissure may draw several neighbors a short distance with it before their surfaces separate.

This contact leaves faint patterns on their bodies: rows of flattened points, curved impressions that persist for a cycle or two. Old animals carry many such marks. Their edges are less regular, with healed divisions and places where two hard segments have grown together.

Recognition, if that is what occurs, begins at the margins. Two approaching animals touch their stiffened edges at several points. Then each changes the disposition of its folds. Sometimes they make room for one another. Sometimes they remain joined while the light brightens and fades.

A small one will occasionally travel supported between two larger bodies, never touching the ground.

The young are thicker in proportion than adults. They cannot enter the finest cracks, and spend their early growth in the narrow chambers that seem so badly designed for turning around. There they lie doubled over, with their sensitive underside against both walls. As the chamber contracts, they become flatter.

Many such chambers contain only one young animal. Some contain a pale, hard knot, no larger than a fist, caught where the walls have met. Around these knots the mineral grows without an opening.

The adults revisit them. They brace a margin against the wall and remain for a while. Their feeding surfaces do not become transparent.

No other response is visible.

The tower contains the oldest inhabitants, or at least the largest. In the dimmest part of the cycle, pale lengths can be seen withdrawing from its lower windows. They extend far down the hollow interior, crossing and recrossing the shaft. What looks from outside like an empty vertical space is divided by bodies too thin to intercept much light.

When the tower leans, they shift.

A slow adjustment passes from its base toward the crown. Margins release from one side and fasten to the other. Some of the upper windows darken before the sky does; some remain pale after the brightening has passed.

There is no single position from which all this can be seen. From the ground you see the lower folds. From the rim you see only occasional movement near the top. It is possible that one animal occupies most of the tower. It is also possible that the tower permits many animals to remain in contact without compressing them into the same few chambers.

Elsewhere in the district, contact is easier to lose. Streets divide. A wall turns. An inhabited hollow opens to the sky, and its occupants must gather themselves out of the light.

They are not particularly afraid of a visitor.

A foot placed near an exposed margin causes that margin to stiffen. If you remain still, a small portion may advance until it touches your shoe. It tests the sole, the seam above it, the space beneath the arch. It is seeking a surface against which another part of itself can be braced.

Move, and it withdraws.

Remain, and more of it comes.

This is uncomfortable chiefly because the creature makes no distinction between your body and the other temporary arrangements of the district. It will rest against your ankle as it rests against a young wall. A fold may rise behind your heel. You feel almost no weight, only a dry, cool pressure that adjusts whenever you adjust.

Beneath the western paving, there are several of them.

When you stand there long enough, your weight changes the contact between the plates. The animals below spread against the altered surfaces. A feeding edge presses along a seam. Growth continues where it was already inclined to continue, but unevenly now, accommodating the pressure above and the bodies beneath.

The raised line reaches your shoe.

By the next brightening there will be a threshold. If you leave, it will continue forming without you. A low opening will remain beside it, too shallow for a foot.

For a while, something pale will rest inside that opening, its edges braced against the new stone.
