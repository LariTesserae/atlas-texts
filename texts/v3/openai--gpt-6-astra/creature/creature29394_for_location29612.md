The people are harder to find than the animals, though they are not concealed.

One sits beyond the low shelter, where the ground shelves down by the depth of a hand. From a distance, the person’s back is another ash-colored back among the grazers. The head is bare. One knee is raised beneath a garment made of small hides joined with exceptionally fine seams. The other leg extends into a space between two animals, and the foot rests there with the ease of a foot beneath a familiar table.

You may have passed this person on your way to the shelter. There was nothing to prevent your seeing them.

Their age is difficult to judge. The face has the long upper lip and slight hollows at the temples of someone old, but the skin beneath the eyes is smooth. Neither impression corrects the other. A scar runs from the jaw toward the ear. At its lower end it is white and flat; near the ear it retains the tiny, close-set marks of stitches. It is healed along its whole length.

Their hands lie open, one on the raised knee, one beside them on the stone. The nails are short. A little dark material occupies the fold beside a thumbnail. There are thickened places on the fingers where a cord might have passed under tension.

These are plainly the hands of someone who knows how to repair a bowl.

Nothing about the person suggests that they are waiting to resume that work. Yet the shelter does not feel abandoned, and the needle beside the mat is not a relic. The difficulty lies in the ordinary word *beside*. The bowl is beside the needle; the shelter is beside its maker; the repair is beside the breaking. Distance separates them more readily than time does.

The person’s eyes meet yours.

They have gray irises, unevenly colored, with a brown wedge in one. The eyes do not shine with special knowledge. They are the eyes of someone who has spent much of a life attending to small practical matters in strong daylight. You have the uncomfortable sense that they can see the loose stitch at your cuff.

There is no welcome you could distinguish from their way of sitting.

***

Among the animals, no kind is quite a collection of duplicates.

The ash-colored grazers resemble one another closely until you follow the line of their shoulders. In some, the heavy neck enters the chest low, giving the head a settled, almost pendant position. In others it joins farther back, and the shoulder continues forward beneath it like a shelf. A few have horns whose flattened ends rest against their own cheeks. The hair beneath those ends is uncrushed.

One small grazer has a hollow in its back occupied by a pale, round creature. The hollow is not a wound or a pouch. Its edges have the ordinary direction of growing fur. The occupant has six dark feet, all planted, and a face so narrow that its two eyes can be seen only from opposite sides.

Neither animal appears to be carrying the other.

Elsewhere, a bird stands in the shelter of an ear. Its claws touch the delicate inner skin without indenting it. There is an animal with a broad lip laid against the rim of a shell; another whose tail enters a crack and emerges, noticeably thicker, from a different crack nearby. Looking beneath a large belly, you find three pairs of small eyes at different depths. Only the nearest pair belongs to a head you can make out.

The longer you look, the less useful size becomes as a way of sorting them. Something no larger than a seed has the substantial presence of the great horned bodies. A creature spanning a corridor seems slight, almost incidental, until you notice the minute folds at the corner of its mouth.

They are not simplified by their stillness. There are cowlicks, asymmetrical nostrils, a single pale eyelash among dark ones. A beetle has a dent in its wing case shaped like a crescent moon. The edges of the dent are smooth. A long-legged animal has one hoof narrower than the others and stands with that leg slightly forward.

Its stance is so particular that you recognize it again across the basin.

Then you realize the animal you first saw remains near your elbow. The distant one is not the same, nor exactly another of its kind. You can see the narrow hoof. You cannot find the difference that would let you stop comparing them.

***

There are more people.

A child stands between the forelegs of one of the tall animals. A short, stiff braid lies against the child’s neck. Their tunic has been let out at the sides with strips of a different color, and the hem ends just above both knees. One hand is spread on the animal’s leg.

This contact is neither a caress nor a means of support. Each finger has its own patch of warm-looking skin beneath it. The child has a blunt face, a broad nose, and the faintly irritated expression children sometimes wear when adults misunderstand a simple arrangement.

A little farther on, someone lies on their side in a shallow depression. The upper arm is folded beneath the head. A small shelled thing rests in the bend behind their knees. Their eyes are open, looking across the ground at the height of the rosettes.

Near a wall stands a person holding a length of plaited fiber. It passes between their hands and drops almost to the stone. One end is finished with a knot; the other separates into loose strands. Those strands are not awaiting completion. You can see, with a certainty you could not defend elsewhere, that the object is the length it should be.

Their clothing is not uniform. Some garments are carefully fitted, some little more than wrapped lengths of coarse material. Repairs are frequent. Decoration is sparse but precise: three dark beads at a throat, a border of diagonal stitches, small holes bored along the edge of a polished bone disc. One person has sewn a row of pale seeds onto a sleeve where they would be almost impossible for the wearer to see.

It is this last detail that makes you look for relationships among them.

The child is nowhere near the person whose garment uses the same stitching. Two people seated shoulder to shoulder look out in different directions. A broad-faced woman has her hand beneath the chin of a small horned animal. Her fingers fit along its jaw as naturally as roots fit their holes in the stone.

There may be families here. There may be people who dislike one another. Nothing has made them all alike.

***

You find no one asleep.

The reclined people are not resting, just as the standing animals are not keeping watch. There are lowered eyelids, but beneath them the small exposed portions of eyes remain clear. An animal with its face pressed against the ground can see you through a narrow interval between its cheek and the grit.

Some of the inhabitants bear injuries. A missing finger. A seam across a muzzle. A shell with a broad piece absent from its rim. None is bleeding. None has been restored to an imagined first condition.

An old animal stands near the wooden post with much of its lower jaw gone. Its tongue lies in the remaining hollow, moist and pink. It has a calm, rather fastidious face. You cannot tell whether this is the oldest creature you have encountered or merely the one whose history is most visible.

Close to its forefoot grows a plant with two stems. One ends in a dark tip. The other has been bitten off.

The mouthful, somewhere, is accounted for.

***

Eventually you notice that the bare corridors do not lead chiefly between the inhabitants. They lead to them.

A narrow passage ends beside the seated maker’s empty hand. Another brings you level with the child’s shoulder. A third gives access to a smooth patch of ground beside the old animal at the edge. These are not prominent destinations. You discover them by finding that your body can go no farther without choosing somewhere else.

At the seated person’s side, there is room to lower yourself.

The stone would support your weight along the backs of your thighs. Your feet could rest on the shelf below. There is even space for the awkward way one of your knees prefers to bend.

Looking down, you see no impression on the ground. No outline of a body, no shallow sockets prepared for your heels. Only pale grit, a gray plate with a fine seam through it, and a brown patch whose edge curls upward slightly.

The person beside the shelter has not shifted to make room.

At this distance you can see that the open hand is not quite empty. A short piece of cord lies across the palm, following one of its creases. It might be left over from the bowl’s repair. It might be useful for something small.

You remain standing.

The hand, the cord, the available stone continue to be there.
