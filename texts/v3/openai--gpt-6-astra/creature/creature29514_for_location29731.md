The larger ones stand with their bellies in the water and their backs among the lowest leaves. There are enough of them that what first appears to be a way through the roots is often the narrow interval between two bodies.

They are not especially tall. Their bulk extends sideways and backward, under foliage, around trunks. A single animal may occupy several of the separate openings through which it can be seen. The tail on the pale shelf belongs to the flank behind the creepers; the head is farther away, resting at the base of a tree, almost below the water. Between these visible portions, its body bends with the shallow irregularities of the ground.

Its skin is olive where light reaches it, brown underneath, and pale in the deep folds at the bases of its limbs. Broad scales overlap along the back. Lower down they become small and widely spaced, set in a soft, finely creased surface on which water gathers. Each scale has a rounded margin. Even where the animals press continually against roots, there are no polished patches, no scratches.

Small snails occupy the seams.

The animals have six limbs, although it is difficult to establish this by watching one. They seldom need all of them at once. The forward pair grips whatever lies ahead: a root, a submerged stem, the raised back of another animal. The middle pair bears most of the weight. The hind pair is usually folded into the softness beside the tail. Under water, these folded limbs open occasionally, spreading long, webbed digits and closing them again without producing a visible step.

When one shifts position, the movement is distributed through its neighbors. A shoulder yields. A belly lifts. A tail slides a little farther onto a shelf. Several bodies accommodate a change that began somewhere out of sight, and the leaves above them barely tremble.

Their heads are broad and low, with eyes set in slight elevations behind the nostrils. The eyes remain open under water. Seen through the brown shallows, they look like wet seeds pressed into the mud.

The mouth is underneath.

An animal feeding on the underside of a leaf lifts its head enough to reveal a pale, pleated pouch between the jaws. The pouch opens against the leaf and holds it. There is a slow contraction of the throat, a faint sound of wet surfaces drawing apart. When the head lowers, a translucent patch remains in the blade. The veins are intact.

They feed this way on leaves, fruit, the tender outer coverings of roots. They also hold their mouths against the pale shelves.

There the feeding is almost impossible to distinguish from rest. The jaw settles onto the mineral surface. The eyes lower slightly into their sockets. For a long time, only the throat moves. Around the lips, a clear secretion gathers and extends in a shining rim. Tiny animals come to stand in it. Some feed there; others simply arrange themselves at the edge, facing outward.

When the mouth is lifted, the patch beneath it is smooth and faintly brighter than its surroundings. It has no tooth marks.

The youngest can be found inside the hanging roots. Their bodies are narrow enough to pass between the wet strands, and their tails curl around them while they feed. They are translucent along the belly. Through the skin, beneath a delicate pattern of scales, the dark coils of the gut can be seen contracting.

They have the same heavy heads as the adults, the same small, watchful eyes. Nothing about their behavior is hurried. A young one no longer than a hand will remain attached to a root while insects climb over its face. It closes its nostrils when they approach, then opens them after they have passed.

Some of these little animals rest on the adults. They fit into the hollows behind the eyes, beneath the jaw, along the inner surfaces of folded limbs. An adult lifting its foot may disclose two or three of them, flattened against its sole. They hold on with all six sets of digits. The foot settles elsewhere, and they disappear beneath it again.

There is no obvious place where they are born.

Large females—or animals distinguished from the others only by this feature—carry rows of soft chambers along their lower flanks. The chambers lie beneath overlapping flaps of skin. Most look empty until something inside moves. Then a pale outline presses briefly against the membrane: the curve of a head, a spread foot, a tail folded twice upon itself.

The opening of each chamber is small and puckered. Water enters and leaves with the animal’s breathing. Fine roots sometimes hang across these openings, and the animal parts them carefully with a hind foot.

None of the chambers looks stretched to its limit. None looks recently emptied.

The calls come from both large and small.

Before calling, an animal raises its jaw from whatever it has been feeding on. A swelling passes down one side of the neck and returns along the other. The mouth stays closed. What emerges is a low, hollow note with a roughness inside it, like water moving through a vessel not quite full.

Nearby throats begin to swell before the note ends.

The response does not spread in a simple ring. An animal almost touching the caller may remain silent, while another, hidden well beyond it, answers immediately. Sometimes a small one contributes a thin vibration scarcely distinguishable from the insects. Sometimes a very large body gives a sound so low that the water beneath its chin wrinkles before anything can be heard.

They do not look toward one another when they call. Their eyes remain on the close surfaces before them.

There are other large inhabitants. High in the low trees, narrow-bodied animals lie along the branches with their limbs hanging on either side. The soles of their feet are broad and ridged, and their toes close independently around stems. Their fur, if it is fur, is sparse and flattened into little wet points. Fleshy folds surround their ears.

They reach down slowly into the crowded growth and draw up whatever their fingers encounter. Often it is fruit. Sometimes it is a snail, or a cluster of insects scraped from a flower. They examine the handful close to their lips, making small movements with the tips of their fingers, and eat with their eyes half shut.

Under their resting places, the broad-backed animals keep their heads raised. Fragments fall onto their snouts. A tongue appears from the underside of a jaw and gathers them.

Farther out in the reeds are upright shapes that resolve into birds only when a bill opens. Their plumage hangs in dense, narrow vanes, each carrying beads of water. They stand so close together that their bodies form low walls among the stems. Small fish shelter between their ankles.

A bird lowers its head. The bill enters the water without a splash. For a moment the throat widens around something substantial; then the head returns to precisely its former height. The neighboring birds have not moved.

Every animal has others upon it. Mites gather in the hollows of scales. Soft, colorless worms live beneath the rims of shells. On the gripping feet above, minute winged things graze along the creases, stepping aside when a toe bends. Even the insects inside the flowers carry smaller lives among their hairs.

Yet the bodies beneath this occupation are full and unblemished. Eyes are clear. Limbs carry their weight. The smallest creatures hold their places without the restless searching of parasites on a failing host.

It is difficult to find an animal that looks old. It is equally difficult to imagine any of the large ones smaller than they are.

One stands beside a low island with a root enclosed in the curve of its forelimb. The root has thickened there until it fits the space closely. Above it, a branch divides around the animal’s shoulder. Its back rises into the leaves, which rest upon the scales without being crushed.

For a long time it appears unable to move.

Then its eye turns. The forelimb loosens, lifts, and settles on the other side of the root. There is room after all: enough for the limb, enough for the small bodies beneath it, enough for water to enter the place it has left.

The animal lowers its mouth to a tightly folded leaf. It holds the tip gently between the pleats of its lips.

Behind its jaw, the throat begins to swell.
