The maker of the earthen hood is usually visible only as a damp lower lip.

It rests just inside the open entrance, behind the line where the cold air begins. The lip is pale grey, thickened at either end, and divided by a notch so fine that it sometimes looks like a hair lying across it. When a loose grain rolls within reach, the notch opens. Two small, hard surfaces meet behind it. The grain disappears.

Nothing else happens for a while.

Then one of the white pellets blocking another entrance moves outward by the width of its own shadow.

The animal is larger than its doorways suggest. At rest it occupies most of the hood, folded loosely around a central space which it keeps empty. Its skin resembles the underside of a mushroom, although the delicate ribs can travel across its body. They gather wherever it bends. Straightened, it would be long enough to reach from the hood to the root-hair fence, but it never straightens completely. One portion always remains curled, holding a little of the cold.

It has six limbs near the mouth. Farther back, the distinction between limbs and folds becomes difficult to maintain. A flap grips the ground, narrows under the animal’s weight, and acquires a joint. Once released, it slowly broadens into skin again.

The six front limbs are permanent. They have short, dark nails, worn smooth on their inner edges. These are among the few things in the chamber that show wear.

On an ordinary morning, the animal does not come out.

It tests the chamber with a narrow extension of its lower lip, laying this against the ground and drawing it back. The extension returns bearing almost nothing: a bead of water too small to fall, a pale stain, sometimes a seed-legged runner that has mistaken it for a path. The runner is allowed to leave. The lip remains extended until all its feet have found another surface.

The hood-maker eats something smaller.

Where a runner has stood, there is often a minute thickening beneath each footprint. The animal scrapes these places with its mouth. It takes only the soft upper grain. A footprint remains, sharper than before.

It feeds more actively after a drop has fallen. Then its lip travels over the new banks, pausing at burrow mouths, feeling around their edges. The inhabitants retreat only a little. A swimmer occasionally passes through the thin skin of moisture on the lip and is carried partway toward the hood before slipping down through it.

By the time the bank begins to harden, the hood-maker has withdrawn.

Its meals leave pale bands inside its body. Through the skin they can be seen moving slowly backward, breaking apart at the folds and gathering again beyond them. Some vanish. Others eventually reach a pocket beside the mouth, where the front limbs knead them into pellets.

The pellets are not all alike.

Those made after rain remain soft enough to dent. Those formed during the long quiet intervals are hard and slightly translucent, with an off-centre hollow. The animal rolls each one between two nails before deciding where to put it. Sometimes it rejects a pellet it has worked on for hours. It carries the rejected piece outside and presses it into the base of the hood, where it becomes indistinguishable from the wall.

A rejected pellet attracts no attention.

An accepted one, left briefly unattended, draws animals from several directions. The many-legged runners stop well short of it, bodies raised. One of the flat ceiling creatures may descend the hood headfirst, bringing its green branches into the light. Even the bristled predator will approach, though it keeps its forelimbs folded.

They disperse as soon as the hood-maker returns.

There are no eyes visible on the hood-maker. Along either side of its mouth lies a row of soft pits, usually closed. When someone steps on the stair, they open.

Each pit contains a bead of clear fluid. As the limestone lowers, the beads flatten together. The animal turns within its house, a slow, crowded movement that puts its mouth against one plugged doorway, then another. It takes a pellet inward. Cold air begins to flow through the newly opened hole.

This is when the ridges form in the soil.

They start close to the entrance, very fine and evenly spaced, then extend until they meet established ground. The hood-maker puts its lip between two of them. For the duration of the pressure overhead, it remains there, feeding.

If the foot stays unusually long, the animal emerges.

First come the six nailed limbs. Then a broad, low portion of the body, ribbed all over and faintly flushed with yellow. The rest follows reluctantly. Its folds adhere to the inside of the hood and must be peeled free one at a time. The sound is softer than the release of a ceiling animal, but more prolonged.

Outside, the hood-maker is almost shapeless. It settles over unfinished ground and spreads its weight. A skirt of thin skin reaches between the white islands. Beneath that skin, little points of support appear and disappear.

It can move quite quickly in this state.

Its front limbs pull; its farthest folds gather; its middle passes over places that do not yet have a surface. For a moment its underside can be seen through them. There are scars there, narrow grey seams, and several grains of dark soil embedded too deeply to be shed.

At the root-hair fence it stops.

It does not put a limb through. It lowers its mouth between two hairs and waits until the pits beside its lip are full. Then it lifts its head with some difficulty, as though the fluid has weight, and returns to the hood.

After these journeys, one of the plugged entrances will sometimes open from within.

The new animal that comes out is small and glossy, with six front limbs and no folds behind them. It drags a white pellet attached to its rear by a broad membrane. The pellet catches on everything. The young animal pulls until it comes loose, proceeds a little farther, and is stopped again.

There is no assistance from the hood.

For its first few hours the young one shelters beneath whatever it can reach. Its pellet gradually softens. A crease develops across it, then another. By the time the youngster has found a suitable place against the limestone, the pellet has opened into a thick, awkward tail.

It begins building before this tail has learned to grip.

The first wall is rarely in the right place. It may enclose an area that disappears when the stair is used, or stand where the next drop spreads deepest. Small abandoned hoods occur throughout the chamber. Some contain nothing. Some have been occupied by other animals. One lies on its side beneath the arcade of shells, its doorway polished by passing bodies.

The successful young remain hard to notice. Their houses resemble a clot of wet chalk or a bank left by receding water. Only the open hole distinguishes them, and the faint ridges outside it.

At the oldest hood, the lower lip eventually returns to the doorway. Its edge is roughened from feeding. One of the nails has split.

A runner crosses it and stops, finding the surface unexpectedly cool. Behind the runner, a foot-sized patch of ground holds for a moment, then fades.

The lip stays still.

When the runner has gone, the hood-maker reaches a little farther out.
