When the last basin is empty, a little wetness remains above the crack in its floor. It has the shape of a thumbprint. By noon the stone around it is dry, and by midafternoon even the mineral crust has lost its shine, but this one patch continues to reflect the sky.

Its lower edge sometimes moves uphill.

Most of the animal is elsewhere.

It extends beneath the basin in a film thinner than the water that feeds it, follows a buried joint through the rear wall, and comes out under three separate shelves. In some places it is no wider than a hair. In others it spreads to the size of an open hand. The crowded stone conceals its continuity. What appear to be isolated damp spots are parts of one body, joined along routes that a person could discover only by dismantling the notch.

It has no permanent outline. Where the rock opens, it thickens; where the passage narrows, it draws out until its two surfaces almost touch. A single hard grain can divide it for a season. When the grain shifts, the divided edges meet and grow together without leaving a scar.

There are scars elsewhere. These show as milky threads within the clear flesh. They are particularly numerous beneath the upper basin, where the animal has repeatedly frozen too quickly to move.

Cold alone does not injure it. Usually it makes its own ice.

As the temperature falls, it gathers water against its outer surface and allows that water to harden in a particular order. First the exposed margin, then the broad faces, finally all but a few narrow passages leading inward. The living part withdraws into those passages. By full night, much of its apparent body is a sheath of clear ice, with something faintly yellow enclosed in it.

That yellow is easiest to see by starlight. In the extravagant daytime brightness, it disappears.

A sudden shadow is dangerous. So is a flake falling across a channel that has not finished draining. An entire lobe can be sealed off with too much water inside it. The next morning there will be a cloudy patch, a torn edge, and a little more pale material in the basin’s deposits.

The animal grows slowly enough that these losses matter.

It feeds along the blue breaks.

A fresh fracture is visited first by a thin searching edge, so transparent that its arrival can be mistaken for a change in the angle of the light. This edge presses into the exposed grain. Over the following days, tiny cloudy knots gather behind it. Whatever the animal takes from the stone is present in very small quantities; a surface may remain almost unchanged after years of feeding.

The dark crystals are left behind. Occasionally one can be seen enclosed in the flesh, passing gradually toward an outer margin. The animal expels it with a small excess of water. When that water disappears, the crystal remains on a shelf.

Some of the black specks in the notch have been moved many times.

The animal also carries stones it does not discard. These are flat chips, usually smaller than a fingernail, held in thicker portions of its body. They make it cumbersome in narrow seams. To get one through a constriction, it may have to spend several afternoons turning the chip a fraction at a time.

Yet it keeps them.

Under a chip, a pocket of flesh stays damp long after the surrounding film has begun to dry. Beneath larger pieces there is room for a fold, and within the fold the animal can preserve a little unfrozen liquid through the night. Its stones are shelter carried inside itself. Some are old enough to have yellow surfaces on both sides. Others retain a blue edge.

When it moves a loaded fold across bare rock, the chip occasionally touches down.

The sound is a dry click.

It moves most freely during the short interval when the sunny faces have cooled but the shaded ones have not yet frozen. Then the differences between one surface and another are small enough for it to cross them. Thin lobes descend from undercuts. A wet line broadens behind a slab. At the foot of the rear wall, two clear edges meet and become a single thicker edge.

If all its exposed parts could be lifted from the rock at once, they would scarcely fill two cupped hands. But there is no way to lift it. Its body passes behind stones weighing many tons, and some of its oldest flesh occupies seams that have since closed.

Those portions remain alive. They receive water through the body and give back little that can be detected. The animal does not abandon them even when maintaining the connection requires it to take a longer route around a newly opened fracture.

Consequently, its movements have a peculiar hesitation. A leading edge may explore an excellent wet surface and then withdraw because somewhere far behind it a thread has reached its limit. It is always arriving with something still attached.

The shelter contains its thickest folds.

They lie between the roof slab and the rear wall, tucked into a space too shallow for a finger. During the brief western illumination, warmth enters the stone above them. The folds loosen. One translucent rim sometimes protrudes beneath the smoke-blackened patch, carrying three or four pale chips side by side.

Long ago, the animal reached that place by following warmth which lasted beyond sunset.

The person sitting under the roof had warmed the wall with a back and shoulders. Water condensed nearby. There was salt on the stone, and grease, and minute fragments of skin caught in its roughness. For several nights the animal advanced farther into the shelter than it had ever gone.

The person noticed it.

At first, they scraped it away with the edge of a stone. A portion of the animal still contains the resulting injury: a blunt, white boundary deep in a fold that has grown around it. Later, the person poured water along the rear wall.

Whether this was kindness, curiosity, or an attempt to keep the water from freezing in a vessel cannot now be known. The animal benefited. It occupied the damp track. When the channel from the basin was cut, it followed that too.

For a time, the notch held two inhabitants whose needs overlapped imperfectly. One needed liquid water and a place out of the wind. The other needed water to pass slowly between liquid and ice. The fire that helped the first could empty whole surfaces of the second.

Under the smoke stain, its body still avoids a patch of stone no different in temperature from the stone beside it. The route around that patch is broad and well established.

Elsewhere it continues to enter the space once warmed by the seated person.

Each afternoon, when the bar of sunlight reaches the shelter, a clear fringe spreads down the inner wall. It crosses the six grooves, filling their weathered bottoms. At the shallow seventh mark it pauses, though there is nothing there to stop it. Then it extends toward the place where a shoulder rested.

The fringe withdraws as the light climbs away.

It has done this through the loss of the bedding, the falling of the basin-wall wedges, and the slow paling of every exposed surface that was blue when the person departed. What it retains of that occupation is an arrangement of movements: advance here; remain under this edge; take the longer route around the burned place.

There are other such arrangements in its body, associated with shelters that no longer exist.

Tonight the frost reaches the rear wall early. The fringe has barely entered the grooves when its outer edge stiffens. It gathers itself back, leaving a delicate white line in the lowest cut.

Under the roof, the thicker folds close around their stones. Water moves inward through the animal, past sealed branches and old injuries, toward the narrow spaces that will remain liquid longest.

One chip is poorly placed. As the flesh tightens, it turns and strikes the wall.

The click is very small.

A moment later, deeper in the notch, another part of the animal draws taut and holds.
