The first part of it you notice is an edge that the mineral coating ought not to have.

It lies along the back of the bathhouse bench, where the worked stone meets the wall. A pale seam, slightly puckered. Most of it is no thicker than the lip of the broken cup, but at one end it turns under itself and leaves a narrow interval of black. Water enters there. When you lean closer, the interval closes—not quickly, and not quite completely. The water gathers for a moment before finding another way down.

You could mistake this for something settling under your weight. The bench is old. The whole building is held together partly by what the springs have laid over it.

But if you stay still, the edge opens again.

What lives here has very little exposed surface. It occupies the backs of shelves, the undersides of rims, the space between the swollen sluice and its stone groove. These are not separate hiding places it travels between. Much of its body lies in them at once, connected through the wet joints by narrow lengths of flesh. In some places the connections are broad and flat, like material trapped beneath a lid. Elsewhere they have drawn out into cords scarcely distinguishable from the seams they follow.

It does not fill the hollow. You could lift many stones and find only stone beneath them. Its extent is irregular, and some apparently convenient cavities remain empty. It needs water passing close, but not water pressing hard. It needs a surface against which it can brace itself. There are stretches of open pool it has never crossed.

The thickest part is beneath the bathhouse bench.

Here the rock leaves a low recess, wider at the back than at its mouth. Something the color of soaked clay fits inside it. Seen in the dimness, it has the appearance of a folded joint: a heel drawn close to a thigh, perhaps, though no arrangement of human limbs would account for all the turns. There is a fullness at the center and several flattened portions tucked around it. One rests against the underside of the bench with enough pressure to have kept a patch of stone free of deposits.

Its skin is not quite smooth. Tiny mineral grains adhere to it, loosely on the folds, densely on the portions that remain exposed. The oldest surfaces have acquired the same dull crust as the submerged pottery. Beneath that crust, however, the body is pliant. When it shifts, hairline fractures open across the coating. A little brown water enters them. Later, a flake comes away.

The hollow contains these flakes everywhere. Most are small enough to disappear among the gravel. A few retain a curve that no stone here quite explains.

It feeds along its edges.

On the underside of a pale fold is a darker fringe, too short and fine to be seen without lifting the fold from the rock. The fringe holds against the microbial skin. Over several days it advances across a patch, leaving a cleaner surface behind. Water brings other small nourishment into the recesses of its body. There is not much. It spends little.

The warm inlet is not its favorite place. There the deposits accumulate too quickly, and the water irritates the soft fringe. Nor does it approach the entrance often. The thin film on which its outer portions depend can fail there for an afternoon. It remains mostly in the sheltered middle temperatures, where a hand held in the water soon ceases to feel the water as something separate.

There is a mouth, although you might spend a long time looking at it without deciding that this is what it is.

Under the bench, two folds meet in a short horizontal crease. Occasionally they part around a soft, blunt projection. The projection touches the stone in several places, withdraws, and appears again a little farther along. It seems less suited to taking anything in than to examining what lies within reach. Once, a loose grain adheres to it. The grain is brought inside. Another is rejected and left on the floor.

There are no eyes you can find.

Light matters to it nevertheless. A hand passed between the roof opening and the bench produces, after a delay, a slight tightening in the exposed flesh. A second shadow produces less response. By the fourth or fifth, nothing visible happens.

A foot placed on the paving has a much greater effect.

The nearest edges seal against the stone. Beneath the bench, the folded mass contracts. Water is pushed from a seam beside the doorway in a clear bead that swells, trembles, and falls. The contraction travels farther than you expect: a pale lip at the central pool draws back almost a minute later.

Then it waits.

If you sit on the raised slab, it eventually resumes feeding. If you descend the steps, it remains closed until well after you have left the water. It knows the difference between pressure resting in one place and pressure moving toward it, even when both are very gentle.

The bathhouse has changed the shape of its life. Its body can follow a fissure in natural rock only as far as that fissure permits, but the laid blocks provide a succession of sheltered joints. The bench gives its central bulk a roof within a roof. The hand-cut drain carries a reliable film along one side of it. Where the builders made room for a seated person’s feet, it has room to loosen folds that would otherwise remain compressed.

It has not kept the sluice working deliberately. Its presence in the groove does, however, prevent a deposit from sealing the last narrow passage beneath the board. A strip of flesh withdraws and extends there, slowly scouring one side. Water continues to escape.

Sometimes that strip becomes trapped. The mineral binds it before it can pull free. Then, for several weeks, the body narrows on either side of the caught portion. The connection thins. At last it parts, leaving a small dead length in the groove, and a new edge begins working around it.

Survival here includes a good deal of this quiet abandonment.

The broken cup is within its reach. Along the back, where the clay touches the niche, a soft lobe has grown around the lower curve. It does not occupy the cup’s interior. The cavity is too exposed and sometimes dries. Instead it holds the fragment against the wall, flesh fitted to the old vessel’s unevenness.

If the cup were removed, it would leave a shaped hollow that might persist for years.

There are other such shapes in it: a shallow rounded depression beneath the bench, a straight-sided channel through one fold, a place where two lobes almost meet around nothing. Some record stones that have shifted. Some may record objects carried away while people still came here.

It is difficult to tell how old the central body is. Its margins are continually being lost and remade. There may be no portion of it that was alive when the bathhouse was used, although it has never wholly left the recess beneath the bench.

On certain days, when the spring delivers slightly more water, a small swelling forms near an outer edge. Over time this acquires a feeding fringe of its own. Most such growths remain attached and become another fold. Occasionally one separates. It creeps a short distance under the mineral rim, traveling by movements too small to watch comfortably, and begins to fit itself into a vacancy.

One has reached the lowest step.

It is about the size of a thumbnail, translucent except where grit rests on its back. It lies just beneath the dark waterline, held to the dressed edge by its whole underside. When a little excess water crosses the step, its margin lifts.

For a moment you can see how little substance it has, and how carefully that substance holds its place.
