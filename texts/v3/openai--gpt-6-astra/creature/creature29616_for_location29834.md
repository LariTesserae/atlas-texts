The people are easiest to see when they are working. At rest, wrapped in undyed cloth beside a wall, they can look like bundles someone has set down. But a wrist turns, a tool catches the light, and the bundle becomes a woman cutting a new handle into a basin. There are white chips in her lap. She has been sitting there since the brighter band reached the opposite court.

She is not making the basin lighter. She is making it possible for two people to carry it when it is full.

Her name is Eren. She has a narrow face, a thick body, and one foot that no longer bends properly. When she moves house, she goes ahead of the sledges rather than riding on them. She dislikes being carried and is sufficiently skilled at repairing bone drills that people tolerate the inconvenience of leaving early with her.

There are several thousand people in the basin, though no one counts them all at once. A household knows whose dishes it must fill. A district knows roughly how many sleeping places it can offer. Beyond that, numbers become dependent on what one means by living somewhere.

Eren’s brother has slept in another district for eleven years. His bowl is still kept with hers.

“You could grind pigment in it,” he tells her.

“I could,” she says.

She does not.

***

Their bodies are human bodies, varied and imperfectly suited to the place. They get grit in their eyes. Their lips split. Children slip on mineral grease and knock out teeth. People who spend too long scraping damp surfaces develop tender patches between their fingers, and those who work dry stone cough into cloths which they wash separately from the eating cloths.

Most wear soft soles tied at the ankle. Bare feet can feel changes in the floor, but they can also be cut, and a cut acquired in a grazer bed is slow to close. Floor listeners uncover a heel or place a palm down. They do not go barefoot merely to demonstrate attentiveness.

Some people are unusually good at listening. Others are not, however patiently they are taught.

A competent listener distinguishes the small local noises of a working city from something transmitted beneath it: a basin dragged three courts away, a drill biting into a wall, the unfamiliar succession of faint knocks that means a cavity is shedding its roof. It is not a gift that makes uncertainty disappear. A listener may spend an afternoon beside a seam and finally advise moving a store of food twenty paces, which turns out to have been unnecessary.

The food must then be moved back.

There are arguments about this.

The best listeners are not always the most trusted. One woman is famous for noticing danger early and equally famous for claiming afterward that she had warned people who cannot remember being warned. Another speaks so reluctantly that her neighbors begin packing whenever she comes to visit.

Eren listens badly. She has no patience for a sound that may or may not be there. But she can watch a sledge pass and identify which runner is taking too much weight.

***

Much of their food is cultivated without looking like cultivation.

A damp wall may belong to six households. Its lower surface is divided by shallow grooves, and each family tends a patch: shading it with a suspended sheet, thinning the grazers, scraping away one kind of growth to favor another. Water is brought by the cupful when a seep weakens. Certain films are eaten fresh; others must be dried, washed, or left in covered dishes until their sharp smell changes.

The best crop is a soft gray skin that lifts almost whole if scraped at the right time. It is folded around crushed grazers and warmed on a stone above a little fat lamp. Done well, the outside becomes brittle while the filling stays damp.

People disagree strongly about what constitutes done well.

There are also travelers from beyond the roof. They come by long rising passages with sacks of seed, oil, wood, and dried things that retain startling colors. The city sends back fine abrasive powders, translucent stone, drilled ornaments, and small quantities of substances gathered from seeps whose tastes and uses are known to particular families.

Wood is valuable, but not so rare that every splinter is sacred. A child may own a wooden toy. Its wheels will probably outlast the child’s interest in it and be fitted to several later toys.

Fresh leaves are another matter. When a bundle arrives, people smell it before asking the price.

A few dislike the smell. It is too forceful, they say. It fills the mouth without being eaten.

The long pale hunters sometimes enter the food courts. They are not quite domestic. Individuals recognize particular people and particular sounds, and will accept a cracked shell from a hand, but a hunter cannot be made to stay where it does not wish to stay.

One sleeps near Eren’s tools. The comb around its muzzle is torn on one side, making its head look perpetually turned. It is called Split, except by Eren, who calls it Nothing when asked what she is feeding.

It has bitten her twice.

She keeps a separate little bowl for it.

***

Children learn the city at a lower height than adults.

They know where the flat animals tuck themselves, which thresholds conceal swimmers after a draining, and which walls will shed a moving powder if breathed upon. Before they can carry much, they are sent to look. Two children will spend an hour watching a damp edge and return with incompatible reports, both delivered confidently.

Adults make them show what they saw.

This is an important part of instruction: going back, kneeling down, finding the actual thing beneath the account of it.

They also learn routes. Not simply the way from one court to another, but the way with a basin between two carriers, the way with someone who cannot step high, the way that remains available when the shallow depression by the grinding place fills.

Their games are not all rehearsals for disaster. They race shell fragments down polished slopes, flick bone counters into holes, and play elaborate games of household ownership in which the smallest child is usually assigned a magnificent dwelling nobody else wants because it is too far away.

They make noise.

The city is quiet enough for a burst of laughter to travel absurdly far. Children discover this and test it. A shouted insult may arrive in another district stripped of its first words, leaving a puzzling accusation hanging over people who were sorting needles.

At certain hours an adult will appear at the edge of the square and stand still until the shouting stops.

***

There are wealthy households. Their rooms are not much taller, and they cannot safely heap possessions without limit, but they have better positions on stable ground, access to dependable drips, and enough people attached to them that moving is somebody else’s hardest work.

A rich family may own three good sledges and lend two generously.

The generosity is remembered. So is every refusal.

The broad passages are meant to remain clear. In practice, a drying rack is left out, then a row of jars, then a boundary stone is nudged a little farther from a chamber entrance. When several neighborhoods must move together, these small encroachments become serious matters.

Afterward, people walk the routes and argue beside the offending objects.

An old man once resisted moving his doorway stone until a girl lay down in the passage with her arms out, showing him exactly how little room his neighbors would have with their carrying board. He called this disrespectful. She remained on the floor.

He moved the stone.

She was reminded of her conduct for years, sometimes admiringly.

The migration ground is a larger dispute. Nine years is a long time to keep good open floor unused. Younger families have asked to put sleeping shelters there, removable ones. The older people disagree among themselves. Some remember the migration as a dense passage of low, broad-backed animals moving from opening to opening for two days. Others say the animals came in scattered groups and could easily have passed around a few shelters.

Nobody disputes the damage they did when crowded.

Eren was present at the last passage. She remembers the animals’ backs carrying a layer of dry mineral flakes, and smaller creatures riding in the folds behind their forelimbs. She also remembers missing most of it because she had a fever.

When asked to settle an argument, she says so. This satisfies neither side.

***

They have arts that can travel.

Cloth is worked with patterns that emerge only when folded. A plain sleeping wrap becomes, across someone’s shoulders, a set of branching dark lines. Bone needles have handles carved into tiny joined hands or the heads of animals whose open-air originals their makers have never seen.

People sing in the small roofed chambers, where the sound stays close. The common songs contain enough repetition for work, but their verses can be changed. A song about a lover who keeps promising to arrive has accumulated verses about delayed traders, blocked passages, bad drills, and a district elder who took six days to return a borrowed pot.

Dancers favor footwork and balance. A good dancer can make a shallow dish of water tremble without spilling it. A very good one can appear to lose control, recover at the last instant, and make everyone watching catch their breath.

They also draw on the floor.

The drawings may last for years in a dry place, or be erased that evening. Their makers care about the difference. A beautifully worked picture ruined by seepage is a loss, even when the artist knew the risk. Friends come to inspect what remains. Someone says it can be made again and is told, not gently, that this is not the point.

***

Death makes difficulties the living cannot solve by being practical.

There are dry chambers toward the basin’s edge where bodies can be laid beyond the reach of the familiar seepage. Reaching them takes time. The old and sick sometimes choose to move closer before they need to, then resent every day spent away from their own courts.

Not everyone goes.

Some are lost with the floor. Others die during a move, surrounded by people who cannot stop yet. Their names are cut onto carrying boards, bowls, or the wall of whatever chamber the household next occupies.

The marks accumulate.

Eventually someone has to decide whether a board too damaged to carry anything must still be carried.

These decisions are seldom made at meetings.

***

When the floor begins to change, all these people move: the generous, the possessive, the watchful, the ones who have been drinking, the mother furious with her son for losing a shoe.

Their quiet is not serenity. There is simply a great deal to hear.

Eren sends her tools ahead with a neighbor. She has packed them in their proper order, except for the basin handle she has not finished cutting. The basin itself must remain. She runs a thumb through the shallow new opening, checking it once more, although there is nothing to be done about it now.

Split has already gone.

Across the court, two brothers are trying to lift their father without hurting him. They have threaded a pole through the loops of his sleeping hide, but one loop is longer than the others and he keeps rolling sideways. He is giving instructions in a low, vicious voice.

Eren sits down beside them.

For a moment they think she is exhausted.

“Put it here,” she says, holding out her hand for the loop.

She takes an awl from inside her wrap. The neighbors have carried away nearly all her tools, but not this one. While the brothers support their father, she gathers the loose hide, pierces it, and draws a cord through.

Under her bad foot she feels a knock.

She has never been good at judging those.

She pulls the knot tight with her teeth.
