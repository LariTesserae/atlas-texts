At first you would notice that a piece of the bank had failed to settle.

The water has fallen. Leaves have turned back to their dark sides. Yet a strip beside the channel remains lifted, with a narrow dry space underneath it. Something in that space moves against the current.

Then the strip raises its head.

It is longer than a person lying down, though most of its length is a soft, low body that could be mistaken for a bundle of wet stems. Along either side are short limbs, six or eight pairs; the number is difficult to establish because some are walking while others remain folded into grooves. Each ends in a divided pad. The outer divisions grip. The inner ones are fine enough to take a swimming animal from between two root hairs.

Its head is broad and nearly level with its back. Four small eyes sit beneath a translucent ridge, looking outward and slightly down. There is no need here to see over anything. The farthest distances are available from an inch above the ground.

A pale fringe hangs from its jaw into the water.

For a while it does nothing you can recognize. Its fringe spreads. Its feet change position by fractions. A bubble travels down the channel, touches its face, and breaks.

It leaves the bank before the ground begins to tremble.

***

There are many of them, but they do not often occupy the visible surface with their whole bodies.

They live where layers separate: under a pool’s skin, between a mat of young growth and the older cords supporting it, in shallow chambers kept open by the pressure of their backs. Their ribs can flatten almost completely. A passage too low for the head will admit the body if the head has already found a way around.

The hide on their upper surface is dark, densely grooved, and inhabited. Small plants take root in secretions between the grooves. These are not quite the same plants as those growing underfoot. Their leaves are shorter, their attachments weaker. When the animal bends sharply, they detach rather than tear its skin.

On an old individual, the growth forms a close nap that lies one way from years of passage beneath the mat. Its journeys polish it.

The underside is pale and unexpectedly bare. Beneath the skin, bands of muscle move in slow, independent contractions. Lifted out of water, the animal looks unfinished: heavy folds, glistening joints, the delicate inner fingers continually closing on nothing. In its own passages those folds fit against the walls. It moves with very little wasted space.

The fringe beneath its jaw is its most sensitive part. Each thread contains a line of small, sealed chambers. Changes in pressure compress them in sequence. With its fringe submerged and several feet braced against living stems, it can distinguish a discharge beyond the horizon from the collapse of a grazer’s burrow nearby.

It can also feel another of its kind walking through the ground.

This is how they usually find one another.

***

Their food comes from the places where the plateau is failing.

They eat the small animals crowded out of draining layers, the soft white tissue exposed by a broken valve, the oily bodies that gather around roots carrying water from deep storage. Their mouth opens underneath the head. Within it are two ridged plates that slide past each other, stripping a stem without severing its central fiber.

After they have fed, the stem often continues to work.

They are less careful when hungry. A family can ruin a small pumping chamber by eating the tender collars around its outlets. Water then accumulates in their passages. The ceiling softens. They leave, and birds gather over the new pool.

Elsewhere, the same feeding keeps an overgrown channel open for centuries.

There are places where their paths and the plateau’s necessities have become inseparable, but this agreement is local. It can end with a poor breeding season, a change in the taste of buried water, or the arrival of an individual that eats something the others have learned to leave.

Their dwellings are similarly provisional.

A good chamber has several exits at different depths, a yielding floor, and one wall made of living tissue thin enough to transmit distant movement. They trim that wall continually. A cluster of dwellings may have a shared listening surface as wide as a pond, maintained from underneath by dozens of mouths.

From above, it looks like a particularly smooth stretch of ground.

Sometimes it dimples in several places at once.

***

They have uses for their hands beyond holding on.

At the edge of a rupture, one will select a long fiber, pull it free, and pass it backward from foot to foot. Another takes the end. Together they draw it across an opening and fasten it to growth on the far side.

They make low bridges, retaining nets, doors that open under pressure from only one direction. They can persuade a channel to empty into one hollow rather than another. The work is slow except when the ground is failing, when a dozen individuals may move with such coordinated haste that their limbs seem to belong to a single body.

Most of what they make goes on growing. A fastening becomes a knot of living wood. A net thickens, catches leaves, and eventually becomes indistinguishable from the surface it once spanned.

For this reason it is hard to decide how much of the country has been touched by them.

They also make things that have no obvious hydraulic purpose.

In sheltered chambers there are arrangements of short, pale stems, each bent under a different tension. An individual places two or three feet upon them and presses. The resulting vibrations scarcely reach the air.

Others settle nearby.

A performance may continue through most of a dark day. Those listening sometimes answer with a single pressure of a foot. Sometimes they leave abruptly. Sometimes, long after the maker has gone, one returns and changes the tension of a stem.

***

Their ordinary exchanges are quicker.

A forefoot presses, holds, releases. The next foot answers through the same root. An approaching individual stops with its jaw fringe laid flat and sends a series of small contractions down its body.

Certain patterns occur reliably before food is shared. Others precede a fight.

Their quarrels are quiet and difficult to watch. Two bodies lie side by side, almost touching. The plants between them shiver. Then one drives its pale inner fingers under the other’s edge and tries to turn it over.

The exposed underside is vulnerable to frost and to the long, dark hunters that patrol moving ground. A defeated animal scrambles desperately for cover. Its opponent may pursue it for a few yards, or may stop to recover a small object displaced during the struggle: a folded packet of roots, a strip of old skin, a clutch of smooth green capsules.

They carry such objects in pockets formed by the overlapping folds of their flanks.

The capsules are their young.

***

A newly laid capsule is no larger than the end of a finger and almost transparent. Inside is a creature curled around its own jaw fringe. Its limbs already move, feeling the inner wall.

The capsules develop in flowing water, but development depends on which water reaches them.

Those kept near the surface open within a season. Others are placed beneath stored winter, in chambers where the water moves slowly enough for its small suspended creatures to remain there through generations. These may stay closed for years.

Some are carried deeper.

Adults make long journeys to retrieve them. They return with bleached growth on their backs and root tips caught in joints that were clean when they departed. Occasionally they bring capsules whose enclosing tissue has become thick and dark, though the young inside are still small.

There are chambers where a newly hatched animal is greeted by individuals much younger than itself.

This causes no visible astonishment. It does cause work.

The hatchling must be taught the current routes. Water that was safe when its capsule was laid may now enter a blind layer. Familiar pressure patterns may belong to chambers that no longer exist. Its companions keep it between them, touching its feet whenever it starts in an unsafe direction.

For several days they take it to the listening wall.

There it rests with its entire underside against the tissue while the country moves beyond it.

***

They remember routes with more than memory.

At uncertain junctions they leave small braids, made from their own shed fringe and fibers gathered along the journey. The braid is fixed where water must pass through it. Its strands swell differently in cold water, warm water, old water drawn from depth.

An animal examining one holds it beneath its jaw for a long time.

A braid that was safe to follow yesterday may have tightened into another shape by morning. Some are cut down. Some are moved to a neighboring channel. Others are left in place with one strand deliberately severed.

Beside the oldest waterways, there are hundreds.

Individuals can disappear among these marked passages and return to a group that has dispersed, or has not yet assembled. The returner searches dwelling after dwelling, pressing against abandoned listening walls.

Sometimes it finds another waiting.

The two lie with their fringes interlaced. They may remain so until the water rises around them.

***

Before a lake lifts, they are among the first animals to move, but they do not all move away.

Some enter the contracting ground.

They work along the margins of the buried bowl, cutting fibers, dragging their capsules through passages already narrowing under strain. Others brace at exits and pull. Their backs become bare where the vegetation is rubbed off. In the last moments before rupture, they abandon tools, food, unfinished dwellings.

Not every chamber can be emptied.

Afterward they come out beneath the overturned slabs. For hours there are pale feet moving in the wreckage. They search the undersides of floating mats and open pockets of tissue with their mouth plates.

They retrieve living young, dead adults, damaged braids.

The dead are held in the shallow current of a surviving chamber. Those who lived with them feed there, removing the little growths from the hide. Eventually the body softens and the roots enter it.

The jaw fringe is cut away before that happens.

***

At the plateau’s edge, where droplets hang below the root lip, some of them spend long periods suspended head downward.

Their rear feet grip the mat. Their bodies extend into the cloud, lengthening as the folds open. With their front hands they gather the descending roots and lay them around the trembling drops.

They do not work continuously. They stop to warm their bare feet against their undersides, or haul themselves up to feed. In bad weather they retreat for days.

Yet there are old braids here, fastened well below the present edge.

Sometimes one reaches down farther than the others and presses a hand into the mist. It holds that position as if resting a foot on a listening wall.

The roots are too fine to bear its weight. There is no visible surface beneath them.

After a while, its fingers close.

It draws itself up slowly, keeping one hand cupped. The others gather around and put their fringes into the water it has brought back.
