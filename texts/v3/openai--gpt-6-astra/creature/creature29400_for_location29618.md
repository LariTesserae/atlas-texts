Turn one of the smaller shelled animals over and it closes around the disturbance.

The feet vanish first. They withdraw in rows, each row passing beneath the next, until the pale underside becomes a tightly pleated oval. Two mouthparts remain exposed a moment longer, working against one another. They are amber at the tips, translucent nearer the flesh, and so densely ridged that they look blunt until they catch the light. Between them is a soft patch stained purple by its last meal.

The animal has no obvious head. A narrow sensory fringe runs all the way around it, protected by the overhang of the shell. Part of this fringe tastes the surface; part rests against it and receives vibration. When the creature is set upright, the feet on the downhill side emerge first. It steadies itself, touches the limestone with its entire rim, and begins to move.

It can graze in any direction. It has a preferred direction only when among others.

In a crowd, each animal keeps a few bristles against its neighbors. Their shells conceal this network of contact. Beneath what looks like an immobile pavement, fringes are stroking, withdrawing, pressing down. An approaching body communicates its weight before it arrives. An animal with damaged feet is allowed to settle into a depression; one carrying young resists being climbed over, and the pressure travels outward until room opens around it.

There is no permanent leader at the front of a migration. The first animals to encounter a change in moisture alter the rhythm of their feet. Those beside them repeat it, sometimes before moving themselves. Across a crowded shelf the instruction can travel much faster than any individual can walk.

Different disturbances produce different rhythms. Drying brings a long, uneven scrape. The approach of a heavy grazer brings short impacts with silences between them. At the touch of a predator, the nearest animals strike their mouthparts against the undersides of their own shells. The sound is small in air and powerful through bodies pressed together.

Occasionally, a warning continues after its cause has gone. A whole hollow empties because an animal at its edge was frightened by a falling chip of stone.

The largest shelled creatures are very old.

Their outer plates no longer resemble those of the young. Successive rims have spread beyond old fractures; mineral deposits have thickened the pits; abandoned attachments remain as knobs and pale scars. Some carry small fields of other animals on their backs. These passengers feed on windblown debris, on films sustained by mist, and on the waste pushed out through openings between the host’s plates.

A large individual may move less than its own length in a season. Underneath, however, it is continuously active. Its feet knead the wet paste. Grooves in its underside carry water from the seep toward the shell’s perimeter, where younger animals gather. Thin muscular curtains open and close along these grooves, keeping the supply from draining too quickly.

Its bulk shelters the organisms it eats. Grazing strips alternate with strips left untouched, and its slow rotation brings fresh growth beneath the mouthparts. The richest feeding surfaces are those it has occupied for years.

When such an animal dies, the loss is first felt as a change in dampness. Water escapes from beneath one edge. A groove stops flowing. The smaller inhabitants move inward, then outward again as the body begins to soften. For a time the dead animal feeds far more mouths than it did alive.

Eventually the shell settles. Several generations may live under it before its plates separate.

The young begin there, or in similar enclosed places. Their first covering is flexible enough to fold, and for a few days they can squeeze through openings narrower than their bodies. They seek the breathing currents of adults and crawl toward them. Inside the shell rim are shallow recesses where the outgoing air stays moist. The young lodge in these recesses, scraping food from the adult’s surface.

They leave when their shells become too stiff to fit.

At this stage their greatest danger is another juvenile. Around a drying seep, the smallest animals prise one another from the stone and feed through the soft underside. Their mouthparts, so effective against mineral crust, need little alteration for this work. Adults also eat exposed young, though they seldom expend effort uncovering them. What appears from above to be a nursery is an arrangement of refuges, each just deep enough to admit one body and exclude a larger one.

The pale jointed grazers exploit spaces the shelled animals cannot reach.

Each has several pairs of walking limbs and two finer pairs folded beneath its narrow head. At a fissure, it braces itself and inserts these fine limbs deep into the rock. They return coated in dark material, which it draws through combs beside its mouth. Some fissures are worked until the limbs come back clean. Others are abandoned after one touch.

Within the translucent belly, food passes slowly through a long, folded chamber. The red movement sometimes visible there belongs to the chamber wall, contracting to mix the contents. Microorganisms flourish inside it. A grazer crossing a barren stretch is carrying a reserve of living food, still growing in the warmth of its body.

This makes the belly valuable.

The black predators approach from below the grazer’s field of sensation, keeping their weight spread over the stone. Their smooth appearance conceals thousands of minute gripping pads. They can hold to wet limestone, to shell, to the stretched skin between joints. Once attached, they flatten so closely that the victim cannot get a limb beneath them.

They do not hurry the injury. A narrow feeding blade works at one point while the rest of the body maintains its seal.

The two long feelers remain outside the wound. One follows the victim’s movements. The other rests on the ground, listening to the gathering scavengers. A predator that stays too long may be buried beneath animals rasping indiscriminately at grazer, attacker, and stone. Many of the black bodies bear pale, irregular margins where pieces have been lost.

Above them, the rain-catchers spend much of their lives compressed into sheltered cracks.

Unfolded, their membranes extend beyond the short structures that beat them through the air. The outer portions are almost passive: broad, soft-edged surfaces patterned with channels. Water gathers in these channels and runs toward absorbent folds beside the body. A fully wetted animal becomes too heavy for sustained flight. It descends awkwardly, grips the rock, and draws its membranes inward by degrees.

For hours afterward its body swells and contracts.

Some of the water enters storage sacs. Some passes through the gut, carrying nourishment washed from the membrane’s cultivated film. By the time the animal folds itself back into shelter, its wings are pale and nearly clean. During the interval before the next rain, the film grows again.

Their resting crevices contain eggs, cast skins, and small blind scavengers. Beneath a successful colony the limestone stays damp even through a long dry period. Shelled juveniles assemble there. Predators learn the route.

Farther down, beyond every opening large enough to admit these animals, life continues in the pores of the rock.

The hair-thin worms seen on dry ridges may extend a surprising distance below the surface. Only their narrow feeding ends emerge. Their bodies occupy branching passages enlarged by secretions and repeated movement, and in the wetter depths several worms may share a chamber. Some graze its walls. Others hold still in flowing water, their internal folds supporting organisms nourished by what the water carries.

A shift in the buried flow reaches them first.

They contract. Water is displaced through the pores. Animals feeding at crack mouths draw back, and the change passes into the shelled crowds as a thinning of the familiar vibration. Each body stops to receive more clearly what its neighbors have detected.

For a little while the whole surface listens.

Sometimes the disturbance fades and feeding resumes where it left off. Sometimes a warm seam cools permanently. The animals around it wait, scrape, move a short distance, and wait again. Over the following days the crowd loosens. Old routes fill with bodies traveling away.

Elsewhere, moisture darkens a fracture that was dry the day before. Its first inhabitants are too small to see. Then a worm appears at the edge. A juvenile shell follows, still soft enough to enter the crack.

It feeds alone for a while, its scraping audible only through the stone.
