The first inhabitants to enter the new pool are almost transparent.

They arrive along the floating roots, traveling inside the film of water rather than through the open water beneath it. Each consists of a branching thread and several small bladders. The thread advances by dissolving at its rear and growing at its tips; the bladders follow as their contents pass into new chambers. Nothing is carried whole. In an hour, an individual may have moved the width of a leaf while leaving every part of its former body behind.

Where two meet, their tips stop short of touching. Fluid collects between them. For a time the droplets contain sharply separated colors, amber on one side, a blue so faint it is visible only against the white root beneath. Then either the colors mingle or one thread withdraws.

Those that mingle grow a common bladder.

By nightfall, the pool’s edge is stippled with them. They gather metals from the rainwater and bind them into the first delicate arrays of a sensory membrane. The root tolerates their presence. It has no such membrane of its own yet, and through them it begins to register the approach of water down the newly formed slope.

For the small colonists, the root is shelter, food, and a possible future. Some will remain on its surface. Some will be enclosed as it thickens. Their descendants may still occupy the same branching channels when the root has become a buttress wider than the pool.

Others will be digested before morning.

Farther down, where the new ridge joins older growth, something more substantial is opening.

It has waited through three changes of drainage in a narrow pocket beneath a folded rind. Most of its body lies flat against the pocket’s walls: overlapping sheets of dark, supple tissue, their edges tucked into one another. Fine cords enter the surrounding wood. They do not draw enough nourishment to injure it. Their principal use is to feel.

They have felt the pressure rising for days.

Now water touches a row of closed mouths along the lowest sheet. The mouths open one after another, exposing combs of pale filaments. Water enters, passes through the body, and emerges higher up as a slow ooze. Suspended seed dust, fragments of torn membrane, and the transparent settlers are retained between the combs.

The sheets become thicker. A crease at their center unfolds.

What emerges resembles a leaf until it bends against the current.

Its upper surface is green-black and finely pebbled. Beneath it, translucent ribs enclose a branching darkness. There is no head. Along its forward margin are six blunt lobes, each capable of separating into smaller lobes when it meets an irregular surface. One presses against the new ridge. Another rests on the old rind. The others explore the water between.

The whole exposed body is scarcely larger than a broad frond. Much more of it remains inside the land.

It lives at the junction of tissues that grow at different speeds.

Here a young slope pushes upward against a buttress that has scarcely changed in a century. Without intervention, the contact will eventually tear. The inhabitant makes the contact livable. From some surfaces it secretes a lubricant that allows fibers to slide. From others it releases compounds that slow cell division. Where the strain is greatest, its lobes enter the rind and establish soft, wet partitions.

Around these partitions, the ridge can rise without splitting open.

The surrounding growth feeds it. Sweet fluid wells from specialized pores wherever its cords have been accepted. Other cords are attacked, sealed in bitter tissue, or stripped of their outer cells. It spends much of its life negotiating a margin only a few finger-widths deep.

A successful individual can occupy such a margin for hundreds of years.

It will not retain this shape.

As the ridge rises, the body lengthens along the join. The lowest sheets die and are consumed. New ones develop above them. Side branches follow promising fractures, sometimes for distances that leave their original feeding pocket far behind. A body that began beneath one fold may eventually become a thin, interrupted presence extending through an entire mountainside.

Its separated parts remain in contact through the membranes embedded in its ribs. A change of strain at one end alters the growth of the other. Some signals follow the cords. Others cross through paired arrays that were formed together before the body spread, arriving without disturbing the tissue between.

Damage to one such array produces an enduring asymmetry. A distant branch continues to make allowance for a weight that is no longer there. It thickens its support, receives no answering adjustment, and slowly withdraws from an empty space.

These are among its oldest injuries.

It has senses suited to a place where sight would be continually obstructed.

Light matters chiefly as something stored in the body and exchanged with its neighbors. Its translucent ribs distinguish changes in the quality of that light with great precision. Through a few accepted feeding cords it can detect whether the foliage supplying them is sunlit, submerged, diseased, or beginning to flower.

Pressure gives it a more extensive world.

Every sheet is crossed by chambers of fluid. Minute differences between them reveal the movement of the surrounding land: a stem slipping in its sheath, a reservoir filling, the slow commitment of a slope toward collapse. Rhythms travel through the wood from farther away. Rain has one texture. The opening of seed cases has another. The great hollow trunks announce changes in their internal water long before those changes reach the seam.

The inhabitant adjusts itself continuously within this information. Most adjustments are small. It opens a pore, tightens a cord, withholds a growth compound from a strip of neighboring rind.

Occasionally it closes every feeding mouth.

Then the broad sheets press tightly together, and fluid is driven into a few reinforced ribs. Its lobes flatten. The body becomes a dense, narrow wedge.

A moment later, the seam shifts.

The movement may be less than the thickness of a seed. Against the accumulated load of the ridge, that is enough to strip away everything left projecting into the join.

Afterward the sheets slowly separate. Torn filaments drift from the mouths. New tips form along the injured margin.

Smaller things live in the sheltered spaces between those sheets. Some graze on the inhabitant’s secretions. Some clean its feeding combs. One kind grows as a row of hard, glossy beads, each bead enclosing a folded net. When exposed to the fluid of a damaged cell, the nets spring out and adhere to the wound. The inhabitant grows around them, incorporating their mineral strength into its repair.

Another kind resembles the first closely enough to be admitted. Its net grows inward.

An infected sheet develops a bright patch that spreads against the direction of the sap. The branching darkness beneath it disappears. Eventually the sheet detaches, curls into a tight cylinder, and is carried away by water. It may fasten elsewhere before the inhabitant’s remaining tissue has sealed the gap.

The distinction between offspring, parasite, and escaped piece of a body is not always durable here.

The seam-dweller itself reproduces in more than one way. A branch cut off by the closing land may establish an independent circulation and continue alone. These divided individuals usually avoid one another when they meet again. Their growth compounds are too similar; prolonged contact causes each to suppress the other’s margins.

A different kind of reproduction requires the high canopy to turn.

In the days before a turning, the inhabitant diverts nourishment into small chambers along its upper edge. Inside each chamber, layers of tissue fold around a paired light-holding array. The outer rind hardens. A tuft develops at one end, packed so tightly that it resembles a white grain.

When the descending wash reaches the seam, the chambers burst.

The grains enter a turbulence that no exposed crawler could survive. They pass down shafts, strike waxy walls, vanish into reservoirs. Wetting releases their tufts into long, sticky fans. Most adhere where they cannot live. Their reserves are eaten by the surface growth.

A few catch between structures that are moving apart.

There the fan pulls taut, and the strain opens the grain. One root enters each side of the gap. Between them, protected by the remains of its casing, a first sheet develops.

It inherits a bias toward certain pressures, certain flavors of sap, certain intervals between the deep sounds of the trunks. Those biases were laid down while its parent was forming it. Young carried from the same seam often respond differently to danger because they matured during different weather.

As they grow, their own experience alters the arrangement of the inner membranes. Repeated strain leaves a persistent pattern. A fracture successfully relieved becomes easier to recognize elsewhere. Some patterns survive the loss of almost all the tissue in which they first formed, copied forward as the body renews itself.

An old inhabitant consequently occupies more than its present seam. Its responses contain the shapes of vanished ridges.

This can preserve a slope. It can also ruin one.

Water changes course; supporting growth is replaced; the deep knots alter where weight is taken. An old response may open a passage that should now remain closed. When that happens, the surrounding tissues cease to feed the seam-dweller. Bitter fluid enters its cords. New rind advances across its sheets.

Its outer parts may take years to die. During that time they shelter germinating grains and hold pockets of water warm with decay. Some of the young fasten to the dying body itself, opening their first feeding mouths into the fluids that escape it.

At the foot of the new ridge, the exposed lobes have now found purchase.

One enters the yellow tissue revealed by the recent split. The tissue contracts around it, then loosens. A clear droplet appears at the point of contact. The lobe absorbs it and presses a little farther in.

Under the water, the transparent colonists continue up the floating roots. Above them, rain gathers along a fresh pleat and begins to spill.

The inhabitant opens another sheet beneath the falling water. For now there is room to feed, room to extend, and a tolerable difference between the movement of one wall and the other. Deep in its ribs, a branching pattern settles into a new arrangement.

The ridge rises around it.
