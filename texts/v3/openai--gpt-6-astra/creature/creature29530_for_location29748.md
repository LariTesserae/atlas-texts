What lives there still uses the trench to cross the basin.

It could go around. At either end, the blue powder gives way to the same pale crust that covers the rest of the hollow. There is no enclosing wall. But going around would require it to arrive on the other side by a route it had not maintained, and it has become particular about how it arrives.

Beneath the ceramic span, a departure still leads to an arrival. The steps retain their order. It crosses there even when the journey takes longer than remaining where it is for another hundred years.

There is no body to see on the steps. There is, briefly, a distinction between the steps it has crossed and those it has yet to cross. The blue particles on one side stand a little more distinctly apart. On the other, their edges seem pressed together, although no particle has moved. This difference travels beneath the span. Afterward, it fades.

That is as close as the hollow comes to containing a footprint.

Its shape is easier to notice elsewhere, though never all at once. Along one of the polished channels, a stretch of mineral will acquire the appearance of having something behind it. The crust is no thinner there. Nothing presses against its underside. Nevertheless, the impression is exact: a small surface held away from another surface by something accommodated between them.

A little farther on, there may be an elbow in a shadow whose stone has no corresponding angle. Above the basin, one portion of the roof can seem lower without obscuring anything it left visible before.

These occurrences belong to the same inhabitant. They do not necessarily occur together.

Once, its parts may have been closer to one another. The scratches above the shelf suggest a creature that could brace itself between floor and ceiling, then draw something hard across the roof. Several scratches begin in pairs. Toward their ends, the members of each pair diverge, one becoming shallow while the other deepens. The surfaces have worn too much to show whether this happened through effort or through some difficulty in remaining paired.

It no longer reaches up.

Most of what it does now consists of keeping a few separations from becoming excessive.

The distance between a grain and its shadow. Between a surface and the direction from which it can be touched. Between setting out for the ring and being at the ring.

The surviving machines help with these things. Near the staple, it can gather enough of itself to proceed. Within the black arc, it can rest without having to decide repeatedly which side of the ground it occupies.

It does not inhabit either device. There is something almost domestic in the way it approaches them from outside, and in the small hesitation before it enters their influence.

At the ring, it always pauses beside the buried portion.

The three faded marks face inward. Whatever else they mean, they distinguish this place from the rest of the circumference. The inhabitant passes them in the same order each time. Then a few grains inside the ring become plainly visible in the weak light: little broken crystals, each with a top, each lying on a floor.

Nothing appears among them.

For a while, however, the empty space they surround is unequivocally empty. Its edges can be followed. Its depth can be judged. There is room for something there, and the room does not alter while it is being considered.

This is its rest.

It has very little left that could be called appetite. The bright particles below the rise remain unconsumed. The blue powder is older than many of the mountain’s exposed faces. Even the light retained in the ledges diminishes at its own slow rate, without being drawn toward the inhabitant.

Yet it prefers some conditions to others.

It spends more time beside a surface that is almost level than beside one that has settled into a reliable slope. A grain poised at the end of a channel can occupy it for a long interval. When the grain finally rolls, the inhabitant does not follow. It remains at the small place from which the grain departed.

There, for a time, the crust supports the precise absence of a weight.

It attends to such places until they become merely bare ground.

This may be a form of nourishment. It may also be a habit acquired when the hollow contained more things, and departures were worth waiting beside.

The curve at the limit of warmth is different. It approaches that boundary seldom, and always by the broadest of the polished routes.

Sunlight gives the mineral outside the curve a quality it cannot tolerate for long. The grains become insistently local. Each belongs to its patch of stone; each changes temperature with its immediate neighbors. Under those conditions, the inhabitant begins to separate into incidents that no longer imply one another.

An altered shadow remains an altered shadow. A hesitation in the depth of the floor becomes a trick of the light. Whatever connection allows these things to be parts of a living whole grows weak.

It withdraws before the connection fails.

Sometimes, after warmth has retreated, it goes back to the curve. Its approach makes no mark. But a few grains on the cold side turn until their flatter faces lie toward the entrance.

They receive the next silver wash more evenly.

The arrangement lasts until some other downward takes them.

There is no reason to suppose the inhabitant sees the mountain. From where it waits, the opening may be no more than a region through which conditions arrive: pressure, brightness, a little warmth that exhausts itself on the floor. It has never carried anything to the threshold. It makes no attempt to widen it.

Its concerns lie farther in.

The dark chip on the upper shelf is one of them.

It visits the chip less often now that the shelf is rising. The route continues to lengthen even though the visible ground scarcely changes. A crossing begun when the mouth is bright may finish under an illumination held over from a vanished star. On reaching the shelf, the inhabitant must spend some time establishing which of the chip’s appearances is the one it has come to attend.

Angular from below. Rounded from beside it.

It waits until these can be understood as views of a single object.

Then the chip’s shadow shifts slightly downhill.

The correction is never complete. Something about the stone prevents it from lying wholly within the conditions the inhabitant can preserve. By the next visit, the shadow has usually crept back uphill.

Still, it makes the journey.

The chip is not an egg. Nothing inside it is waiting to emerge. It has no hidden seam and gives off no faint answering pulse. Its material is much like the dark continuous layer beneath the crust.

One face, visible only from the shelf, bears a shallow depression.

The inhabitant settles against that depression with a precision it displays nowhere else. The air does not stir. The chip does not rock. For the length of the contact, the distance between the shelf and the roof ceases to increase.

Perhaps the depression once fitted a part of it.

Perhaps holding the chip was one of the last actions it could perform when it still had parts that fitted anything.

When it leaves, the shelf resumes its rise.

It is careful on the descent. Near the ridge with the divided crest, it follows the bare strip where dust will not settle. There it has to become less definite than it was beside the chip. Something that rested must turn into something that can pass. The change takes place unevenly, leaving small stretches of the route briefly more navigable behind it.

A grain may roll.

Centuries later, another grain may use the same shallow course.

Much of the basin has been shaped by this traffic. The worn undersides, the isolated fans of dust, the channels that terminate on what would ordinarily be uphill: all have accommodated the inhabitant’s attempts to get from one familiar place to another without losing too much along the way.

It does not seem to understand the whole hollow. At some ledges it stops. There are surfaces it has ceased to cross, though their appearance has not changed. Once in a very long while, it begins toward a destination and returns to the staple instead.

Under the span, the order of the steps is reassuring.

It passes over them slowly. A few blue particles slip down a riser. It waits, then crosses again. On the second passage, the particles settle into a cleaner edge.

This is the sweeping.

No hand performs it, and nothing is carried away. The inhabitant simply repeats an action until the place can bear that action clearly.

Afterward it goes to the ring.

There, in the modest patch the machine still preserves, it leaves a small interval between itself and the black inner edge. The interval is unnecessary. It could rest closer.

But it has always left room to turn.
