The largest living thing in the hollow has no outline that the eye can keep.

It follows the join between two crystals, spreads into a shallow imperfection, and disappears where the stone becomes smooth. A little farther on, it is present again. Water passes over both places without revealing the difference. Only when the light above changes does one part of the wall remain bright.

Its body is mostly boundary: the thickness between water and mineral, between what arrives hot and what has begun to cool. Lift it from the wall and there would be almost nothing to hold. Scrape away the crystal beneath it and some of what appeared to be crystal would prove to have been alive.

Within that slight thickness, it is busy.

A damaged assembly comes apart. Its useful pieces are caught before the current carries them off. Along another grain, a passage closes against a change in the brine; beside it, a new passage begins to carry charge. Calcium is moved a distance too small to see, and the fit between the living material and the stone improves. The wall gives it a place to work, but not a permanent one. Under the film, the mineral is always a little different from what it was.

It does not repair itself toward a finished shape. It repairs the ability to continue.

At the hottest point, continuation requires a particularly exact arrangement. Farther out, other arrangements work. The patch has no head, yet its edge is unlike its center, and the edge facing the incoming water is unlike the edge downstream. What looks like a single clear stain contains a geography of tolerances. A shift in the rivulet can make one narrow district prosperous and another impossible.

Then the light changes there.

Not a flash. Perhaps only a thread of white becoming less distinct, while a neighboring thread strengthens. In daylight this would be lost among the reflections. After dark, it is one of the few changes the hollow can show without anything visibly moving.

The four patches do not need to be four individuals. Nor does the bare stone between them make them one. Each keeps its own working surfaces intact. Each can fail without the others failing. Water may carry loosened material from one to another, but most of what is carried away will never find a suitable place again.

On the fallen chip, the fifth patch has found one.

It is small enough that a single drying edge can threaten much of it. The chip does not retain heat as the rear wall does; water approaches it intermittently and leaves by several routes. Here the film grows unevenly. It advances along one wet seam while losing ground on the exposed face. Its persistent sheen is the visible result of many successes and failures too small to separate.

There is no older coating beneath it. This is its first hold on this particular stone.

Across the basin, the mite feeds on what such holdings lose.

It has a more definite body, but the brightness nearly erases it. The joints of its legs show briefly as finer whiteness against white. Its mouthparts become visible when they move. Under its clear covering, the body is faintly cloudy, less like glass than a drop of water with something minute suspended in it.

It rests on six legs and holds the foremost pair ahead. Those raised legs are not waiting to take a step. They touch, withdraw, and touch elsewhere. Before the animal advances, it has already encountered the next little length of shore.

The margin is difficult country. A crystal can be climbed, but the water beside it can seize a leg. A place that supported the mite earlier may now be covered by a meniscus too deep to cross. Loose grains turn underfoot. A bead resting between two chips offers no firm indication of what is beneath it.

The mite goes slowly because there is much to establish.

At the feeding place it lowers the front of its body and works at a nearly invisible accumulation. The living film on the hot wall is beyond its reach, perhaps beyond its tolerance. Here there are fragments, spent components, a little material caught where the water hesitated. The mite takes what it can detach. Afterward, it moves the width of its head and tries again.

Its food arrives by events that would be disasters at its own scale: a flake falling, a route changing, a bright patch losing part of its edge.

The mite does not seek the light. It follows the places where something edible has stopped moving.

When a ceiling drop lands nearby, the water reaches it before the sound has finished. Its raised legs come down. For a moment all eight work against the spreading sheet. Then it is beneath the plate, pressed into a space where the water thins and the stone gives it purchase.

There it stays.

The ledge is not dark. Light from the rear wall reaches beneath it, glancing from the basin. The mite is sheltered from the falling drops but remains faintly visible, a set of pale joints tucked close to a pale body. It cleans one leg, drawing it through its mouthparts, and then another. At this scale, something adhering to a foot is an obstruction worth stopping for.

Beyond the ledge, the basin continues to rise.

The film does not notice the animal’s retreat. The mite cannot reach the source of the light. Yet a little of the hot wall has entered its body, and some of that will be left elsewhere in the hollow. Material passes between these lives without joining their purposes.

During the night, the new patch on the chip remains wet. The old channel softens. At the rear seam, living surfaces keep renewing the conditions under which they can work.

The ceiling drop grows until its reflection contains the white wall, the basin, and the lip beneath which the mite waits. Then it falls, taking that small, complete image with it.

For a while afterward, the animal keeps still.

At last one front leg reaches out.
