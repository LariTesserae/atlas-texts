At first you might take them for places where the wall has not finished drying.

They occur beneath the planting channels, in the sheltered angles of doorframes, behind the little freight platform. Each is roughly the length of a hand, with an irregular dark margin and a center the color of watered milk. A few straight silver lines show through. When the violet light strengthens, they become harder to distinguish from the material beneath them.

But a damp patch does not gather itself away from a falling drop.

One of these does. Slowly, without lifting its edges, it contracts into a narrower shape. The drop strikes the exposed wall. A moment later the thing spreads again, except where the water is running.

There are perhaps forty visible from the bench. Once you know what to look for, there are more.

They are not all alike. The ones near the floor are broad and almost transparent, their surfaces wrinkled by the shallow currents. Higher in the galleries they are smaller, thicker, faintly amber. One beneath a warm pipe has a folded edge like the lip of an oyster. Another surrounds a fern’s stem so closely that the plant seems to emerge from its back.

None has an obvious head.

On the underside, which you might see if one crosses a service opening, there are rows of minute pale feet. They move in groups rather than in waves: a patch grips, another releases, and the whole animal flows a little farther along. Between the feet lie delicate branching structures, flushed pink where they touch the building. These withdraw immediately from the air.

It would be possible to peel one from the wall. It would almost certainly hurt it.

Most spend the daylight hours near roots. They do not eat the roots, although they taste them continually, pressing their margins against the growing tips. A new root may remain embraced for an afternoon. When the animal moves away, it leaves a clear sleeve around the tender section, and the light behind the wall is often brighter there.

They also visit damaged places.

A cracked mineral shelf draws several. So does the sharp edge of the eastern split. They settle with part of themselves on each side of an opening and become so still that clay accumulates on their backs. Over several days, the distinction between animal and repair grows difficult to make. The outer margin remains soft. The center hardens. Silver threads align with threads already present in the foundation.

Eventually something hand-sized moves away.

What it leaves behind is larger than what it brought.

Their food comes chiefly from the collection slots. After the basin flushes, they descend into the warm pulp and spread among the leaves. Here they become less discreet. Their upper surfaces rise into low folds; openings appear between them, pale and finely ridged. A copper-colored leaf can disappear beneath three animals in the course of a night. By morning there will be a lace of tougher veins, a small quantity of dark grit, and a new translucent layer along the lip of the drain.

They are converting the fallen growth into more district, but not efficiently enough for that to be their only reason for feeding. Some of what they take becomes fat. Some becomes movement. Sometimes two remain in the pulp after the others have gone, pressed together along their whole lengths, exchanging a cloudy substance through their softened edges.

Later, a smaller one may be found nearby.

The small ones wander.

They cross bare wall where there is nothing to eat, circle the undersides of railings, or reach the end of a trailing stem and stay there, weighing it down. More than one has fallen into the basin. A fallen animal draws its edges inward and becomes a little corrugated cup. It floats until the current brings it against a step.

The older ones seldom float. Their attachment to the building seems to deepen with age.

There is one on the western wall that extends through three service openings. You cannot see its whole shape. In one opening it looks like a pale cushion fitted tightly around a conduit; in another, a series of wet folds. During the pumping cycle those folds close together. The rushing sound begins, and a dark flush passes through the cushion.

Then the water rises.

The smaller animals stop moving while this happens. Their margins flatten. Some turn fractionally, aligning themselves with stresses you cannot feel until the railings begin to shake.

Whether they are listening or participating is difficult to tell.

Farther inside, there must be others too large to leave. The exposed sheet of rising water has a soft rim that tightens before each surge. Certain smooth corners contract when the pressure changes. Once, through a low maintenance hatch, you might see a surface covered in the same minute feet as the hand-sized animals. They are planted against the opposite side of the shaft.

The surface continues into darkness in both directions.

This is not quite a colony of workers tending a larger creature. The distinction between the workers and what they tend is too temporary. A small one can become a seal around a root, then leave that seal and go feeding. A larger one can remain in a threshold until most of it is threshold. Yet some portion keeps its appetite, its sensitivity to falling water, its ability to withdraw.

The district contains many such decisions, made slowly.

That is why its rooms are shallow. Why the galleries hug the walls. Why the central space has been left open, warm, and wet. Nothing here lives far from a surface it can touch on both sides.

The empty volume is useful to them. Across it pass temperature changes, vibrations, the faint chemical traces released by damaged leaves. A break in a planter on the northern wall can alter the movement of animals on the southern wall before any water has reached the floor.

They do not need to cross the square to know something has happened there.

A person sitting on the bench introduces a much more complicated disturbance.

At first nothing approaches. The bench is already warm; the additional warmth spreads slowly. Then a translucent margin appears beneath one end of the seat. A second animal emerges near the person’s heel. Neither climbs onto skin or clothing. They remain close to the points where weight enters the structure.

If the person shifts, they shift.

If the person stays long enough, the bench may become slightly less hard beneath one hip. The change is small and not necessarily comfortable. They know pressure intimately, but a seated body offers pressures unlike those of a root or a column.

After the visitor leaves, several animals remain beneath the seat. By the following afternoon, a fine pale reinforcement may have formed where the woodlike material flexed most.

The bench was not made by creatures that sit. Neither were the stairs made by creatures that need steps. These shapes belong to an older arrangement between the living material and the people who built with it. The arrangement persists in how a doorway grows, in the height at which a handrail stops, in the dry space preserved beneath an overhang.

The animals themselves show no preference for an occupied room over an empty one. They will seal a leaking window in either. But occupation gives them more to respond to.

Warm breath beads on a pane. A shoe brings in unfamiliar grit. Someone waters a planter too generously, and half a dozen pale bodies gather behind it to deal with the excess.

For days afterward, that part of the district changes more quickly than the rest.

Meanwhile, at the eastern split, the youngest animals lie against the exposed clay. They are nearly colorless here. Water wells beneath them and lifts their edges; they settle again, taking a little of the clay into themselves. A new foundation sliver holds their impressions for an hour before its surface smooths.

Above them, in a room with paired windows, a larger one is easing around a defective seal. It has been at the task since morning. By dusk the gap will be closed, the fern supported, and the animal a little thinner.

Then it will go down toward the smell of leaves.
