The largest animals in the notch cannot be seen whole. Too much of each is inside the mountain.

What shows is a length of white flank, a folded feeding comb, sometimes a soft elbow projecting from a seam. Behind these, the body divides. One branch follows a vein downward; another narrows to a ribbon and passes between surfaces of rock so close that water scarcely enters. An animal whose exposed portion would fit in two hands may have extremities several paces apart. Its oldest parts are thin and dark, pressed almost indistinguishably into the mineral. Only the growing ends are white.

They do not burrow by breaking stone. Their tissues wet it with something that makes a boundary between crystals available. They enter that boundary, thicken, and wait.

Some were here before the notch opened.

Along the fresh fracture at the back of the basin, several bodies have been torn across. Their severed branches show as pale stars in the black rock. Most have sealed themselves. One still leaks a bluish fluid, very slowly, and a collar of small animals has assembled around the injury. They keep their mouths against it. When the injured animal contracts, they lift together, still attached, like the fingers of a glove being filled.

The rupture exposed them to a great deal of space.

Their feeding combs, formerly spread across narrow underground trickles, have grown broad and crowded. New chambers bulge from old armored trunks. Plates that once lay against stone now have free edges, and around these edges the animals are making an unfamiliar kind of tissue: flexible, densely sensitive, able to close over a surface that gives way when touched.

A few have attempted to extend across open water. Their growing tips hang out from the fissures, blunt and searching. Most eventually bend back to the rock.

One has reached a neighbor.

The joining place is no thicker than a wrist. It is continually being eaten.

***

The creatures doing the eating resemble flat, soft beetles, though they have neither legs nor wing cases. A row of little gripping mouths runs underneath each one. The front mouths hold; the middle mouths feed; the rear mouths let go. They progress along a filament with a barely visible kneading motion.

They crop excess tissue from the connections. Sometimes they crop too deeply.

When this happens, both ends of a severed thread curl toward the grazer. Other threads nearby withdraw. For a while the little animal is left clinging to a length of dead connection with no route back to the stone. It folds itself lengthwise and waits to drift against something edible.

Many of the transparent bells contain one.

You can see it through the bell’s wall, turning with the digestive current, its gripping mouths still opening in sequence.

The bells have no obvious front except when they feed. Then a portion of the rim loosens and extends into a transparent pouch. Food entering that pouch passes through a narrow throat and becomes one more amber shape in the suspended column.

They are anchored by threads finer than their own internal vessels. The threads run upward, downward, sideways; several bells may share the same attachment. When one is disturbed, the others do not flee. They shorten their threads until the column becomes a stack of flattened lenses.

Their young are almost invisible. For the first part of their lives they lie against their parents’ inner walls, distinguished only by the way a line in the distant rock bends through them. Eventually a young bell detaches, passes out through the feeding rim, and hangs nearby on a strand it has brought with it.

The adult then moves a little farther from the stone.

There is room for another.

***

The silver animals at the eastern face are the most exposed inhabitants, and the least armored.

Their silver comes from several layers beneath the skin. Seen straight on, they are bright enough to hide their organs. Seen obliquely, each body becomes transparent at the edges, revealing a dark digestive cord and two long structures that swell alternately along the spine. The swelling travels forward on one side and backward on the other.

Their eyes are small. Far more of the head is given over to sensing pits, each covered by a taut film. Their side filaments carry rows of these films too. A current touches them before it moves the body. An electrical change reaches them earlier still.

They spend much of their lives correcting positions that, to an observer, have not visibly changed.

A head inclines. A filament slips over another filament. A tail unfolds from a shallow curve. Across the mesh, a dozen animals make adjustments so small that only the altered pattern of reflected light reveals them.

Each maintains a little territory, but the territories overlap. Neighbors tolerate the crossing of filaments and object to the crossing of bodies. A newcomer must enter sideways, advancing a short distance and holding still while the animals around it examine its flank.

Sometimes it is accepted.

Sometimes the nearest fish closes its mouth on the newcomer’s filament and remains that way for hours.

There are scars along almost every adult’s sides.

Their mouths are unexpectedly large. The lower jaw can separate at the chin, allowing the whole front of the animal to open around prey nearly as thick as itself. They eat windblown insects, small fish, the soft portions of bells that have lost their anchors. They will also eat their own young.

Yet they make spaces for those young in the mesh.

At certain times an adult develops a cluster of dark grains beneath the silver skin of its belly. The grains become eyes. Around them, faint bodies take shape, packed head to tail inside a shallow chamber.

Before releasing them, the adult leaves its usual position and turns across the boundary, head inward, tail almost at the air. Its neighbors draw close. Filaments gather around the belly.

The young emerge into this loose basket.

For a long time they cannot hold a place in open water. They cling with their mouths to the adults’ filaments, their bodies streaming sideways in the current. Their first meals are scraps caught in the mesh and the soft outer tissue of the filaments themselves.

An adult carrying too many grows dull. It shakes once, slowly, and some of the young lose their hold.

Below the mesh, flat predators wait with their upper edges slightly raised.

***

Nothing in the basin receives the deep concussion merely as noise.

Before it arrives, the stalked shells close. The tube mouths fold inward. A fish that has been crossing a gap puts its jaw against the nearest surface and tests the grip. Even the minute grazers settle toward the stone, although many are still tumbling when the pressure reaches them.

There are occasional mistakes. A bell is caught fully extended and loses part of its rim. A feeding tube closes too late and is turned partly inside out. For hours afterward, the grazers gather around the damaged tissue.

The electrical tremor provides warning, but not everywhere equally. Certain recesses are sheltered from it by the arrangement of the metal-bearing bodies. Animals living there respond to their neighbors instead: to a stalk bending, a mouth withdrawing, a thread going taut.

The warning passes by several routes.

So does the work.

When a white fissure animal dies, its chamber stops contributing, but its plates remain conductive for a while. Small scavengers enter through the exhalant opening and widen it as they feed. The soft partitions disappear first. Later, the plates loosen and settle against one another.

A living branch from farther inside the stone may then enter the emptied shell. It grows along the old chamber, finding the contours, laying a new lining over the dead one.

Some shells contain several such linings.

Their occupants are younger than their houses, and the houses are older than this open water.

***

At night, a small brown animal comes out onto the uppermost ledges.

It is scarcely longer than a thumb, with six blunt limbs and a broad, wrinkled back. By daylight it looks like a scrap of mineral caught beneath a slab. In darkness it climbs until half its body is above the surface.

There it remains, breathing.

Its back unfolds into two wet lobes. Water held between their ridges trembles with the sound from below. Now and then the animal draws the lobes together, squeezes a drop into its mouth, and spreads them again.

It eats whatever the drop contains.

There are not many of these animals. Their exposed skin freezes readily, and they must keep dipping themselves. Each has a regular place at the margin, polished by the repeated purchase of its feet. Where the rock is too sharp, one carries a little pad of shed tissue and sets it down before climbing out.

Near dawn they return beneath the slabs.

One sometimes stays too long. The surface freezes around its limbs. It pulls free if the ice is thin; otherwise it waits until sunlight reaches it, with its back folded tight and its body faintly moving under the clear sheet.

***

The bead on the eastern face is seldom empty.

Small things enter it as it swells: precipitate, a grazer, the discarded gripping skin from a fish’s jaw. On some nights a young silver animal follows them. It is still transparent, with only a narrow bright stripe above each eye.

Inside the bead it turns repeatedly, meeting the curved boundary with its forehead.

There is no rock here. No adult filament reaches it. Each time it pushes forward, the water stretches a little farther into the air.

Eventually the young animal stops trying to leave. It opens its side filaments. They are short, newly grown, with only a few sensing films along them.

The bead steadies.

Behind it, across a thinning neck of water, the basin continues flashing in its crowded seams. The young fish holds itself crosswise to that distant light. Its two spinal organs begin their alternating swell.

Toward morning, the neck narrows enough to bend the view of the whole notch.

Sometimes the bead returns, carrying the little animal backward into the mesh.

Sometimes it separates.

For a while afterward, a round brightness can be seen below the basin, descending through the cold air. It falls much more slowly than the drops melting from the neighboring snow. Within it, the fish makes continual small corrections.

Then a cloud passes underneath, and it is gone.
