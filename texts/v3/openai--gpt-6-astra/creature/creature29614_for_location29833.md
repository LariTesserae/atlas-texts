The people who live here learn to look at water without trusting what they see.

A child can judge distance across a room before she can judge depth across a pool. Her grandmother lowers a white bead on a cord into the blue beside the walkway. The bead remains sharply visible long after the child thinks it ought to have touched bottom. Then it disappears—not gradually, but behind a fold in the rock.

The grandmother draws it up again.

This is a lesson given several times, at different pools. Knowing one pool does not count as knowing water.

Young children wear small chalk-colored floats between their shoulder blades. The floats are awkward in doorways and make it difficult to lie down secretly when there is work to do. Older children remember them with contempt and put them on their younger siblings with a care they would be embarrassed to have noticed.

People do fall in. There are rescue poles along the walks, and slings with broad straps, because a person lifted from a hot pool cannot always bear to be held under the arms.

Most of the dangers are ordinary enough to have equipment.

***

Their clothes hang loose from the shoulders and fit closely at the ankles. A trailing hem will take up water; a wet hem can carry dissolved salts into the small abrasions above a shoe. Laundry is therefore more important than cleanliness alone would explain. At the end of a shift, work clothes go into a first rinse that nobody touches barehanded, then a second, then the carefully measured clean water.

Good cloth is something that can survive this for years.

People mend at tables near the drains, where dropped needles can be found. The older apartments have tiled shelves instead of wooden cupboards. Beds stand on narrow glazed feet. There is a dry box in almost every household, sometimes no larger than a bread tin, containing papers, medicine, a photograph, a seed brought from somewhere below.

The seed is not necessarily intended to grow. It may simply be a horse chestnut, dark and smooth, that someone has had since childhood.

They own bright things. Visitors are sometimes surprised by this. Inside the pale apartment blocks are blue bedcovers, orange bowls, patterned curtains kept away from the windows during the strongest light. A woman on the fourth floor has painted her kitchen ceiling a dark, improbable green. She has never seen a forest, and does not claim that this is what one looks like.

Food arrives on the cable railway in sealed crates. Grain, oil, dried fruit, beans, occasional fresh vegetables cushioned so thoroughly that their packing occupies more space than they do. The children compete for the long strips of paper.

Some of the local animals are edible after preparation. Most are not worth the trouble. The broad-headed channel animals concentrate metals in particular organs, and those organs are not in quite the same places throughout the year. There are people who know how to dress them safely. There are also people who refuse to eat anything caught above the escarpment.

These groups marry one another and argue about supper.

***

A large part of the town’s work consists of noticing small differences before they become expensive.

The maintenance crews include cutters, pipe borers, electricians, screen tenders, and people whose task is to walk a fixed route with a ledger. The last are not necessarily senior. A new employee may be given twelve pools to learn and spend a month believing there is nothing to learn about them.

Then a familiar lip is dry.

Or the silver film appears on the wrong side of a channel.

Or the animals are absent.

Absence is difficult to record. The form has a column for water level and another for temperature, but no satisfactory way to distinguish a bank that happens to be empty from a bank that has been abandoned. Experienced walkers write things in the margins. Their handwriting becomes part of the town’s working knowledge.

“Empty, including underneath.”

“Returned before rain.”

“Check after next pressure change.”

The ledger shelves are full of such remarks. Some observations have acquired explanations. Others have merely acquired forty years of repetition.

The best workers do not pretend this is the same thing.

There is a threshold cutter who can tell which apartment he is working outside by the layers in the stone. At one door there is a thin orange band from the year an upstream pipe failed. At another, fragments of blue glaze mark an old step that was crushed and built over. He works kneeling, with water running past his knees, shaving enough away for the door to clear.

Inside, an elderly man waits to see whether it will open.

They have known one another all their lives. The cutter complains that the door should have been replaced ten years ago. The elderly man says it was an excellent door when it was fitted. Neither expects the other to change his position.

When the cutter finishes, he opens and closes it three times. The old man does it once more after he has gone.

***

The pale animals have no use for the distinctions people make between a building and a bank.

To a grazer, the shaded underside of a doorstep is stone with a favorable film. A newly cut drainage groove is a feeding lane. The warm outer casing of a service pipe can support a colony until maintenance shuts the pipe down, at which point the colony begins a migration that may take two days to cross a corridor.

People step over them. Then, if they are still there when someone needs to move a trolley, they are swept onto a tray with a wet brush.

The larger channel animals are less easily relocated. Their bodies are broad but flexible, with an edge of folded skin that seals against the rock. Water passes over the back while the belly remains in a thin pocket of cooler seepage. Lift one badly and that seal breaks with a soft, unpleasant sound.

They grip with all six feet.

A person accustomed to handling them waits for the head to turn, slides a flat blade under one side, and offers a second surface for the feet. Even so, the animal may flush a dull violet beneath its chalky ridges and remain that color for an hour.

Children name them according to injuries, resting places, or apparent habits. Split Foot. Under Four. The one that comes back.

Adults use these names too, particularly when giving instructions.

“Don’t clear that outlet yet. Under Four has young.”

No one is quite certain how long they live. Their size is unreliable, and their outer skin flakes away in patches, taking familiar marks with it. Sometimes an animal thought to have died returns to its old channel. Sometimes it is probably another animal.

There is one beside the west gauge house that has been the same animal for three human generations, according to everyone except the people responsible for the records.

***

The swimmers come nearer the surface at evening.

From above, a large one resembles a length of pale cloth held just below the water. Its eyes are small and directed sideways. Along its jaw lie rows of sensitive pits that close when the mineral load becomes too high. It finds grazers by their scraping and catches them with a brief outward unfolding of the mouth.

In the deep pools, there are swimmers with scars made by no tool used in the town.

The screen tenders occasionally recover a juvenile from a pipe too narrow for its mother to have entered. These are put back like the grazers, though farther from the intakes. Workers lean over the tray to watch the nearly invisible limbs open.

There is always someone who wants to keep one.

For a few weeks it lives in a basin supplied by a carefully adjusted trickle, eating what it is offered, its spine faintly visible through its back. Then the owner wakes one morning to find the basin empty. The animal has squeezed through a gap nobody considered a gap and followed the drain.

Keeping one successfully would require making so much of the plateau that people generally abandon the idea.

***

Quiet here is not a temperament.

People quarrel. Children shout between the apartment blocks to hear how distinctly a friend can answer. Someone plays a wind instrument badly with the shutters open. At celebrations, tables are pushed together and music comes through the porcelain roofs into the wet squares.

But the habit of attending to faint sounds survives underneath other activity. A person telling a story may pause at a click from a service riser, listen, and continue. At dinner, someone steadies a cup and watches its surface before reaching for the food.

Visitors mistake this for anxiety. Usually it is no more anxious than looking both ways before crossing a road.

The young sometimes leave because they are tired of paying attention.

Below the mountains, they discover soil that can be walked on without considering what is moving through it. Water comes from a tap at one temperature and goes away through a hole. Rooms can stand dark and unused for months. They find this restful or vaguely objectionable, depending on how long they have been away.

Those who return bring back unnecessary shoes, recipes requiring fresh leaves, partners who learn to dry their feet thoroughly. For a while they speak loudly indoors. Then their voices settle to the room.

***

Once each year, one group of pumps is stopped for inspection.

The change is felt before it is heard. A tremor disappears from the railings. Water lowers across several shelves, exposing wet bands of stone that will darken and then pale as the day advances.

People come to the walkways. Even those who know exactly what has been shut down find themselves listening.

Along a shallow channel, the broad-headed animals raise themselves on their legs. A few move toward the remaining flow. Others stay where they are, bellies lifted clear of the rock.

Without the pumps, their tapping is easier to feel.

A child puts both palms on the walkway. Her father starts to tell her to stand up, then kneels beside her instead. At first he feels only the uneven pressure of his own hands.

Then something touches the stone from underneath.

It is a short sequence, repeated after a pause. Farther along the bank, another animal answers. The child looks at her father to see whether he felt it too.

He nods, and they keep their hands where they are.
