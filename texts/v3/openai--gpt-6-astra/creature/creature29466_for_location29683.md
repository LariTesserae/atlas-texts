Beneath the intact roof, something has kept a place clear.

Not the whole chamber. Sediment lies thick against the uphill wall, and roots have entered through the joints in the ceiling. One pillar is buried to half its height. But a strip of floor remains exposed between the doorway and a depression at the back, and along this strip the stone has the faint polish of repeated contact.

The depression appears to contain folded leaves.

They are thicker than the leaves outside, brown at their edges and almost black where they overlap. Mineral crystals have settled on the uppermost fold. A root grows across another. Nothing about them suggests that they have arrived recently, or that they will ever leave.

Then, as the garden brightens, a fold draws inward.

The crystals spill. Beneath them is a surface the color of unglazed clay, crossed by shallow grooves. The grooves close in succession, moving a small quantity of water toward the center of the body.

The inhabitant has been awake for some time. Movement is a later decision.

***

Spread upon the floor, it would be wider than the doorway. It passes through by gathering itself lengthwise, drawing some parts beneath others. There is no rigid division between trunk and limb. Six thick extensions emerge from under its mantle, but only four usually bear its weight. The forward pair divide at their ends into short, sensitive branches.

One branch on each can press against the others.

These made the marks in the bowls.

Its underside is pale and crowded with small muscular pads. The pads take hold of stone without enclosing it, release, take hold again. On smooth shale it advances almost imperceptibly. On broken ground it can pull itself a body’s length in a single motion, though afterward it rests with its mantle lifted, water passing through the exposed grooves.

It does not swim. If dislodged, it opens itself wide and waits to meet the mountain again.

The mantle is living tissue, but much of what covers it is not. Old root fibers, plant skin, and a fine brown felt have been pressed into the outer surface. At the margins, the inhabitant continually loosens and rearranges this covering. In the hollow where it rests, the felt has grown into the garden outside. Before it can go out, it must detach itself.

This takes most of the garden’s brightening.

It works carefully around the root crossing its back. The root is young and easily damaged. Rather than tear it free, the inhabitant peels away a small piece of its own covering and leaves that piece behind.

Then it goes to the doorway.

***

It has no face that would remain a face from every angle.

Along its forward edge lie several dark pits, covered by clear tissue. These register the gardens’ light, the passage of a shadow, the difference between an opening and a wall. Finer knowledge comes through contact. Its branching hands taste the floor. The mantle feels the water at many points at once.

A warm thread passing one side of its body tells it which channel is still open. A bitter trace beneath that warmth tells it that a bed farther uphill has begun to decay. Pressure against the lifted edges tells it whether the northern pass is quiet.

It waits at the threshold until these things agree.

The roof above it holds a row of silver beads. They have gathered in the seams between the slabs, trembling whenever the inhabitant moves below them. Over a long interval they join, slide uphill along the stone, and escape through the small opening at the back.

They come from the cultivated floor of the chamber, from the felt on its body, and from certain preparations left in bowls. In the oldest houses, where the vents have crusted shut, a bright, curved pocket sometimes occupies the ceiling. The inhabitant avoids those houses until it has opened their roofs.

It carries no light. When the garden darkens, it continues by touch.

***

Its first task is not feeding.

A stone has shifted in the nearest warm channel. Only slightly: enough to let some of the seepage escape across bare ground. The garden still appears abundant, but one outer bed is receiving colder water.

The inhabitant rests one hand in the channel and the other among the stems. For a long time it remains between them.

Then it prises up the stone.

The effort exposes the soft pleats under its mantle. A thin cloud rises from the channel. It wedges two smaller chips under the stone, tests the flow, removes one chip, and replaces it with another scarcely thicker.

By the time it is satisfied, the luminous seam beneath the nearest leaf has begun to close.

It feeds afterward from the oldest brown mat.

Not by biting. It presses a soft part of its underside against a patch it has cleaned, then remains there while the mat releases a cloudy moisture. Beneath its covering, related growths live in shallow pockets of its body. Those must also be fed: with warm seep water, with the rust-colored matter collected in the last clay bowl, with small amounts of crushed stone.

The pestle is not a weapon or a relic. It is simply far from where it is now needed.

The inhabitant retrieves it on its next journey to that chamber.

***

There are others in the valley, although a traverse from ridge to ridge might reveal none.

One rests inside the two standing walls beside the cleft. Its covering is pale because its seep carries a different mineral. Another has grown around a fallen roof support and can no longer leave without dismantling part of its shelter. It has begun this work. Several stones have been set aside.

Farther west, in a basin that has been cold for a very long time, three occupy a single garden. The grooves there were cut for one. Each time the flow weakens, they move closer together.

They do not often travel to visit one another. A journey requires leaving the body’s outer growth behind, or carrying it into water where it may fail. It requires passing over open stone without warmth or food. There are stretches of the valley where an inhabitant can remain alive but cannot become ready to move again.

Nevertheless, there are paths.

At certain places beside them, a flat stone rests upon two smaller stones. The flat stone can be depressed with a hand. Released, it strikes its supports, sending a brief knock through the bedrock.

The inhabitants use these where the gardens are too far apart for other signals.

A traveler presses once, waits, presses again.

An answer may arrive after the traveler has already settled down to wait. Sometimes it is scarcely distinguishable from a stone cooling or a root taking strain. The listener lays more of its body against the path.

There are signals for a channel stopped, for warmth newly found, for a roof that must be lifted by more than one body.

There is also a sequence repeated without any work following it. An inhabitant sends it from a garden. Another sends it back.

***

The one beneath the intact roof remembers the stair before its latest rebuilding.

Its memory of this is mostly a matter of effort: where a limb once found support, where the body had to narrow, where a warm current touched its underside while it rested. On reaching the altered step, it sometimes extends a hand into the place the old step occupied.

The pale column has moved farther since then.

The inhabitant climbs to it carrying a wedge of shale. It lays one branching hand against the smooth surface. Warmth enters the hand, slight but unmistakable.

The column is alive in a way the inhabitant recognizes without understanding completely. It has a skin that heals around damage. It changes its pressure against the mountain. Sometimes the water beside it carries a taste that persists on the hand long after contact.

What happens above is outside the inhabitant’s experience.

Once, much earlier, it followed a column upward along a cliff until the cliff ended. The column continued. With nothing left to grip but the pale skin, the inhabitant began to climb that.

It returned with two of its dark pits clouded and its outer growth stripped from one side.

Now it feels the column at the stair, finds where the stone has begun to pinch it, and works the shale wedge into a joint. Eventually a block shifts outward. The space is small, but water enters it.

The inhabitant leaves the wedge there.

When the great current comes, the column tightens. The inhabitant flattens itself against a lower step and listens through its whole underside.

The deep notes pass along the crest. Some are familiar. One has changed.

***

There is a young one in the chamber beneath the roof.

It lies attached to the underside of a shelf, where the water is warmest and drifting grit cannot settle on it. It is no larger than one of the clay bowls. Its mantle is translucent. Through it, the first feeding grooves can be seen opening and closing.

For now, it has only four extensions. The two that will become hands are small thickenings along its forward margin.

The older inhabitant touches it whenever it returns.

Sometimes it brings a little brown felt and holds it against the young one until it adheres. Sometimes it clears the shelf above, preventing roots from crowding the narrow space. On this return it brings nothing. It has spent its strength at the stair.

It settles beneath the shelf with one hand raised.

After a while, the young one loosens a pad from the stone and attaches it to that hand.

Outside, the current continues across the upper terraces. Sand hisses against the cliffs. The pale columns sound one after another, and a few grains of mortar fall from between the roof slabs.

The older body adjusts beneath the younger, taking its small weight.

They remain like this while the garden goes dark.
