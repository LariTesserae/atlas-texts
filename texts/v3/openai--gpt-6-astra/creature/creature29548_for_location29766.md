What lives here can first be distinguished from the stone by the way a grain of powder rests against it.

Along the foot of the cream-colored bank, where the cavities open just above the water, powder gathers on every dry ledge. It settles in pits, whitens the lower edges of cracks, makes small slopes beneath the overhangs. But at one cavity there is a smooth place that remains clean. A single grain rests against its margin. As you watch, the margin lifts, the grain passes beneath it, and the surface settles again.

The movement exposes no leg or mouth. It is only a brief allowance made by something soft.

Most of the animal is inside the bank.

What shows is a portion of its back, pale gray under a transparent outer layer. It has the low, folded shape of a cloth pushed into a narrow opening, except that the folds have weight and do not quite touch one another. Water occupies the spaces between them. Its skin is wet without reflecting the sky; the light enters it a little way and stops.

It is difficult to judge its size. The visible part could belong to an animal no larger than a hand. Then, several paces along the bank, another smooth surface makes the same minute adjustment. A third lies deeper in a neighboring hole.

They may be separate animals. For a long time there is no reason to decide otherwise.

Nothing comes out to inspect you. There is no eye at the exposed margin, no exploratory feeler. When your shadow crosses it, the animal does not react. When you speak, the fine water caught between its folds remains untroubled.

But if you put your weight on the slab in front of the bank, a crease closes inside the nearest cavity.

It does not close quickly. The two sides draw together with an exactness that makes the movement easy to miss. At the same time, the thin metallic note from a hollow farther inland loses something of its edge.

Lift your foot and the crease opens again.

The animal has felt you through the rock.

***

In the flooded chambers below, its body is spread rather than coiled. Broad portions lie against the ceilings; narrower portions pass through seams that would scarcely admit two fingers. In some places it is a membrane pressed to stone. Elsewhere it thickens into muscular cords, bundled around the mouths of passages.

There is no central mass that could be called its trunk. Yet it has an underside, and an inside, and parts that cannot substitute for one another.

Along its underside are shallow cups, each with a soft rim and a hard, dark center. These hold it against the stone. They do not all grip at once. A cup releases, shifts less than its own width, and fastens again while the others remain attached. In this way a broad sheet of the animal can move across a chamber ceiling without ever hanging loose in the water.

Its interior is crossed by pale channels. Through the thinner exposed places, these look like scratches on the submerged face of glass. Fluid passes through them in opposite directions, separated by walls too fine to see. Occasionally a faint cloud travels along one channel and disappears into a thicker fold.

The animal has no bones. Some of its supporting parts are mineral, but they occur as small plates, laid closely enough to bear a load and loosely enough to slide. Where the body turns through a tight passage, the plates overlap. Where it spreads against a flat roof, they separate.

A piece lifted out of the water would probably collapse under its own weight. Here, almost every surface has something to lean against.

Its shape is partly its own and partly the shape of the available space.

***

The roar does not deafen it.

Its sensitive surfaces are buried in the attachment cups, against the rock. Each cup receives the underground vibration at a slightly different instant and under a slightly different load. Across the body, these differences form something more useful than a sound.

They tell it where a throat has narrowed. They tell it which chamber contains gas and how far the gas extends. A loose flake, pressed against a passage by water, has a different presence from a flake still joined to the wall. The animal can distinguish them without approaching either.

The continuous bass of the basin is the condition in which it senses everything else.

A footstep interrupts that condition sharply. To the animal, your arrival is not first a figure moving beneath the sky. It is a brief excess of weight, followed by another, crossing a sequence of familiar slabs. Each step changes the pressure between its skin and the ceiling below.

Standing still makes you less conspicuous, but not absent. The stone remains bent under you by an amount neither your eye nor your hand can detect.

The animal accommodates this. A fold empties here; a muscular cord tightens farther down. Water takes a slightly altered route.

It does not know whether you are alive.

That distinction has little use until something enters the water.

***

It feeds on what the warm water brings from below.

Deep in the body, folded tissues hold a dark, fine-grained substance. Under magnification, that substance would resolve into crowded living cells, smaller and simpler than the animal around them. Water passes over them through innumerable narrow spaces. What they take from it is invisible. What they return enters the pale channels of the larger body.

The arrangement does not yield much.

There is enough to maintain the membranes, enough to tighten a cord or move an attachment cup. There is enough to replace the small injuries made when a sharp grain enters beneath the skin. There is little to spare for lifting a body against gravity, or crossing dry ground, or searching.

So the animal occupies the places where nourishment is already passing.

Some portions are very thin because the water there is poor. Other portions, nearer the rising warm flow, are thick and closely folded. Its body records these differences without making them visible from above. The few clean patches along the bank reveal almost nothing of the substantial organ lying under the river.

It also takes particles. The grain that vanished beneath its exposed margin may eventually be discarded, or its useful fraction may be dissolved away. There is no haste in the sorting.

The thumbnail-sized animals under the falls remain untouched. Their fringes work in water that has already passed over parts of the larger creature. Now and then one of them releases something too small to see, and the current carries it into the chambers.

The resident below accepts that too.

***

It can withdraw.

This becomes apparent if a small stone is placed against the smooth patch at the cavity mouth. At first there is only the ordinary yielding of flesh. Then the transparent surface thickens. Fluid arrives from somewhere inside the bank, and the exposed fold begins to roll inward.

The movement continues beyond what the visible body seems capable of supplying. More skin turns under; a pale cord slides across the back of the cavity. For an instant the opening contains water alone.

Farther along the bank, another patch swells.

There is one animal in at least those two holes.

How much farther it continues cannot be learned by looking. Its thinner connections are the color of the water. Some pass beneath slabs too broad to lift. Others enter the dark iron-rich bed where the pressures become considerable and the whine is strongest.

It need not occupy the whole basin. There may be hundreds like it, each confined to a few chambers. Their margins may meet in the deep passages. They may recognize one another by pressure, or simply press together until neither can advance.

No boundary is apparent on the plain.

***

There are injured places on it, though no injury appears recent.

Inside one accessible cavity, a fold ends in a thickened rim instead of continuing across the roof. The rim has sealed. Its little supporting plates lie close together, their edges dark beneath the skin. On the opposite wall is another sealed end, several inches away.

Perhaps a connection was broken there. Perhaps two animals once pulled apart.

The water between the ends is clear. Neither reaches toward the other.

Elsewhere there are small rounded chambers within the body, each containing a compact arrangement of folds. These might be young. They have attachment cups already, minute dark points turned inward against their own skin. But they do not emerge while you watch. The surrounding tissue supplies them through a narrow channel that opens and closes independently of the larger flows.

It is possible that they have been ready for a very long time.

Readiness need not imply that anything will happen next.

***

The animal is not responsible for the plain’s great steadiness. The river would still pass through the porous beds without it. Gas would still sound in the narrow throats. The low islands would keep their dry backs above the water.

But where it lives, the steadiness has become intimate.

A certain pressure holds one fold open. A certain ledge supports a portion too heavy to support itself. A thin sheet of water reaches a sensitive surface at precisely the angle to which that surface has grown. The animal need make only small corrections.

It does make them.

Beneath the bank, one cup lets go and settles again. Fluid travels through a pale channel. A membrane tightens across an opening, then eases until the flow resumes its former thickness.

From above, none of this disturbs the river.

You can sit beside the cavity long enough for the warm stone to become uncomfortable beneath your legs. You can put a hand into the water and feel no current, though water is passing continuously over your fingers.

Inside the bank, the exposed margin slowly conforms to the altered pressure of your presence.

When you leave, it will have that small adjustment to undo.
