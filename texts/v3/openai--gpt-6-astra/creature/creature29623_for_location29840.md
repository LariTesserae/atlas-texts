At first, the animal at the burrow mouth appears to be a piece of earth pushed farther out than the rest.

Its back is the color of the plain. Dust lies deep in the fur, which grows pale at the tips and dark against the skin. The head is lowered between the forefeet. Only the nose moves: a soft, divided pad testing the air beneath the grass, then the air a little higher up.

It waits until a shadow reaches the entrance.

Then it comes out far enough to show that it is larger than the hole made it seem. The body is about the length of a rabbit’s, but lower and heavier, with loose skin over the shoulders and a belly carried close to the ground. Its ears are small, almost hidden. Its tail is short and thick at the base, sparsely haired underneath. The hind feet have broad, tough soles; the forefeet turn slightly outward, ending in three long digging claws and two shorter digits that can hold a seed or pull a thorn from the fur.

It does not stand upright to look around. It lifts its chin, listens, and presses one forefoot against the ground.

Behind it, another nose appears.

These animals live in households, though a household cannot be counted by watching a single entrance. Their burrows have several mouths, sometimes on opposite sides of a mound, sometimes beneath shrubs that seem too far apart to belong to the same system. An old female may sleep under the north side of a tower and emerge at dusk beneath a fallen branch twenty paces away. Half-grown young use narrow exits the adults have ceased to fit through. A passage may be closed for weeks and then reopened from within when a new patch of shade makes it useful.

They favor the skirts of large termite mounds. They seldom dig into the inhabited core. The clay there is hard, and a breach brings defenders and repair workers until the opening is plugged with living bodies and wet earth. Instead, the animals follow the softer boundary between mound and plain, where roots, old chambers, and layers of excavated soil make the digging uneven but rewarding.

Their tunnels curl around roots too thick to sever. They widen abandoned chambers. They leave pillars beneath brittle roofs.

Occasionally they make mistakes. After a wet night, a passage collapses and leaves a shallow trench in the surface. For several evenings afterward the household uses another route while one or two adults work at the obstruction, backing out with earth held between their forelegs and chests.

The fresh collars of soil around their entrances are often reddish. By the following afternoon, pale dust has begun to cover them.

***

They eat slowly when they are safe.

A fallen pod is held against the ground with one hand while the incisors work along its seam. The husk is turned, inspected with the lips, and opened from the other end if necessary. Small seeds disappear at once. Larger ones are carried home in cheek pouches that make the face temporarily broad and grave.

They also eat succulent leaves, soft underground stems, beetle larvae, moths found resting near dawn, and the swollen bases of grasses. At certain plants they bite a narrow strip from a leaf rather than severing it. An old feeding shrub may carry a succession of pale scars, each above the last as the plant grows.

A root is worth more care. The animal uncovers it from one side, scrapes away the skin, and gnaws until moisture darkens its lips. Sometimes it leaves the root attached and pushes loose earth back over the wound. It may return several nights later to feed there again.

Most of their water comes this way. The rest comes in seeds, insects, and the leaves they carry below before sunrise.

Their sleeping chambers lie beneath the reach of the day’s quick heating. The nest material is dry grass stripped into fine lengths, mixed with the softer inner fibers of bark. Damp food is kept in a different chamber; seed stores are higher and closer to the surface. A household that fails to separate these loses its stores to mold after rain.

The deepest rooms smell of fur, chewed vegetation, and clay. Here the animals sleep pressed against one another, noses tucked into the narrow spaces beneath a neighbor’s shoulder. Their breathing is slow. Even the young become still.

***

On the surface, they seem cautious to the point of indecision. Below ground, they are brisk and sometimes irritable.

An animal meeting another in a narrow passage puts its nose against the other’s cheek. Usually one backs into a recess. Sometimes neither does. There is a brief bout of shoving, a muffled squeal, and then one squeezes over the other, rubbing dust from the roof into both their coats.

They recognize one another by scent, but much of their social life is conducted through touch. A returning forager brushes its flank against the animal waiting at the entrance. Youngsters push beneath an adult’s chin. Sleeping companions groom the fur behind one another’s ears, where ticks gather and the short forefeet cannot easily reach.

Their warning signal is made with the hind feet.

One hard blow means that something has changed nearby. Several irregular blows send the animals at the surface toward cover. The sound travels well through packed clay. An individual deep in the burrow may stop chewing before any alarm is audible above ground.

Different households know the familiar impacts from adjoining systems. They do not share sleeping chambers, but they sometimes respond to one another’s warnings. Along an old thoroughfare, alarm can travel from mound to mound faster than the predator that caused it.

A bird landing heavily may interrupt feeding across a considerable patch of country.

***

They have few young at a time.

The newborns are dark, thin-skinned, and scarcely furred, with closed eyes and forefeet already disproportionately large. For their first weeks they remain in the deepest occupied chamber. More than one female may nurse them, and adults without young bring bedding or sleep across the nursery passage.

When they first emerge, their coats are much darker than those of the adults. They have not yet accumulated the fine dust that settles permanently among the hairs.

They follow closely, touching their noses to the hindquarters ahead. At a fork, a youngster may continue straight while the adult turns. It stops almost immediately. The head lifts; the little forefeet spread against the soil. Then it gives a high, faint complaint that carries poorly through the air but is enough to bring back the nearest adult.

Learning the country takes longer than learning to dig.

There are roots that yield food and roots whose sap burns the mouth. There are covered galleries that can be broken for a few termites, and others where the defenders have jaws capable of fastening to an eyelid. A broad opening beneath a mound may be an abandoned burrow, or it may hold a snake that has learned to remain absolutely still.

The young learn many of these distinctions by smelling the mouths and paws of animals returning home.

Others they learn less gently.

An adult with a missing toe is common. So is a torn ear, though there is very little ear to lose.

***

Rain brings them to the entrances even in daylight.

They dislike water falling directly on their faces, but the smell of wet ground holds them there. As soon as the downpour weakens, they begin to work. Drainage grooves are scratched across the uphill sides of burrow mouths. Wet bedding is dragged out. Animals pass one another carrying young, grass, or earth.

For several nights afterward, they travel farther than usual.

Tender plants appear in places that were bare. Dormant bulbs send up shoots. Insects emerge from the soil, and winged termites gather at openings in the towers. The animals feed until their bellies hang low, then carry what they can.

Some of the seeds they bury are never recovered. These open beside tunnels, in spoil heaps, or under the thorn branches where a forager paused to empty an overfilled cheek. A shrub grows; its roots strengthen a roof; its shade makes a new entrance possible.

An older animal will sometimes continue using a route after the shrub that sheltered it has died. Its feet follow the shallow polish between the roots. It pauses at the same junction, beside a stump now loose in the ground, and chooses the same branch of the path.

Eventually another route becomes safer. Grass grows across the old one.

***

At night they can be seen more clearly, though never all at once.

One sits under a low branch turning a seed between its hands. Two move along a mound base, their bodies briefly touching as the path narrows. A youngster scrapes enthusiastically at soil already loosened by an adult. Farther off, something heavier brushes through the scrub, and every visible animal stops.

For a moment the country seems empty again.

Then a nose lowers. The seed turns once more. The youngster resumes digging, more quietly now.

By morning, the household has carried below a small part of the night’s abundance and brought up a small part of the buried plain. There is a new heap beside the entrance. A root has been exposed. One passage is longer than it was.

The last animal home pauses with its hindquarters still in the light. Dust clings to the damp corners of its mouth. It listens behind itself, then folds its forefeet under its chest and slides down into the cool.
