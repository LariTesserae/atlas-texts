At first there seems to be something caught beneath the foot of the mast.

It has the colour of the sealant around the baseplate, a grey with a little yellow in it. One edge lifts in the current and settles again. There is a washer nearby, half buried in shell fragments, and a short piece of black hose. The thing appears to belong with these: another item too heavy to be carried away and too small to have been collected.

Then a line of suckers opens along its underside.

The octopus has put most of itself into a space that does not look large enough to contain it. The mast stands on a raised concrete plinth, secured by eight bolts. Beneath one corner of the plate, a defect in the grout has left a low opening. This leads into a deeper pocket around a levelling screw. Water passes through, but weakly. There is room at the back for the mantle, and enough clearance at the entrance for the beak.

Outside, two arms lie among the shells. Another extends up the shaded side of the plate. The skin over it has raised small, irregular points resembling the growth on the steel. Where the arm crosses a bare patch, those points subside.

Its eye is just inside the opening.

The eye does not take in the whole basin. From this height there is the edge of the plinth, a narrow reach of floor, the lower portions of other structures when the water is clear. Much of what happens above arrives as a change in brightness. Much of what happens farther away reaches it through the material under its arms.

When a pump starts, the mast shivers before the loose shells begin to tremble.

The octopus withdraws the arm from the plate. For several seconds only the tips remain outside, their suckers attached beneath the lip. Then one comes out again. It touches the washer, moves over its rim, explores the hole. The washer has been here for weeks. It is examined nevertheless.

There are crab fragments around the entrance, but not a great heap. The operating current takes away the lighter pieces. A storm clears almost everything. What remains is caught against the upstream bolts: thick claws, the hinges of mussels, a piece of shell with a neat opening through it. The octopus sometimes carries these away. Sometimes it draws them closer and holds them across the entrance, leaving spaces through which water can move.

The black hose is part of this loose barricade. So is the washer.

Neither is food, and neither needs to be mistaken for food to be useful.

When the maintenance lamps come on, the octopus remains under the plate for a while. Small fish begin to gather in the illuminated water above the plinth. Their shadows pass across the entrance, long and distorted. A shrimp emerges from beneath a bolt head and picks at the brown film on the concrete.

An arm reaches toward it without the rest of the animal moving.

The shrimp springs backward. The arm follows a little way, then adheres to the floor. Another arm comes out on the opposite side of the entrance. The mantle narrows, the eye turns, and the octopus flows from beneath the plate.

In the open it is much larger than the opening suggested. Spread loosely over the concrete, it would cover most of a maintenance hatch. It seldom spreads so evenly. Several arms remain gathered beneath it while the others advance, attaching and releasing. Its body travels above a shifting arrangement of supports.

On the rough grout, the suckers find an easy purchase. The smoother concrete is different. An arm laid across a dusted patch sometimes slides before it grips. The octopus lifts that section and places it elsewhere.

It goes first to the downstream side of the plinth, where a shallow fan of sediment has collected. Here its skin darkens into small mottled patches. Papillae rise over the head and mantle. It becomes less like sealant and more like the untidy bottom beside a rock, although there is no rock here.

A young flatfish lifts out of the sediment ahead of it.

The octopus stops.

The flatfish settles a metre away, plainly visible against the pale slab. For a time neither animal moves. Then the octopus takes a different direction, toward the expansion joint.

The joint is useful in several ways. Grit collects there. Small crustaceans shelter beneath the projecting edges. It is also a continuous feature across a surface on which most distances have little to distinguish them. The octopus follows it with an arm tip trailing in the recess.

Its route is not straight. At a cluster of mussels it pauses and opens its arms around the shells. At a bolt socket it inserts a thin tip almost to the bottom. It spends several minutes over a torn strip of mesh that has trapped shell fragments and weed. The mantle stays outside while two arms search beneath it.

A crab runs out.

For a moment the octopus lifts into a loose canopy. The web between its arms falls over the crab and the surrounding floor. The edge closes in several places at once. There is movement underneath, a brief raising of one arm, then stillness.

The animal remains there while a school of fish passes overhead.

This is how it occupies the plain: not as a territory evenly known, but as a collection of places joined by crossings. The grout pocket. The mesh. A socket with a broken fastener in it. The underside of a cable where the current has hollowed out the sediment. There are broad areas between them in which it seldom stops.

Its body can make itself difficult to see on a complicated surface. Here, concealment is less reliable. The slab offers few edges to break its outline. Even when the skin is pale, even when the arms are held flat and the mantle lowered, a dark seam remains where its body meets the concrete.

Under a lamp, that seam throws a shadow.

When a large fish comes out of the blue, the octopus does not always attempt to vanish against the floor. Sometimes it rises, enlarges, darkens. Two arms lift, their undersides turned outward. The sudden change interrupts the fish’s approach.

At other times the octopus runs.

Its movement then is untidy and fast. Arms pull against the bottom; the mantle pitches forward; a pulse of water carries it beyond the reach of its own attachments. It lands, grips, launches again. If the fish follows closely, a dark discharge expands between them.

Ink persists strangely here. In the even flow it stretches into a narrow, lengthening cloud. Its first dense part may remain behind while the octopus travels cross-current, low over the slab. Within moments the black becomes brown, then a faint discolouration in the water. The pumps will eventually take it through.

The octopus returns to the mast by more than one route. Sometimes it approaches from downstream and climbs directly onto the plinth. Sometimes it comes along the joint, stops at the washer, and reaches beneath the plate before bringing its body close.

Once inside, it draws the hose toward the opening.

Only the outermost suckers remain visible. These slowly change their attachments as the flow presses against the hose.

There are other octopuses in the basin. One occupies a length of duct left beside a partition. Another lives beneath a buckled access cover, where the cavity is roomy but the loose metal strikes the frame during storms. Their bodies belong to a coast of cavities, ledges, stones and weed. Here those requirements are met unevenly, often by defects.

A correctly seated cover offers nothing.

An overtightened bolt, a little lifted steel, can make a place habitable.

The octopus under the mast has no need to distinguish between accidental and intended space. The pocket is dark. Water can be moved through it by the mantle. A large fish cannot enter. Small prey passes nearby. These properties remain useful whether the mast is carrying a lamp, a ladder, or a sensor that has ceased to work.

The divers arrive in the middle of the day.

Before they are visible, their boat sends a vibration through the water that differs from the continuous machinery. The octopus draws back. The hose shifts across the entrance, pushed partly aside by an arm, then pulled close again.

A shadow gathers around the plinth.

One diver settles a knee on the floor. The other clips a tool bag to the mast. Their gloves are large pale shapes, moving without the quick adjustments of fish. Bubbles strike the underside of a bracket and collect briefly into a silver patch before escaping up its edge.

The octopus is now deep enough that its eye cannot be seen.

A spanner falls beside the plinth.

The impact travels through the grout pocket. Inside, the arms tighten against the steel and concrete. The diver retrieves the tool, brushing shell fragments away with a glove. The washer rolls a short distance and lies flat. The hose lifts into the disturbed water.

For a moment the entrance is unobstructed.

The diver does not notice it.

The work takes less than an hour. A cable is unclipped, a housing removed, another fitted in its place. When the divers leave, the new cable has a slightly longer loop than the old one. Its lowest point hangs close to the plinth.

Sediment settles over the cleared patch.

The octopus stays inside through the remainder of the afternoon. At evening, an arm appears. It reaches to where the hose had been, searches the floor, then extends farther. The tip encounters the new cable.

Suckers attach one after another along the sheath. The arm follows it upward until the rest of the animal must come out to continue. The octopus climbs a little way up the mast, mantle hanging downward, and investigates the clip.

It does not recover the old arrangement that night. The washer remains out on the slab. The hose has lodged against another bolt. Instead, the octopus brings two mussel shells to the opening, one empty, one occupied. The occupied one is held beneath the body for a long time before it joins the other.

Over the following weeks, fine debris catches in the low cable loop. A frayed strand of rope winds around it. Small shrimp gather in the shelter immediately behind it, where the concrete begins to retain a skin of sediment.

The octopus searches there whenever it emerges.

Its skin has a pale scar near the base of one arm. Another arm is shorter than the rest, tapering abruptly into new growth. Neither feature is visible all the time. Colour passes over the scar; the shortened arm disappears among the others while it feeds. In full view it is an individual animal, damaged and growing. Beneath the plate it is again a little movement among industrial fittings.

Above it, the sensor records the current. The readings change when gates are adjusted, when the wind drives water across the basin, when the pumps alter their load. Along the floor, an arm reaches into that changing flow and holds there.

The suckers turn outward. A particle touches them and passes.

Then something small catches, struggles, and is drawn beneath the steel.
