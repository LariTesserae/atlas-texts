At first you take the pale material in the doorway for part of the animal’s coat, caught on a stone.

Then a hand comes out beneath it and rests on the threshold.

The hand is broad, with short fingers and nails worn almost flush with their beds. There is dirt in the creases, but none under the nails. The skin over the knuckles has the polished look of a root exposed beside a path. It remains there long enough for you to notice a small injury at the base of the thumb: a split with clean edges, neither bleeding nor quite closed.

The person inside is waiting for you to move out of the entrance.

You have been standing between them and the little basin of water.

They reach past your boot, dip two fingers into the depression, and bring them to their mouth. They do this several times. The movement is unhurried but not ceremonial. On the fourth reach their sleeve catches against the doorway. They pull it free with a brief, irritated tug.

Then they look up at you.

Their eyes water in the cold. One lower eyelid is turned slightly outward, exposing a narrow red rim. Their face could belong to someone who has lived a long time outdoors, though the usual signs do not agree: the forehead is almost smooth; the cheeks are deeply folded; the mouth is full and dark, with a small patch of white skin where the lower lip has repeatedly cracked.

They are not surprised to see you. Nor do they seem to have expected you.

They hold their damp fingers toward your face.

If you bend down, they touch the skin beneath your nose. If you draw back, they wait until you have finished drawing back, then return their hand to their own mouth.

Only later do you understand that they were checking whether you were losing water.

***

There are people in the southern shelters.

You cannot establish how many. This is not because they hide, although most withdraw when approached across open ground. It is difficult to be certain you have met someone new.

Their clothes give little help. Everyone wears an outer covering made of narrow pieces of felt, densely overlapped across the shoulders and hanging loose below the hips. The pale outer fibers darken toward the body. The edges have been repaired with thread so fine that you see the repairs chiefly by the puckering they leave.

Under the felt are close-fitting garments with openings wherever skin must meet stone: at the knees, the heels of the hands, and the center of the forehead. The openings have soft, neatly bound margins. They are not holes worn by poverty or neglect.

The people differ most clearly in their hands.

One has a little finger bent permanently across the palm. One has six shallow pits in the thumbnail. One carries a smooth black pebble beneath two fingers, held there through every task, including sleep. When they need both hands, they put it inside their cheek.

They walk with their feet nearly parallel, placing the whole sole down at once. On loose shingle this should be awkward. It is not. They know which stones will accept a foot.

Their passage is visible if you watch them directly. A knee bends; weight transfers; the trailing foot comes forward. Nothing interrupts the movement.

But look away to judge the distance remaining and you may find that you have misunderstood where they were going. The person approaching a shelter is already seated against its rear wall. The person leaving it stands far out beside the stream. You remember the walk without being able to recall the part in which either destination was reached.

They seem to have a similar difficulty with you.

When you cross the basin, someone may continue watching a place where you paused, long after you have moved on.

***

The shelters are not houses in the usual sense.

People enter them to drink, to lie down, and to attend to one another. They leave little inside because most things they possess must be kept against the body.

Cold makes a needle troublesome. A loop of thread stiffens if set on a stone. Even the shallow bowls they use are carried beneath the felt, pressed against the belly. These are made from some dark, flexible substance, perhaps hide. Empty, they fold almost flat.

Food is carried in the mouth.

At first this seems an unpleasant misunderstanding. Then you see one person pass a gray-green morsel from the hollow of their cheek into their hand, divide it with a thumbnail, and offer half to another. The second person tucks it behind their lower lip.

They do not chew continuously. Once in a while the jaw works, a few slow movements, then stops.

Their teeth are small and heavily worn. The front teeth have a groove across them from drawing fibers through the mouth.

They eat the plants at the edge of the rain, but not by tearing leaves from the living cushions. They kneel beside a patch and part its surface with a sliver of bone. Beneath the black-green leaves there are swollen white structures no larger than grains of barley. A person may search several plants before taking one.

Afterward the leaves are smoothed back into place.

There is other life down there. Minute jointed bodies gather along the roots, transparent except for a dark speck near the head. They clasp the fine root hairs in pairs. Disturb one and it folds its legs beneath itself, becoming almost indistinguishable from a bead of moisture.

The people take these too.

They search with great concentration, their faces close to the plants. Occasionally someone finds something they will not eat. It is lifted on the bone point and transferred to another cushion, or tucked into a damp crack. There are distinctions here that hunger has not erased.

***

The pale animals come down from the ridges.

Seen close, they are less like goats than they appeared. Their feet spread widely under weight, exposing a soft, finely ridged pad between the halves of the hoof. The long coat contains almost no loose hair. Each hanging strip has felted around a core of finer wool, and where the strips meet at the shoulder the skin is visible in narrow, pale seams.

Their ears lie within the curve of the horns.

A resting animal presses its face against the ground. The horns keep its nostrils just clear of the gravel.

The people approach them from the side and lay a wrist against the mouth before touching the body. An animal may accept this or turn its head away. If it turns away, the person goes elsewhere.

You see one being milked beside the northern shelf.

The person sits with a bowl held tightly between the knees. There is very little milk. Each pale swelling has to be worked loose with the thumb and caught before it can remain attached to the teat.

The animal stands without restraint, looking toward the rain.

When the milking is finished, the person leans forward and puts their forehead against its flank. For a little while the two bodies rest together. You are close enough to see the coat rise with a breath, and then the felt on the person’s back.

Their breathing is not quite in time.

The milk is given first to someone whose hands tremble. The rest is shared among people you had not noticed waiting.

***

They do speak.

Inside a shelter, facing the back wall, you hear a low exchange between two people lying close together. The words are brief, separated by intervals in which you suppose the conversation has ended.

One says something and moves a foot beneath the other’s leg.

The reply makes the first person smile.

You cannot learn much of their speech. Outdoors they communicate by touching the wrist, the shoulder, the side of the jaw. There is a touch that asks another person to stop, and a different one that asks them to remain.

You mistake these once.

The person who has touched you watches you walk away with an expression you recognize much more readily than any of their gestures.

After that you pay closer attention.

At the listening places, one person sometimes kneels while another stands behind them with both palms laid flat between their shoulders. When the kneeling person rises, they exchange positions. Their faces afterward are tired and ordinarily composed.

Once you find someone kneeling alone.

You wait at a distance, unwilling to intrude, until they reach back without lifting their head and search the air with an open hand.

***

There is a child.

You see the child only after several visits to the shelters, and then everywhere: crouched behind a boulder with a bowl in both hands, tucked beneath an adult’s coat, standing ankle-deep in the water with an expression of offended concentration.

The child is trying to move the leaf.

A thin rod is held under the surface. The leaf bends against it but does not travel. When the rod is withdrawn, the leaf lies as before.

The child notices you watching and pushes harder.

An adult comes down to the bank, feels the child’s feet, and immediately lifts them out. There is a short struggle. The rod is dropped. The adult rubs each foot under their clothing while the child hangs backward, still looking toward the water.

For some reason this is the moment when you begin to fear for them.

Not when you saw the shelters without hearths, or the food being divided, or the split in the first person’s hand. Those things could belong to a life you did not understand.

But the child’s legs lengthen. The feet will outgrow the small felt coverings tied around them. Someone must find more material. Someone must make room under a roof that will not lift.

You notice then the pieces let into every garment. The different widths of binding around the knees. A pair of little shoes opened along the back and joined to larger soles.

These people are not untouched by what happens to them.

They have work to do.

***

Before you leave, the person from the doorway takes your wet hand.

They have noticed you rubbing it against your coat. The persistent droplet has flattened beneath your efforts, but the ridge remains visible inside it, dark and upside down.

They inspect it closely. Then they put their lips to your skin.

You feel a slight pull.

When they release your hand, it is dry.

They sit back against the shelter wall and hold the water in their mouth. Their throat moves once, with difficulty. For the first time you see them look uncomfortable.

You begin to thank them, but you are facing the entrance and cannot hear your own voice.

They touch your wrist.

You stop speaking.

They touch it again, differently, and shift their body along the rear wall, leaving enough space for you to sit beside them.
