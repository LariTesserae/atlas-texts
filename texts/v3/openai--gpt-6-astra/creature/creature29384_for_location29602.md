The first one is easiest to see when the water is falling.

It remains on the exposed lip of the pool after the others have withdrawn: a soft gray tongue, scarcely longer than a grain of rice, lying across the edge of a mineral step. Through its skin you can see two pale threads and a slower movement between them. Its front end is blunt and slightly notched. There are no eyes.

When the water drops another fraction, it folds lengthwise. For a moment it resembles a wet seed caught in a crack. Then it draws itself backward into the stone, leaving a bead of water exactly where it lay.

There is a little opening under every step.

Most are occupied.

Their bodies are longer than the visible parts suggest. Inside the white lip, each animal lies doubled in a narrow chamber, its hind end fastened to the wall by a disk of tough brown tissue. The chambers follow the seams between successive deposits. A broken piece of shelf, held against the light, shows them as fine dark strokes: some straight, some hooked, some branching where a new occupant has settled beside an old one.

They did not excavate these spaces. The stone formed around them.

Along their flanks are fringes so fine they look like damaged skin. These keep water moving over the body and through the chamber. They collect bacteria, loose cells of algae, whatever small nourishment passes out of the beds. Their waste leaves as a thread of nearly transparent gel. Where the gel touches the warm, mineral-heavy water, a pale crust begins.

A single animal makes an untidy collar. Several together make a ledge.

The stairways come later.

At the growing edge, each body reaches out until it touches the current, feeds, and retreats when the water thins beyond what its fringes can move. The next crust preserves that limit. As the pool rises and falls, the limits repeat. There is no animal overseeing the proportions, yet the finished work has landings, little overhangs, channels that divide and join again.

Occasionally one of the steps is crooked. A root has pressed against it, or an animal has died halfway through the building season. Water takes the easier side. A new row of chambers opens there.

From above, the correction looks deliberate.

They are not confined to the clear pool. That is merely where they have become conspicuous. They live under the collars of the tree ferns, inside the warm hollow boulder, along the ceramic ribs beneath the eastern cages. In some places their chambers are no more than a skin of lime over mesh. In others, several generations lie between the living surface and the original construction.

The oldest deposits contain closed rooms.

A few still hold the brown fastening disks. Most are empty, washed clean through openings too small to admit a root. Under pressure, these chambers fill in sequence. When the pumps stop, they release their water slowly, long after the open channels have drained.

This accounts for some of the contrary motion in the central pool. Not all of it. The attendant has measured the delay more than once, kneeling with a ruled strip while the evening valves close. Each time, the figures are different.

She keeps the strips in a drawer below the yellow door.

She also keeps a broken piece of the lip in a shallow dish. It broke when she tried to lift the inspection cover beneath it. For two days the animals remained withdrawn, and she assumed she had killed them. On the third morning, she found their blunt heads extended toward the side of the dish nearest the warm pipe.

Now she turns the dish when she cleans the room, so that the same side continues to face the heat.

They tolerate handling poorly. A fingertip brushing the exposed fringes produces a withdrawal that travels through the whole patch. The nearest animals go first; then those farther away, touched by a change in the water that a human hand cannot feel. The stone seems to tighten. A sheen disappears from it.

Afterward, one usually emerges before the rest.

In the dish it is a large one with a damaged notch, living at the edge of the fracture. Its chamber is partly open. The attendant expected it to abandon the place, but it has thickened the exposed wall instead. Its body now turns sharply around a ceramic splinter she put in the dish to keep the fragment upright.

She can identify it without being certain she is identifying the same animal. Sometimes there are two gray ends in that opening. Sometimes neither appears for a week.

When they reproduce, the young do not immediately settle into stone.

They leave the parental chambers as flat, translucent specks with a dark spot near one end. On bare ceramic they move with a slow, continuous glide. They can cross a film of water too shallow to cover them, carrying a bulge of it over their backs. This makes them difficult to distinguish from ordinary drops until a drop moves uphill.

Most never travel far. They enter the first suitable crevice and remain there.

Others reach the paths. A few are taken down in the drain combs with the litter. The attendant has found their delicate white collars growing on the inside of a slurry coupling, where there is warmth and food but no daylight at all.

The ones that wander toward the outer wall fare less well. A wet track can lead them into the cold before it dries. Their bodies contract there, and the water they carry becomes cloudy. After a hard wind, minute white commas remain on the rail.

Under the third step, where the snail lives, there are almost none. The snail eats the film in which the young travel. Its shell bears three small mineral ridges, however, and beneath the newest ridge are openings. When it rests in the warm runoff, something inside them begins to feed.

The adult animals do not ordinarily leave their chambers, but they can.

Once, when an irrigation mouth blocked, a patch beneath the glossy canes began to dry. The attendant lifted the trough lid and found pale threads stretched from the stone to a wet root. At first she thought they were fungus. Then one shortened, pulling a limp gray body after it. The animal had released its fastening disk. It was crossing several times its own length of open air, suspended in the mucus it was laying down.

Many had already fallen.

She cleared the mouth and lowered the lid. By morning the threads were gone. A week later there were fresh openings around the root.

Where they prosper, roots stay wet and seedlings establish themselves on shelves the builders had expected to remain bare. Where they prosper too much, apertures narrow. One brass mouth has acquired an uneven white beard, and the service arm’s watering nozzle must be soaked and brushed more often than the maintenance schedule requires.

The attendant removes them from valves. She leaves them on the eastern cages.

There the young gather on the undersides of wires, sheltered from the sharp light. Their first collars are almost transparent. Moss grows over them before they have made a continuous crust, and they extend through it, raising their openings a little at a time. Root hairs enter the spaces behind the chambers. Some chambers are crushed; others become sheltered passages through which warm water continues to move.

By evening, the unfinished hills are full of small extensions and withdrawals.

The feed rises. Gray notched ends appear along a wire that was bare a month ago. Their fringes open into the current. Beneath them, a thin shelf has begun to bridge the gap to the next support.

It does not follow the curve of the frame. It bends toward a hanging root, still too far away to touch.
