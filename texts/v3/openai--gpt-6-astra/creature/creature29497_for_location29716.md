In one of the passages where the new water first arrives, something pale lies folded around the foot of a wall.

At rest, it is difficult to say how much of it is alive. There are shells on its back, and old worm tubes, and a sponge the color of diluted milk. Between these rises a low ridge of flesh. The ridge moves occasionally, but so do the sponges elsewhere. Fine sediment has settled into its creases.

Then a narrow part of the animal separates from the floor and reaches around the corner.

It is not quite a head. The mouth is farther back, underneath, and there are no eyes. Its forward edge divides into soft, unequal fingers, some held against the wall and some lifted into the water. One is much longer than the others. It enters a hole, remains there through several slow pulses of current, and withdraws.

The rest of the animal follows.

Unfolded, it is longer than the opening in which it was resting. Its body is flat along the bottom and thick through the middle, with loose margins that can tuck beneath it or spread across the mud. Rows of small gripping feet run along its underside. They attach in succession, hold, release. On firm surfaces this produces an even glide. On sediment it advances less neatly, gathering itself and pushing forward, leaving a shallow, interrupted trough.

The hard pieces on its back are not all things that have settled there. Beneath the shells and tubes are plates of its own, overlapping under the skin. They are thin at the edges and heavy down the middle. Where one has broken, flesh has grown over the fracture without restoring its former shape.

It can shorten until the plates meet. In a narrow recess, with its soft margins pulled in, it presents little to a hunter but a rough, poorly fitting door.

This particular animal has a notch on its left side. Three of the small feet there are missing, and the margin closes crookedly over the gap. When it crosses a smooth face of stone, that side slips a little. It compensates by keeping the opposite feet attached longer.

It has been doing this for years.

Its range includes two courts, the lower portions of several towers, and a broken wall beside a seep. It does not visit all these places on every journey. A favorable current can hold it at one corner for most of a tide. A dead animal lodged overhead can draw it up a tower it otherwise seldom climbs.

What it knows of these places comes in separate kinds of contact.

The fingers find the mouths of tubes and the spaces between them. Short bristles beneath the forward edge register grit, soft mud, living skin. Along its flanks, folds of sensitive tissue respond to water passing over them. An opening ahead is apparent before it can be touched: the current loosens there, or divides, or presses sideways against the body.

The water also carries traces of things that have happened elsewhere. A shell has opened. An animal has been injured. Fresh mud has been disturbed.

Sometimes it stops for a long time at a junction, with half its fingers in one passage and half in another.

It feeds without much visible change of posture. The mouth extends against a surface beneath the forward body, encloses a small patch, and works. Bacterial films, trapped particles, soft growth on the bases of tubes: these disappear under it. The cleaned patch emerges behind, darker and more distinct than the surrounding wall.

Where the food is thin, it keeps moving. Where a scrap of flesh has caught, it settles over it.

Small crustaceans sometimes gather at the edge of such a meal. They reach underneath and retreat. The animal presses down when they become too persistent, but it cannot seal every crease against every surface. A little food escapes. A few of the smallest scavengers remain under its margins and travel with it until it next lifts itself.

Its own back supports animals it cannot reach.

The sponge occupies the oldest central plate. Worms stand along the rear plates, where the body bends least. Their crowns open while it rests and close when it passes beneath an overhang. One small shell near the front has grown at an angle that catches against narrow openings. The animal twists slightly whenever this happens. The shell has a polished patch on its outer lip.

These passengers add weight and resistance, but they also make its outline less distinct to a mouth or feeler searching a wall. A fish testing the rough back encounters much the same textures it would find nearby.

Beneath the side folds are its breathing surfaces. Water passes over them as it moves, and when it rests the folds make small, regular motions of their own. Near the seep those motions quicken.

It grazes the outer bacterial mats but seldom goes far into them. Warmth alone does not turn it back. It responds to the water that comes with the warmth, lifting the front of its body, spreading its fingers, then moving sideways until a cooler stream crosses its flanks.

The richest patches are not always patches it can remain on.

At the turn of the tide, it leaves the broken wall and climbs.

It follows a seam through the builders, placing its feet on firm rims rather than the withdrawing crowns. At the upper edge it spreads its forward fingers across the arriving current. The longest bends downstream. Shorter ones remain stiff, trembling under the small impacts of suspended grit.

For a while it gathers what catches against them and passes it underneath its body.

Then something touches a finger with enough force to make it curl completely.

The animal closes over the touch. Most often it catches nothing. Sometimes there is a larva, or a minute swimmer weakened by its passage into warmer water. Once held beneath the forward edge, these are eaten as readily as the films on the floor.

There is no abrupt change from grazing to hunting. The mouth continues its slow work.

During one part of the season, the animal becomes heavier underneath. Small capsules adhere in sheltered rows between its feet. It moves with the middle of its body slightly raised and spends more time in the passage where the current is steady.

The capsules are clear at first. Later each contains a pale, folded shape.

Water reaches them whenever the side margins lift. The parent cleans the spaces between them with two short appendages usually kept near its mouth. Sediment gathers, is loosened, and falls away.

When the young emerge, they do not resemble the rough-backed thing carrying them. They are soft and nearly transparent, with a few beating fringes. For a short time they collect in the protected water beneath the body. Then the parent moves, or a stronger pulse enters the passage, and they are gone.

Some drift upward. Some enter the feeding crowns on the neighboring wall.

The animal remains in the passage afterward. There is still food there, and the water still moves favorably over its breathing folds.

Much later, as it squeezes beneath an old arch, the projecting shell on its back breaks free.

The animal pauses at the sudden release, contracts, and goes on. A clean oval remains on the plate, bordered by ragged sponge.

Before the pale scar has dulled, a larva settles near its edge. It adheres, loosens, adheres again. The next time the animal rests, the larva adds the first small part of a tube.

For several tides, that part of the settlement travels.
