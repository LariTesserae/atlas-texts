There is an animal people learn to recognize with the outside edge of the foot.

The sole tells you too little. It is thickened by walking, or covered, and the animal is scarcely softer than the stone beneath it. But the skin below the smallest toe can feel the difference. There is a slight yielding, followed by a resistance that increases if you continue to press.

Children are taught to step away when they feel this. Not because the animal is dangerous. Because a foot held there too long may be mistaken for something it should grow around.

In lamplight, it looks like a place where the dust has been wiped away.

***

Its common form is a dark sheet stretched across two or more kinds of mineral. The sheet may be smaller than a hand. It may cover the floor of a shelter, climb the back wall, and disappear into a crack narrower than the edge of a scraper.

Along its margins it has little folds, usually closed. A person who lies beside one for long enough may feel a fold opening against their wrist. Inside is a row of blunt structures, hard as teeth but without cutting edges. These touch the stone one after another.

They are the animal’s feet.

Most never move from the places where they first take hold. New feet form along an advancing margin; old ones loosen far behind it. A sheet can travel the length of a sleeping person in this manner, though nobody would remain lying there long enough to watch the journey completed.

Its upper surface has no recognizable head. Here and there are narrow slits, and beneath them wet tissue can sometimes be glimpsed before the edges draw shut.

The sight disturbs people born far from the stairs.

They know fluid exists inside a body. They have seen injuries and helped at births. But the deliberate exposure of something wet, without distress and without any apparent urgency to cover it, seems an extraordinary expenditure.

The slits are how the animal feeds on things too large to absorb through its underside.

A dead chip-grazer laid across one will gradually settle. At first it seems merely to be sinking into softer ground. Then the slit extends around it, taking the shape of the little body with such exactness that the jointed legs remain visible until almost the end.

Afterward the surface closes.

People who keep these animals near their houses give them the scraps that cannot be softened for eating. The scraps need not be fresh. A piece of dried shelf-animal skin may lie untouched for a long time before a slit develops underneath it.

This is one reason a clean floor is not necessarily an empty floor.

***

They are called hems in several settlements.

The word refers to the turned edge of a sleeping hide, where something flat acquires a little thickness and becomes easier to find by touch. Elsewhere the same animals are named after healed wounds, or after the broad marks left by a vessel that has stood in dust.

Travelers sometimes argue about whether they have seen the same creature.

A hem growing on a wall develops hanging folds. One living beneath a low roof makes short, thick columns between the surfaces. Across a rich mineral boundary it becomes fleshy enough to show the impressions of feet for some time after a person has passed. On poor ground it is thin, hard, and almost impossible to distinguish from a change in the rock.

The smallest forms were once thought to be its young.

Some are. Others are the remains of much larger animals that have withdrawn from unfavorable stone. A patch no wider than a thumbnail may retain tissue older than the settlement beside it.

People avoid using size as a measure of age.

***

A hem takes the faint tension between minerals into its body, as the other grazers do. It differs from them in being able to carry what it gathers some distance away.

Within the sheet are cords of darker tissue. Where a lamp shines through a lifted edge, these can be seen branching and joining again. Parts of the animal resting on barren stone remain alive through their connection to parts that feed.

This allows a hem to cross a passage.

It also allows one to grow through a house.

An old sleeping shelf may have a hem beneath it, connected by a narrow strip behind the wall to a feeding seam outside. A household will sometimes leave a notch for the strip when replacing a broken slab. If the notch is too narrow, the animal thickens there and begins to push the stone aside.

It cannot lift much. It has a very long time in which to lift it.

There are houses with a low step across the doorway that no one intended to build.

***

Other animals live on it.

The pale chip-grazers settle along its edges and put their mouths against the skin. They appear to take something from it without puncturing it. A well-fed hem may have so many of them attached that its outline becomes a pale, uneven stitching.

The long shelf animals lay their young in folds on its upper surface. These folds remain closed during small disturbances and open when the floor is still. If a person walks across them, there is a faint succession of tightenings beneath the foot.

Hunters learn to wait nearby.

A hem can close over a hunter, but seldom does. The effort is considerable, and the hunter’s discarded meals arrive more often than the hunter itself becomes an inconvenience.

This arrangement is not peaceful in every detail. Sometimes a fold closes on the wrong thing. Sometimes the young eat through the tissue sheltering them. A damaged area becomes rigid and is slowly abandoned.

The abandoned tissue is useful.

It can be peeled from the stone in broad pieces, provided one finds the direction in which the old feet release. Pulled the wrong way, it breaks into flakes. Pulled correctly, it comes up with a succession of tiny clicks felt more clearly through the fingers than heard.

People make soles from it.

A good pair has the former underside facing out. The blunt feet grip sharp mineral without catching, and the material wears through gradually rather than splitting.

Nothing is killed to obtain it. This is regarded as convenient, not virtuous.

***

A living hem recognizes repeated pressure.

At first, a person resting on one causes the nearest folds to close. After many visits, the folds remain open. Small grazing animals continue to feed beside the person’s hands.

If the person shifts their weight in an unfamiliar way, the closures begin again.

Households say that the hem knows who sleeps where. There is some evidence for this. There are also cases in which a rolled hide, weighted with stones, has been accepted in place of an absent sleeper.

The distinction matters less to the households than a stranger might expect.

A woman who spent much of her life repairing cord slept on the same shelf beside the same hem. When her fingers stiffened, she moved her work closer to its edge so she could rest her wrists on the yielding surface. In time, two shallow hollows formed there.

After her death, her daughter used the hollows to sort short lengths of cord.

For years the animal continued to close its nearby folds whenever anyone approached, except when the daughter worked with her hands in those places.

The family did not agree about what this meant. They kept using the shelf.

***

Far into the low countries, hems become difficult to measure.

A strip along the floor joins a thickening on the ceiling. A passage that appears to divide two animals turns out to contain connecting tissue beneath its dust. Elsewhere an apparently continuous sheet has separated into independent bodies, the old joining cords lying dry between them.

No one knows how far a single animal can extend.

The people who travel there carry a tool with a broad, rounded end. They use it to lift an edge before putting their weight down. Living tissue resists slowly. Dead tissue comes free, or breaks.

In the lowest passages, there is sometimes no bare stone to lie on. The hem occupies both surfaces, leaving a gap just wide enough for a body.

Its short columns bend as a traveler passes.

Some detach from the roof with a small, intimate release, like lips parting. Others do not yield, and the traveler must go around. Cutting them is possible, but the exposed inner tissue draws animals. In so little room, even harmless animals can become a serious difficulty.

Experienced travelers proceed by pressing with a shoulder, waiting, and pressing again.

The hem does not make room for them. It responds to sustained pressure by changing where it holds.

The distinction is worth remembering on the return journey. The opening made behind a traveler need not remain open.

***

In one of the nearer settlements lives a man who has crossed the low countries often enough that people bring him unfamiliar pieces of shed skin.

Most he can identify by touch. A few he keeps.

One piece is thick, pale, and covered on both sides with the little marks left by feet. He says it came from a place where two hems had grown against each other for so long that each had begun treating the other as ground.

He found no stone within reach there.

When asked how he knew which way to go, he says he kept one hand in the lower current and followed it. He had to stop frequently. The air was difficult to feel, and the animals moved under his palms.

He does not describe this as a place people should avoid. He gives careful directions to the last shelter and advises taking a fresh pair of soles.

There was nothing to hunt in that stretch, he says. Nothing loose enough to carry away.

But when he lay still, small mouths settled against the worn material beneath his feet. One by one, they tested it.

He had to keep moving his toes.
