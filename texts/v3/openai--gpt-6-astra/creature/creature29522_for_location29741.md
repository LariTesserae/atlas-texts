The people have broad hands, though not all of them are large people. The breadth comes partly from how they use them. A hand is a thing to spread against stone, to brace beneath a shell, to lay across another hand in the dark. Their knuckles are heavily creased. Fine white powder remains in the creases even after washing. Around the nails, the skin is thick and often split.

They keep their feet wrapped in several layers of hide, with loose grit shaken out at every safe stopping place. The outer soles are replaceable. The inner ones are personal: softened to the shape of a particular heel, patched until scarcely any of the first material remains. A person finding someone else’s discarded foot wrapping can sometimes recognize its owner without unfolding it.

They are not especially sure-footed by nature. Children fall. Adults put a foot into holes they should have remembered. The old become cautious, then irritated by their own caution. What distinguishes an experienced traveler is less balance than a willingness to stop.

On a crossing, a person may remain still for several minutes, one foot advanced, watching a line of shells no larger than fingertips.

Someone impatient behind them will have to wait.

***

The shell-bearers do not all retreat from rising water for the same reason.

Some cannot withstand the pressure. Some feed best in the thin advancing edge and move ahead of it as grazing animals might follow a growth of new leaves. Others are seeking the little creatures displaced from gravel when water begins to push through from beneath.

The people distinguish these kinds at a glance, even when the shells are filmed over and their ridges worn almost flat. They notice the arrangement of feet, the way an animal turns, whether it leaves a clean track or a disturbed one. A stranger might see every shell in a basin begin to move and conclude that the basin is dangerous. A local person may walk straight through them.

But if a certain small, blunt-backed kind begins to close its chambers, that person turns back.

This animal rarely moves at all. It settles as a juvenile beneath an overhang and grows around the irregularities of the stone. Its shell cannot be lifted whole. The people do not eat it, although they eat many less accessible things. They keep its exposed lip clear of sediment with a strip of flexible bone.

Near each occupied shelter there are usually several such animals, some older than the occupants.

Their silence can empty a house.

***

Inside a shelter, the floor is not entirely covered. Sleeping places have pads of layered skin, and stored food rests in shallow stone dishes, but an oval of bare rock remains near the entrance. People put down their bundles and touch it before doing anything else.

Visitors do this too. It would be foolish to ask whether the crossing behind the house is safe when the answer is already passing through the floor.

It is possible to learn the broad differences quickly. A filling chamber feels unlike a vent drawing air. Learning which chamber is filling takes years. Water alters the passages. A collapse may give an old vibration a new meaning. Older people sometimes disagree, each recognizing something the other insists has not happened for decades.

They settle such disagreements by going outside.

Their language is spoken, and in a lull it is quick, full of shortened words and interruptions. During the roar they use signs close to the body, where mist and distance will not obscure them. At arm’s length, signs become touches. A pressure at the wrist asks someone to stay. Two fingers beneath the palm ask them to lift what they are holding. A flat hand placed firmly against the chest means stop, with none of the qualifications that speech might allow.

There are household variations. Someone who marries into a distant group must learn them, and is teased for mistakes long after making them has ceased to be dangerous.

People whose hearing has diminished often notice the change first during the quiet. They can still work, travel, quarrel, recognize a familiar step through a sleeping pad. What they miss is a voice from another room, or the small sucking noise that tells a shell has loosened from its bed.

Others learn to come into view before speaking to them.

***

Much of the work is done with animals that must not quite be killed yet.

A shell lifted carelessly spills the contents of its pumping chambers into the flesh. Certain grazers become bitter if disturbed before opening. The broad scavengers have to be held in clean water until the dark material has passed from the folds beneath them. People keep these animals in shallow enclosures made of fitted stones, sorting them by what they eat and by what they will eat if left together.

A child is often given charge of a pool containing three or four young shell-bearers.

It is not a game, though the child may make it one. The animals must be turned if they lodge against the outlet. Their water must be exchanged when the tide falls. A dead one has to be removed before the others begin feeding on it.

Children give them names. Adults also give them names, but usually deny that the names mean anything.

There are arguments when one is ready to eat.

The largest edible shells take several people to open. A wedge is worked beneath the rim, then another, while someone keeps a steady pull on a loop passed around the foot. The animal contracts with such force that the wedges sing against the shell. Everyone waits. The danger comes when it tires: a tool can spring free, or a hand follow too far into the opening.

An accomplished worker knows when to stop pulling.

The flesh is dense, pale, and faintly sweet near the attachment scars. The chamber linings are tougher. Those become containers, lashings, repairs. A good shell roof is assembled from the remains of many meals, but not from whatever happened to be eaten. Curvature matters. So does the direction in which a plate will split after years of cold.

A person may remember a particular animal whenever rain runs over a particular place above their bed.

***

The heavy, whiskered hunters visit the shelters, though they are not kept there.

They learn where the brine pits are. They learn which doors have weak lower stones. One may spend several evenings pushing its muzzle beneath a rack, withdrawing whenever someone approaches, then return during the first thunder of a pressure change, when no one can hear tendon lashings part.

They are less clumsy than they look. Their forefeet can hold a shell against the ground while the crushing plates test its rim. When choosing where to bite, they turn their heads with an almost fussy deliberation.

The people recognize individuals by scars, broken whiskers, unequal plates. One animal is tolerated beside a certain house because it breaks the very thick shells whose occupants would otherwise be difficult to reach. The household retrieves fragments after it has fed. Elsewhere, the same animal is driven off because it once caught a child by the calf.

Neither judgment is considered binding on the other household.

Their young have a loose skin fold behind the shoulders. When startled, they flatten themselves into cracks and brace outward, becoming remarkably difficult to extract. For a few seasons they live largely on grazers and small scavengers. Only later do their heads thicken and their crushing surfaces meet properly.

A juvenile trying to open its first large shell can occupy an entire afternoon.

The thin-legged followers wait around it in a loose ring. They dart forward at every promising noise, then retreat with quick, offended movements when nothing has broken.

***

Of the pale swimmers, the people know less.

They know where the animals surface, which narrow passages admit them, and how far water must rise before a broad back can cross a particular sill. They have found their young in enclosed basins after exceptional tides: pale, blunt-ended creatures with soft fringes folded along the throat.

They do not agree about what the adults eat.

Some say they scrape the walls of deep passages. Others have seen one turn beneath a heavy hunter and felt the blow through the shelf on which they stood. The two things need not exclude each other, but arguments persist.

A stranded swimmer is an event that draws people from several miles away. It yields food, roofing, lengths of inner skin, a jaw large enough for an entrance. It also decays rapidly from within.

For days afterward, everyone smells of it.

One household may acquire a new doorframe. Another replaces its carrying straps. A person who could not reach the basin in time receives a share carried back in a sealed skin, because there will be another stranding, or an injury, or a season when the warm seam beside their own home ceases to feed anything.

These obligations travel farther than most people do.

***

The shelters are scattered because their inhabitants need different parts of the water.

Too many collecting from one fissure will strip it. Too many waste pits beside one basin will foul the grazing surfaces beneath its shelves. A good house site therefore includes not only a roof and a safe approach, but places downstream—or what will become downstream at the appropriate pressure—for cleaning flesh and emptying chambers.

Distance does not make the households independent.

A missing marker can be more urgent than a cry. A waiting stone found laid on its side tells the next traveler to take another route and look for whoever set it down. Certain crossings are checked by whichever household reaches them first after the turn.

People visit to exchange food, borrow wedges, inspect a repair, settle an account. They also visit because they are bored with the people under their own roof.

The long noise does not make them solemn. They perform imitations with their hands: a neighbor’s excessive care over a harmless pool, a hunter testing an empty shell, an old person refusing to admit that a favorite passage has changed. Some jokes require a real object and several minutes of preparation.

Laughter is visible even when it cannot be heard.

***

During the quiet intervals, people sometimes continue signing for a little while.

Then someone remembers and speaks.

Voices are startling at first, not loud but exposed. There is suddenly a difference between speaking for everyone in the room and speaking only to the person beside you. Children discover this before they learn to use it tactfully. Adults lower their voices to discuss someone who was safely inaudible a moment earlier.

At night, a parent may sing during the lull. The songs have endings, but are seldom allowed to reach them.

When the first deep vibration returns, a child lying close can still feel the voice through the parent’s ribs. For a little longer the mouth goes on moving.

Outside, the small fixed shells begin to open. Their chamber lips separate by degrees, showing a narrow wetness darker than the water around them. Farther down the shelf, an answering note sounds.

Someone at the entrance puts a palm to the floor, then reaches back and touches the ankle of the person nearest them.

There is work to finish before the basin fills.
