The figure with the tray stops beneath the arcade and sets down its load.

For a while it keeps both hands on the rim. Its shoulders continue to tremble after the tray has become still. Then it straightens, reaches beneath the overlapping plates at its throat, and draws out a small pad of fiber darkened with oil. It wipes the joints of its fingers, one at a time.

There are four fingers on each hand. One opposes the others; another bends backward as readily as forward. This is useful for carrying a tray and making a sign without putting the tray down. Their nails are broad, blunt, and translucent, except on the smallest finger, where the nail grows into a narrow hook. Almost everyone files this hook to a different shape.

The figure tests a finger against its thumb. Satisfied, it picks up the tray.

It is old, though you would not immediately know this. Its face is smooth. Age shows elsewhere: in the cloudy edges of the joints, in the pale repairs along the ribs, in the increasingly elaborate clothes needed to keep the warmth from escaping.

Its garment is a sleeveless coat made from the inner skins of amber fruit. Years of additions have brought the hem almost to its ankles. One patch is a different yellow from all the others. The wearer dislikes this patch and touches it whenever passing a reflective surface, but the material is excellent and there is no reason to replace it.

The coat has nine pockets. None closes in the same way.

In the shallowest is a little figure carved from chalk, missing its head.

***

Unclothed, the inhabitants would look unfinished.

Their bodies are narrow, with long, low-set arms and legs that fold deeply at the hip. Flexible seams cross the abdomen. The chest is protected by several thin plates, overlapping toward the throat; beneath them, something contracts slowly enough to watch.

Their substance is neither flesh nor shell. In the young it has the translucence of warm wax, so that a strong light reveals the darker cords along which the body moves. It thickens with age. Old hands look carved until they open.

They are softest when warm. In a cold draft their movements become careful, then clumsy. Fingers fail to close. A person caught too long near the entrance may have to be carried home on a roof tile and laid beside a warmed wall while someone patiently flexes each joint.

They find this humiliating, even when it happens to someone else.

Water is worse. They know it as the clear rot that sometimes enters the outer mortar. A damp patch is marked with crossed slivers of pale leaf, and no one works nearby alone. They have tools with very long handles for removing contaminated plaster.

Yet they are not fragile in the ways their size suggests. A fall from a balcony often ends with the fallen person lying motionless for a little while, assembling their dignity. Cracks can be sealed. A severed fingertip may be fitted back if it has not cooled.

They are much less capable of recovering from a neglected sorrow.

This is not because their hearts are especially tender. There are simply few distractions, and almost every route through the city eventually passes the same door.

***

Their eyes occupy much of their faces, but remain nearly closed in the glare. What you see are two dark slits beneath projecting brows. Indoors, in the leaf-packed sleeping chambers, these widen into irregular ovals.

They can see the mica trembling before they can see a face across a square.

Their mouths are small and set well back beneath the nose. They eat slowly, pressing slivers of the amber bladders against the roof of the mouth until the skins collapse. Some fruits contain a bitter paste; some a nearly clear oil. The best have a cloudy center that leaves a sensation like coolness along the teeth.

A meal can last through the tending of an entire roof.

They carry food tucked inside the cheek and offer one another tastes on the flat ends of little bone-colored sticks. To refuse is ordinary. To wipe the stick before returning it is insulting.

They also eat certain dusts.

The tray beneath the arcade contains three kinds, separated by low partitions. One is for mortar, one for the gardens, and one is a reddish grit that will be ground finer and mixed into food. The old tray-carrier has been accused of taking the best red grains home.

This accusation is made whenever the subject becomes useful. It has survived two of the people who first made it.

***

They have voices.

In a sleeping cell, with the door shut and a hand resting lightly on the wall, one may hear a dry, intimate clicking. It comes from the throat plates touching. Children do it before they learn signs. Lovers sometimes continue after the meaning has been understood.

Outdoors, speech belongs to the hands, shoulders, and posture of the whole body.

A question begins before the fingers move, in the inclination of the head. A statement made with the feet planted together is understood differently from the same statement made while walking away. There are signs that require contact: a knuckle against the wrist, two fingers beneath an elbow. These can carry quite complicated meanings in a crowded stairwell.

Distance makes them formal. A person on a roof uses broad motions, with clear intervals between them. Across a courtyard, affection must be expressed with the same large, unambiguous gestures used to report a damaged shutter.

This occasionally produces misunderstandings.

One gardener has spent years denying that a certain exchange concerned love. The other has never denied anything, which is generally considered worse.

Their names are movements. The old tray-carrier’s name involves the hooked finger turning beneath an open palm. Nobody now living knows what it originally described. Written on clay, it is represented by three marks resembling a small ladder with a broken side.

There are older names scratched inside doorways.

Some can still be signed. Others require a shape of hand that nobody recognizes.

***

There are eleven inhabitants at present, if the person beyond the sealed western passage is still alive.

Food disappears from the ledge there. Fresh scratches occasionally appear on the clay.

This is sufficient for some of them. Others maintain that roots could move a bowl, and that the scratches have become repetitive. No one proposes breaking through. The person behind the wall, if there is a person, went to considerable trouble to establish a boundary.

Of the ten who can be seen, two share a household, three sleep wherever their work has taken them, and one is a child.

The child is almost the height of an adult’s knee. It has a bright, flexible body and a head that seems too heavy for its neck. Its throat plates have not hardened. When excited, it produces a faint rattling that can be felt through the hand holding it.

Everyone claims not to spoil it.

It owns more things than anyone else.

Among its possessions are six shutters too small for any existing window, a tray of deliberately unsorted grit, and a length of root tied in a knot that three adults have unsuccessfully tried to untie. It visits the abandoned market to make exchanges with people who are not there. For one empty counter it brings food. At another it leaves sharp fragments, arranging them with the points away from the customer.

Nobody remembers teaching it this courtesy.

The child is permitted to approach the central hollow as far as the last rooted terrace. Beyond that, the dust offers little purchase and the wind can take a small body off its feet.

It goes there to throw things.

A flake of fruit skin, released at the right moment, will travel farther than the eye can follow.

***

The adults enter the bare center rarely, tethered in pairs.

There are mica fragments to retrieve, shutter pins, seeds from outside. Once a dark hair arrived and lay across the slope in an immense loose curve. They cut it into useful lengths.

Their harnesses hang beside the entrance to the lowest street. Each has been fitted to a particular body. Two have hung unused for so long that the root beside them has been trained through their shoulder loops.

Crossing is difficult. The light removes the little contrasts by which they judge distance. The roar passes through the limbs. Dust moves against the ankles with the persistence of running water, although they would not make that comparison.

Those who have crossed speak of the relief of reaching the opposite wall.

Sometimes they also speak of something else: the unsettling pleasure of having no doorway close enough to enter, no task within reach of the hand.

They do not have a word for sky. They have a sign for *overhead without shelter*, used when discussing a missing balcony.

It has acquired other uses.

***

Most of their labor is maintenance, but they are not solemn about it.

They cultivate useless plants with great enthusiasm. A particular fern produces tips that coil into rings before opening. These rings serve no purpose and weaken the frond, yet six households keep the variety. People steal cuttings and then pretend the plant appeared by itself.

There are contests involving the mica: brief arrangements that send a patch of light into an inconvenient place at a chosen time. One inhabitant can illuminate the inside of another’s food bowl from four roofs away.

There are fashions in hand staining.

There are old grievances about stairs.

The empty council chamber still matters because nobody agrees whether its benches may be dismantled. Useful wood is trapped beneath the climbing stems, slowly deforming. Those who favor removal become particularly eloquent whenever a household pivot breaks. Those against it point out that the chamber is still sound.

At meetings, they stand outside its door.

Their gestures must be kept small to avoid striking the leaves.

***

They tolerate the ivory animal beneath the mica without understanding it.

Its transparent shield reveals almost nothing. The child believes it can recognize individuals. One gardener believes it is always about to die. The oldest inhabitant remembers it being this size.

They leave the flowering stem beneath its plate uncut.

When it descends, someone usually pauses to watch. Six feet follow one another through a sequence so unlike walking that it remains interesting. Its mouth grazes the pale powder. Behind it, the flowers close.

For a moment there is work taking place that does not belong to any of them.

***

When a foot comes down on the doorstep, they all know.

The changed note reaches them through whatever they are touching. Hands stop. A sleeper opens both eyes. In the western passage, perhaps, someone puts a bowl quietly on the floor.

The child looks toward the entrance.

The adults look up.

They cannot distinguish a visitor from a member of the household above. They have never seen a whole foot. They know weight, duration, the faint rain of loosened chalk. Sometimes several impacts follow one another. Sometimes the pressure remains so long that a shutter must be adjusted.

Then it goes.

Under an arcade, the old tray-carrier brushes dust from the mismatched patch of its coat. On a roof, someone finishes a sign interrupted halfway through.

Beside the doorway where a root has thickened, a gardener sets down the dark glass knife. Cutting would be quicker, but the stem bears good fruit farther along. There is space behind the hinge if the root can be persuaded into it.

The gardener warms the stubborn bend between both palms.

After a while, another pair of hands joins the work.
