The first part of it to become visible is usually a foot.

It comes out from beneath the lowest step and rests on the pale floor, just beyond the line of grit. There are four toes, unequal in length, each ending in a broad, soft pad. The foot is almost the color of the mortar. Only when it presses down can you see the faint pink branching inside it, and the little clearing made around each toe as loose grains move away.

For a while nothing follows. The foot remains there, taking the current.

Then an elbow emerges, higher than seems possible, and the animal draws itself out sideways.

It is no longer than the earthenware bowl is wide. Much of that length belongs to its forelegs, which fold close along its body when it swims. The hind legs are short and set far back. Its belly is flat; its back rises in a low, uneven ridge, with two small hollows behind the head where bubbles sometimes lodge. There is no proper tail, only a narrowing of the body into a blunt point that swings a little before each change of direction.

In bright water it looks white. Under the stair it is the gray of wet clay. Neither color quite belongs to its skin, which is thin enough to let the surroundings enter it. Over the blue tile, a faint blue gathers underneath the ribs.

Its head is broad and low. The eyes sit well apart, almost on the sides, and close from below with cloudy lids. Between them are two short folds that open in quiet water. When the inlet begins knocking, the folds close.

The mouth is on the underside. You see it only when the animal climbs a wall: a soft transverse slit, with a firmer edge at the front. It sets this edge against the glaze and draws itself forward. Its toes spread. Its throat works. Behind it, in the nearly invisible growth on the tile, there is a cleaner track.

There is enough food here, though scarcely enough to look like food.

It browses at the inlet and along the shaded joints, passing over some surfaces several times and refusing others that seem identical. At the green lip of the spout it feeds from the lower edge, where the film is tender and continuously wet. It cannot hold its place directly under the stream. When it ventures too far, the falling water folds one foreleg beneath it and rolls it back into the basin. It rights itself before reaching the floor, lands with all four feet spread, and stays still.

Afterward it approaches from the other side.

Its skin is delicate, but the pads are thick. Each has a slight central depression that seals against a smooth tile. On mortar, where the seal fails, the toes curl and feel for purchase. The chisel marks on the steps are useful to it. It climbs them with a slow, hitching motion, belly dragging, its long forelegs finding the next groove before the hind feet leave the last.

It has not yet learned every edge.

There is a narrow crease across its back from the underside of the lowest step. One toe has a red spot where it caught in an unfinished joint. The injuries are small, and heal quickly in the warm water. Still, on reaching that joint it now lifts the foot high, making an awkward, unnecessary-looking gesture that would be difficult to understand without having seen the accident.

Most of the day it rests in the cooler seam beneath the stair.

There it lies with its head turned toward the inlet and one forefoot extended onto the floor. The water passing over that foot tells it things the enclosed noise does not. A stronger flow bends the loose skin between its toes. A weakening flow lets the skin fall into wrinkles. When someone interrupts the channel above, it lifts its head before the spout has quite stopped pouring.

Silence makes it restless.

While the basin is running, it can sleep through the hollow knock of the pipe, through voices in the courtyard, through feet on the stair directly over its back. But when the wooden plug is fitted into the inlet, it comes out. It crosses the floor, climbs two steps, returns to the bottom. Its head moves from side to side. The little folds between its eyes stand open.

The water is still there. It can swim and breathe and feed. Nevertheless, it goes to each place where water ought to be moving and waits against it.

At the overflow it is cautious. The notch is wide enough to admit its head, and beyond it there is a wet darkness through which the sound travels very clearly. Once, it put both forefeet over the edge. The outgoing sheet pressed its belly against the stone and began drawing the short hind legs after it.

It backed away with its mouth open.

Now it approaches the notch sideways and keeps one foot attached below the rim. It reaches into the two threads of algae there, combing them with its toes. Small things gather among the threads. Sometimes its mouth closes on something too small to see.

Toward evening it crosses the blue tile.

It does not go directly to it. It follows a joint out from under the steps, stops at the swollen lip of a white tile, and moves around the patch of thin glaze. Then it settles with its belly on the blue square and its forelegs laid ahead of it.

The tile is fractionally lower than its neighbors. Water moves differently across it. A little food collects along one edge, and its glaze is smooth in a way the others are not. These may be reasons. But the animal sometimes stays there long after it has finished feeding, eyes half covered, while the stair’s shadow passes over its back.

If someone looks down through the courtyard opening, it does not necessarily move. Looking is not among the disturbances it knows. A shadow falling quickly across the water will send it beneath the step; a face held motionless above it may be watched in return.

What it sees of the face is broken by the surface. The eyes arrive apart from the mouth. Brightness runs through the forehead. It lifts its own head a little, exposing the pale seam of its throat.

The brush frightens it more.

The first time the bundled fibers entered the water, it flattened itself against the floor. When they touched its back, it sprang so violently that it struck the underside of the step. For the rest of that day it stayed beyond reach, gripping the rear wall of its recess.

Since then, the person who cleans the basin has left the lowest step alone.

No arrangement has been made. The brush simply stops short of that corner. The bowl fills and empties. Pale washings spill over the rim. Beneath the step, the animal holds fast while the familiar water becomes cloudy around it.

Later it comes out and works along the newly cleaned surfaces, finding almost nothing.

It is growing. The crease on its back has shifted as the skin stretches. Its forefeet reach farther across the blue tile than they did when it first began resting there. Under the lowest step it used to turn without emerging; now one elbow must come out into the light.

At night, when the courtyard has cooled, it sometimes climbs above the water. It stays close to the damp plaster, testing each dry patch before putting its weight down. Beside the liverwort it pauses. Droplets cling to the low green lobes, and it lowers its mouth carefully between them.

Then it folds its legs beneath itself on the narrow ledge.

From there the basin is not empty. It is an expanse of moving reflections, with warm walls descending into it, a white stair entering from one side, and deep below, a blue square just large enough to lie on.
