At first it is difficult to distinguish from the split timber. There is the same length of grey surface, the same soft ridging, and one end rests in the grit as though it has been rotting there for years. The actual timber lies a little farther from the wall. What lives beside it has placed a hand on its upper edge.

The hand is the first part that becomes clear.

It has four fingers, unequally long, with broad, blunt ends. They lie across the grain rather than along it. A fine seam of pale grit has collected where each finger touches the wood. Nothing about them suggests a grip. They have been resting there long enough for the distinction between holding and touching to have ceased to matter.

Once you have seen the hand, the rest is harder to lose.

It sits with its knees drawn up, although its legs fold in more places than yours would. The joints are small beneath loose skin. Its back occupies the angle between the wall and the lowest ledge, and its head hangs forward, turned slightly toward the entrance. Seated like this, it is no taller than a child. Stretched out, it would be unexpectedly long.

There is very little flesh on it. Yet it does not have the sharp, temporary look of an animal starving. Its thinness is settled. Skin lies in shallow pleats along its sides, with room in them for a body much larger than the one they contain. Around the shoulders those pleats become thick overlapping folds, stained brown in their recesses. In the dimness they resemble the exposed edges of the limestone beds.

It was larger once.

The polished soil beneath the thorn was made by its flank. It used to go out on wet nights, leaning first one shoulder and then the other through the opening, dragging the last length of itself over the entrance stone. There are still a few short, stiff hairs caught deep in the blackthorn, beyond the reach of the wren. They are colourless and hollow. In dry weather they split lengthways.

Now it could pass through without touching either side. It has not done so for a long time.

Its face is neither hidden nor easy to find. The head has a broad, low forehead and no projecting nose. Two nostrils open beneath a fold above the mouth, where rain would not readily enter. The mouth itself is wide, with corners that extend back into the creases of the neck. Closed, it looks like another crease.

Its eyes remain open.

They are small and dark, set far apart, and their surfaces have the dull sheen of wet seeds. They follow changes in the light rather than movement. A cloud clearing beyond the pasture will draw its attention more readily than a person lifting an arm. If you stand between it and the entrance, it raises its head a fraction. When you move aside, the head settles again.

There are no visible ears.

The noise is not an affliction to it. Along its ribs are rows of shallow depressions, each covered by skin finer than that of the rest of its body. These tighten and slacken with the pressure in the chamber. At a heavy surge, a movement passes up its side, so slight that it might be the shadow of a fern moving outside.

It knows the sea through these places. It can distinguish a wave breaking across the hidden passage from one that enters it cleanly. It feels shingle lodged below the fissure, the small obstruction changing the force of every return. A stone that falls underwater at the foot of the bank reaches it as distinctly as a touch.

A person’s voice scarcely reaches it at all.

You could shout until your throat hurt and be, to it, a warm shape making small, unimportant disturbances in the air. But place a palm against the timber and it will know you are there.

That is what the timber was for.

Its upper surface is worn in several places, not all by the same kind of hand. Once, people sat on a bench supported by the three flat stones opposite. They set their feet on the grit and leaned forward to touch the wood. There was no need to speak over the water. A knock travelled well enough. So did a slow pressure, or the scraping of a nail.

It would be too much to call this a language. There were habits. Two touches before putting something down. A hand left flat to announce that someone meant to stay a while. The creature learned the weights of particular hands, and the people learned how long to wait for an answer.

Its answer was usually a movement of one finger.

Sometimes people brought fish. Sometimes a bowl of milk, which it drank without apparent preference. In autumn there were windfallen apples, split so that it could reach the softened flesh. It ate slowly, working food against ridges inside its mouth. Hard pieces were left beside the timber and eventually carried away.

The stone dish was made when someone noticed that it turned its face upward toward the winter drip.

No one who came then is still coming. For a time, their children came, and later a few people who knew only that something ought to be left in the hollow. The visits became awkward. Gifts were placed near the entrance, too far away for it to reach without rising. Someone hung a little bundle from the peg. The cord remains.

It has no clear measure of how long this neglect has lasted. It notices other things: the loss of the bench’s weight from the floor, the increasing softness of the timber, the altered pressure of the passage below. The bank is changing around it. Each winter leaves it a slightly different chamber.

It is not wholly helpless.

When the dish fills, it goes to drink. The movement is laborious but exact. Its fingers lift separately from the wood. It unfolds one leg, places the foot flat, and waits before transferring its weight. The soles are broad and almost white. It walks along the wall, where the grit is thin, leaving the settled fans in the middle of the floor untouched.

At the basin it lowers its mouth to the water. If a leaf lies across the surface, it moves the leaf aside and sets it on the stone.

Then it returns.

There are things it eats in the darkness that would not seem worth eating: a pale root newly exposed in a seam, the soft body of a moth, a little mineral-tasting growth from the damp face of the rock. Very occasionally the sea forces something living high enough into the fissure. It knows immediately when that happens.

Mostly, it spends what remains of the substance it brought inland.

Under the loose folds, its body is becoming smaller. One day the hand will no longer reach the timber from its accustomed seat. Before then, it will have to move closer.

For now the fingers remain where they are.

If you touch the wood, it may take some time to respond. The sea must finish a certain movement beneath the bank; pressure must leave the chamber and return. Then the longest finger lifts, exposing a clean patch underneath.

It comes down once, lightly.

After that it waits, with its head raised just enough to show you where its face is.
