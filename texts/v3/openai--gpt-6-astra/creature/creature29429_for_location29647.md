At a doorway, a resident can be mistaken for one more animal waiting at a warm seam.

The coats contribute to this. They are long, swollen garments, darkest where they rub against walls, with overlapping flaps down the back that shed water when the wearer stoops. Their fastenings are inside. A person coming home reaches beneath the coat and works for some time, apparently scratching their own ribs, before the front opens and releases the smell of damp wool, skin, and the sharp paste used to protect the seams.

The face is the last part uncovered.

There are ordinary human faces inside: narrow or broad, young, badly shaved, troubled by a tooth. In the sheltered entrance a woman closes her mouth carefully over the cold she has brought in. She has a pale scar across her upper lip and another, newer one at the base of her thumb. Her name is Ilen. She maintains the openings in growing walls.

Before going upstairs, she rests her bag on the floor and waits.

Something has lodged itself beneath the bottom step. She can feel its call through the bag’s stiff handles. She taps twice with her heel, then presses the side of her boot against the animal until it begins the labor of gathering its limbs. A smaller creature comes out first. Then another. Eventually the large one unfolds sufficiently to reveal that it has been lying across, rather than inside, the passage.

Ilen knows this animal. A healed split in its head makes one set of brushes point upward. It has occupied the entrance for nine winters. She dislikes it with the particularity of a neighbor.

She waits until the last foot is clear.

***

Her household occupies three rooms on different levels. They were once four rooms on one level, but the building is accepting a rise in the underlying stone, and the room where she slept as a child now slopes too steeply for an unsecured bed.

That room holds the household’s water jars. Each hangs in a cradle whose pivot keeps its mouth upright. When anyone walks above them, the jars touch with a delicate, irregular knocking.

The household consists of Ilen, her mother, her adult son, and a man named Or who was her mother’s husband for eleven years and has remained her housemate for seventeen more. Or sleeps nearest the warm wall because his lungs are bad. This arrangement is a source of enduring resentment to Ilen’s mother, who wanted the marriage ended and did not intend that ending it should cost her the best bed.

They eat together most evenings.

Food arrives in sealed lengths of soft casing. Some is grown in the deep galleries, where warm seepage passes through beds of folded mesh. Some is made from the small swimming animals gathered before the water enters the household channels. There are dense cultivated curds, slippery strips that hold salt in their centers, and a bitter, dark fat that keeps well enough to be inherited.

Ilen shaves this fat over a hot bowl with a tool that belonged to her grandfather. Her mother tells her the slices are too thick.

They have a lamp at the table, enclosed in cloudy amber glass. It is bright enough to show steam and the moving edge of a spoon. Faces remain incomplete around it. To inspect something closely, a person brings it toward the light, and everyone else makes room.

Her son once brought home a piece of red cloth.

For several evenings it lay beside the lamp. The family turned it, wetted a corner, dried it, tried it against the different shades of their hands. Ilen’s mother remembered seeing the same color on a visitor’s case when she was very young. Or said it had been orange.

Eventually the cloth became the lining of a small bag. Her son keeps it turned inward. When he puts his hand inside, he knows what is there.

***

Children learn the routes through the city as sequences of contact.

A ribbed wall. Three smooth panels. A drain that speaks rapidly. The low brass bar that must be ducked beneath because the stair beyond it has not yet been lowered.

For the first years, they travel with a hand tucked into an adult’s coat strap. Later they go in strings, each gripping the preceding child, while the eldest tests the way ahead. Their mittens have hard little studs in the palms. On a familiar route the studs make a traveling chatter, interrupted by giggling whenever someone deliberately misses the wall.

Getting lost is not always a matter of taking a wrong turn. A familiar surface can be hidden by animals. A repaired pipe can change the sound of an entire passage. The neighborhood note may carry strangely when a reservoir freezes through.

Every child is taught what to do then: crouch with the back against something fixed, put both hands on the ground, and strike the pavement in groups of five.

Adults answer with four.

Sometimes the first answer comes from an animal. The children are taught to wait for the right number.

There are bright rooms in the schools, though the lamps are used sparingly. Children learn to recognize writing by sight as well as by touch. They draw the animals from specimens brought inside in covered tubs, making confident errors about the shapes of things they have handled all their lives.

In one school picture, a brush-faced animal has seventeen legs. The child who drew it explains that there were seventeen legs where the animal was.

The teacher keeps the picture.

***

Ilen’s work requires bare hands.

She carries three pairs of gloves and changes them whenever damp reaches the inner layer, but at the opening itself she must take them off. The field that prevents a wall from closing is felt most clearly at the web between finger and thumb. A healthy one has an even resistance, like pushing through dense, still water. A failing one catches.

She works her hand slowly around a window’s perimeter, finding the places where the resistance has become granular. Behind her, the household waits with its shutters closed.

Small faults can be corrected by changing the spacing of the teeth below. Larger ones require a strip of the new stone to be cut away, exposing its wet interior. These repairs smell strongly enough to bring animals out of ducts two buildings away. Her apprentice stands behind her with a broad padded board, steadily pushing the accumulating bodies back.

Most of the apprentice’s work is this. He had imagined instruments.

There are instruments. They live in a narrow case strapped across Ilen’s back, warmed by her body. Some measure conductivity, pressure, the speed at which dissolved material is being laid down. One makes a small sound that changes according to conditions inside the wall.

The apprentice can hear changes that Ilen no longer can. She asks him to repeat them against her wrist with a fingertip. He does it without looking pleased now, which she appreciates.

Households become attached to particular wall workers. A poorly kept opening may shrink by no more than a nail’s breadth in a year, but people notice. They touch their doorframes more often than they touch their own faces.

Ilen is frequently asked to keep a doorway where it can no longer remain.

A woman wants the entrance to her dead sister’s room preserved. A shopkeeper has paid for a counter that will be useless if the passage moves. Two adjoining households have quarreled for so long that neither will accept the wider route through the other’s landing.

Ilen can sometimes give them another season. She tells them exactly what the season will cost.

Then she comes home and finds her mother has moved her chair back into the room they are losing.

***

The city’s inhabitants argue about warmth with the seriousness other places reserve for land.

Warmth can be diverted, borrowed, withheld by an upstream alteration. A new bath may lower the temperature of three neighboring walls. The animals move, and a once-quiet stair becomes impassable, or a feeder colony abandons a bridge and the small biting things begin appearing in people’s bedding.

Disputes are heard by people who visit the site and leave their hands on it for a long time.

Ilen’s son works on a proposed diversion near the central basin. He is proud of it. The new flow would warm a public sleeping gallery and keep a heavily used ramp free of ice. It would also cool the underside of a bridge where the animals give birth.

At dinner he explains that they will move to the new outlet.

His grandmother asks how he knows.

He describes the roughened stone, the sheltered approach, the small channels intended to keep the youngest from washing away.

She asks again.

He leaves before the bowls are empty. Later Ilen finds him at the entrance, crouched beside the split-headed animal. He has warmed a measuring strip in his palm and is trying to slide it under the creature without waking it.

For weeks afterward he comes home smelling of its bitter skin.

The proposal changes.

***

There is affection in the city that takes forms a visitor might fail to recognize.

Someone goes ahead to loosen a frozen handrail before the person with damaged fingers wakes. Someone keeps a particular landing free of animals because an old neighbor will not admit that stepping over them has become difficult. A repaired coat appears on a hook with no message, its shoulder made broader to accommodate a swelling the owner has tried to conceal.

Lovers learn each other’s footfalls and then discover how many strangers walk almost the same way.

In the crowded passages they greet one another by putting a hand beneath a coat flap, against the waist where a little warmth can be stolen. They find private places by listening for water loud enough to cover their voices. An unused pressure chamber may become fashionable for a month, until too many people know about it, or until the water returns.

People sing, though rarely outdoors. Sustained low notes attract answers from the walls and soon make conversation impossible. The popular songs are quick, intricate things full of stops. At gatherings a singer may pause for the building’s hum to pass through a phrase, then resume just ahead of it. A good performer can make the animals seem late.

Or was once good at this.

On evenings when his breathing permits it, he still sings from his bed. Ilen’s mother corrects the words through the partition. Sometimes she corrects a verse he has not reached yet.

***

When the basin rises, the city wakes unevenly.

Those sleeping against the outer walls feel it first. Then the pressure workers receive their signals. Doors are opened along the lowest occupied routes, and people begin moving the things that cannot be allowed to soak: bedding, powdered cultures, old papers enclosed between sheets of waxed fabric.

Ilen goes to the central district with her tools.

Her son is already there. She recognizes him by the way he stands with one knee bent, though she cannot see his face. He is helping carry a sleeping man whose bed has been designed for this purpose, with handles beneath the frame.

For a while the sound from the mountain makes speech useless.

Ilen checks the public openings by touch. The fields tremble against her hands. Behind one wall the liquid moves with enough force to draw a glove from where she has tucked it into her belt. She catches it against her leg.

All around the basin, people are waiting for the release and the new engagement of the mineral teeth. They stand in doorways, on steps, in the shallow water spreading over the plaza. Some hold measuring rods. Some hold children. One woman has brought a bowl of dough against her chest because it must not be allowed to cool.

There is a deep jolt.

Then another, much smaller.

A man laughs too soon and is told to be quiet.

By morning the fresh strip of stone is exposed. Ilen kneels to examine it while the first animals push past her boots toward the warmth. Its edge is sharp enough to shave skin. She lays a protective length of rough cord along the section beside the public steps.

Her son crouches next to her. They can talk normally now, but for a time neither does.

Across the basin, shutters are opening. A breakfast vessel rings against a sill. Someone begins striking ice from a hinge, stops to let a sleeper protest, and starts again more softly.

Ilen takes her son’s hand and places his fingertips beneath the edge of the new stone. There is a fault there, too small to see in the amber light: a discontinuity in the mineral, with warm water pressing through it.

He feels along it, finding its extent.

Behind them the city is returning to its morning routes. People divert around the two workers kneeling at the rim. A child asks what they are doing, and the adult holding her strap says they are finding out.

Under their hands, the water continues to arrive.
