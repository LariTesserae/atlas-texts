What lives here does not often cross the open courts. It travels around them, inside the thickness of their walls.

At intervals, where a facing brick has fallen out, something pauses behind the opening. There is a wet nose, the trembling ends of whiskers, sometimes the reflection of one eye. Then the opening is empty. A little dust sifts from a different joint farther along.

There are many of them, but their passages conceal their number. One may be heard worrying at a snail in a drain while another is already crossing a roof above it. A visitor takes these noises for the movements of a single restless animal. In fact, several territories overlap vertically. A warm cavity beneath the upper paving belongs to one female and her young; the cooler passage underneath is used by two males; below that, where the wall meets a buried tank, almost all of them come to drink.

They are about the length of a forearm before the tail begins. In an open space they look unexpectedly low, with narrow shoulders and a long middle carried close to the ground. Their fur lies in coarse, dark rows when wet. Dry, it lifts enough to make them seem larger, especially around the hips. Brick dust works into it and remains there. The oldest animals are reddish along the flanks, almost the color of the surfaces against which they squeeze.

The tail is thick at its base and sparsely furred underneath. It drags when they are unhurried. On a narrow wall it rises and curves, making small corrections independently of the head. An animal reaching down toward water will hook its hind feet over a sound edge and press the underside of its tail against the brick behind it. The bare skin there is ridged and usually dirty.

Their faces are neither blunt nor finely pointed. The muzzle is broad enough to accommodate strong back teeth, but the nose projects, soft and mobile, beyond the mouth. When feeding, they repeatedly wrinkle it away from whatever their teeth are doing. Their ears are small, rounded, and often scarred at the margins. The ears fold almost flat when the animal enters a tight passage, then spring forward once it emerges.

Their forefeet are the most delicate part of them. Each toe ends in a short curved claw, but they use the pads as much as the claws, feeling along an edge before transferring their weight. At a distance this gives them an air of hesitation. Close by, the movement is quick and assured: touch, press, step. On familiar routes they scarcely slow down.

They eat figs, beetles, soft shoots, fallen eggs, and whatever small bodies the water leaves in the overflow mouths. They turn snails against the ground until they find the lip of the shell. A large snail may occupy one for a considerable time. It braces the shell between its forefeet and works with a concentrated, peevish persistence, stopping now and then to lick slime from its nose.

Certain feeding ledges accumulate shells. The fragments are pushed outward by each new arrival until they spill down the wall. Beneath one such place, a court that receives little rain has a pale crescent of broken shell several fingers deep.

They are not particularly clean animals, but they are particular about where they sleep. A nest chamber must be above the highest stain left by water, close to an exit, and narrow enough that a larger creature cannot turn around inside it. They bring in dry leaves one mouthful at a time. Leaves too wide for the entrance are dragged backward, crushed, or abandoned. Outside a favored nesting hole there is often a small obstruction of failed bedding.

During the day they lie pressed together in these chambers, their tails over their noses. Those sleeping alone curl tightly. A group relaxes into shapes that seem boneless: a head under someone else’s belly, a hind leg resting across a neck. They wake intermittently to scratch, shift, or bite a neighbor who has become uncomfortable.

At night their sociability is less generous. A ripe fruit is defended. A narrow passage may be blocked by an animal unwilling to retreat even when retreat would take only a few steps. The resulting quarrel consists of low, breathy complaints, followed by a sudden scuffle that shakes powder out of the mortar. Usually one backs away. Occasionally both become so intent on facing each other that a third slips between them and takes the food.

Their ordinary contact call is a soft double click made at the back of the mouth. It carries poorly across a court but travels well along a wall cavity. Young animals answer more often than adults. When a family is moving through the masonry, the clicks pass beneath the louder insect noise, stopping wherever the passage opens and resuming on the other side.

The young have pale toes and fur that has not yet acquired the red dust. For their first excursions they follow the scent of the adult ahead, nose nearly against the base of its tail. They climb readily and descend badly. At a drop, they turn, reconsider, and sometimes try to lower all four feet at once.

An adult does not usually retrieve a hesitant youngster. It waits below, looking elsewhere, until the youngster finds a way down. But at the mouths of deep tanks the females become abrupt. They shoulder the young away from the edge, occasionally taking an ear or the loose skin of a cheek between their teeth. There are drinking places the young are not permitted to approach until they can brace themselves properly.

They know only small portions of the settlement.

An old female may have used the same six courts for most of her life. She knows which gutter contains beetles after rain, which fig ripens first, and where a vault can be crossed on its thick outer haunch rather than its thin crown. Beyond that circuit are other animals’ smells, unfamiliar gaps, and stretches of paving she has never tested. Given a choice, she turns back.

This makes a collapse more than a disturbance. It can sever the connection between a sleeping chamber and the only safe descent its occupants know.

For several nights afterward, they revisit the broken route. They stand on the surviving edge, sniff downward, and withdraw. Some try the approach repeatedly in a single night. Dust gets into their noses. Loose pieces fall under their feet. Eventually one ventures along a root or through a newly exposed room, and the others begin using its path. The former route retains their smell for a while, though it no longer leads anywhere.

Near dawn, a female comes out beneath the lip of an upper terrace with half a fig in her mouth. Two young follow. One stops to investigate an ant trail and receives several bites on the nose. It sneezes, rubs its face against the paving, and hurries after the others.

The female passes a broad stair without taking it. She enters a split between a buttress and the wall it once supported. The first youngster follows easily. The second is still carrying a dry leaf it picked up somewhere below. The leaf catches across the entrance.

For a moment the youngster pulls hard enough to shake its whole body. Then it releases the stem, turns around in the gap, and reaches out with one forefoot. It hooks the leaf by its edge, folds it against the brick, and draws it inside.

The wall gives a few small clicks. After that, only the ants remain visible.
