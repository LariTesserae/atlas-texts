What lives here is spread too thinly to cast a shadow.

Most of it occupies the olive stain in the shallower grooves. Some lives on the lower portions of the charophyte stems, below the pale crust that makes them stiff. A little extends onto apparently bare limestone, where it would take a fingertip drawn across the surface to discover that the stone is not quite as clean as it looks.

There is no fingertip here. The surface remains undisturbed.

It is a living film, but that name makes it sound more continuous than it is. At its edges it breaks into small colonies, then into scattered cells separated by distances that matter to them. A shallow groove that looks evenly olive from above contains crowded districts, empty patches, older material, new growth. There are places where the film is thick enough to retain what passes through the water, and places where it has scarcely established itself at all.

Its substance is mostly water held in a delicate material of its own making. This gives it a slight purchase on the limestone. It also keeps its inhabitants close to one another. What one cell releases need not immediately disappear into the lake; another may use it first.

The film is not one organism. Some of its cells take energy from daylight. Others live on the small supply of organic matter that reaches them, or on what their neighbours shed. There are long, narrow forms and rounded ones, cells that occur singly and cells that remain attached after dividing. Their lives overlap without becoming identical. One portion can be growing while another is being consumed from within.

At the scale from which the hollow can be seen whole, all this amounts to an uncertain colour.

It has no use for most of the room.

The central bed of silt is not a space it is waiting to cross. The recesses do not offer it refuge merely because they resemble shelters. Light, surface, and the arrival of usable substances determine its extent more closely than the shape of the basin does. A few centimetres up the wall may be a more consequential journey than the whole distance between the three loose stones.

Along one groove, the film ends where the light becomes insufficient. The edge is irregular. A small projection of limestone receives enough to support a few more cells; a hollow beside it does not. Elsewhere, a faint trace continues beyond the visible green, occupied by lives that do not need light in the same way.

There is no decision at the boundary. Over time, the boundary nevertheless becomes exact.

The cold slows everything. Material passes into cells, is altered, passes out again. Damaged parts are replaced. A cell lengthens. Much later, there are two where there was one. In another patch, a cell fails, and its contents become briefly available to the surrounding life.

The film contains these small deaths without acquiring a wound.

The pumping station reaches it through the surface to which it clings. The deeper hum and the finer vibration are present at its attachment points, in the mineral immediately beneath its soft material. There is no organ listening to them. They supply neither food nor instruction. Yet the film is physically subject to them, just as it is subject to cold and pressure: its yielding substance rests against stone that is never entirely still.

The note does not gather the scattered colonies into a creature. It passes through their differences.

If the motors stopped, the limestone would cease carrying that particular tremor. The film would remain attached. Its exchanges would continue. Nothing would lift a head or turn toward the headland.

Its responsiveness lies elsewhere. A change in available light alters what some cells can produce. A trace of nutrient arriving in the water makes possible a little growth. Substances accumulate within the film and affect neighbouring cells before they diffuse away. Its surroundings enter it in small, selective ways.

The slow seepage matters without becoming a current. Water reaches the lower joints and is gradually replaced. Dissolved things move where settled grains do not. Across the apparently motionless boundary between water and growth, there is enough passage to sustain a sparse existence.

Sparse is important. This is not a rich, hidden garden concealed by the limits of human vision. Magnification would disclose activity, but also considerable separation, meagre supplies, and long intervals in which little happens. Much of the limestone really is bare. The deeper recesses remain unoccupied by the olive growth. The film has not secretly filled the hollow.

It persists in the favourable inches.

Over weeks, an edge may advance a little. Another may lose its colour. A fragment too small to notice can detach and leave the basin, while elsewhere a newly arrived cell finds a place to adhere. The resident changes its membership without visibly changing its shape.

From above, it continues to resemble a property of the rock: an olive cast in a groove, a thin brown margin below a tuft of mineral-stiffened stems.

Closer, it has thickness. Closer still, it has neighbours.

And at the closest scale, where a single cell holds to the limestone through the cold, there is no large room, no wooded headland, no silver-grey expanse overhead. There is a surface, a changing supply, light sufficient for certain work. The cell maintains its boundary. Water and dissolved substances pass across it.

Against the immense, faintly trembling stone, it continues to make a little more of itself.
