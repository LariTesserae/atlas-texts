It lives mostly beneath the floor.

Not deep beneath it. There is little depth to be had in much of the basin: a hand’s breadth of roots, a soft accumulation of dead stems, then water with suspended brown particles that turn slowly when disturbed. It lives in that interval, with its back against the underside of the vegetation and its feet resting, where they can, on the older bottom.

From above, its passage is a slight traveling fullness. A pool rises against its edges. Several rushes lean together and then separate. On ground so willing to move under its own weight, these are not persuasive signs of an animal.

You might stand a few yards from one for an hour.

Then a portion of the moss would open its eyes.

They are small eyes, widely spaced, set on raised places toward the front of a broad, blunt head. In strong light the pupils close to crooked seams. The skin around them is bare and dark, but the rest of the head carries a short, dense coat, brown at the roots and almost green at the tips. Algae grow there. So do a few actual moss stems, caught and retained in the coarser hair along the shoulders. These are incidental passengers, not camouflage the animal has arranged, although old individuals can carry quite a garden.

An adult is about the length of the rubber gloves hanging in the hut window, laid wrist to fingertip twice over. Its breadth makes it seem larger. There is no neck worth mentioning. The body spreads behind the head in a low oval, and when it leaves the water it must gather itself considerably before anything resembling legs becomes apparent.

Its feet are its most elaborate parts.

Each has long, jointed toes joined near their bases by soft webbing. Underwater, the toes close into a paddle. On the meadow they open individually, feeling for stems, spreading the animal’s weight over an area much wider than its body. A foot put down on doubtful ground does not immediately take weight. It searches. Two toes may find a root braid while the others reach across an opening and hook into something firmer.

Watching it walk, you understand how badly a boot fits this place.

It seldom goes far above the surface. Sun dries the exposed skin around its nostrils, and the open air offers little concealment. But in the late afternoon it sometimes climbs onto a firm cushion and lies with its feet tucked beneath it, letting warmth enter through its back. Water drains from the coat in little clear threads. As it dries, its apparent green diminishes. The fur lifts, and underneath the meadow-coloured exterior is an animal the colour of wet chestnut wood.

Its mouth is underneath the head. There is a soft, divided upper lip and, behind it, two pale cutting edges that meet with surprising precision.

It eats in the suspended tangle.

The newest roots are bitten through, but selectively; it leaves the tough runners that carry the mat’s weight. It strips the tender bases from sedges, scrapes films from submerged stems, takes larvae and small swimming things when they come within reach. Much of its feeding consists of drawing a stem slowly through its mouth. Afterward, the cleaned length slips away behind it.

Its feeding chambers become little rooms beneath the meadow. The roof is living and the floor is loose. Water passes through them, sometimes more readily than before, and a channel that appears to have chosen its own course may be following several generations of these excavations.

It does not intend to make waterways. Occasionally it makes a bad one. A chamber collapses, a cushion tilts, and a patch of green becomes a pool overnight. The animal leaves by another passage and returns later to eat at the newly submerged edge.

It knows the basin through contact.

There are stiff hairs beneath its chin, along its flanks, and on the backs of its wrists. Pressed against roots, these register movement at a distance: a bird landing, a companion pulling at a stem, the dull alternating shocks of someone walking from the track toward the intake. The animal pauses well before a person comes into view. Its feet spread. Its belly lowers. Water closes over its eyes.

The roar does not trouble it in the way it troubles a visitor.

It has grown up inside that sound. The overflow is present in the water, the peat, the hollow stems, the bones of its jaw. Most of the time it has no reason to attend to it. But a change in the machinery reaches it everywhere.

When something catches on the trash rack, animals resting beneath the far side of the basin lift their heads.

When a sluice is adjusted, they move toward the thicker mats.

And when the works stop, which happens rarely, the meadow becomes unexpectedly active. Heads appear in pools. Narrow wakes cross stretches of open water. Without the familiar vibration, they keep testing their surroundings, touching roots, surfacing, sinking again. Their own sounds can be heard then: short wooden clicks made somewhere at the back of the mouth, and a soft bubbling note exchanged at close quarters.

There are usually two or three in a stretch of suitable ground, though they are not always together. Each keeps several resting places. Some lie beneath old cushions with dry hollows at their centres; others are under the margins of pools, where a body can remain submerged while the nostrils rest in air.

The best shelter is under the hut.

There, between the concrete piers, roots have grown into a thick roof protected from frost and trampling. The animal using it has polished a route through the moss. On cold mornings a faint mist sometimes rises between the boards beneath the stove, though the stove itself has not been lit.

The maintenance workers know about this one. They have never agreed on what it is. In the cabinet beside the spare fuses there is a photograph of a dark, wet shape beside a boot, taken too close with a flash. A note underneath says to check below the walkway before lowering anything heavy.

In early summer there are young.

They have too little fur to resemble the ground and seem almost offensively visible: smooth, slate-coloured bodies with enormous pale feet. At first they remain in a chamber lined with chewed sedge. Later they follow an adult beneath the mat, gripping the fur above its hind legs when the water deepens.

They learn the surface cautiously. One forefoot emerges, opens, closes. The head follows. Often the whole attempt ends in a backward slide.

Their first expeditions leave small star-shaped impressions in the pale moss. These fill with water and disappear more quickly than a boot print.

At evening an adult may lead them to the young margins, where loose stems reach out over open water. There is food here, and room to swim beneath the thin cover. The adult goes first, lowering its weight until the vegetation bows without breaking. The young spread out behind it.

For a while, in the low light, every one of them is visible.

Then they begin feeding. Their heads descend, their backs settle among the stems, and the small movements become difficult to separate from the movement of the plants. One remains in sight longer than the others, a youngster struggling to draw a sedge blade under with it. The blade bends, slips free, springs upright.

A moment later it bends again.
