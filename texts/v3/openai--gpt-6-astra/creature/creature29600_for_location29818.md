The living thing is easiest to distinguish from the country just after a break.

For an hour or two, the torn edges behave differently. One withdraws its sap and turns dull, drawing a thin skin across the opened vessels. The other remains wet. Along it, small translucent knobs appear, each no larger than a fingertip, though the wound may extend across half a valley. They swell unevenly. Some become leaves. Some flatten against the sealed edge opposite them. Others lengthen into white cords and begin searching the gap.

Where a cord touches living tissue, its tip spreads.

By morning the break is loosely laced. By the next rain, some of the laces are carrying water. You could cross there without knowing that the two sides had ever been separate—or that, beneath the new surface, one side is still trying to close itself against the other.

There is more than one life here. There is no reliable way to count them.

A continuous green plain may contain hundreds of bodies, joined so thoroughly that water drawn in by one passes through the others before reaching a growing edge. A ridge divided by three ravines may still be one body, connected far below by a pale runner suspended through the chambers. Separation is an event, not a condition. So is joining.

The oldest boundaries can sometimes be felt underfoot. They are warmer than the tissue around them. Below the rind, two arrangements of vessels meet in a thick, troubled braid. Water slows there. New growth accumulates. A shallow rise develops along the boundary, gathering mist on one side and casting a narrow afternoon shadow on the other. Eventually the rise becomes a watershed, and what began as a difficulty between neighboring tissues determines the courses of several streams.

Elsewhere, the graft is almost perfect. Nothing marks it except a change in the way the leaves turn before rain.

***

The smallest complete inhabitants resemble tightly folded scraps of the surface.

They collect in the overflow channels beneath the raised basins: green crescents with a tough outer back and a seam of pale tissue tucked inside. They have no traveling stage except the flood. For months they may remain caught among fibers, scarcely changing, sustained by water and the light that reaches their backs.

When a basin empties violently, thousands go with it.

Most are carried into places where they cannot open. They swell inside cracks, expend their reserves pushing against older growth, and become soft pockets that the surrounding tissues gradually absorb. Some reach exposed water. There they loosen.

The first unfolding is small. Two lips part along the inner seam. Between them appears a glossy cup, shallow enough to hold a drop. Fine threads descend from its underside, their ends weighted with beads of clear gel. The cup widens, gathering dust, rain, and fragments of damaged leaf. Its underside becomes furry. Gas collects among the hairs.

Within days it is a floating rosette.

Within weeks, if warmth and light persist, it has made several others around itself. Their edges meet and fuse. Water beneath them darkens. The threads grow thicker and branch, not yet as supports but as an immersed feeding surface, sorting the dissolved remnants of older country from the water.

The first chamber is an accident of buoyancy: an arch of tissue with a little air trapped beneath it.

The growth preserves that accident. It reinforces the arch, subdivides it, makes others alongside. Soon there is a patch firm enough for rain to drum upon. Its center rises while its edges continue spreading across the pool.

From above, it looks like the old surface healing.

Sometimes it is. Sometimes a new inhabitant has begun to roof over the water left by another’s collapse.

***

Maturity has little to do with size.

There are young bodies extending over several valleys, lavish with translucent leaves and weak, rapidly assembled chambers. There are old ones no larger than a basin rim, dense and dark and almost motionless, their vessels buried in layers of abandoned tissue.

The old growth is valuable to its neighbors. It holds.

Fresh runners reach toward it, flatten themselves against its flanks, and wait for an opening. The older body responds by thickening its skin. Along some contacts it produces a bitter amber liquid that gathers in beads and hardens before falling. Along others it permits attachment.

A successful attachment may remain shallow for years: a purchase for fibers, a place to transfer weight. Later, a vessel on one side aligns with a vessel on the other. The intervening walls soften. A little fluid crosses.

What happens next depends on its composition.

Some connections widen immediately. Others blacken in a narrow ring and are cut away. Around an incompatible graft, each body grows a smooth collar, and the collars press against one another until one is lifted out of the light. These contests are slow enough to look like architecture. Their sharpest movements happen inside, where channels are opened or strangled and a distant surface suddenly finds itself without water.

A basin can fail on a clear afternoon because of such a meeting far beneath it.

The collapse sounds no different from any other.

***

Much of what lives here never becomes green.

In the warm chambers are films the color of cream, rust, or weak tea. They coat submerged fibers and gather thickly around places where sap escapes. Some form hanging skins, stirred by the passage of warm air. Others remain within the tissues, occupying small wet compartments that the larger growth maintains around them.

They do the slow work of making old country available to new.

A fallen support may remain outwardly whole while its center becomes warm slurry. Its useful substances pass into the black water, then into the descending threads, then upward. The hollow shell is left behind until something heavy settles on it.

Other films make gases. Beneath a sealed floor, their bubbles gather against the ceiling until the floor lifts slightly. The surrounding growth senses the strain and either opens a vent or strengthens the chamber to use the pressure.

Occasionally it does neither quickly enough.

Then a pale blister rises through the turf, stretching the minute leaves apart. It may stand all afternoon, larger than a house, trembling whenever the wind changes. When it bursts, the material thrown out is already alive with the things that softened it.

There are patches where these smaller lives flourish too fiercely. Heat builds faster than it can escape. Roots turn away from the water. The surface above loses its shine, then sinks. Around the sinking patch, the green growth closes channels and raises a rim.

Such pools can remain isolated for a long time. Their surfaces are brown and strangely still. New runners approach, test the water with a few threads, and withdraw.

Eventually one stays.

***

The country responds through pressure, chemistry, light, and the passage of strain.

A footfall closes tiny valves beneath the surface. Water is briefly trapped there, firming the flattened stem. Repeated pressure produces a broader rib. If the pressure stops, the rib may persist long after its usefulness has passed.

A tear sends several disturbances at once. The sudden loss of tension reaches connected fibers first. A pressure change follows through the vessels. Slower substances travel afterward, altering growth at distances where the original wound is no longer visible.

These messages can disagree.

An intact basin may tighten because the tissue beneath it reports a loss of support. Its tightening pulls on a neighboring ridge, which begins reinforcing itself just as excess water arrives from somewhere else. For a time, the whole district works against its own repairs. Channels bulge. Seams open. A tower discharges water into a plain that is already drowning.

Then, gradually, the conflicting movements settle into an arrangement that holds.

What remains is a memory made of unequal thicknesses, closed passages, enlarged joints. A slope that has fallen once grows differently afterward. The change may persist through fragmentation and regrowth, carried in the structure of a surviving edge. The new terrace inherits precautions against a weight it has never borne.

Some are useful. Some make it crooked.

***

At the advancing margins, the inhabitants become easier to see.

There the green surface ends in lobes, each with a darker center and a thin, almost colorless lip. The lips explore open space. They curl downward, encounter the body’s shaded underside, and sometimes attach there, making a loop. They extend sideways until they meet another lobe. Where nothing can be reached, they stiffen, swell, and try again.

Below an overhang, hundreds may be growing at once.

Some hang as straight cords. Some divide into brushes of fine, damp threads. Others have broadened into straps whose tips move slowly through the hot air. Droplets form along them and fall into depths crowded with more growth.

A gust turns the straps together, exposing their pale inner surfaces. For a moment the underside of the high country seems covered in unfinished leaves.

Many will dry before they find a purchase. Their substance will be drawn back upward, leaving hollow ribbons that cling through several storms. A few will touch. At those points the margins thicken. Weight shifts toward them. Above, a shallow depression disappears from the plain.

By the following season there may be room for another chamber.

The successful tips are buried in its joints. They continue living there, compressed into the work of holding up what comes next.
