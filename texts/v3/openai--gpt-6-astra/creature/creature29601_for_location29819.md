At first you see where it has been.

Along the underside of a pipe, between two supports, the rust has been rubbed smooth in a strip. Something passes there often enough to keep the metal dark. Below it, on a surface too soft to retain a footprint, there are paired depressions already filling with water.

The workers know these marks. They hang their lunch tins from wire rather than leave them on the platforms. When they turn a pump back on after maintenance, they strike the casing several times with a spanner and wait.

Occasionally, something comes out.

It lowers itself from the space between the pipe and its concrete saddle, apparently without bones. A shoulder emerges, then a long flank, then the hindquarters peel away from the iron. For a moment it hangs by its front feet. The toes are disproportionately long, with pale joints and little blunt nails, more useful for hooking than for digging. Then it drops.

There is less splash than there should be.

In the water it looks smaller. Most of the body disappears, leaving a low wedge of head and two patches of wet fur over the hips. It swims without lifting a wake until it reaches the fresh sediment. There it changes its manner of moving. The forelegs spread, the belly settles, and it slides forward in short, deliberate thrusts, distributing its weight over nearly its whole underside.

This is what the paired depressions are: the places where its hands have pressed.

Up close, it is about the length of a worker’s arm, though its dimensions are difficult to judge. Fed, it becomes broad behind the ribs. Hungry, it can pass through an opening that seems to admit only its head. Its fur is fine and close, dark at the roots, usually coated with enough silt to give it the color of whatever bed it last crossed. Along the throat and belly the hair lies in channels. Water drains through these when it climbs out, dripping from the elbows.

The head has no striking feature until it begins to feed.

Then the muzzle opens sideways.

What looked like a blunt nose is bordered by two soft, muscular lips, folded tightly against the jaws. In the shallows these unfold and press against the bottom. The animal advances with its face spread over the mud, feeling through it. Every few inches it pauses. A small contraction passes through the cheeks. Mud escapes beside the mouth, and something firmer is taken inward.

It eats worms, shrimp, snails too small to bother a bird, the eggs fixed beneath floating debris. A fish trapped in an overflow may be caught between its forefeet and bitten behind the head. Mostly, though, it feeds on things a person could hold in a palm without noticing they were alive.

Its eyes are small but serviceable. It watches above the water while its mouth works below. The nostrils sit high on the muzzle and close whenever the head goes under. There are no visible outer ears, only two slits that disappear when the wet fur is smoothed backward.

It is not deaf.

A dropped bolt sends it under at once. The continuous roar of the pumps seems not to trouble it, but a change in that roar will bring several heads to the surface. A loose bearing, before the workers can hear it, draws animals away from the nearest settling bed. They stop feeding and lie with their jaws resting on the mud.

The workers disagree about what this means. Some say they feel the machinery through their teeth. Others say a failing pump changes the water first.

Either way, it is useful.

They do not live in the mud itself. The mud cannot be trusted with a chamber. Even in the older beds, a tunnel may fill from below or close without leaving an opening at the surface.

Their homes are attached to things that stay.

Under a maintenance platform, where two concrete beams meet, is a mass that resembles flood rubbish. Reed stems project from it. There are strips of sack, fish line, a piece of insulation, and several lengths of the red string used to mark surveyed ground. The outside is plastered with silt. Water drains from its lowest point.

Inside are narrow passages lined with shredded grass and the dry inner fibers of reeds. The nest is larger than its occupants require because much of it is no longer used. As the basin rises, they build upward. As a bed drains and compacts, they extend a lower entrance. Old chambers remain sealed in the structure, holding shell fragments, fur, and bedding pressed almost flat.

One nest has incorporated the handle of a lost screwdriver. Another hangs from the underside of a staircase and moves slightly whenever someone climbs it.

They tolerate one another in these shelters. Away from them, they can be vicious over a feeding patch. An animal arriving at an occupied overflow first touches the resident’s flank with its muzzle. Sometimes it is admitted. Sometimes the resident turns and opens its mouth, showing small yellow teeth, and the newcomer backs into deeper water.

Within the nest they sleep piled together, their long hands caught in one another’s fur.

The young are born dark and thin-haired, with their toes folded against the wrists. For several days they cannot spread them properly. An adult carries each one in its mouth when the bedding must be moved, gripping the loose skin above the shoulders. The young hang with an alarming slackness. Set down, they immediately begin feeling for warmth.

A rising pool can empty the lowest nursery in minutes.

The adults work without much apparent urgency. One climbs down, enters the water, and returns with a young animal. Another receives it at the upper opening. They repeat this until the chamber is empty, then begin bringing up the bedding. A dry twist of grass is worth retrieving. So is a strip of woven sack. Sometimes they continue after the entrance has submerged, arriving wet with a small mouthful of material, squeezing past the waiting bodies into the dark.

They are least visible when most active. Rain dimples the water so thoroughly that their swimming heads disappear among the impacts. Under cover of it, they cross between beds, investigate newly drowned ridges, and climb onto the road to nose through the rubbish caught at its edges.

After a night of rain, the pipe supports carry fresh polish. A sack has been pulled halfway beneath the hut. There are muddy hand marks on the legs of the boot rack, and one upside-down boot contains a sleeping animal, tightly turned, its nose tucked beneath the smooth sole of a hind foot.

Farther out, under the maintenance platform, the nest has gained another layer.

Its lowest entrance is almost closed by the new ground. The upper one is fresh, its rim still showing clean fibers. From inside comes a brief scuffling. A long hand reaches out, tests the concrete, and remains there while its owner listens.
