A child learns to take a splinter without lifting a whole strip.

The lesson is given on old ground, beside the houses, with a blunt knife and a piece of discarded rib. The child must find the end of a fiber, turn the blade across it, and make a cut no longer than a thumbnail. Cut along the grain and the wound may run beyond the hand. On living ground it may run beyond sight.

The adults do not frighten children with this. They sit close enough to put a hand over the knife.

Most of what keeps a person alive here is taught at that distance.

Their gloves have a bare patch at the base of one finger. Through it they can touch a floor without exposing the whole hand. They test for trembling, for the faint yielding that precedes a bulge, for moisture where yesterday the tissue was dry. A person entering a house often kneels in the doorway before greeting anyone. In unfamiliar country, people stop talking while they do this.

Their clothes are layered, close-fitting at the wrist and ankle, dull with repeated scraping. The outer layer is made from shed membrane, folded until its individual transparencies become opaque. It is light, sheds frost, and tears readily at a puncture. Everyone carries patches. The knees are usually a different age from the rest of the garment.

Underneath are soft layers of worked fiber. A new coat is stiff and noisy. An old one moves almost silently and holds the shape of its owner when hung from a peg. Clothes pass through a household by alteration: an adult’s sleeve becomes a child’s vest, a worn collar becomes the lining of a glove. The last useful scraps go around water vessels.

There is considerable skill in sewing something that will not conduct a tear.

Their faces are often browned around the eyes. Even without warmth, the long brightness hurts. They wear narrow visors, or strips of dark membrane with slits cut through them, and turn their heads to look rather than moving their eyes. At home the visors are pushed up into the hair. A visitor may know someone for years on the trails and be surprised, at a meal, by the breadth of their gaze.

They are not all quiet people. Their houses are quiet from outside.

Inside there is arguing, the clack of spoons, children drumming their heels against the sleeping platform. Someone is always scraping something. The sound travels through the ribs of the shelter and makes a busy, domestic rasp. They sing while drawing fibers through a comb, keeping the pull even. The songs have room for additional lines because a batch of fiber seldom lasts exactly as long as expected.

Many songs concern people who were too pleased with their own equipment.

A celebrated traveler may survive in memory principally as the owner of a sledge that overturned in front of three houses.

They eat more of the place than a stranger first realizes.

Most exposed tissue is too tough, too poor, or too full of defensive sap to be useful. The tender growth at a fracture is easier to chew but can blister the mouth. The white grazers can feed there because their black mouthparts form a close seal around the cut. Sap passes into a chamber behind the jaws without touching the softer tissues of the face.

People have no such protection. They take narrow plugs from particular layers of older growth, close the holes with fitted wedges, and bring the plugs home in covered baskets. A good food-cutter can tell by the resistance of the tool when to stop.

The plugs are shaved, washed, and packed into shallow trays with a little of the previous batch. Over several days the fibers loosen into a sour, dense paste. Each household keeps its trays wrapped near the occupied sleeping platform. When everyone must leave, someone carries them inside their coat.

A house can be rebuilt from its ribs. A food culture lost to freezing has to be begged from a neighbor.

The pastes differ. One is yellowish and sweet at the finish; another is gray and has an odor outsiders find difficult. People are loyal to these differences. Marriage may bring two cultures into one house, where they are kept on opposite sides of the platform and never served with the same spoon.

There are also sap concentrates, fragile sheets of dried wet-seam growth, and, occasionally, meat. A killed grazer is a large event. The news travels farther than the smell. People arrive with empty containers and something to offer: a cutting edge, a report of rain, labor for moving a shelter.

Little is roasted. Fat and moisture are too valuable to let drip away. Meat is cooked in a lidded vessel over a small heartwood fire, in a hearth that hangs clear of the floor.

Children who have eaten meat will sometimes insist for weeks that they remember it perfectly, though their descriptions become increasingly improbable.

The grazers are taller close up than their tracks suggest.

Each of the six legs folds tightly beneath the body when the animal rests. The joints fit into shallow recesses along its flanks, leaving a shape like a piece of fallen white tissue. Frost gathers on the back. Unless the mouth is working, there may be nothing to distinguish it from the ground.

Its feet are divided into several hard points that can spread, twist, and close independently. On a plain it walks with a delicate, fussy gait. On the edge of a vertical leaf it becomes much more assured. Three feet hold while the other three search. The body turns sideways. The head continues feeding as if the change of orientation were unimportant.

They do not gather in herds except at a very large wound. Even then, they keep their distance. A grazer coming upon another’s feeding cut touches the edge with its mouth and usually moves away.

Their paired tracks are the marks of the foremost feet. The other four tend to enter the same holes.

People follow these trails when a route has become doubtful, but never trust them entirely. A grazer can cross surfaces that will not support a laden sledge. It can also remain motionless for days in a niche that affords a human being neither water nor shelter.

Some animals become familiar.

There is one, near a settlement of five houses, with a dark joint on its left middle leg. It passes along the same rib whenever growth elsewhere bends that rib into reach. Its visits may be separated by weeks or by years. The people disagree about whether it is the same animal their grandparents knew.

They call it by a name ordinarily used for a troublesome aunt.

No one has tamed it. Once, during a dry interval, a woman put a pan of thawed water in its path. It stood beside the pan until she went indoors. By morning the water was gone and the pan had been bitten through.

For a long time she kept the two halves above her door.

The smaller silver animals are easier to live beside and harder to keep.

They fit beneath a fingernail, though their bodies can flatten to much less than their apparent thickness. A detached skin may contain hundreds, each pressed into a different crease. In clear light they look metallic. Held against the roof, they are nearly transparent except for a dark thread that runs from the head to the rear of the body.

They feed on the soft growth inside breathing seams and breed in the short wet interval after an opening. Their young are carried beneath the adult’s plates. When the seam begins to change, the plates lift, and a scatter of smaller silvers spills out and runs with the others.

Travelers watch which side of a seam they emerge from.

Children try to keep them in little folded boxes. They add shavings, droplets, fragments from every sort of tissue. For a day or two the creature hides beneath the wettest piece. Then it becomes still. Adults have learned to recognize the careful, excessive preparations that precede a child bringing one home.

There are no cages for grazers.

A few settlements have tried to raise them behind barriers. The animals either climb out or stop eating. The surviving barriers are useful for drying laundry.

*

People travel in small companies because a large company cannot drink quickly enough from a closing seam.

Two or three sledges are common. One person goes ahead without a load, testing the surface and setting short markers where the grain becomes difficult to read. Another watches behind. A change in the angle of a familiar ridge is often clearer after it has been passed.

The person at the rear also picks up anything dropped.

Losing an object in plain sight is peculiarly easy here. Set a pale cup down on a pale floor, turn to tighten a binding, and there may be a hundred yards of apparently identical surface in every direction. Tools are stained dark. Children’s mittens are joined by cord. Water vessels have trailing ties that can be trapped under a knee.

Travelers carry news in pieces of the country.

A shaving from a newly opened route can show its grain better than a description. A strip that stays luminous late into the night proves the age of a crossing. Such samples are kept in narrow cases, separated so they cannot grow together. At a settlement, the case is opened on the table, and each piece is handled while its owner explains where it was taken.

The listeners ask about adjacent tissue, wind direction, the sound made by a runner, how far a dropped chip slid.

Only afterward do they ask who was met along the way.

Even then, directions attach themselves to people. A route may be called the way to a woman who has been dead for forty years. Her children live elsewhere, but everyone understands which ledges are meant.

Some travelers make the same circuit for most of their lives. They are welcome partly for their knowledge and partly because they have known everyone at several ages. They remember who was once afraid of an open seam, who could haul twice their own weight, whose mother had a laugh that interrupted stories.

When one fails to arrive, absence spreads slowly around the circuit.

For a while, each settlement assumes the traveler is at the next.

*

The oldest residents often become the keepers of the floor.

This is practical work. They are home more, sit lower, and have time to notice a door rubbing at one corner or a vessel that no longer stands quite level. A house’s runners can conceal a change for some time. Small things reveal it first.

Under the sleeping platform they keep loose witness-pegs, each fitted into a shallow hole. If the floor stretches, a peg falls sideways. If it compresses, the peg becomes difficult to turn.

Every morning someone checks them.

There are households in which this is done ceremoniously, with the children summoned to watch. In others an old man reaches down without opening his eyes, rotates three pegs, and goes back to sleep.

Their authority is not absolute. Old people can mistake stiffness in their hands for movement beneath them. They can become reluctant to abandon a familiar place. A settlement may argue for days over a change that all agree is real.

Dragging a house is exhausting. Leaving an old island can mean losing access to a food layer cut and tended for generations. There are graves to consider, and buried stores, and the sheltered rack where beams have almost finished seasoning.

So a warning does not empty a place at once.

First, someone packs the good vessels.

Then the children are told to keep their outdoor layers on.

The dead are wrapped and placed in chambers cut from tissue that no longer shines at night. The chambers are shallow. Deep cutting can wake growth even in an old island.

Families return to check the covers. Eventually the joints close. Sometimes a chamber lifts as the layers beneath it thicken, and its cover can be seen high in a face that no one can now reach. People point these out from passing sledges.

Sometimes the chamber disappears.

There is no agreement about whether the dead nourish the place. The question is discussed with unusual care around those recently bereaved. A common practice is to place something beside the body that will remain recognizable if the chamber is opened by a fracture: a black wedge, a carved spoon, a tool with a distinctive handle.

People do not expect to recover these things.

They want the finder to know that someone was put there.

*

When the fresh green line appears beneath the houses, the first person to see it is a girl retrieving a dropped fastening.

She has her cheek close to the floor. From above, the line is almost invisible; at that angle it catches the light along its whole length.

She calls her father, who tells her not to put a finger in it.

Then he lies down himself.

By evening, everyone has looked. The line runs beneath two runners, across the space between the houses, and under the rack of seasoned beams. One end cannot be found.

The oldest woman brings out a lamp hood and holds it over the floor. They wait for the stored light to show. Beneath the hood the old tissue glows faintly, and the new line remains dark.

No one sleeps in the two houses that night.

The girl is pleased with the attention until she sees the food trays being wrapped. Then she asks whether they will come back. Her father says they will leave markers.

In the morning they begin freeing the runners.

They work carefully. A runner can adhere to the floor in places too small to notice until the house is pulled. The black wedges go in a little at a time. Someone inside removes cups from their pegs so they will not knock together. A child is sent to collect the witness-pegs and returns with a fist closed around them.

Far out on the pale ground, the familiar grazer stands facing the settlement.

It has found the other end of the line.
