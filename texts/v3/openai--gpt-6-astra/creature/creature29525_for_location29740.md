The little animal has made a hollow in the moss without breaking any of it. The stems bend beneath its weight, and their translucent leaves lie against its belly. Through the thinner fur there, green light shows faintly between the ribs.

It is about the length of a forearm. Its hind feet are large, folded together under one hip; its forefeet are small enough to close around a fern stem. Each finger ends in a broad, dark nail with a notch in the edge. These are not digging claws. They fit over roots, securing the animal wherever the mountain’s force would otherwise lift it away.

The paw across its nose is loosely fastened to a thread of moss.

Its tail follows the curve of its body, then continues into the empty bed beside it. Near the tip, the fur lies flat along one side, as though habitually pressed against another flank.

You cannot see its ears at first. There are two narrow folds behind the jaw, silver-edged and almost closed. Farther down, just above the shoulders, another pair of openings exposes the deep red of living skin. Those face the stone overhead.

The animal listens both to the air and to whatever is bearing the air’s weight.

Its fur is finer than it first appears. Each hair divides near the tip into several colorless filaments. These catch droplets without becoming wet themselves. Close to the skin, the reddish undercoat is dense and dry. Along the spine the hairs have thicker, dark roots, visible through the silver like seeds through the flesh of a fruit.

The mountain’s hum changes when you put your hand near them.

Only a little. A pressure in the ears eases; one of the lower sounds becomes easier to distinguish. With your palm held a finger’s breadth above the animal’s back, you feel a succession of soft impacts. They could be a heartbeat, except that moving your hand toward its shoulders makes them come faster, and moving toward its tail makes them come more slowly.

Hold your hand still and there is a single, sustained push.

It is asleep from where you are kneeling.

This remains true until you lower your head.

From near the level of the moss, a narrow opening appears beneath the paw. There is an eye inside it, dark brown around a pupil shaped like a crooked seam. The eye is looking at you.

Nothing has opened. The silver lashes occupy the same positions. You can rise and find the animal asleep again, then kneel and meet its gaze.

Its wakefulness lies close to the ground.

At this height you also see that its muzzle is longer than it seemed, with a soft, divided upper lip and a pale scar running from one nostril into the fur. The scar has healed unevenly. A few hairs grow across it in the wrong direction.

That small imperfection makes it easier to believe in the animal than anything else has done.

It has been hurt somewhere. It has carried an injury through the ordinary business of closing.

There is dirt beneath two of its nails that does not match the black soil of the hollow: a dry, reddish grit. A grass seed is caught deep in the fur behind its leg. No grass grows here.

Perhaps it came up the mountain.

Perhaps there was once more mountain to come up.

The slit of its eye follows the space your face occupies, though the pupil does not move. Shift sideways and you find yourself still within its attention. Shift farther, until your shoulder touches the projecting slab, and you reach the edge of that attention. The eye is now directed past you, toward the open water.

It is a surprisingly definite boundary. On one side, you are being watched. On the other, you are not.

The animal has other things to attend to.

A transparent snail rests against a root near its mouth. Inside the shell, blue organs loop around a dark central knot. One antenna touches the animal’s lip. Just beyond it, a cream-colored flower hangs under a liverwort, its opening almost against the moss.

From above, the mouth appears closed. From beside the root, you can see the tongue touching the flower.

The tongue is narrow and deeply grooved. Nectar fills the groove. Along one margin, several grains of pollen have lodged in minute backward-facing hairs.

Here the animal feeds.

A few inches away, beneath the jaw, its throat is swallowing. You can feel the swallowing if you set a fingertip very lightly against it: an insistent, muscular passage that neither finishes nor repeats. The nectar is sweet enough to smell in the cold air.

The animal is warm throughout, but the heat gathers especially in the soles of its feet and beneath its chin. These are the places it rests against things. A root under one hind foot is swollen and pale, supporting a tuft of smaller roots that enter the fur.

There is no wound where they touch it.

The intimacy seems practical and very old.

Look carefully into the other groves and you begin to find accommodations for bodies of this shape. A fork padded with liverwort. A dry pocket underneath a curtain of hanging roots. Two smooth channels through the thick fern stems, running close together. None is occupied.

Under one tree, downward-facing flowers open around a low branch at exactly the height of the little animal’s mouth.

It belongs among these plants. It also bears the remains of another country on its feet.

Beside the slab, the empty moss has two shallow depressions for haunches. A root crosses it at an angle convenient for a forepaw to clasp. The overhang is lower there. Whoever lay in that place would have rested with its head near the sleeping animal’s chest.

You put two fingers down on the moss.

Warmth gathers around them.

From this position, the exposed eye is difficult to see. Instead, you can see a small patch of skin beneath the animal’s chin, nearly bare, reddish and finely creased. The throat gives off a sound too low to hear clearly through the mountain’s labor. Your fingertips receive it through the moss.

Move them toward the animal and the sound roughens. Move them away and it softens into a smooth vibration.

There is a place between where it has the irregular texture of a voice.

You stay there a while.

The animal does not mistake your fingers for another animal. Its attention is still directed toward your face, higher up, where a large unfamiliar creature is breathing damp air beneath the slab. There is room in its attention for both facts: your strangeness, and the pressure you are putting on the bed.

Along the underside of its tail, the flattened fur begins to make sense. The hairs are combed outward toward the empty place. They would part readily around a body settling there. Underneath them are little folds of warm skin.

The tail is arranged to be put around someone.

You can follow that gesture with your hand. Near the animal’s hip it is only a tail at rest; farther along, there is the slight muscular resistance of lifting; at the tip, your fingers encounter the firm, delicate pressure of being held.

The hollow has given the gesture enough space to exist whole.

There is no way to tell how long the other bed has been empty. Nothing in the moss will testify to a departure. No warmth diminishes. No crushed leaf recovers. The little animal’s scar belongs to a history you cannot enter, and the red dirt stays dry beneath its nails.

But the empty bed is not sealed or guarded. Your hand fits into a portion of it. A small creature could approach from the water, pass beneath the silver tail, and find roots ready to take its weight.

When you withdraw your fingers, they are warm to the knuckles.

Under the slab, the animal sleeps with its paw across its nose. Close to the moss, it watches the open ground. Its tongue takes sweetness from the flower, and the low voice in its throat travels through the roots into the place beside it.

The fur along its tail is parted there, all the way to the skin.
