The person in the doorway is carrying a chair.

This is difficult to see at first. The chair is dark, the doorway darker, and the person has paused with it held sideways against one hip. They are waiting for the water to finish crossing the threshold. When the last of it withdraws up the doorjamb, they step out.

They are a little shorter than you, broad through the ribs, with long forearms and a head carried slightly forward. Their clothing consists of a stiff, sleeveless garment fastened along one side and loose trousers ending above the ankles. There are old repairs at both knees. Their feet are bare.

Each step leaves a pale print. The paving dries beneath their soles, then darkens again.

They set the chair beside the doorway. One leg has been shortened and fitted with a ceramic extension. They test it with a hand, turn it a little, sit.

Only then do they look at you.

Their face is not blank, although you may initially mistake it for that. Its movements take place in unfamiliar places. Two folds beside the mouth tighten; the lower eyelids lift independently. The eyes themselves are small, very dark, and set beneath heavy brows without hair. Fine grooves run from the temples down the neck and disappear into the collar. Moisture collects in them without falling.

They pat the broad stone beside the chair.

It is an invitation, not a warning, though the stone is hot enough that you hesitate. They notice, look at your shoes, and go inside for a mat.

***

Their skin is porous, but not everywhere in the same way.

The soles and the undersides of the fingers take up water readily. The back is almost sealed. At the joints, where the skin folds, it is soft and pale; over the shoulders it thickens into a dull, close-grained surface resembling unglazed pottery. An old injury remains visible as an area where the grain runs wrong.

They drink from cups. They also stand in shallow water when tired.

These are different needs. Water taken through the feet does not quench thirst, any more than warmth satisfies hunger. After a long shift a person may spend twenty minutes on a wet tile, saying very little, then ask impatiently whether there is anything to drink.

Their bodies alter through the day. Someone coming off work in a dry gallery is narrow-faced, with loose folds at the wrists. After resting, those folds fill out. The change is not dramatic, but it is enough that a visitor sometimes fails to recognize the person who gave them directions an hour earlier.

Their clothes allow for it. Their chairs allow for it. Rings are worn on cords rather than fingers.

Children are told not to compare people’s morning and evening sizes.

Children do it anyway.

***

There are more inhabitants than the courts suggest, though far fewer than the stairways seem to remember.

Some are asleep. Some are beyond the pressure doors, in districts reached only during a particular part of the cycle. Some have gone to visit someone and will not be able to return until the following day. A journey of six hundred meters can require a packed meal, while another of four kilometers may take less than an hour.

They give directions in terms of events.

Past the hall that is filling. Wait until the second float drops. Take the stairs while the handrail is warm.

Visitors are not expected to understand this immediately. There are people who enjoy explaining it and people who find visitors exasperating. At one junction someone has painted a patient sequence of numbered instructions. Below it, in another hand, is an addition correcting the third step. Below that is a small, vigorous argument.

The city has been surveyed many times. No single survey is useful for every purpose.

A child’s first independent errand usually stays within one pressure district. The child carries something that does not matter very much, although this is never admitted. An empty tin. A folded cloth. A message already sent by another route.

At the destination, an adult makes a ceremony of receiving it.

***

They speak in voices with little carrying power.

Up close, their speech is rich in soft clicks and brief, resonant vowels. Across a hall it becomes indistinguishable from water working through a grate. They can shout, but shouting costs them something; afterward they press their tongues against the roofs of their mouths and swallow repeatedly.

To call someone at a distance, they strike a railing.

Not every railing. The proper ones have polished patches, and sometimes a small hammer on a chain. The signals are local, full of abbreviations. Three measured blows might mean a gate has jammed in one district and that supper is ready in another.

There is a woman in the eastern galleries whose family recognizes her by the impatient second blow of any signal she gives.

Her brother says she has always done it.

She says the rail is badly mounted.

***

The machinery occupies much of their attention, but not all of their lives.

There are meals to prepare, hems to mend, people one would rather not encounter at the washing recess. There are households that keep immaculate ledgers and households where the whereabouts of a cup becomes a recurring grievance.

Food arrives from deeper levels in sealed ceramic carriers. Some of it is cultivated in warm vats; some is brought up from chambers where a tough white growth is peeled from hanging plates. Prepared badly, it is fibrous and sour. Prepared well, visitors ask what has been added to it.

The answer is usually something the cook cannot obtain anymore, followed by a long explanation of the substitute.

They like crisp food. It is difficult to keep anything crisp here.

A paper packet opened at the right moment can silence a room.

They have games played with heavy counters on boards pierced by drainage holes. They have songs that require a second singer to begin before the first has finished, so that neither melody can be heard alone. They decorate the undersides of shelves, where color lasts.

In one dwelling a child has painted a bright animal above the bed. The animal has too many legs even by local standards. An adult has carefully fitted a drip shield over it.

***

Their sleep accounts for some of the city’s emptiness.

Ordinary sleep lasts no longer than yours. But at intervals—more often in youth, less predictably with age—a person becomes difficult to wake. For several days beforehand they lose appetite, feel cold in warm rooms, and become preoccupied with small unfinished tasks.

They arrange cover for their work. They return borrowed objects. Sometimes they quarrel with the person who will care for them.

Then they lie on one of the porous stone beds, and the bed begins to darken.

For weeks or months, water moves slowly through them. Their breathing grows too slight to see. A thin mineral crust forms along the outer grooves of the skin and is softened away with cloths. They must be turned. The room must not become too dry, nor the floor vents be allowed to clog.

Most wake without difficulty.

Some wake changed in ways their families notice before they do: unable to bear a familiar food, newly fond of company, relieved of an old pain. There is no certainty about what causes these changes. Physicians keep records. Relatives offer explanations.

A man may emerge after eighty days and discover that his sister has used his absence to dispose of a cupboard he insisted could be repaired.

This can occupy the household for some time.

There are beds in rooms very far apart that are nevertheless tended every day. There are also empty beds kept ready for people whose intervals have become irregular.

No one calls all these rooms vacant.

***

They know much more about the water than they can explain.

A maintenance worker can place two fingers against a sweating pipe and tell you which gate, three halls away, has failed to seat. Ask why a certain gutter climbs a wall and you may receive a technical account, an old story, or the name of someone better qualified.

The answers do not always agree.

The oldest channels are treated with particular caution. New bypasses are tested, amended, sometimes abandoned. There have been accidents. Behind one sealed passage are the names of eleven workers and an apprentice, cut into separate tiles because their families could not agree on a single inscription.

The canopy is another matter.

They observe it. They have instruments for its height and density, and records of the luminous openings. They know when to cover exposed copper and when a lowering fold means the western passages should be cleared.

They do not all believe the same thing about what it is.

You will hear a theory over dinner that your host’s companion dismisses with the weary familiarity of someone who has heard it for thirty years.

***

The person beside the doorway has brought the chair outside because a friend used to sit there.

The friend is not dead. They have moved to a lower district, where the air is easier on their chest. Visits are possible, but inconvenient, and each has lately been waiting for the other to make the journey.

This evening the person has decided to repair the chair’s back.

They have a coil of waxed cord in a pocket, and while you sit on the borrowed mat they begin threading it through the frame. Their fingers are thick, delicately grooved, extremely precise. Occasionally they stop to dampen the cord in the cup at their feet.

You ask about the pale animal under the stairs.

They glance over. It has raised itself on its six feet and is waiting for your shadow to move.

That one, they tell you, comes out whenever there is work to supervise.

They resume threading.

Above the court, a copper seam opens in the canopy. For a few seconds the chair, the cup, the small patient animal all cast shadows in the same direction.

The person lifts the repaired back toward the light to inspect the knot.
