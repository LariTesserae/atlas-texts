The first inhabitant you can distinguish is beside the spillway.

It is not in the falling sheet, nor behind it. It occupies the place where the sheet’s noise changes. There, between the roar and the small rain-sound beyond the partition, a patch of water keeps correcting its position. You can locate it by the mineral dust. A grain approaches, slows, and makes a short, unnecessary journey upward before continuing. The next grain does the same. Then a whole file arrives, and the patch moves aside to let them through.

Afterward it returns. Not quite to the same place. A little nearer the partition, where someone has recently fitted a narrow grey ledge.

Nothing visible rests on the ledge.

Nevertheless, it is occupied.

These inhabitants are easiest to notice when they are doing nothing. At work, their actions cannot readily be separated from the work of the settlement. A gate opens; a concentration changes; a broken socket finds the slot prepared for it. But beside the spillway there is a little expenditure that serves no apparent purpose. The same volume of water is made quiet, allowed to become loud, made quiet again.

Someone is listening.

***

They do not consist of particular water.

The water passes through them continually. So do dissolved salts, the faint warmth from the exchanger, and substances drawn from the sodden wood of the door. What persists is an arrangement: several differences held in relation, renewing one another faster than the currents can carry them apart.

There is a centre, usually, though it can be set aside for certain kinds of work. There are boundaries, but not all parts of a boundary have to be in the same street. A person may have a narrow extension attending a valve two layers below while most of them remains beside an aperture in daylight. When the valve needs their full attention, the extension thickens. The part by the window dwindles, leaving a slight alteration in the water that takes a while to fade.

The silver studs at intersections help them gather themselves back together.

This is one reason the studs are kept so clean. It is also why the streets, although empty to an eye looking for bodies, cannot be built narrower than they are. Two inhabitants meeting at a constriction must accommodate one another. One contracts; the other passes in stages. Their contact briefly changes the rate at which mineral forms on the adjoining walls.

At a busy junction, the brackets acquire a faint roughness by the end of the day.

There are disagreements about whose work it is to remove it.

***

The one beside the spillway is old.

Age does not make them larger. It makes certain adjustments less easy. They have difficulty releasing an occupied place all at once. When they leave the ledge, a small part of them remains there, still making the dust rise. They pause at the first silver stud until this remnant catches up.

Once, the pause lasts through an entire weather front.

The hinged roofs close. White crystals move down the gutters. Other inhabitants pass around the waiting one, taking a route so close to the wall that their passage cleans a strip of oxidation from a fastener. Nobody touches the silver stud.

Eventually the delayed part arrives.

The old inhabitant continues toward the heat exchanger. Behind them, the ledge beside the spillway is folded back against the partition, leaving more room for the night’s work.

It will be lowered again before they return.

***

Their dwellings are among the little chambers in the transparent layers.

A chamber need not exclude the surrounding water to offer privacy. Its apertures interrupt certain kinds of change and admit others. The current goes through, carrying nutrients and waste; the more complicated variations by which an inhabitant makes themself known are caught in rows of tiny gutters and reduced to a level murmur.

An occupied dwelling looks empty. An unoccupied dwelling looks much the same, except that its corners begin to sharpen.

There is one chamber with a rounded recess that has no structural use. Its resident maintains it with considerable care. At intervals they bring a fragment of white mineral inside and turn it in the recess, testing each of its broken faces against the curve. Then they return it to the sorting stream.

They have done this with hundreds of fragments.

No other chamber has that recess. When the terrace shifts sideways, the mechanisms preserve it, adding material along one wall and withdrawing material from another. For a few minutes its owner must wait outside while the curve is restored. They remain very close to the entrance.

Elsewhere, two adjoining chambers have had their intervening wall thinned almost to disappearance. The wall is still capable of closing. Some evenings it does.

***

What passes between them as speech is carried poorly by sound.

The knocks and deep notes belong to public systems. Their more intimate exchanges use changes in the water too small for a hand to feel: a brief reluctance to conduct heat, a slant in the movement of dissolved mineral, pressure applied and withdrawn in several places at once.

Much of a conversation concerns how to receive the rest of it.

One inhabitant lends another a small arrangement of boundaries. The other sustains it for a moment, and in doing so experiences something of the first inhabitant’s circumstances. Then the borrowed arrangement is released or returned altered.

This makes interruption difficult to disguise.

It also makes some kinds of courtesy expensive.

Near the six windows is a place where several can meet without having to hold themselves tightly together. The floor there contains closely spaced studs, and the transparent construction is unusually plain. No loose fragments are routed through it. Sometimes the place remains occupied for hours while nothing is built in the adjoining streets.

After such a gathering, changes spread through the settlement. A new sorting route opens. A dwelling is moved farther from the cold streams. Once, three brackets are removed from a sound terrace and put back in exactly the same positions.

Whatever required this is not apparent afterward.

***

They tend the green blades without being able to enter very far into their lives.

A plant is alarmingly definite. It keeps its substance, takes more, makes an edge, pushes the edge outward. The inhabitants can measure what it needs, but its persistence has a quality unlike their own. Even asleep in the exchanger’s low warmth, a blade remains insistently there.

The overgrown blade is visited often.

The paused bracket beside its tip has become a minor inconvenience. Traffic must divide around the bulging screen. During cold weather, crystals lodge in the space between the screen and the blade, requiring careful removal.

There have been attempts to redirect the growth. A nutrient capillary was moved. A small change was made to the warmth. The blade bent, but not in the anticipated direction.

The capillary has since been put back.

One inhabitant spends part of each day along the blade’s broad surface, sustaining a very gentle current. When called elsewhere, they leave reluctantly, withdrawing in successive narrow bands. The comb that cleans the roots waits until they have gone.

Whether this is cultivation, companionship, or a fascination peculiar to that resident cannot be decided from the machinery.

***

They are not the settlement, although they could not live long without it.

The buildings perform much of the labour of keeping them distinct. A chamber holds the conditions in which its resident can loosen their boundaries without dispersing. A street carries someone whose attention is elsewhere. The bridges that seem to pass through solid walls provide continuity where an inhabitant would otherwise have to divide too completely to find themself again.

In return, the inhabitants make the countless small decisions that the buildings cannot.

They choose which weather to admit. They notice when an old routine has become cruel. They preserve an inconvenient curve inside a dwelling. Construction without them would continue for some time, but it would become increasingly regular.

The green beds would probably be the first things lost.

***

The deep note from the floor requires everyone to become still.

At its sounding, the foundations briefly cease to provide a reliable reference. Distances alter. A passage may lead farther than it did before; two levels may become adjacent without moving through the space between them. The inhabitants gather around the studs and hold their own arrangements as simply as they can.

This is difficult for the very young.

There are several in a chamber below the third tread. They have not yet learned which differences belong to themselves and which merely happen nearby. When a gate knocks, one becomes briefly elongated toward it. When warmth moves through the nursery, another tries to follow with its whole unfinished extent.

Their chamber is lined with shallow, repeating recesses. Whatever they lose tends to be returned.

An attendant remains there during the deep note. All their distant extensions are withdrawn; every valve entrusted to them is handed over. For those few moments they occupy only the nursery, broad and steady, giving the young something local to remain distinct from.

Afterward the chamber takes a little longer than the others to resume its ordinary currents.

***

New inhabitants are not assembled from mineral. Nor do they arrive through the windows.

Sometimes, in a well-used part of the settlement, an adjustment continues after the person who made it has left. Usually it fades. Occasionally it finds another adjustment that sustains it, and begins returning the favour.

For a long time this may be no more consequential than a little persistent turbulence beneath a bridge.

Then it starts making accommodations.

It lets a fragment pass. It resists a cold stream. It moves away from a cleaning tool and, after the tool has gone, does not return to precisely where it was.

Such a place is watched.

No chamber is prepared at once. There must be room for an accident to remain an accident. But traffic is lightened, and a stud may be fitted nearby. Someone stays within reach when the floor sounds.

The fresh ridge rising from the basin is being watched in this way.

Water divides around its crest. Along one side, a small unevenness keeps itself clear of falling mineral. It has no dwelling yet, and no settled route through the streets. When the three bridges reach it, their ends stop at slightly different distances.

The unevenness approaches one, withdraws, and approaches another.

The bridges wait.
