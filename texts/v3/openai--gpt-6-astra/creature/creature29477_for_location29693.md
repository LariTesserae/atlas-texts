There is a place under the second shelf where the grazers do not settle.

It is not quite bare. Their feeding tracks cross it, and sometimes a small one stops there long enough to leave a pale oval in the film. But the larger animals turn aside at its edge. The ceramic has been rubbed clean along a low, crooked passage, polished not by water but by something passing through it in the same direction, many times.

At the back of this passage is a foot.

It rests sole outward against the wall: five short digits, a broad pad, a little black nail set well back from each tip. The skin is gray-pink and deeply creased. Condensation gathers in the creases without making the foot slip. Now and then one digit lifts and comes down again in a slightly different place.

The rest of the animal is tucked above it, in a space that looks too shallow to contain anything larger than a folded rag.

She is about the length of a hand, excluding the tail. Her coat is brown where it is dry and almost black where she has been through water. Along her flanks the hairs lie in narrow, persistent ridges. Mineral dust whitens the ends of those over her shoulders. She has a blunt muzzle, very small ears, and eyes that show little in the amber light except when she turns them directly toward the service strip.

Her tail is thick at its base and bare underneath. As she sleeps, it passes around an exposed reinforcement rib. The tip lies beside her face.

Three young are pressed against her belly. From below, they are difficult to distinguish from the folds of her skin. One has an ear that has not yet opened fully. Another sleeps with its mouth against her coat, gripping a tuft it no longer needs.

She does not wake when the pipes change pitch. One eye opens, but her breathing keeps its pace. She knows this change.

What brings her out is the first water touching the underside of her shelf.

Her foot withdraws. For a while nothing replaces it. Then her nose appears at the other end of the passage, followed by a flattened head and one shoulder. She comes through sideways, with the slow, yielding motion of something being squeezed out of a wet cloth. Once clear, she shakes herself only once. Her coat lifts enough to show how slight her body is beneath it.

The young remain in the pocket. She has pushed a wad of stripped insulation across its lower edge. It would not hold back a determined animal, but it holds back a sleepy one.

She waits with both forefeet on the floor.

The roar has not diminished. It never does. Nevertheless, something in the chamber has become available to her: the first scattered patter overhead, the movement of grazers beneath their ledges, a brief disturbance transmitted through the ceramic when the predator in the valve sleeve shifts its weight.

Her whiskers touch the floor in front of her feet. They are worn short on one side.

Then she moves.

She does not hurry across the open shelves. She takes the undersides, traveling belly upward where there is room and sideways where there is not. Her feet spread over the rounded lips. The nails seldom catch; it is the soft pads that hold her. At each awkward turn she puts down her tail before releasing a hind foot.

At the first gully she lowers her muzzle and draws it along the sugary joint compound. Her tongue works quickly. There are eggs there, almost invisible, laid in a line beneath an overhang. She takes part of the line, then wedges her face farther in to reach a pale segmented animal retreating through the damp material.

It comes free trailing a few grains of cream-colored coating.

She eats it with her eyes shut.

Her rounds are not tidy. She passes good feeding places without stopping, doubles back, spends a long time worrying at a seam that yields nothing. Once she sits on her haunches and scratches the side of her neck with such force that a hind foot loses purchase. Her tail tightens around a rib. She continues scratching.

A small patch beneath her jaw has no fur at all.

Near the basin, the winged animals lift before she reaches them. Most merely exchange one pipe for another. A few return almost at once. She watches one settle overhead, raises herself slightly, and lets herself down again. The jump would take her across a wet, hot surface with no good place to land.

Instead, she reaches into the fringe of filter feeders at the basin’s rim.

They fold their feathery mouths. Her finger follows the crust, probing. When she brings it back, something clings to the nail. She bites it off.

The valve sleeve rocks.

She freezes with one forefoot raised. The predator has caught a transparent swimmer; its folded limbs are busy at its mouth. For several seconds she watches. Then she steps past, close enough that her flank brushes the sleeve.

There is very little food in it for the trouble it would give her.

By the time the overhead shower thickens, she is thoroughly wet. Water runs down the channels in her coat and falls from her belly. She drinks beneath a coupling, placing her mouth against the metal before the drop is large enough to fall. Her upper lip fits the curve with the ease of habit.

Below, the black pellet rests in its hollow. For a little while it is still.

When she leaves, the next drop begins to grow.

Her nest is not made of one material. The bottom is a compacted felt of her own hair, fine roots pulled from the turf, and fibers teased from insulation. Above that lie scraps too large to have arrived accidentally: a curled flake of coating, the transparent cover of a dead insect’s wing, a narrow strip of flexible seal. The pieces are greasy from handling.

The seal is the important one. It lies across the uphill side of the pocket, where a seam sweats during the purge. She has pushed its edge into the joint compound and weighted it with two chips of ceramic. Water gathers against it, travels sideways, and drips outside the nest.

It leaks. She repairs it often.

Today she finds one chip displaced. She noses it back, pushes too hard, and sends it over the edge. It lands on the shelf below and begins to walk in the vibration.

She watches it go. Then she backs down, retrieves it in her mouth, and tries again.

Inside the nest, the largest young has climbed over the insulation wad. Its feet are broad and nearly hairless, absurdly finished-looking on its narrow limbs. It stands in the entrance with its belly resting on the sill. When the mother returns, it reaches toward her with its whole front half.

She pushes it back with her face.

It comes out again.

This time she lets it follow.

The journey is less than the length of her body. The youngster keeps three feet down, then four, then becomes unable to decide which one to lift. Its mother waits beneath the shelf with her head turned. Water beads on the young animal’s whiskers. It tries to wipe them away and slips.

Her tail moves under it.

For a moment it hangs with all its feet clasped around the tail, eyes squeezed shut. She stays where she is until it finds the ceramic again. Then she takes it back.

Later, all three will come down to the basin. They will put their noses too far into the water and recoil. They will stalk settled flakes of paint. One will discover the little rise near the drain and remain there, shifting its feet, while the others go on ahead.

For now they fit beneath her.

She returns to the pocket after the shower, carrying a mouthful of fresh fibers. Before settling, she turns several times, pulling at the bedding, bracing her hind feet against the ceiling. The young complain through their bodies: tiny shoves, mouths opening against her skin. She tucks the new fibers around the wet edge and draws them close.

From the passage, only the sole of her foot remains visible.

If someone opens the loose panel, she feels the fastening turn before light enters. Her digits spread against the ceramic. If an arm comes through, she withdraws into the deeper fold, and the young cease moving beneath her.

There are other places she could go. A gap beside the hottest conduit leads into a long dry run. The drain opens onto the station’s exterior. She has been a short distance through both.

But here the wet seam passes beside her bedding rather than through it. Here she can reach the basin without crossing the hot pipe. Here, even asleep, she has a foot against the shelf and her tail around the rib.

When the panel closes again, she waits. Then the gray-pink sole returns to its place, and one small digit lifts, tests the wall, and settles.
