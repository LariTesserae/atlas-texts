The part of it nearest the entrance is a thickness on the underside of the broad leaf.

It is easy to mistake this for the leaf’s midrib. It has the same silver-gray surface, the same subdued finish. But the leaf’s own veins pass beneath it. At one end it narrows into a seam along the iron; at the other it divides into three flattened lobes, fitted between the leaf and a root strap. Each lobe has a shallow depression in its center. There is nothing in the depressions. They are not mouths, and they do not close.

Farther inside, the same substance occurs beneath a mineral shelf. Here it is cream-colored and thicker, pressed into the angle between stone and metal. A green filament passes through it without interruption. Around the filament the cream substance forms a close, raised collar, as neat as the seating of a cable in a manufactured seal.

There is more of it behind the ceramic housing.

Most of what lives here cannot be seen from the entrance.

It has no outline that could be followed without dismantling the garden. Even then, an outline would be difficult to establish. In some places its surface is distinct from the roots; in others, a root changes its texture by such small degrees that the distinction has to be made arbitrarily. The dull silver threads in the broken leaf belong to it. So does a narrow, nearly transparent layer beneath the violet undergrowth. So does something occupying the stamped bowl of the number 6 on the housing, where the black root tissue takes an inward turn and acquires a smooth, pale back.

It is not simply the whole garden. There are leaves to which it has no connection. Several of the pleated rosettes are entirely themselves. They press against it, and it gives them exactly as much room as their shapes require.

Elsewhere the separations are less certain.

A plant near the basin has nine overlapping leaves. Eight contain the usual green tissue. The ninth contains, between its upper and lower surfaces, a flat chamber filled by a single piece of silver-gray substance. Nothing enters or leaves this chamber. There is no stem within the stem, no concealed route by which a smaller creature could have reached it. The leaf and its occupant are distinct in the way the two halves of a joint are distinct: each makes the other’s boundary intelligible.

That is the sort of body it has.

Its exposed surfaces are plain. Nothing on them resembles an eye. There are no arrangements of dots that reward a second look by becoming a face. In a cavity so crowded with paired blades and forked roots, a face would be easy to invent, but it would be an invention.

The closest thing to a head is a pad behind the yellow insulator.

This pad is less than half the size of the insulator and contains a dense succession of folded layers, silver alternating with a green too dark to distinguish from black. The layers are not coiled. Each has its own shape, and each fits against the next without repeating it. A little of the brass contact is enclosed between them. The rest remains exposed.

There is no privileged center there. Behind the housing is another such pad, larger and much less regular. A third is spread so thinly beneath the graphite that it has the shape of the basin rather than any shape one would assign to a body.

The graphite above it retains the impressions of leaves.

Under each impression the pad is minutely different.

It does not remember the pressure. Remembering would put the pressure elsewhere, in a past from which something had been saved. Here the leaf, its impression, and the difference beneath it are all present together. The underside of one serrated edge corresponds to a line of close-set silver wedges. The rounded end of another leaf corresponds to a dark oval without internal detail. Between these is an unmarked expanse as smooth as the back of the ceramic.

This is where the garden is least like a collection of plants and most like someone’s interior.

There is discrimination in it. The exposed brass is not equivalent to the iron surrounding it. The narrow strip of sky under the entrance leaf is not equivalent to the darkness behind the bolt. Each is given a different elaboration in the body: brass among the folded layers, iron against broad gray surfaces, the opening beneath the leaf against the three shallow depressions.

There is no change in any of them.

Its awareness, if that is the right word, does not travel from one thing to another. It has no glance to cast toward the entrance, no attention to withdraw from the rear wall. What touches it has its place within it. The shape of that place is exact.

The sound is part of this exactness.

It is not listening through the depressions beneath the leaf. The note is as completely present in its covered parts as in its exposed ones. The low tone occupies the broad pads; the harder tone is especially distinct in the fine silver structures, though not louder there. The metallic pressure fills everything between.

There is no pulse beneath it.

Put a fingertip against the mounting plate, if a fingertip could survive the contact, and the impression would be of a vast mechanism operating somewhere inaccessible. Against the creature’s surface, it would be the same impression. Yet there is no interior distance here in which an operation could hide. The silver structures have no clearances. The folded layers have no sliding faces. Every part is already against what it fits.

Its body contains the sound as the brass contains its color.

It has no need to eat the plants. No part of it is equipped to scrape or pierce them. Where a root enters its substance, the root is neither damaged nor enlarged. Where its gray tissue occupies a leaf, the leaf keeps its ordinary thickness. There is no store of nourishment, no waste chamber, no reserve awaiting use.

Nor does it keep the garden alive. Some of the plants touch only stone. Their intricate leaves are as complete as the leaves wrapped around its lobes. It is a resident, not the explanation of the place.

There is even a small space it does not occupy.

This lies beneath the perforated strip, toward the rear of the basin. It is triangular, scarcely large enough to admit a grain of coarse sand. One side is graphite, one side metal, and the third is its pale tissue. The tissue has a finished surface there. So do the other two sides. No root crosses the space.

The little vacancy is not an injury or an unfinished portion of the body. It belongs among the things the body is beside.

That matters. Without it, one might think the creature only an appetite for fitting, a substance compelled to enter every interval until nothing remained outside it. But it has an edge even here, where an extension would have been almost immeasurably small.

It is possible to be very thoroughly at home without occupying everything.

Near the entrance, its three flattened lobes lie beneath the broad leaf. The central depressions have slightly different shapes. One is round. One is long and narrow. The third has a straight lower margin, corresponding to the edge of the iron lip.

Beyond them is the blue depth.

There is no transparent membrane across the gap, no last protective wall. The body ends within a fraction of a finger-width of that immense outside. Its silver-gray surface is bare to it.

Nothing in its construction suggests that the height is frightening. Nothing suggests indifference, either. Along the lobe nearest the opening, too fine to see except from the side, the tissue has a green border. It is not present on the other lobes. It fits the exposed edge from end to end.

That is all the provision it has for the abyss.
