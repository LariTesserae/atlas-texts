One of the inhabitants occupies the underside of a ridge.

Only a little of it is exposed: a smooth, pale thickening where the ground folds down toward a pool. Most of its body extends sideways through the ridge in sheets thinner than the skins of the surrounding roots. In some places these sheets lie close together. Elsewhere they separate around a reservoir or follow a bundle of conducting fibers for a considerable distance before joining again.

It has no central swelling. To uncover it whole would require taking apart the ridge, and even then there would be difficulty deciding which tissues to keep.

Where it touches certain neighbors, their cells share a wall.

At other contacts there are two walls, a film of water, and a chemical negotiation that has continued without interruption for an immense span of time. Substances offered on one side cause pores to open on the other. The quantities change. A passage that admitted salts freely may narrow until it passes almost nothing; another, far away, widens in compensation.

The pale thickening beside the pool is the youngest visible part of this inhabitant. It is also older than anything in it. Material enters from beneath, becomes its surface, and is reclaimed. The small crease along its upper edge persists through all these replacements.

There is no reason to call the crease a face. Nevertheless, it is one of the features by which this particular life could be recognized.

Another of its kind would make a slightly different fold.

They are not all the same, though the country makes their differences difficult to see. One retains more water than its immediate work requires. Another releases certain minerals reluctantly, drawing them back through a succession of tissues before finally allowing them to pass into the common flow. One repairs a cut by closing its outer skin first. Another seals the deeper passages and leaves the surface parted until the pressures underneath have settled.

These tendencies extend through their bodies. A disposition found in a membrane under one plain may recur in a narrow strip of tissue several days’ walk away.

Between those places lie portions of other inhabitants.

Their territories are interleaved. A single upright fin may contain the outer tissues of several lives, each continuous with a different breadth of country below. The fin remains one structure. Its inhabitants maintain different conditions within it, separated by boundaries too thin to discover by touch.

This arrangement permits an extraordinary intimacy without requiring agreement.

Most of their exchanges are so well accommodated that they resemble the circulation inside a single body. But there are seams where fluids slow, where an offered compound passes through several transformations before it is accepted. Along one such boundary, two inhabitants spend a little more energy maintaining themselves than either would spend elsewhere.

Nothing swells or discolors. The surface above remains tender and clean.

The disagreement has a shape, however. It is a long, shallow curve beneath the ground. Over time it moves.

One participant closes passages behind that curve and opens others ahead of it. Its neighbor does the reverse. Living tissue is withdrawn, reclaimed, replaced. The amount at issue in any one place is smaller than a strand of hair, but the boundary is very long.

A pool above it may become fractionally deeper.

That is as close as this country comes to the disturbance of bodies struggling.

***

They encounter the world through what enters them.

Water brings the condition of distant membranes. A dissolved substance arrives in a proportion that can occur only when a particular deep organ is under strain. A reduction in pressure travels through fluid before the changed fluid itself arrives. Electrical differences spread along adjoining tissues, faster in some directions than others.

Each inhabitant receives these things across much of its body at once.

It can respond differently to the same substance depending on what preceded it. After a long interruption in supply, certain pores remain unusually ready to open. After exposure to a damaging compound, a succession of inner membranes retains the means to bind it. The material holding this readiness is replaced along with everything else, but the readiness passes into the replacement.

What has happened to them therefore remains active in what they are.

One tract of low, folded growth still maintains an expensive filtering tissue against a substance that no longer arrives from below. The offending source has shifted or exhausted itself. Yet the tissue continues to be renewed, and the clean fluid continues to pass through it.

Elsewhere, a supply route remains doubled because one of its branches failed long ago.

The country contains many such provisions. Seen all at once, they might appear excessive. Seen through the lives that maintain them, each belongs to a particular history of continuing to exist.

There are no old scars on their surfaces. Their histories lie in the work they keep doing.

***

An inhabitant can become two without changing the outline of the ground.

It begins with a region whose internal exchanges grow more dependable than its exchanges with the rest. Perhaps a deep supply has strengthened beneath it. Perhaps the surrounding tissues have become less willing to pass something it needs.

The region establishes its own balances. It stores enough of certain compounds to survive a delay. Its electrical cycles cease to follow those beyond its edges quite so closely. At the remaining connections, passage becomes conditional.

For a long time, separation is still reversible.

Then one connection closes and does not need to open again.

The two lives may continue feeding each other. They may share the same exposed skin, the same tall rib, the same pool pressed between their roots. To someone standing above them, nothing has been born.

But a failure on one side no longer necessarily becomes a failure on the other.

There are also unions. An exchange becomes so reliable that both participants cease maintaining the means to do without it. A reserve dwindles. A redundant process is abandoned. Eventually their continued existence depends on the same uninterrupted circuit.

Neither has swallowed the other. Their former boundary remains, performing some lesser task.

This is why counting the inhabitants would be difficult even with perfect knowledge of the tissue. Some are unmistakably separate. Others are partway through changes whose outcome has not yet been decided.

The large one beneath the ridge is nearly divided in one direction and deeply joined in another.

***

Death occurs here.

A region loses the ability to maintain a difference across its membranes. For a while its neighbors supply what it lacks. Fluids are diverted; stored compounds are released. The assistance may restore the failing processes.

If it does not, the contacts change.

The surrounding lives begin accepting substances they would previously have refused. They draw useful material out through the same passages that had carried support inward. Fine new tissues enter alongside the old ones. The failing region becomes smaller, although nothing above it sinks.

Eventually its last self-maintaining circuit opens.

There need be no dead patch left behind. The place where it lived can be fully alive at the moment it ceases to be.

Something is nevertheless lost. The particular sequence in which it opened its pores, the reserves it preferred to keep, the unnecessary precaution inherited through a thousand replacements—these do not all survive in those that take its substance.

For a while, adjacent inhabitants continue making offers in the accustomed proportions.

The offers receive different answers now.

***

If a person stood beside the pool, the inhabitant beneath the ridge would encounter a compression, a displacement of water, and unfamiliar compounds passing from skin into the wet surface.

It would not encounter a person.

Small passages beneath the weight would narrow. Fluid would move sideways. The stretched outer cells would begin preparing replacement material. If the person remained, the response would spread until other parts of the body were contributing to it.

A hand placed on the pale thickening might feel it become slightly firmer.

This would be easy to mistake for recognition.

In fact, the inhabitant would be preserving the crease along its upper edge, restoring the exchanges beneath it, keeping a nearby reservoir from bearing too much pressure. Across its farthest tissues, other adjustments would continue, some connected to the hand and some to events deep below the country.

When the person left, those responses would subside at different rates.

The footprint would rise. Its edges would disappear. The original grain would return.

Underneath, a few membranes might remain more readily permeable than before. Some minute allocation of reserves might have changed. For a time, perhaps a very long time, that part of the inhabitant would be prepared for a weight that had not come back.

Above it, the pool would stand level against the pale fold.

The fold would keep its shape.
