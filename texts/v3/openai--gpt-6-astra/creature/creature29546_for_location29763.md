The woman who pours the water keeps her smallest fingernail long. With it she can lift a hair of clay from a channel without leaving the broad hollow a tool would make. The nail is yellow, strengthened on its underside with a trace of glue. When it breaks, which happens less often than one might expect, she becomes short-tempered for several days.

Her name is Vey. She is neither the oldest nor the most experienced of the basin workers, but she is good at imagining water before it arrives. She can look at an unfinished floor and see where a spill will spread, which sleeping recess it will enter, where it will stand long enough to soften a pillar’s foot. Other people have to crouch to see these things. Vey usually crouches anyway.

She has broad feet, a narrow face, and one shoulder permanently higher than the other from carrying baskets as a child. A fold of old cloth protects each knee. Her clothes fit closely at the wrists and ankles; a loose hem can take up a surprising quantity of water before its wearer notices.

When the test pour is finished, she catches what she can at the outflow. Some has been lost to the new clay. She records the loss with a nick in a damp tablet and puts the recovered water in her jar.

Then she goes home the long way, because someone has built a wall across the short way since morning.

At first she thinks it is the wrong wall. She stands looking at it with the particular resentment of a tired person confronted by competent, necessary work.

“Already?” she asks the man smoothing its face.

He lifts one hand, palm down, and lowers it a little.

That much sinking. No more explanation is needed. She turns back.

***

People here know one another partly by the way they move through a low opening.

Vey goes shoulder first. Her brother ducks too early and keeps his head lowered several steps after he is clear. A neighbor with a stiff back turns sideways, places one hand on the lintel, and passes beneath it as if allowing someone else to go ahead. Children can impersonate all three with considerable cruelty.

Most adults have a tender place somewhere: a hip rubbed by a basket rim, an elbow that will no longer straighten, a patch of scalp repeatedly grazed against roofs. They are careful about these places in others. A person may grab a friend roughly by the collar, laugh into their face, and still remember not to touch their left arm.

They wash their hands more often than their bodies. Before eating, the fingers are scraped with a thin wooden edge, wiped on a cloth, then cleaned in a little water passed down the table from the youngest to the oldest. Clay remains in the beds of the nails. It gives every hand, even a baby’s, a faintly worked appearance.

There are people who enjoy decorating themselves. Scarcity has not cured them of it. One woman wears six loops of glazed pottery beside each ear, and must hold them still whenever she leans over an open basin. Young men spend hours polishing small squares of black tile to fasten at the throat. A red cord may be unraveled and divided among three sisters, each claiming hers was always the brightest portion.

Vey once owned a strip of cloth dyed a deep blue. She kept it wrapped for years, wearing it only in warm rooms where it would not become wet. Now it lines the collar of her daughter’s coat. Usually it cannot be seen.

She knows it is there.

***

Their home has acquired a buttress.

It comes through the wall beside the cooking shelf and slopes into the space where Vey’s brother used to sleep. For the first few weeks they disliked it. Now there is a lamp niche in its side, a row of pegs driven into its drying surface, and a shallow depression where the brother rests his heels. He says his new sleeping place is better because nobody steps over his face.

Vey thinks he is lying, but appreciates it.

The room is warm for part of the working cycle. A flue passes behind its rear wall, and when the firing crews are busy they move their bedding against it. When the wall cools, they hang a cloth across the room to make the occupied part smaller.

There are five of them, sometimes six. The sixth is an old man from the next passage who comes to borrow heat and stays to complain about the food. He has his own bowl here. No one has acknowledged what that means.

Food grows in places too low or awkward for people to use comfortably. There are beds of pale, pleated fungus on trays of exhausted fiber and kitchen scrap. There are vats whose thick surface can be lifted, rinsed, and pressed into cakes. A few districts cultivate long white shoots in the warmth beside kilns. The shoots are expensive when crisp and cheap once they begin to collapse.

The flat grazers are eaten too, though not all of them and not from every drain. Children learn the distinctions before they are allowed to lift covers alone. A healthy animal clings hard enough that it must be persuaded off with a smooth sliver of tile. One that falls into the hand is left alone.

The old man likes his food browned until the edge nearly burns. This requires more heat than the others consider reasonable.

“You can taste it, then,” he says.

“We can taste it now,” Vey’s brother tells him.

“Yes,” says the old man. “That is the trouble.”

***

Children spend a great deal of time waiting near work.

They wait for a basket to empty so they can carry it back. They wait for a drain to be opened, for a ladder to become free, for an adult to stop holding something that cannot safely be put down. They develop games that can be interrupted without conclusion.

Vey’s daughter makes small people from the clay scraped off tools. They are all kneeling, because upright figures fall over and use more material. She sets them in the seams between paving tiles. Occasionally a repair crew works around one.

She has begun giving them things to do. One holds a thread. One leans into a hole. One has both hands over its ears.

Her uncle objects to that last figure.

“Who is it?”

“Nobody.”

“It looks like me.”

“It hasn’t got a face.”

He turns it around to inspect it. Its back is remarkably like his.

The girl is old enough to fetch water but not yet trusted to decide whether a vessel is clean. She resents this division of responsibility. She knows how to keep the jar from knocking her knee, how to cover the mouth when dust falls, how to stand aside without setting it down. To have it sniffed on arrival seems an insult to the whole journey.

Vey sniffs it anyway.

Once, the girl asks whether the chambers below were built for smaller people.

Some are so low that excavators must lie on their sides to shave the walls. The old man says the floors have risen. Her uncle says the roofs have settled. Both are likely true.

“But were they smaller?”

Nobody knows. They let her go on wondering.

***

The floor-listeners are not uniformly solemn.

One is a large, cheerful woman who needs help getting down and more help getting up. She likes someone to describe what they are cooking while she waits for the district to quiet. Another insists on removing every lamp from the room, though he admits the light makes no difference. A third has almost no hearing through the air and must be addressed with a hand on his wrist.

Their judgments can disagree. People argue about whose mark should be followed, how much space a support really needs, whether an alarming sound came from their own floor or from a crew at work farther down.

After a failure, these arguments are remembered word for word.

After a successful repair, they tend to be forgotten.

Vey’s brother once spent two days beneath a collapsed washroom with a broken ankle and half a jar of water. He heard the rescuers early, then heard them go away to bring supports. He says the going away was the worst part.

Now he dislikes any joke that ends with everyone suddenly falling silent. Otherwise he jokes as much as before.

He has never visited the family that lives in the rebuilt washroom.

He passes them often. They exchange news, lend tools, occasionally eat together. The reluctance is attached to the room, not the people.

***

Something else lives behind their new buttress.

At first they hear it only when the flue warms: a minute clatter, like a fingernail stirring broken cups. Then the girl finds a heap of shed grazer skins in a crack beside the floor.

Her uncle wants to stop the opening. The animal may loosen wet mortar, and its armor makes it troublesome to catch. The girl argues that the skins prove it is eating the things that would otherwise come into their food.

They compromise by pressing a collar of fired fragments into the edges of the hole. This protects the clay and leaves the entrance open.

For several evenings nothing happens.

Then, while Vey is repairing a knee cloth, the creature comes halfway out.

It is narrower without the shadows around it. The tile chips on its back are held among hooked bristles, overlapping badly, with soft joints visible between them. One chip has a blue line across it. Vey recognizes the glaze from a broken serving dish, though it might be another dish from the same firing.

The creature feels along the floor with two pale appendages. Its mouthparts move independently, testing dust.

From somewhere beneath the house comes a mallet blow.

It stops.

Vey, whose needle has caught in a thick seam, stops too.

When the next blow does not come, both resume.

***

There are hours when nobody in the household is improving anything.

The clay is covered. The tools have been cleaned. A cloth blocks the draft at the door. Vey’s brother lies with his heels on the buttress, singing softly through the verses he remembers and humming through the others. The old man has gone home, taking food in a covered bowl while maintaining that he came only to return a scraper.

The girl is asleep with one hand inside her collar, touching the blue lining.

Vey sits beside the lamp and turns her jar slowly, looking for a suspected crack. She finds only a pale mark left by someone’s finger before it was fired. She rubs it once with her thumb.

Tomorrow she will ask for two more workers at the basin. They will probably give her one. The inlet still needs widening. Someone must go underneath to check what the weight will bear.

But the jar is sound, and for the moment she has finished with it.

She puts it on the shelf and moves her feet closer to the warm wall.
