The spider is smaller than the head of the old screw. Out on the paving it would be almost impossible to distinguish from a fleck of sealant, except that the fleck would have a shadow arranged around it in eight fine strokes.

Under the access housing, where it spends most of its time, it is the color of weak tea. Light passes through the lower joints of its legs. Its abdomen is darker, with two pale marks that become visible only when it turns. One hind leg is shorter than its partner. When the spider draws them close against its body, the unevenness disappears.

It occupies a shallow recess where a gasket stops short of a corner. The recess was not designed to remain warm, but warmth reaches it from a coupling inside the housing. There is room for the spider, a little folded silk, and the dry husk of something it ate several weeks ago. At the outer edge, condensation forms. Farther in, the metal is dry.

The spider rests between these conditions.

Its web looks unfinished. There is no round center, no orderly series of spokes. A few lines cross beneath the lip of the housing; others run down to the paving, attaching themselves to points that are no longer quite where they were when the lines were laid. As the channels change, one thread slackens and another tightens. Sometimes a fastening pulls free.

The spider repairs these failures without reconstructing what was there before. It follows the remaining tension, adds a line, abandons a line. Near its recess, old silk has accumulated into something denser than the rest of the web: a small cloudy patch, full of attachments that no longer lead anywhere.

This is the oldest part of its dwelling.

It does not experience the court as a space nine paces wide. Most of the court has never been under its feet. The exposed mountain reaches it as cold, and occasionally as a jolt through the housing when a fragment strikes the paving. The walls reach it in the long tremor of descending water. The maintenance door announces itself before it opens: pressure moves across the web, bowing every loose thread in the same direction.

Then comes the brightness.

The spider withdraws a fraction deeper into the gasket.

There is a person who sometimes kneels beside the housing. Their sleeve sheds pale fibers. Their tools make enormous, distinct events in the metal, separated by intervals during which the spider cautiously extends one foreleg onto a thread.

Once, the housing was opened.

Its roof lifted away. Cold air moved across the place where there had never been moving air. The spider ran into the angle beside the coupling and held itself there while a white light searched the compartment.

When the cover was replaced, most of the web came down with it. A few strands remained attached to the paving. The spider stayed hidden through the rest of that day and began laying new silk after the court had darkened.

The new web was lower. This has made it more liable to collect water.

A large drop changes a thread’s behavior completely. It draws the silk into a steep curve and holds it there. Small disturbances no longer travel cleanly along it. The spider approaches such a drop with its forelegs extended, touching and withdrawing, sometimes putting its mouth to the water. If the drop is too large, it leaves that part of the web alone.

On cold mornings, a bead can freeze before it falls. The burden remains until the warmth returns.

There are days when nothing edible arrives.

The spider grows very still then. Even beside the warm joint, its movements become slow. Its abdomen loses a little fullness. A fly that entered the court yesterday or last week persists inside it as the ability to wait.

When a catch does come, it is usually small: a pale gnat from a service corridor, something with narrow wings that has followed the light through the gray doorway. It may blunder around the court for hours before finding the silk. It may leave without finding it at all.

The spider cannot alter this.

But a trapped fly sends a disturbance unlike water, unlike a door, unlike the steady fine shaking that sometimes arrives through the pipes. The spider comes out quickly. For a few seconds its legs are difficult to follow. It holds itself away from the struggling body, turns it, fastens it, and returns toward the recess drawing the catch behind.

Afterward, the web is quiet in a different arrangement.

There are other living things inside the spider, and on it, too small for the court’s clean daylight to reveal. The bare joints and unstained rock do not make it solitary in quite the way it appears from above. Nevertheless, no second spider has crossed its web since it came here.

It probably arrived through the maintenance door. Perhaps it rode in a fold of fabric, or clung beneath a case set briefly on the paving. It was smaller then. It crossed a distance that would now be dangerous, found the underside of the housing, and stopped.

That stopping has acquired layers: old silk, discarded prey, a translucent skin caught high in the recess. The cast skin still has eight legs. When moisture reaches it, it folds inward; when it dries, it opens a little.

The living spider rests below it.

Tonight the drain will warm. Water will pass beneath the grating, and a new flake will separate from the mountain. The spider may feel the impact. It will lift its forelegs, wait, and settle again.

Later, when the last runoff has left the wall, it will emerge to inspect a loose attachment. One foot will find the edge of a paving groove that was not there yesterday. It will test the surface, move slightly to one side, and fasten its thread.
