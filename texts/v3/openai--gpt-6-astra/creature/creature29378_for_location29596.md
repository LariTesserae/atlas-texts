In the oldest residential blocks, someone is paid to look after the spaces beneath the doors.

The work belongs partly to the heating authority and partly to the department that measures the ground. Neither pays a full wage. Together, with the room that comes with the position, they provide a living.

Mera has been doing it for twenty-six years.

She is broad through the shoulders and has a habit of standing with one foot slightly advanced, as though waiting for someone to finish measuring her for a coat. Her right knee no longer bends all the way. On stairs she turns sideways and takes the weight on her left leg, carrying her tool bag downhill of her body. The bag has worn a smooth patch through three successive coats.

She carries a plane, narrow wedges, a little mirror on a jointed stem, and strips of paper cut from obsolete municipal forms. The paper goes beneath a door. If it passes freely, she writes the date on the frame. If it catches, she finds out why.

Often it is only a hinge.

People are disappointed by this more frequently than one might expect. They have felt a difference in their rooms, heard something during the night, noticed that a cupboard opens by itself. They would like the ground to be responsible. Mera shows them the loose screws. Sometimes they continue telling her about the cupboard while she tightens them.

There are four hundred and twelve doors in her block, including the ones nobody opens. She knows which swell after laundry day and which bind when the foundry starts its second press. She knows where a tenant has laid a rug too close to the threshold and where an old man puts a rolled towel to keep out an animal he claims not to feed.

Her own door is the worst in the building. A length of cord lifts it a little as it swings.

Inside, the room is warm along one wall. The other wall grows a fine white fur behind the dresser.

Mera keeps her bed between them.

***

The people who live here are not especially patient.

They complain about trams, demand repairs before winter, and grow angry when a delivery promised for the morning arrives at dusk. A ridge taking thirty years to clear a railway does not make three hours in a freight office feel brief. There are arguments in queues. There are couples who separate during the raising of an apartment block because, after weeks of inconvenience, one of them says the wrong thing about a borrowed cooking pot.

Mera’s block has a residents’ committee that has been unable to choose a color for the entrance hall in eleven years.

In the meantime, she paints the lower part gray.

Children add things to it with chalk. Usually these are animals, tramcars, or people with very long arms. Recently a child drew a mountain with a door in it. Mera painted around the drawing for several months because she liked the handle, which had been carefully placed low enough for the child to reach.

She has no unusual knowledge of the mountains. When tenants ask whether she thinks the eastern face will finish forming within their lifetimes, she asks which tenants they mean.

Her father lived to ninety-three. Her sister died at forty.

***

There is a narrow cupboard off the entrance hall where Mera keeps the pieces she has taken from doors.

Most are ordinary curls of wood, useful for lighting the little stove in her room. But some doors have needed shortening so often that she keeps samples labeled by apartment and year. The strips hang in bundles from hooks, their painted edges making thin colored lines.

One bundle contains eleven shades of green.

Another is dark with grease from a kitchen where three generations have fried the same kind of flat bread. The youngest of that household now runs a repair stall beside the tram station. When he comes to visit his mother, Mera sometimes smells hot oil on his clothes and finds herself expecting to see him carrying his school satchel.

She has saved the bottom edge of a door that no longer exists.

It came from a room occupied by a woman who made ceremonial hats. The hats were large, impractical things, with stiff folds meant to catch light from several directions. People wore them at marriages and at the opening of new bridges. Mera remembers the woman as elderly, though she has recently worked out that the woman was younger then than she is now.

The room became too narrow to rent after a foundation adjustment. It holds a water tank.

Once a year Mera checks behind the tank with her mirror. There is still a little paper flower caught between two pipes.

***

Her grandson stays with her three nights a week.

His name is Tavi. He is twelve and has begun to dislike being introduced as anyone’s grandson. He likes the freight elevators, the smell of electrical varnish, and a girl whose family maintains the reservoir filters. He dislikes the mineral grazers because one took a glove out of his pocket and chewed it until the lining hung from its mouth.

He wants to work below the cloud walls.

Mera does not tell him that he will miss the city. She has never been far enough down to know what there is to miss instead.

For his birthday she bought him a book of lowland trees. It came through the parcel service with a damp corner and a customs label describing it as agricultural instruction. The trees stand in deep soil, with people beneath them for scale. Tavi studies the people almost as much as the trees. They are wearing thin clothes. In one picture a woman has put her bare hand on a metal fence.

He has pinned that page above his bed.

On mornings when Mera leaves before he wakes, she puts breakfast in a covered pan against the warm wall. By the time he gets up, the underside is usually crisp.

Sometimes the wall has cooled during the night. He eats it cold and leaves a note complaining.

She keeps those notes in the same drawer as his school reports.

***

Several kinds of animal know Mera.

The grazers recognize the sound of her tool bag and approach because blue survey chalk is kept in its outer pocket. She has changed pockets twice. They continue to search the old one first, then lift their narrow faces toward her with an air she finds objectionable.

She allows them the worn ends of chalk only after the surveyors have left.

The channel creatures are less easily persuaded that a person is worth noticing. There is one beneath the laundry outlet with a shell broken along its rear edge. The exposed tissue has healed into a dark, flexible lip. When it moves through shallow water, the lip folds inward.

Mera first saw it seven years ago. Or she first saw an animal with that injury seven years ago; there are disagreements about how long they live.

During basin cleaning it climbs the escape board, settles on the paving, and holds something in each hand. Usually a stem or a pebble. Once it held two screws.

Tavi gave it a third screw. It put down one of the first two before accepting it.

For several weeks afterward he tried to determine whether it always kept the heavier objects. His results filled six pages and established nothing. The creature sometimes chose a leaf over a bolt and once refused everything because it was holding a length of string in both hands.

Mera admires its grip. She has watched it sleep without letting go.

***

The room below hers is occupied by a violin teacher.

They have been lovers, though not recently. His name is Senn. He owns a good table, six chairs, and more bowls than one person needs. On rest days they eat together, usually in his room because she finds it easier to go downstairs while hungry than while full.

He has a narrow face that has grown narrower with age. When listening, he closes one eye. Mera used to believe this was an expression of doubt and would repeat herself more firmly. Now she waits.

Senn does not like the noises below the ground.

He will tolerate machinery at any hour, children practicing scales, the rattling of an unsecured tank. But an answering hammer beneath the cliff makes him stop whatever he is doing. His hand remains on the table or above a pot until it is clear there will be no second answer.

Mera has learned not to speak during that interval.

He wants them to move to the southern shelf, where the buildings are younger and the cellars have been quiet for years. He brings her notices of available rooms. She reads them carefully. They discuss window sizes and tram connections, and whether his table would fit.

There is one notice she has not given back.

***

People assume that her work consists of noticing movement.

Much of it consists of deciding what can be left alone.

A bedroom door may be allowed to scrape. A fire door may not. A widening gap beneath a nursery door matters differently from the same gap beneath a storeroom. Sometimes a resident needs quiet more than an immediate repair, and sometimes Mera recognizes from the way someone asks about a hinge that the hinge should be fixed that day, even if it is working perfectly well.

She has made mistakes.

Once she planed a door in the western stairwell and exposed wood that smelled strongly of apples. By morning the frame had narrowed again. She planed it a second time. The following day a crack opened across the stair above it.

No one was hurt. The landing was closed for nine months.

In the inquiry she was asked why she had failed to report the repeated change.

She said she had wanted to finish before lunch.

That answer remained in the report. Some of her neighbors thought she should have given a better one.

***

Every autumn, the block is lifted for adjustment.

Cupboards are latched. Tanks are partly drained. Residents who cannot manage the temporary stairs are taken to the public hall, where there is tea and insufficient privacy. The violin teacher carries his instrument out himself. Tavi, if he can avoid school, watches the hydraulic crews from behind the barrier.

Mera stays inside until the last inspection.

She goes from floor to floor with her paper strips, testing the doors. At the bottom of each stair she pauses and lets her knee settle. The empty building makes small sounds around her. A spoon moves in a sink. Water knocks once in a pipe that has been shut off. From an apartment on the eighth floor comes the persistent chirping of someone’s forgotten cage bird.

She fetches the bird.

Then she sits outside with it while the crew takes up the weight.

The movement is too slight to see. Men watch gauges. A woman beneath the western corner calls out a number, waits, and calls it again. Mera can feel the change through the soles of her boots before the foreman raises his hand.

For a short time the entire block is supported by machinery. Beneath it, workers sweep away the pale new stone.

Mera sits with the cage on her knees. The bird has stopped calling. Its feet tighten and loosen on the perch as the building rises through some small part of an inch.

When the residents are allowed back, she holds the entrance door open. Senn goes through carrying his violin. Someone asks whether the laundry will reopen that evening. A little girl wants to know if her room is higher now.

“A little,” Mera says.

The girl looks up at the windows, trying to find the difference.

Mera waits until she has finished looking. Then she follows her inside, sets down the cage, and kneels with some difficulty to check the space beneath the door.
