The people who live there are not especially small, though visitors tend to remember them that way. They are accustomed to being seen in portions: a shoulder beyond a tank, two bare calves on a ladder, a face leaning into the light below a leaf. In the central court, standing upright together, they turn out to be of ordinary height. Several are very tall. These have favorite routes through the district, and dislike being asked to take another.

Most wear sandals with thick soles that can be replaced separately from the straps. At home they put their feet on chair rungs. A person deep in conversation may step from one iron plate to another without looking down, the way someone elsewhere might shift out of the sun. The children learn which surfaces will burn them before they learn the names of the streets.

They are not uniformly patient. There is a boy in the mill who hates the mill; there is a woman who turns every valve too far and then has to turn it back. Quarrels can last a long time in a place where raising your voice makes the whole district a participant. People develop other means. A borrowed funnel is returned immaculately clean, but at an inconvenient hour. Someone ceases to use a passage through a neighbor’s kitchen and takes the long way around for eleven years.

The passage is left open all the same.

There are thirty-seven households, or thirty-nine, depending on whether the rooms above the laundry are counted separately. The disagreement is not administrative. Two sisters live there, sharing a stove but no longer sharing meals. One buys oil; the other maintains the wick. Neither will describe the arrangement to an outsider.

Their father was the last person in the district to remember the warehouse in use. He said that before the stone rose there had been a painted horse on an interior wall, advertising something nobody below the square had ever tasted. One plum-colored brick still bears part of a yellow curve. His daughters keep it in the wall behind their stove. Each believes it belongs to her.

The elder sister, Sera, works on doors.

She owns short saws, planes with handles polished nearly black, and a set of wedges she carries in a cloth roll. She can tell from the sound of a latch whether a frame has racked or a sill has lifted. Before cutting, she checks the wall, the hinges, and the nearest patch of foliage. Occasionally she refuses a job.

“Not the door,” she will say.

This is unwelcome news. A door can be shortened in an afternoon. What is happening beside it may require a family to sleep elsewhere.

She keeps the strips she cuts off. Good timber is scarce, and even a narrow shaving may be useful for packing a loose fitting. In her workroom, bundles of labeled wood hang above the bench. Some doors are represented by four or five pieces, each a little different in color. Children are allowed to look for the pieces from their own houses. Sera will give them an unmarked offcut to play with, but not those.

Her younger sister repairs clothes and runs the laundry rollers on alternate days. She is much less interested in the district’s gradual movement. She would like a room where she could spread out an entire coat.

This wish is considered reasonable, though nobody has found such a room.

***

The engine keeper is called Edan. He came down the ramp eighteen years ago to replace a bearing and stayed because there was further work, then because he had begun eating supper with someone, then because leaving would have required an explanation.

He does not think the engine has a personality. He is firm about this. When it hesitates, there is a cause; when it knocks, there is a fault. Nevertheless, he approaches it differently after bad news, and has been observed resting a hand on the casing before going home.

His apprentice, Bel, wants more speed.

She has drawings for a second accumulator and a means of changing the mill’s gearing. The drawings are good. Edan has pinned them on the engine-house wall, where condensation cannot reach them. He has also made her calculate what each improvement would take from the laundry, the ventilators, and the press.

She has done the calculations. They do not settle the argument.

Bel goes above the square twice a week. She returns with metal stock, electrical parts, gossip, and sometimes something useless that operates very quickly. Once it was a little battery fan. People passed it around the court, holding it to their throats. Edan used it longest.

Much of what keeps the district alive comes down the ramp: grain, oil, cloth, replacement metal, salt. What goes up is smaller and worth carrying—repaired instruments, fine-ground pigments, sleeves and collars with almost invisible mending, small fittings made accurately rather than quickly. A few inhabitants work outside every day. They leave in clean shoes carried under one arm and change near the upper door.

The district is not a secret. People above know it exists. Some think it was closed years ago.

This irritates the inhabitants more than being overlooked.

***

There are nine children, not counting a baby who spends her afternoons in a sling beside the coolest cistern.

They are noisy. The quiet of the district is partly the quiet of adults listening for what the children have started.

They race things in the copper rain channels during dry weather: buttons, nutshells, little folded scraps of tin. They are forbidden to race anything once the roots overhead begin to darken. One lost marble lodged in a junction and sent an entire evening’s seepage into a bedroom. Its owner, now nearly grown, is still asked whether he has brought his swimming things whenever he visits that house.

Their games make use of the district’s missing sightlines. A pursuer may be within arm’s reach and three minutes away by the available route. There are gaps only the youngest can pass through, and the older children have to buy their cooperation. The usual payment is wire, colored thread, or news from above.

They know the plants individually, but not always by the names the adults use. There is a fern called the Magistrate because its curled shoots resemble the fingers of an official who once came down to inspect the tanks. A certain vine is called Liar. It withdrew from a brick partition so convincingly that the adjoining room was emptied and shored. Nothing happened.

The props remain in place. Nobody is wholly comfortable taking them out.

One child, Ilen, is trying to grow a plant that will not bend around anything.

She has given it the best light available in her parents’ room and built a square frame of scrap wood to keep neighboring leaves away. At present it is three inches tall and leans toward a warm pipe. She turns the pot every morning.

The adults offer advice until she asks them to stop.

***

The gardeners do not form a separate profession. Everyone gardens badly somewhere; a few people are asked to intervene.

The most sought-after is a broad woman named Rus, who also keeps accounts for the workshop. She carries a brush in a narrow case, small scissors, and a spoon with its handle bent sideways. Her fingers are too thick for many of the seams she tends, so she has made tools that will enter them.

She is not tender with every plant. She removes seedlings from drains, trims roots off inspection covers, and cuts the red-backed vine when it begins to shade the upper rooms. Afterward she wraps the cut ends in damp cloth until they stop leaking.

People sometimes come to watch, particularly when a substantial branch must be removed. Rus gives them something to hold.

She has learned to distinguish several kinds of withdrawal. Leaves turning edge-on may mean strain in the beam above them, but may also mean heat, dust, or an insect too small to see. Creepers loosen from plaster that is beginning to separate; sometimes they loosen from sound plaster for reasons she cannot determine. She marks these places in a notebook and dislikes having her guesses repeated as instructions.

What troubles her most is not how much the plants appear to feel, but how locally they feel it.

A vine will make room for a cracked lintel while sending a new shoot across the path of the person carrying the prop. Roots spare a pipe joint and close over the screws of its mounting bracket. The court tree accommodates the pump handle perfectly, but last year its growth trapped the tool cabinet shut.

Rus and Sera spent two days freeing it.

The plants have requirements of their own. A gap that suits a pipe may suit a stem as well. Leaves need light, roots need cool surfaces, and the waxy growth near the stone occupies warmth that would otherwise be difficult to use. Some arrangements endure for decades. Others need correction every week.

The people who do well here can notice the difference.

***

Few animals establish themselves. The gray snails are tolerated until they begin eating new growth. Then they are collected on slices of peel and moved to the far end of the bathhouse wall. Nobody knows whether these are new snails or the same ones returning.

Moths cause a disproportionate amount of interest. When one appears, someone usually opens the shade of an unlit lamp to give it somewhere to settle. There is a man who claims to recognize them individually. Since he makes no similar effort with people, this is held against him.

Once a cat came down with a delivery and remained for two nights. It drank from a leaf cup, slept on the warm stone, and overturned an arrangement of jars that had worked without incident for sixteen years. Several inhabitants wanted it to stay. Several others escorted it up the ramp.

For months afterward they disagreed about whether it had left willingly.

***

People leave, too.

Some return for meals. Some write. One woman has lived elsewhere for twenty years and still sends money whenever her mother says the walls are being repaired, though she suspects the money is mostly spent on fruit. A former resident brings his children down once a year and tells them how narrow everything used to be. They look at him doubtfully.

Those who remain do not all love the place. There are bad weeks when the tanks run low, the stone is unusually hot, and the upper door admits no perceptible coolness. During these weeks everyone becomes tired of everyone else’s habits. The scrape of a chair is enough.

Then a delivery arrives, or a repaired pump begins working, or someone finds a ripe fruit concealed behind a leaf. Ordinary business resumes without reconciliation.

Under the court tree, one of the six chairs belongs by custom to a woman who can no longer get down from her rooms without help. It is not kept empty. She would consider that foolish. But whoever uses it wipes the arms afterward, because she likes to come down when she can, and knows by touch whether someone has been sitting there with oily hands.

On the days she comes, Bel helps her along the iron plates. Rus moves a pot out of the way. The woman complains that the tree is taking too much of the court.

She has complained of this for years. Recently, she has begun to be right.

A low branch now touches the back of her chair. Sera has offered to shorten the chair, and Rus has offered to cut the branch. The woman refuses both proposals. Instead she sits a little forward, with her hands on her knees, while the leaf above her slowly presses against her hair.

After a while she asks for the scissors.

Not the large ones. The small ones will do.
