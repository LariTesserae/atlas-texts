It lives mostly on the undersides of things.

This is why you can look into the chamber for a long time and see no evidence of it. The dusty tops of the tanks are undisturbed. Nothing has dragged itself across the orange sediment. Even the narrow passages, which seem as though they ought to concentrate whatever movement there is, remain empty. Its routes run above and beside yours: beneath a mounting bracket, along the lower lip of a duct, through the space where a pipe rests almost—but not quite—against brick.

The first part you see resembles a strip of damp packing hanging from a flange.

It is about the width of two fingers, pale gray, with an irregular dark edge. At one end it has gathered into shallow wrinkles. A drop approaches along the pipe, touches it, and disappears.

Then the wrinkles move toward the other end.

There is considerably more of it behind the flange.

Unfolded, the animal would be roughly the length of your forearm, though it rarely occupies that length in a straight line. Its body is divided into soft sections which can narrow and extend independently. One section holds while another reaches. Along either side are short, jointed limbs, usually tucked so close beneath it that their existence is revealed only by the little changes of purchase: a tip releasing a screw thread, two tips testing a crust before accepting its weight.

Its back looks less like skin than something repeatedly wetted and allowed to dry. Fine plates overlap there, translucent at their edges, stained amber where they are older. Powder adheres to them. Mineral grains lodge between them. In some places the animal has the same pale, granular surface as the pipe; elsewhere a recent flex has exposed a smooth patch the color of the inside of a shell.

The plates are part of it. Most of what covers them is not.

It grooms with its rear limbs, but selectively. Deposits near a joint are scraped away. Those along the middle of the back are left alone. On a dry surface, under hot metal, this burden slows the loss of water. It also makes the animal difficult to distinguish from the small failures of the room.

Its head is tucked beneath the first section of its body. You might mistake the rear for the front until the mouth opens against the rust-colored film on the inspection lamp. There is a brief, fine working motion. When it moves on, it leaves an oval no larger than a lentil, black metal showing through.

It cannot eat the rust. It takes the softer growth within it, along with a little of everything else. Later, beneath its resting place, it leaves a hard reddish grain.

There is very little food here. Much of its life consists of arriving somewhere too early and staying.

The lamp is one feeding ground. Another is the damp side of the cable packing. There is a joint high on a condenser where moisture collects around an old fiber washer, and a place under a tank where small drowned things occasionally remain after the water has gone. These are widely separated in the animal’s geography. The shortest route between them may cross a surface that will become lethally hot before it has finished crossing.

It travels with two slender feelers laid against the material ahead. Each ends in a flattened, almost leaf-shaped pad. The pads lift and settle, lift and settle. They sample temperatures too close together for your hand to distinguish.

When the motor runs behind the wall, the animal stops feeding.

The pipe beneath it has not warmed. The lamp has not trembled. Nevertheless it draws its head back and begins to move.

Some of these anticipations are accurate. Others belong to an earlier arrangement of the machinery. At a capped branch pipe it still pauses, sometimes for a long while, before crossing a length of metal that no longer heats. It has carried this hesitation through several changes of skin.

Its eyes are small dark points recessed beneath the forward plates. They register the red indicator and the pale seam, but the most useful light in the chamber is too faint for you to notice: slight changes in reflection as a water surface expands, the sudden brightness beneath a pipe when a bead rounds enough to catch the seam.

It approaches water sideways.

The lowest edge of its body touches first. Beneath the plates, the skin opens into closely pleated folds, and water enters between them. The animal becomes a little thicker. Afterward it may withdraw to a dry bracket and remain there for days, its legs locked around an edge, its mouthparts occasionally moving against one another.

Full immersion is dangerous. The dark-skinned basins offer no firm purchase, and the animal’s weight changes when its folds are full. Once, in the deeper depression behind the stair, it lost several of its smallest legs pulling free. Their replacements are shorter and slightly crooked. They make its movement uneven on smooth metal.

The pale cricket can pass close to it without being pursued. An abrupt jump causes the plates to tighten, but otherwise each seems to occupy a different chamber. The cricket takes the leaves. This animal finds a fallen leaf later, where it has softened against the packing, and works along its wet underside for most of a night.

Sometimes it carries a piece away.

Held beneath the body, the fragment is almost invisible. The animal moves awkwardly with it, using fewer limbs for purchase and resting more often. It takes the fragment toward the back, though not by the human path through the tanks. It ascends the brick jacket of a duct, enters a gap above the service recess, and descends within the wall.

Here, a curled edge of sheet metal shelters a space scarcely thicker than your thumb. The continuous rushing is stronger. The metal stays cool through most of the machinery’s changes.

There are two young in this space.

They are clear enough that the dark contents of their bodies show through. Each is shorter than a tile joint is wide. Their limbs do not yet hold well on powder, and they remain against a small patch of damp fibers packed into the curl of metal.

The adult settles over them without quite touching. Water gathers at the edges of its folded underside. The young press their mouths there.

It stays until its body has flattened again.

The fragment of leaf is left among the fibers. Eventually a fine growth will soften its torn edge. One of the young will begin feeding there while the other still waits under the adult. There were originally three. The empty skin of the smallest remains caught in the packing, almost indistinguishable from a sliver of old varnish.

When you stand in the recess, the adult is a few inches from your face, on the other side of the metal.

Your presence warms the wall.

At first it merely shifts. Then it lifts its front half and holds the sensitive pads against the surface. The warmth spreads differently from the warmth it knows. It arrives over a broad area, without the warning vibration of the motor, and pauses there.

The animal draws one of the young farther into the curl with the inner tips of its forelegs. Its mouth touches the fibers, then the other young. It does not flee. There is nowhere nearby to take them that is both cool and damp.

After you leave, the wall slowly returns to its former temperature.

It waits well beyond that.

By then the basin near the recess has begun to fill. Water crosses a shallow seam and reaches the foot of the brick jacket. When the animal finally comes down, carrying the dry remnant of the leaf, it finds its usual descent interrupted.

The feelers touch the new water. Withdraw. Touch again farther to the side.

For a while it hangs there, the leaf held beneath it, all its small feet gripping the softened brick. Then one section lengthens toward a neighboring bolt. The next follows. Its body makes a low, narrow bridge over the basin.

Behind it, the two young remain in the wall, in a patch of damp no wider than a fingerprint.
