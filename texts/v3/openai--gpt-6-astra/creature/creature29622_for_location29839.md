The door fitter carries her tools in two bags, because she is past the age at which she can afford to walk unevenly.

Her name is Eda. She is small, with a heavy jaw and hair cut close at the back to keep it from lying wet against her neck. On the inside of each wrist she has a pale scar where, as an apprentice, she reached through a collar before testing it. The scars have no special significance to her. If asked, she will show them; if someone makes too much of them, she will describe the infection.

She works mostly in the older inhabited districts, where the buildings have had time to develop individual difficulties. A door may catch because its frame has risen, because the floor has bowed, because a reservoir was filled on the roof next door. Sometimes it catches because someone has painted it badly. She charges the same for determining which.

At a house she has not visited before, she asks for a cup of water and sets it on the floor.

While the water settles, she asks when the trouble began.

People almost always tell her something else first.

***

The inhabitants are not all patient.

There are people who shout at the freight carriages, people who leave a queue and return to discover it has advanced, people who wrench a stiff handle until the spindle breaks. There are young men who run up the cooler stairs taking three at a time. At certain bridges, where carriages approach from behind a wall, the paving bears white marks showing how far a person can be dragged before the counterweight stops descending.

Nevertheless, most children learn early to distinguish movement from hurry.

They watch the brass indicators. They step over chains that have been slack all morning but are beginning to lift. A child may be allowed to cross six bridges alone while still forbidden to put a hand into the gap beside the kitchen floor.

Their games make use of things that change slowly. They push scraps into narrow joints and come back a week later to see which have been gripped. They choose a mark on a rising pier and claim it until it disappears into the sleeve above. There are arguments about whether a mark can still belong to you when no one can see it.

Eda’s younger son once kept a spoon trapped under a stair for nine months. Every afternoon he tried to pull it out. When the maintenance crew finally released it, he had lost interest, but she brought it home anyway.

It is an ordinary spoon. She uses it for measuring abrasive.

***

Most people have several kinds of footwear and very little space to keep them.

There are thick-soled street sandals, soft house shoes, and the heavy boots issued in the formation yards. Boots are stored soles outward, so that cracks can be seen before someone puts them on. In poorer households they hang above the bed. The smell of warmed sole compound is one of the smells people remember when they leave.

Clothing is loose at the armpits and close at the ankles. A trailing hem can wick hot water from a drain without its wearer noticing until it touches the skin. Work coats have no large exposed buttons. Jewelry is worn on cords, or beneath cloth.

These are precautions, not a style everyone accepts. Girls on the upper commercial streets wear polished metal cuffs directly against their wrists. Some have shallow burns. They turn the cuffs to hide them from their mothers.

People who work below the street often come home chilled. They have spent hours beside cool-water lines, in a draft kept moving to protect some piece of equipment. They sit on warm benches with their palms flat beside their thighs. People who work above the street stand over those same benches and complain that there is nowhere cool to sit.

A household argument may begin with the position of a chair.

***

Food arrives from below in sealed containers, most of which must be sent back.

At the markets there are bins for lids, bins for seals, and a clerk whose job is to reject damaged containers. The food sellers consider him the most powerful person in the square. He considers himself underpaid.

Most staples have been dried, pressed, salted, or otherwise persuaded to survive the journey. There are pale cakes that must be soaked overnight, dark meal cooked with oil, little sour fruits sold by weight. Fresh leaves are expensive and troublesome. When available, they are displayed with their cut ends in second-condensation water, under a cloth that the seller lifts for serious customers.

People do not necessarily like them.

Eda thinks a leaf is something that takes too long to chew. Her older son, who works in the freight accounts office, buys them whenever he can. He says they taste of the country below the cloud. He has never been there.

His grandmother had been there twice. She disliked being asked about trees, which visitors seemed to believe were the only thing worth remembering. She remembered a room with a cold floor, a woman who had cheated her over a deposit, and the noise of water falling outside all night.

For years after her second visit she dried the bottoms of cups before setting them down.

***

There are people whose work is to touch new surfaces.

They enter the molding houses in pairs and keep their hands inside padded mitts until the instruments agree that the inspection face is cool enough. Even then they touch it first with the backs of two fingers. Their task is to find what the gauges miss: a shallow looseness, a dragging texture, a patch that gives slightly and recovers.

They are called finish readers. Some are very well paid. Some lose the sensitivity in their hands before they have paid off their training.

One of Eda’s customers, a reader named Venn, has not worked for three years. His fingers look normal. He can sew a button, pick a small bone out of fish, catch a dropped coin. But if two cups are set before him, he cannot reliably say which is warmer.

He has a pension dispute that fills a drawer.

He also breeds the little condenser animals.

Breeds is his word. His neighbors say he leaves dirty plates around. Behind the cool wall of his sleeping room he has arranged a series of copper strips, shallow gutters, and removable covers. In the wet season the creatures come out in sufficient numbers to make the metal seem to loosen into separate pieces.

He recognizes differences among them: the long-backed ones, the ones with a dark joint on each hind foot, the almost transparent young. He insists they have preferences. When he offers two pieces of gutter film, they do not always choose the wetter one.

Eda believes him about this, though not about everything.

He has tried to give her a colony twice.

***

The city has landlords, debt collectors, sweet sellers, schoolrooms, and rooms rented by the hour. Its inhabitants fall in love across narrow slots of air and discover, after moving in together, how much of the attraction depended on having separate windows.

There are families who have occupied the same address for a century without occupying quite the same volume of space.

Their leases contain measurements taken from municipal reference points. Every few years these must be reconciled with the actual rooms. Sometimes a cupboard becomes legally part of the neighboring property. Sometimes a disputed strip is too narrow for either party to use, and remains locked until the next survey.

People inherit such disputes.

They also inherit water rights, access through private stairwells, and obligations to turn a jack on a particular date. A person may own nothing larger than a bed yet be responsible for one-sixteenth of a bridge.

These arrangements are difficult for newcomers. Eda once spent an afternoon explaining to a recently arrived tenant that paying the passage fee did not entitle him to close the passage door.

“But it opens into my room,” he said.

It did. His bed was visible from the landing.

She helped him hang a curtain. He was still angry, and was right to be.

***

Newcomers do arrive.

Some have contracts. Some have relatives. Some have heard that a growing city must always need more hands. They stand in the station court looking up through the bridges, trying to understand which level is the street.

For the first few days they waste water washing dust from their shoes. They sleep poorly because their beds are warm. They speak loudly in the lanes, then grow self-conscious when every word returns from the opposite wall.

Those who stay learn routes by temperature as well as distance. They learn which fountain is worth a detour, which public room allows people to sit without buying anything, which landlord waits until the cloud season to show an apartment.

Some leave as soon as their contracts permit. They send letters asking how anyone can bear the heat.

Others write asking whether their old room is still available.

***

In the northern blocks live many people who cannot afford an established inconvenience.

Their rents are low because no one yet knows what will go wrong. They move into apartments with excellent light, new pipes, and corridors wide enough for two people to pass. Then a foundation behaves differently from its neighbors. A flight of stairs must be closed. Water is available only after midnight. For six months, everyone on the fourth level enters through a school.

The residents become expert witnesses to their own buildings. They keep notebooks. They mark the widening of cracks on paper strips and photograph water levels in bowls. At meetings they interrupt engineers, sometimes unfairly.

Eda likes working there less than she expected. New work gives her no history to read. She can make a door close today, but she cannot promise it will open tomorrow.

A woman in one of these blocks has painted her whole entrance yellow.

Every time the frame is adjusted, she paints it again. There are yellow shavings in the joints, yellow fingerprints beneath the latch. She keeps the paint in a food tin wrapped in damp cloth.

She is not cheerful. She wanted a yellow door.

***

The larger animals in the shafts are known principally through things people decide not to investigate.

A cable basket is left where it is. A disused access hatch is screwed shut, then quietly unscrewed by someone on the next shift. Dust is swept up to a threshold but not beyond it.

There is no agreement about whether this is kindness or caution.

Once, called to repair a service door below a collecting court, Eda found something holding it from the other side.

It yielded a little under pressure, then returned the door gently to the same position. She waited. She put her ear near the panel. There was no breathing that she could hear, only a faint intermittent sound like the edge of a heavy sack moving over the floor.

She wrote **access obstructed** on the work slip.

When she returned two days later, the door swung freely. In the dust was a broad clean area and, beside it, two small depressions.

She charged for both visits.

***

On cloud days, Eda stops work early if she can.

Her tools do badly in the wet, and customers are distracted by open doors, shifted furniture, cloths laid out to catch moisture. She goes home along a route with continuous railings. Somewhere across the slot outside her kitchen, a man sings when he believes the mist is thick enough to keep him private.

She has heard him for eleven years. He knows only part of the song.

Her younger son lives farther north now. He comes to dinner without warning because advance arrangements make his visits feel like an obligation. Her older son sends word several days ahead, for the opposite reason. When they are both there, they argue about whether she should keep working.

She lets them.

After they leave, she washes the cups and checks the soles of her sandals. She lays out the two tool bags and shifts a file from one to the other, balancing them by lifting each a little way off the table.

Her own door has begun to catch.

For now she has to lift it by the handle as she pulls. The movement is easy enough, and she does it without thinking. There are three paid jobs waiting in the morning.
