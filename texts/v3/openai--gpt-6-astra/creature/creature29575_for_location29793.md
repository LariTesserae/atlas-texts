The larger animal in the crack has been visible for some time before you understand how much of it you have seen.

What appeared to be a pale chip is the side of its head. A narrow thickness beneath it, apparently the rounded lip of the stone, is a forelimb folded lengthwise. Farther back, where the crack turns and the blue light fails, part of its body lies against the wall. Water passes between the body and the limestone. Occasionally this water lifts an edge of skin, and the edge settles again.

It is about the length of a hand, though it rarely occupies that much space in any one direction. Its body bends readily sideways. The ribs are short, the belly narrow, and the limbs emerge at slightly different angles, allowing it to brace against surfaces that offer no corresponding footholds. At rest it has the untidy arrangement of something caught in a crevice. Only when it moves does each part disclose its use.

The head moves first, a little farther into the dark.

One forefoot releases. Its toes unfold from a shallow socket, longer than they looked while gathered together. Each has a broad, soft underside and a thin rim that lifts last from the wet stone. The foot reaches past the head and fastens above it. Then the body draws forward, the skin along its flank collecting into two loose folds.

The whole movement takes several minutes. There is no slipping, no sudden correction. It has tested the next place before relinquishing the last.

Its whiteness is imperfect. Beneath the skin are cloudy bands of tissue, a faint branching of vessels, a long gray interior shadow. The head is more opaque than the body. Fine mineral deposits roughen the older skin over the shoulders, especially where it lies continually beneath a seep. Small flakes come away when the animal presses through a constriction. For a while afterward those places are almost transparent.

It has eyes, but they are buried beneath the skin and show only as two dim points. The long appendages beside its mouth are more active. They reach independently into the water, one held against the rock, the other extended across the crack. Their tips are divided into several threads.

The roar passes through it continually.

There is no interval of quiet in which to detect approaching movement. Instead, it knows the water immediately around its face: where a sheet thickens, where a current detaches from the wall, where something has interrupted the fine, dependable pull toward the depths. Its sensing threads rest in familiar positions under familiar pressure. A crustacean moving upstream bends one of them differently.

The animal does not necessarily pursue.

Food is scarce enough that pursuit has its own cost. Often it remains where it is, the mouth slightly open against a route along which small things travel. Its lower jaw fits a shallow groove in the limestone. Water enters at the corners of the mouth and leaves through narrow openings behind the head. Inside are soft combs that gather what the current carries: fragments of the gray film, loosened cells, a molted shell, matter brought through the porous mountain from somewhere that has soil.

This is most of its feeding.

When a living crustacean comes within reach, the mouth opens farther than the quiet shape of the head suggests. The floor of the throat drops. A small volume of water disappears into it, taking the crustacean along. The sensing threads sweep backward in the disturbance and slowly return.

Nothing reaches the surface of the pool.

Several of the little translucent grazers may live within a finger’s length of its forefoot. They pass over its skin, picking at the deposits there. The animal tolerates them until one enters the short reach of its mouth. Even then, it sometimes lets the opportunity go.

Its usual territory comprises part of a crack, the underside of a shelf, and two pools connected by a channel too narrow to admit its head. It reaches the second pool by going beneath the first. The journey crosses several changes in water pressure and includes a place where it must turn onto its side. It makes this passage so seldom that a person visiting the basin for a season might reasonably believe there were two animals.

There are others, but not in every suitable hollow.

Much of the clean, cold water carries too little food. Some cracks descend too directly into the heavy flow. Others narrow beyond a bend and offer no chamber in which a body can remain without continually working to hold its place. The occupied sites are small irregularities in this arrangement: a backwater receiving a steady drift, a rough ceiling above a quiet pocket, a seep that sustains a thicker film.

An animal may spend years within such a site. Its feet find the same sockets. Its flank rests against the same slight bulge. As it grows, a fold of skin deepens, a hindlimb takes a new purchase, its tail extends farther around the corner.

The young begin in narrower places.

A few eggs adhere beneath a lip of stone where the water is scarcely deep enough to cover them. Their coverings are clear and tough. From above, they resemble little distortions in the wet surface. The parent stays downstream, its body loosely blocking the easiest exit from the hollow.

When a young animal emerges, it is mostly head and tail. Its feet cannot yet span the grooves the adult uses. It travels instead through the smaller channels cut into their sides, finding shelter in pits that would hold no more than a drop if the supply ceased. There it feeds from the film with a mouth too small to take even the crustaceans.

For a long time it remains within the same few inches of stone.

Growth brings it to edges it could previously pass beneath. A socket becomes too shallow. A channel pinches its shoulders. Eventually it follows the water out of the nursery and reaches a surface broad enough to expose its whole body to the light.

It crosses slowly, with all four feet spread.

Above it, the white basin continues toward the cloud. Beneath it, the current presses into a dark opening. At the opening’s rim the young animal pauses, lowering its face until the threads beside its mouth enter the flow. One foot remains fastened to the bright stone while the others feel below.
