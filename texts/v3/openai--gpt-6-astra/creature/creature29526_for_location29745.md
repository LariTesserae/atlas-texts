The largest one has an eyelid that does not quite close.

It occupies the recess above the pressure gauge, where the steel has been countersunk to receive a bolt head. Its back follows the circle so exactly that it seems, at first, to be a washer made of flesh. But a heel rests beside its chin. There is a little hand beneath its belly, palm outward, with four fingers and a thumb no thicker than a copper strand. Along its flank the gills stand open. Their inner edges are red, their outer edges almost transparent.

Its eye is turned toward the inspection plate. Not toward a screw hole or any possible source of light: toward the broad, opaque middle.

The incompletely lowered lid gives it an expression of exhausted attention. This is misleading. There is no evidence that it has ever been tired. The skin under the eye is smooth; the folded hand has no pressure mark. Even the pale blue patch against the steel looks less like bruising than the color of a deep pool seen through ice.

There are eleven of these animals, if each recess contains only one.

In the narrowest recess, two faces are visible. They share so little room that one animal’s upper lip rests against the other’s brow. Their bodies cannot be distinguished behind the bolt. A fringed appendage emerges below them, neither quite a tail nor an external organ, bearing a row of small, closed sacs. Through each sac shows a darker speck. These might be young. They might be structures with no counterpart in anything large enough to have been named.

All eleven have open mouths.

The noise is not a warning. No one in the cavity has room to obey a warning. Nor is it a summons, since the spaces into which another animal could come are already occupied. Their tongues lie low, exposing the soft ridges of their palates. Their throats have the stretched, lustrous look of something under pressure. The cry seems to belong as much to the shape of the bolt recesses as to their bodies.

Near the largest animal’s mouth, a transparent crustacean holds two antennae between its jaws.

The antennae are not bitten through. They enter at the corner of the mouth and lie along the gum, where a tooth has not yet fully emerged. The crustacean itself rests upside down against the underside of the ceramic. Its legs clasp a shallow imperfection that a human finger could not detect. Within its shell, a bead-shaped heart is narrowed at the middle. A dark filament in its intestine stops at a bend.

Perhaps it is cleaning the larger animal. Perhaps it has been caught. The two possibilities are equally complete in the available space.

Most of the crustaceans have broad backs and short, complicated limbs. Their transparency does not make them simple. Looking into one is like looking through several instruments stacked in a drawer: a forked brace, a coiled thread, a row of pouches, something toothed that might be a jaw but lies too far behind the head. The green indicator passes through them and collects along their joints.

They fit the channels between the ridges with their heads facing alternately in opposite directions. Each abdomen overlaps the shoulders of the next. At certain points a hooked foot is engaged beneath a neighbor’s shell, so that the entire row would have to loosen before one body could leave.

None bears the rubbed patches such close packing should produce.

Below them, the milk-colored worms are wider than they first appear. Their thin margins extend under several animals at once. Some have a faint central stripe. Others contain paired white bodies, opaque even in the green light. Their mouths are muscular rings on the underside, pressed to eggs, to the ceramic, to other worms.

One has wrapped itself around a copper crossing. The wire is visible through its flesh as a dark diagonal. At the point of contact, minute branching vessels spread toward the metal. There is no wound, no scar, no evident separation between holding on and being attached.

The fine red animals beneath the worms are not a mat. Each stands on a tiny base, with its body raised into the water. Magnification would reveal a crowd of narrow torsos, each crowned with two combs. Every tooth of every comb is divided again. Their redness comes from a single internal loop so close to the surface that the water seems to have been sewn through with blood.

A space exists between each animal and the next, but only large enough to accommodate the combs.

Higher up, the ceiling creatures have a different kind of room.

Each lives curled inside its own drop, back toward the steel and limbs gathered toward the bulging underside. Six eyes form an uneven ring around the head. Two look outward through the rounded water; two look inward at the body; the remaining pair are set on short stalks bent toward the point where the drop adheres.

They have no obvious mouth. Beneath the eyes is a small depression surrounded by clear bristles. Something pale occupies the depression in several of them, as though food has been reduced to the size and color of a bubble.

One drop contains an adult and a smaller creature pressed belly to belly. Between them lies a fan of limbs too numerous to assign. The smaller one has only four dark eyes. Where the other two would be, its skin contains clear lenses.

In another drop, a claw extends beyond the water.

The exposed tip is dry. A bright meniscus grips the joint immediately behind it. That distinction—dry tip, wet joint—is the entire reach of the creature into the air. Beyond it are several millimeters of blue-black vacancy, crossed by the compressor’s roar.

The inhabitants nearest the plants are almost invisible because they are green-black too. They adhere along the stems in short segmented strips. What looks like a thickened leaf base is a head with its mouth sealed around a pore. What looks like a bud scale has legs folded underneath.

There are eggs in the nutrient gel, each enclosed in a clear angular capsule. The roots pass among them without displacement. One root ends against a capsule wall, its pale growing tip flattened slightly at the point of contact. Inside, an animal has developed a spine and a single dark eye. The rest is a cloudy crescent.

These are not unformed creatures. Their unfinished portions have their own precision. The eye is exactly an eye; the cloudiness beside it is exactly what occupies that place.

At the edge of the largest ridge, the foremost shelled animal stands with its underside exposed. Its rear feet remain in water. Its front feet are planted on the ceramic above the meniscus. The pleats beneath its body are finer than the threads of the copper mesh, and at the center of them is a small oval opening.

Those behind it are differently equipped. Some have taller shells. Some carry fringes along the shell margins. One has a disproportionately heavy forelimb lifted over the back of its neighbor. They do not look like repeated copies of a single species so much as close relatives for whom different arrangements of the body have proved necessary.

Ahead of the foremost animal, there is a bare patch.

It is scarcely wider than its head. The ceramic there is wet and faintly pink in the filament’s light. Beyond the patch rises the glossy neck of material joining the ridge to the nozzle.

The animal’s front feet point toward it. Its feelers extend almost to the white surface, with a minute clearance between their tips and their reflections. Under its raised body, between the intricate pleats, a bead of water contains the upside-down image of the animal immediately behind it: shell, lifted limb, open mouthparts.

The bead is still attached. The feet are firmly placed. There is enough room for the next step.
