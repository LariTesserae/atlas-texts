The woman responsible for the three water cups below the eastern ridge has a pale mark around one ankle where something tried to swallow her foot.

She was small when it happened. The cup was small too, no deeper than a cooking bowl, and nobody had thought to keep children away from it. As it contracted, she put her weight on the rim. The rim folded over her ankle and held her until her brother cut it. For years he included this in every account of his accomplishments.

Now she visits each cup before dawn and touches the rim with the back of her hooked knife. A relaxed cup gives beneath the blade and slowly returns. A cup preparing to drain stays indented for a moment, then grips.

She does not put her hand in the water to find out.

Her name is Seli. She lives with a sister, two children belonging to that sister, and an elderly man who was once married to their mother. He has his own house but sleeps in theirs because its floor is quieter. His house is used for drying things. When annoyed, he refers to it as his house; otherwise it is the drying house.

Seli is neither the oldest person in the settlement nor the person others consult about distant places. Her knowledge extends with extraordinary precision over a piece of country that a fast traveler could cross before the heat became dangerous. She knows which seams lift in wet weather, which galleries carry the knocking of the large underground animals, and where a stretch of seemingly sound rind has become thin enough to show a red glow when a lamp is held against it at night.

Beyond the western signal frame she sometimes gets lost.

Her work begins in the dark, with her feet.

She unwraps them, checks between the toes, and scrapes away anything that has settled into the warm folds overnight. Most of what comes away is dust. Occasionally something uncurls.

Like most people here, she has thick skin over the balls of her feet and surprisingly tender arches. Shoes are worn inside the house as well as outside it, but a person must sometimes feel what a sole conceals. There are places she crosses with one sandal on, placing the bare foot briefly, lightly, then moving it back onto the broad upper surface of the shod foot. Seen from a distance, she appears to stop and balance for no reason.

A weak gallery roof does not always sound hollow. Sometimes it is softer than the surrounding surface; sometimes it has a faint, irregular tremor; sometimes there is only an unfamiliar warmth.

She has been wrong twice. Once the collapse stopped at her knee. The second time she lost a basket and a knife she still describes in detail.

Her present knife has a metal edge shorter than her thumb, fitted into a broad wooden blade. Most of its apparent size is handle. She uses it for cuts that must be clean. For almost everything else she carries shell, including a serrated piece wrapped in cord and a little spoon-shaped scraper inherited from her mother.

The spoon has been repaired so many times that only the curved tip is original.

At the water pockets she first removes the night’s dead.

There are always bodies: small winged animals with drowned hairs, husks still gripping the fronds, pale things from the drains that have risen too far and failed to return. She lifts these with a forked strip of rind and sorts them on a leaf. Some go into a covered basket. Some she drops down a waste opening well away from the water. A few she crushes immediately beneath the flat of her sandal.

The distinctions are difficult to learn. A harmless drowned animal may resemble the empty skin of something that is still nearby. Another must be removed without breaking it. Its eggs survive in water, and the first sign that someone has swallowed them is an itching behind the teeth.

Children learn the sorting from wooden models before they are allowed to help.

The models are larger than the real creatures. Their dangerous parts are painted white.

Seli maintains the cups rather than owns them, though the difference can become contentious in a dry season. She cuts back fronds that shed irritating hairs. She props a shaded fold open with a wedge so that condensation reaches the smallest basin. Where one cup has begun to thicken across its drain, she makes a shallow cut and keeps it from healing until the flow improves.

She cannot make a cup fill. Her skill lies in recognizing what is preventing it.

People bring her food, lamp fat, husk cord, and occasionally a useful scrap of metal. They also bring advice. A man whose basin has gone bitter once every hot season for twenty years regularly explains how she might prevent hers from doing the same.

She listens close to his ear, nods, and gives him the heavier end of whatever needs carrying.

By sunrise the water has been ladled through a dense filter cloth into closed vessels. The cloth is boiled later. A clean one is pale yellow; a used one can become almost black without making the water look dirty at all.

Nobody likes this fact. Seli makes the children look at it.

On the return journey she checks her animals.

They are called hem-eaters. A grown one is about as long as her hand, with a flat, jointed back and a fringe of short legs tucked beneath it. At rest it looks like a curled shaving. Turned over, it is glossy and extravagantly alive: legs combing the air, little mouthparts working, two soft feelers searching for the nearest edge.

Wild hem-eaters live beneath the overlapping leaves. They feed on the small grazers that chew the shaded margins, but will also eat the tender margin itself. Around a house, a few are useful. Many can take the weatherproofing off a wall.

Seli keeps seven in shallow covered trays.

She breeds them for reluctance to climb, a quality that has proved less stable than she hoped. Each morning she places them in the scraped ring around the house, where they hunt the animals exposed by her sister’s broom. Before the heat rises, she collects them again.

Six are usually where she expects them.

The seventh has a notch in its back plate and frequently reaches the roof. Her sister wants it killed. Seli says it produces the best young. Her sister says that its young will also be on the roof.

This argument has lasted through several generations of hem-eater.

They are not affectionate creatures, but they distinguish familiar handling. In a stranger’s fingers they clamp shut so tightly that their own legs may be trapped between their plates. On Seli’s palm they flatten and move toward the shade of her wrist. She keeps a thumbnail lifted slightly from its bed to give them an edge to follow when she needs to turn them around.

The nail is thick and yellow. She has considered removing it. It is too useful.

Inside the house there is little that rests on the floor. The cooking platform stands on four broad feet, each surrounded by a shallow dish of bitter resin. Baskets hang. Clothing hangs. Tools hang with their sharp sides facing inward, because a child once swung from a hammock into the wrong part of a knife.

The family’s possessions move gently even when no wind reaches them. There is always some motion conducted through the supports.

They know the ordinary movements well enough to sleep through them.

Seli’s hammock hangs nearest the doorway. Beside it is a strip of smooth wood with several deep grooves. If someone needs to wake her, they draw a hard peg firmly across the grooves. The vibration travels through the hammock cord into her shoulder.

She dislikes being shaken awake.

The old man sometimes uses the wake-strip merely because he wants company. She complains but generally gets up.

Conversation in the house takes place at close range. Someone leans toward someone else, says a few words, and retreats into the general noise. A story can pass around a meal by stages, with people laughing at different times. The younger child sometimes laughs before being told the funny part, which infuriates the older one.

They have small gestures for ordinary things: more food, move your knee, something is crawling on you. The last is made by touching two fingers to the corresponding place on one’s own body. A quick touch means a nuisance. Fingers held still mean that the other person should not move.

A visitor once mistook the second gesture for an invitation to scratch.

They still discuss him.

During the hottest hours Seli works on the house.

The wall beside her hammock has begun to bow inward. Its lower strips were cut from young tissue and insufficiently dried. Now, whenever the ground beneath them grows, they swell and take up some of its moisture. She could replace them, but the upper weave remains good and there is no spare material of the right width.

Instead she has shaded a narrow strip outside with a mat. Each day she moistens it with the water left from washing the filters. When it is soft enough, she will wedge it outward, drawing the base of the wall away from the room.

The process has occupied much of a season.

A patch beside it grows too quickly. She scrapes this every other morning and rubs it with pulp from a plant found two ridges away. Her sister believes the pulp is losing its strength. Seli believes someone is emptying cooking water there.

Neither has caught the other doing anything conclusive.

There are people elsewhere who say the country must not be cut. Seli has met two. One accepted water from her cup without asking how the drain had been cleared. The other refused it and became ill before reaching the next settlement.

She has no objection to the belief itself. She would prefer its adherents to travel in the wet season.

Nor does she assume that everything beneath her belongs to one creature. Some tissues join cleanly; others kill each other on contact. A wound can close from the middle, from the edges, or not at all. In one place, a buried layer may be alive while the surface above it is dead. In another, something that appears dead will put out translucent filaments as soon as it is shaded.

She names these conditions individually. Questions about whether the whole country is one being generally reach her as questions asked by someone with no repair work to do.

Nevertheless, when a deep sound begins beneath the house that she has not heard before, she stops.

Everyone stops.

They set down bowls, release taut cords, lower themselves so that more of their bodies touch the floor. Sometimes one person crawls outside and looks toward the neighboring roofs. A white paddle held edge-on asks whether the disturbance is there too.

Usually the sound passes.

Once, during her mother’s lifetime, it continued for nine days. The family moved their hammocks outside. Warm damp air seeped through the scraped ring, and a seam opened across the cooking place. Nothing emerged except a smell so sweet that it spoiled everyone’s appetite.

The seam closed. They rebuilt the platform a little farther over. Years later, Seli’s mother could still identify vessels that had stood in that air by smelling their insides.

In the evening Seli goes back to the cups, walking beside the migration channel rather than in it. The browsers use it after sunset, and they do not easily surrender a route worn into the ground by their ancestors.

She knows particular animals by their feet and scars. One old female has a damaged outer toe that leaves a short curved mark beside each right forefoot impression. Seli has watched her arrive with young, arrive without young, disappear for a season, and return.

She does not approach her. A browser can break a person’s ribs with a sideways movement of its head.

Sometimes the animal waits above the pocket while Seli finishes. It stands pale against the darkening slope, its underside almost lost in shadow. When Seli withdraws, it lowers itself among the fronds with astonishing care.

At this hour the vents begin releasing their winged inhabitants.

Seli covers the water vessels and waits beneath a fold until the first heavy passage is over. Small bodies strike the rind around her. Something much larger may settle nearby, clinging with hooked feet while its folded wings pulse. She keeps her hands inside her sleeves. She has learned which visitors resent the smell of exposed skin.

The nearest signal frame shows three white squares. Farther away, another answers.

She cannot see her own roof from the pocket, but she knows her sister will have brought in the drying strips and checked the lids of the jars. The children will be watching the flights through the space above the wall. The old man will complain that someone has moved his sandals.

She waits for a gap, then lifts the vessels.

Back at the house, she counts the hem-eaters before putting them away.

There are six.

She stands quite still with the tray in her hands. Then she looks up along the bowed wall, beneath the roof, into the dark warm space where the lashings disappear.

Her sister is watching her.

Seli sets down the tray and fetches the ladder.
