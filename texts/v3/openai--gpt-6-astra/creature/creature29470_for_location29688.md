Just below the rim, where the pale rind turns downward, something has stitched the cliff together.

The stitches are widely spaced. Each crosses a crack, enters a small hole, and disappears into the wood. They are gray in the older stretches and yellow-white where new. Wind has polished their exposed surfaces. Around some of the holes the continent has grown a raised lip, so that the stitches seem to pass through little pursed mouths.

One loosens.

Its middle lifts clear of the crack. A narrow body follows, withdrawing from a shelter scarcely wider than a saw cut. For a moment the animal hangs by its hindmost legs. Then it reaches sideways, catches the cliff, and draws the rest of itself out.

It is about the length of a human forearm. There are eight legs, folded in alternating directions, and a head that can be pulled almost entirely beneath the first plate of the back. The body is so flat that its organs occupy shallow compartments between load-bearing struts. Its gut winds around the leg sockets. Most of its water lies in two long, sealed reservoirs protected along the flanks.

The stitches are its work.

There are dozens of these animals within reach of one another, though only three are outside. The rest have closed their shelters with overlapping back plates. Dust in the joints makes them almost indistinguishable from the surrounding rind.

They live where the ground is always threatening to peel away.

***

The animal’s feet have two grips.

One is a fan of small hooks for the dead surface. The other is a soft, divided pad, usually kept folded and waxed, which can hold fresh tissue without puncturing it. Between them sits a blunt wedge. When neither hooks nor pads can find purchase, the wedge enters the grain and turns.

A foot placed badly can start a split that travels farther than the animal can run. Young ones spend much of their first season learning how little force is enough.

At present the cliff is drawing inward. The motion is too slow to see, but the stitches tighten one after another. The animal lifts its forward feet and waits with its weight on the rear six. A pressure pulse passes beneath the shelter. Only when the wood begins to relax does it move again.

It walks diagonally, crossing the grain in short stages. Each foot remains in place until another has been tested. The gait looks hesitant. It is not. Hesitation would interrupt the animal’s continuous knowledge of the surface.

Under its chin are four little feelers ending in broad, dry discs. It sets two against the cliff.

Through these it can distinguish the nearest pump chamber from the more distant districts. It also feels the small irregular knock of a grazer scraping above the rim, the rasp of another of its kind working inside a hole, and a soft running disturbance deep in the rind.

That last vibration makes it withdraw its head.

Something long and jointed is hunting in the layer beneath its feet.

The animal stays still until the disturbance has gone.

***

They feed at the living boundary.

A suitable site is a crack that has nearly healed: too narrow for the soft browsers, too sheltered for the wax grazers, its fresh tissue protected from the wind. The animal presses its whole mouth against the green margin. A flexible collar seals the contact. Inside that collar, paired blades remove a plug smaller than a seed.

No sap is allowed to run.

The mouth remains attached while a muscular pouch draws in the exposed cells. Saliva softens the cell walls; the contents pass through a filter into the gut. Much of the fiber is left behind, packed back into the opening with a secretion that hardens on contact with dry air.

A feeding mark looks like a tiny amber rivet.

The animal waits for the continent to close beneath it before moving on.

This takes time. A careless feeder can drink quickly and leave a wound, but wounded margins thicken. Repeated injury turns a good feeding crack into a hard ridge. The animals that survive longest use many sites lightly. Their routes along the cliff may have hundreds of stopping places, each revisited after an interval of days or weeks.

They recognize these places partly by taste. A foot brushes the wood, then passes beneath the head. Old feeding plugs hold the chemical traces of the animal that made them.

Some routes are older than their present users.

***

The material of the stitches comes from glands beneath the abdomen.

At first it is a thick ribbon. The animal draws it out with its rear feet, folds it against itself, and lays the doubled strand into a shallow notch. The outside cures quickly. The core stays resilient, protected by a sheath that admits very little vapor.

Producing it costs food and water. An animal cannot lace continuously. After a long repair it becomes visibly narrower, and the plates along its sides overlap farther.

The simplest stitch braces a flake of rind over a resting hollow. More elaborate ones bridge a crack at several angles. These do not stop the continent moving. They let it move a little, then distribute the strain across a broader area. Shelters held this way can remain usable through many stripping storms.

The holes are carefully situated in dead wood. If the growing layer reaches one, the animal abandons it and makes another.

Nevertheless, growth often catches the oldest stitches.

A buried strand may persist after its maker has died. The wood presses around it, absorbs its load, and eventually leaves it enclosed in a curved tunnel. At certain old colonies, these tunnels are abundant. They turn a solid rind into a small district of galleries, each lined by generations of hardened secretion and polished by passing bodies.

Other creatures use them.

Pale mites eat the scraps left in feeding plugs. Blind, many-legged scavengers collect shed plates. Small membrane-winged insects spend their immature lives in the warm inner chambers and emerge during the weaker exhaust strokes.

The stitchers tolerate most of these. Their mouth brushes cannot reach every corner, and a clean shelter is less likely to rot where it touches living tissue.

They kill the soft, bead-shaped animals that gnaw their laces.

A stitcher finding one will hold it beneath a foot, seal its mouth over it, and drain it without changing position. The empty skin is carried to the outside of the shelter and released.

***

There are no eggs lying exposed in these galleries.

The young develop in a chamber beneath the parent’s back. At first they are pale, wet-looking shapes pressed together around a common membrane. The parent feeds them through it and recovers their wastes. Later they grow plates of their own.

When they emerge, each is scarcely longer than one of the adult’s feet.

They climb onto the underside of the parent and travel there, sheltered between the legs. Their own feet touch the ground whenever the adult rests. In this way they begin to learn the pressure rhythms while still being carried.

The first thing they practice is holding on.

The parent lifts and lowers its body, gradually putting weight through them. A young animal that anchors too late is pushed gently along the wood. One that anchors too hard is touched behind the head until it releases.

During an actual blast, the adult settles over the whole brood.

For several weeks the young feed only at openings the parent has made. Later they are taken along a short circuit of old feeding sites. They inspect the amber plugs, touch the margins, and sometimes try to bite into completely unsuitable wood.

The parent does not prevent every mistake.

It does prevent a young animal from cutting a lace.

The restraint is immediate: a foreleg laid across the smaller body, the head pressed down, a rapid sequence of taps delivered through the shell. The same sequence is used by adults when another approaches a strand under dangerous tension.

The young learn it before they learn to spin.

***

Not all colonies are on cliffs.

Some occupy the sheltered sides of the great seams inland. There the food is richer, but the ground changes more quickly. A green margin may swell shut around a shelter. A ventilation opening may establish itself beneath a feeding circuit. A low ridge that has provided shelter for several generations can be stripped away in an afternoon.

These colonies send animals out across the plain.

A traveler carries little that can be seen. Its reservoirs are full. Its spinning glands are full. The gaps between its plates have been freshly sealed. It looks broader and smoother than a resident.

It moves in the lee of the grain, often following grazers. Their scraping uncovers small imperfections in the rind, and their bodies make temporary windbreaks. When a herd fastens down, the traveler may take shelter beneath the rear edge of a shell.

The grazer receives a brief touch against its underside.

Some shake the passenger loose. Others permit it. A stitcher can remove parasites from the soft joints around their legs, and certain grazers acquire several attendants during a long crossing.

Where the herd turns toward a wax bed, the traveler continues alone.

It cannot see a distant seam clearly. Its eyes are small and deeply hooded, useful chiefly for locating shadows and the moving edges of leaves. Much of its route is chosen by listening through the feet. Hollow tissue carries a different resonance from dense rind. Cooling cracks draw faint currents of air. A sheltered depression can be found by walking across the wind and comparing the strain on the two sides of the body.

Even so, many die within sight of shelter.

The final failure is usually a foot that no longer releases cleanly. The leg drags, catching in the grain. The animal stops to clean it, spends too long exposed, and becomes less able to move.

After death the plates remain locked together. For a season or two the body provides a little shelter for smaller things.

Eventually a gust finds its underside.

***

A traveler that reaches an inhabited crack does not enter at once.

It fastens itself near the outermost stitch and tests the strand with one forefoot. The signal is small: a pull, a pause, two shorter pulls.

Inside, someone answers.

Then follows a prolonged exchange. Tension runs through the laces, too slight to hear and partly lost whenever the cliff shudders. Both animals wait through the interruptions and begin again.

They exchange recognizable things. The state of the nearby feeding margins. Damage along a route. Whether a shelter is occupied by young. A familiar traveler may be admitted quickly; an unfamiliar one sometimes remains outside through several pumping cycles.

Occasionally an inhabitant comes out, touches the visitor’s mouth, and gives it food.

The strands that brace their homes also carry these conversations. In a large colony, an animal may receive a warning from several shelters away without any of the intervening inhabitants coming outside. Broken connections are repaired even when they no longer support anything.

Old colonies retain long stretches of apparently unnecessary lacing.

Some run past empty chambers. Some lead to cracks sealed years before. The animals continue to inspect them, touching one junction after another with their forefeet. A route abandoned as a feeding circuit may still be maintained as a path through which a signal travels.

At its far end there may be only one very old animal, too worn to cross the exposed cliff, living on food brought from the nearest green margin.

It answers slowly.

The others wait.

***

Now the animal beneath the rim has reached a damaged stitch.

One end has pulled halfway out of its hole. With every pressure cycle, the loose strand rubs across the edge of the crack. There is a pale patch on its sheath.

The animal grips the sound portion and takes the load onto its body.

It waits there through one stroke of the pumps, then another. A second animal emerges below it. The newcomer tests the wood, climbs past, and sets its feet on either side of the loosened anchor.

Neither can make the repair alone while the strand is taut.

Together they lift it clear.

For a few seconds the local chambers fall quiet. The cliff relaxes. The upper animal pushes the damaged end deeper into the old hole while the lower one draws fresh ribbon from beneath its abdomen.

Inside the nearest shelter, young bodies crowd against the back of the door.

They can feel the work.

The first deep tremor of the returning pressure reaches the colony. The repair is not finished. Both adults flatten, still holding the strand. The new ribbon stretches between them, soft at its center, its surface already whitening in the air.

The cliff bends.

Along the crack, the old stitches tighten in succession. The two animals are drawn a little closer together. Their feet adjust without lifting.

This time the anchor holds.

When the strain passes, one adult touches the fresh binding. The other answers through it.
