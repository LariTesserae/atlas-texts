The people are easiest to see when they stop working.

In motion, they belong too closely to the place. Their coats have the pale, longitudinal markings of freight wrappers. Their bent backs resemble the curved supports beneath loading platforms. Someone standing with an arm inside a service opening may appear to be another branch of the installation, especially if they have been there long enough for the wall to grow a cuff around their wrist.

Then they withdraw the arm, examine their fingers, and pick a bit of wall out of a joint.

They are shorter than most of their doors. Broad through the middle, narrow at the shoulders, they walk on soft, divided feet that spread under a load. The soles leave temporary dark impressions in the decking. Along a busy route these marks overlap so densely that the ground seems to be remembering a crowd after everyone has gone.

Their skin is matte and finely ridged. Young people tend toward gray-green; older people acquire the ivory patches of surfaces handled for a long time. The ridges run in different directions on different parts of the body. Across the face they are almost horizontal, interrupted by the eyes and by the mobile seam of the mouth.

The eyes are dark, deep-set, and extremely good at recognizing small changes in green.

A warehouse wall that appears uniform to a visitor is, to its attendant, plainly irritated around three hinges and becoming dangerously enthusiastic about a fourth.

Their hands are warm. This surprises anyone who has touched the walls first.

***

Inside them, powder moves.

There are chambers that sift it, fine muscular vanes that keep it circulating, and narrow places beneath the ribs where a blockage can produce days of exhaustion. When injured, they close a hand over the wound before anything else. Losing a little tissue is inconvenient. Losing the finest portion of the circulating salts can change a person’s memory, balance, or ability to recognize a familiar voice.

Every coat has an inner pocket containing a folded compression leaf.

They eat seated, usually twice in a work cycle. Food comes in brittle cakes, soft mineral pith, or small tightly wrapped portions that have to be warmed between the palms. A good meal breaks differently at every bite. Cheap food has a uniform texture and leaves the mouth unpleasantly conductive.

There are stalls that sell roasted junction buds. There are arguments about which districts still know how to prepare them. Some people lick the seasoning from their fingers; others find this vulgar.

Water is a controlled industrial material and a household terror.

Even a tiny quantity can dissolve structures meant to loosen only under charge. Workers who handle incoming freight wear dark face shields and gloves whose outer layers can be discarded in one movement. Children are shown pictures of swelling injuries. They find the pictures fascinating and have to be told to stop passing them around.

Stories about distant places full of exposed liquid are popular with the young.

The usual illustration shows a person standing beside an ocean.

Adults consider this the least plausible part.

***

Their voices are low and dry, produced by membranes behind the mouth. At a distance, conversation resembles paper being sorted. Excitement adds a faint rattle. An old person may have a second, involuntary note beneath every word, caused by a loose partition too inaccessible to repair.

They have names for these voice defects. Some are affectionate.

A child will sometimes imitate a grandparent so accurately that the adults turn around before realizing what has happened.

Most people also speak through contact. Two fingers against a wrist can communicate a warning, an acknowledgment, or a complicated adjustment to the timing of a lift. Crews that have worked together for decades may pass an entire shift without opening their mouths.

This is less harmonious than it looks.

A receiver can make it very clear, through the pressure of one thumb, that a load has arrived badly balanced and that this has happened before.

***

They live in rooms grown along the sides of the work districts, above sorting lanes, or between structures that have ceased to require their original clearance.

A household begins by keeping something in one place.

A seat, perhaps. A cabinet. Three people who repeatedly eat together in the shelter of the same buttress. The surrounding tissue responds slowly. A windbreak thickens. A ledge develops. If the arrangement persists, the wall begins to fold.

Permits regulate this process, although enforcement varies.

In crowded districts, people carefully move their possessions every few days to prevent an unauthorized room from forming. In declining districts, inspectors quietly overlook a new alcove. Someone willing to remain there is worth more than the obstruction.

Inside a home, the walls are usually kept dimmer than the prescribed working level. This requires regular applications of a bitter paste. There are shelves of wrapped food, grooming combs with very fine teeth, spare foot pads, personal tools worn too far to be useful at work but too familiar to discard.

Many households own an object that was made somewhere else.

A little metal cup. A stone. A sealed sphere with something that moves inside it.

Such things are not always valuable. Their appeal is that the house cannot absorb their design. A stone may sit on a shelf for a thousand cycles without the shelf learning to produce another stone.

People handle these objects while thinking.

They also keep defective growths: a hinge that closes in three directions, a strip of sign tissue that changes one letter when touched, a tiny stair plant persuaded to climb around the same pot indefinitely. There are competitive exhibitions. Judges are accused of favoring extravagant malformations over good cultivation.

One well-known specimen has been growing a landing for longer than its present owner has been alive.

It has never produced a stair.

***

Children arrive small, tightly folded, and already inclined to grip.

For the first several cycles, their hands close on anything pressed against them. A parent’s finger, a wrapper, the edge of a feeding tray. Their feet remain curled beneath the body. As the torso unfolds, the family measures the distance between the shoulder ridges and argues about resemblances.

They are carried in slings lined with replaceable feeding tissue.

The slings can be hung from almost any living wall, which will hold them level and maintain the correct warmth. A nursery resembles a row of small loaded pockets. Now and then a hand emerges.

Children learn the maintained routes early. They learn to recognize black survey nodules before they can read delivery labels. They are taught never to follow a familiar person across an unfamiliar seam: the person may arrive safely and the child may not.

They play freight.

One child becomes a warehouse and refuses everything offered. Another issues impossible labels. A third carries a scrap of pith around the same column until someone shouts that it has arrived yesterday.

Adults complain that these games teach bad procedure.

The children have heard this many times.

***

No one is indispensable, officially.

Every task is recorded. Every installation has a maintenance history. Replacement workers can be trained, replacement hands grown, replacement routes cultivated around a failed district.

Nevertheless, there are people who cannot take leave because a certain gate will open only for them.

The gate is not being affectionate. It has accumulated a long series of practical accommodations: the exact warmth of a palm, the interval between two knocks, a trace substance transferred from a particular coat. Removing these dependencies would require shutting down three adjoining freight lines.

So the person continues to work at the gate.

Someone brings them meals. They become an authority on everything that passes through. Eventually they are less a gate attendant than a local institution, consulted about marriages, missing consignments, the quality of new flooring, and whether an unfamiliar silence from the west is worth reporting.

Such a person may be cheerful, tyrannical, absent-minded, or all three at different hours.

The place has not made them wise. It has given them a great deal of time to become particular.

***

Age is unevenly distributed through a body.

A worker may have young hands and a torso dark with old repairs. Someone injured in a freight collapse may walk on legs grown to a newer standard and need inserts to use the older stairs. Cosmetic grafts exist, but the joins remain visible under late-cycle light.

There is no simple answer to how long a person lives.

Most eventually become difficult to maintain. Their internal passages narrow. Their sleep lengthens. New tissue takes poorly. They begin to favor places with a little more charge in the walls.

Families adapt rooms for them. Chairs grow enclosing sides; floors lift slightly beneath the feet. A person who no longer travels may still sort labels, teach children, prepare food, or advise a distant crew through a contact root.

Toward the end, some become confused about the distinction between their own body and the structures supporting it.

They complain that a corridor aches.

Sometimes examination finds a fault in the corridor.

Sometimes it does not.

Both possibilities are familiar to the people who care for them.

After death, the usable salts are recovered. The rest returns through the reclamation system, with a small portion retained if the household wishes. Memorial tissue is slow-growing and deliberately unproductive. It may line a doorway or make a narrow border around a shelf.

Descendants touch it in passing. Eventually the household disperses, the records become obscure, and someone complains that an inconvenient strip of wall cannot be altered because it is somebody’s relative.

Usually the strip remains.

***

The small gray animals have no work designation.

This is a source of continuing administrative discomfort.

They consume dead insulation, but not reliably enough to be classified as maintenance organisms. They interfere with sensors. They nest in expensive places. Attempts to cultivate them have produced beautifully regulated enclosures containing no animals.

The rules protecting their crossings are extremely old.

Some workers believe the creatures detect failures in the power trunks. Others say their bodies preserve a chemistry no longer manufactured anywhere. There are claims that they were brought from the foundations, or preceded them.

Most people have no opinion. When the warning leaves open, they wait.

A delayed crew may watch one pick its way across the road, foot by foot, pausing at harmless variations in the grain. Someone will be irritated. Someone will lean forward for a better look.

Afterward, the delay is entered under its own code.

It does not count against production.

***

There is a worker who passes the unused platform near the end of each cycle.

They are a label corrector, with a repaired cheek and one hand that no longer warms properly. Their work involves cutting away obsolete lettering before new instructions are allowed to darken. At home they cultivate small useless hinges. They have a neighbor they dislike and continue to feed during convalescence.

They see the new doorway before it is wide enough to enter.

At first they assume an old order has surfaced. This happens. Somewhere beneath the present roads, a request has finished traveling through a root.

They check the sign. They scrape away the overgrowth carefully, exposing a destination code no longer listed in their reader.

Inside, the room is empty.

No storage ribs. No fixtures. No receiving surface. The walls have brightened, but only a little. Along the far side is a shallow recess at approximately the height of a seated person’s shoulders.

The corrector stands there until the flooring extends beneath their forward foot.

Then they step back.

They know better than to accept an unrecorded delivery.

On the next cycle, they bring a survey strip. On the cycle after that, a colleague. Together they establish that the room has ordinary dimensions, remains in the same year, and contains no detectable freight.

The colleague suggests sealing it.

The corrector says they should wait for the lettering.

For several cycles, nothing happens. The threshold gathers dust. A thin lip grows along its edge, then subsides where feet have crossed.

Eventually the corrector brings a seat.

They sit in the room after work, testing the pressure in their cold hand. Outside, loads move past without a sound. The room does not close around them. It does not grow a desk or offer a service opening.

Later, their neighbor comes to see what has been occupying them.

There is only one seat, so the neighbor sits on the floor. They discuss the convalescence, a disputed label, the poor quality of food lately available near the west interchange.

The wall behind the neighbor becomes slightly softer.

Neither of them notices until it is time to leave.
