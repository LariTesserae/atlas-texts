One of the animals lies along the back of the intact shelter, where the roof slab overhangs the wall. Its body occupies the shadow so exactly that it is easier to see when it is absent. Then there is a narrow groove in the crowded stone, briefly empty, with scraps of old skin caught along its edges.

It is about as long as a hand with the fingers spread. Six broad feet hold it against the slab. Between them, its belly is soft and deeply pleated; above them, a row of overlapping plates runs from a blunt head to a tail that ends in two short hooks. Dust lodged in the plates gives its back the color of the shelter. Only the joints show pale.

It has eyes, though they are scarcely larger than punctures. Most of the time a thick membrane covers them. Its more useful organs are the whiskers around its feet: short, stiff hairs, each seated in a little cup of skin. Some touch the rock. Others touch whatever animal happens to be passing.

There is almost always something passing.

The animal can distinguish the weight of a folded predator from the weight of grazers piled three bodies deep. It feels the intermittent drag of a ceiling hunter hauling itself along the far side of the slab. Through its front feet it receives the small, regular tremor of feeding beneath the shelter. Through its hind feet comes the mountain.

It rests with these two kinds of information entering opposite ends of its body.

Under its plates are young.

They are not eggs. They hatched some time ago, and now cling in two rows inside a chamber formed by the folded skin of its back. Seen through a lifted plate, they look like moist seeds with their roots tucked in. Their shells are still flexible. They cannot close themselves tightly enough to survive the hollow’s air.

The parent keeps the chamber sealed except when it feeds them.

This is why it waits beside the shelter. The clothlike predators return here after spreading across the floor, swollen underneath with what they have eaten. As they force themselves through the entrance, their edges wrinkle. Occasionally one is pinched between another animal and the stone.

The waiting parent reaches down.

Its front legs are ordinary walking legs until they unfold. Each has an additional section normally laid flat against the next, and a foot that can separate into three blunt fingers. One finger holds the shelter’s edge. The other two slip beneath the returning fold.

It takes very little.

A piece no larger than one of the floor grazers comes away between its fingers. The fold contracts. Bodies behind it press forward, and it disappears under the slab. The parent brings the morsel to a mouth hidden between the bases of its forelegs.

The mouth works for a long time. Nothing drips.

Later, a pale bead appears at each of several openings along the parent’s flanks. Beneath the plates, the young shift toward them.

The parent also eats grazers, eggs, the soft joints of dead hunters, and the thin fresh skin exposed when a neighbor molts. But wet flesh is worth waiting for. A dry shell may fill the gut while yielding almost nothing that can be given to the young.

Its own left forefoot is missing two fingers.

The stump has healed into a glossy knob. When it moves across level stone, the injury makes little difference. On the shelter wall, it must plant that foot twice before transferring its weight. There is a slight hesitation, repeated at every step. Animals passing nearby sometimes investigate the pause with their mouthparts.

It pushes them away without turning.

Others of its kind live in the hollow. They are scattered rather than gathered: one flattened beneath a ceiling rib, another wedged along a seam above the collapsed building, several smaller ones among the wall shelves. Their bodies differ in color according to the dust and mineral crust caught in their backs. Some have no young. These are thinner and quicker, able to squeeze through openings the parent can no longer enter.

When two meet, they touch forefeet.

The contact may last only an instant. It may become a prolonged contest in which each slowly raises the other from the stone. Losing purchase is dangerous here. A body on its back becomes a feeding surface before it becomes a corpse.

The parent has met the animal beneath the collapsed roof many times. Their routes overlap at the triangular opening. Neither yields it readily, but neither attempts to overturn the other now. They brace, exchange a series of small pushes, then pass with their softer sides turned away.

The parent’s route is short. It runs along the shelter’s rear wall, across the roof, down beside the entrance, and back through the narrow space underneath the overhang. On this circuit it knows every dependable foothold. It knows where a slab moves under weight, where a ceiling hunter can reach, where the folded animals bunch before entering.

One of its turns brings it across the five grooves in the lintel.

It places its feet in three of them. The spacing is convenient, though not quite convenient enough; it has to stretch for the last. Its claws have brightened the bottoms of the cuts. Whatever those marks once held or meant, they now help a damaged animal cross a difficult piece of stone.

Before a strong pulse, it stops feeding.

The change first reaches it as a faint disturbance through the hind feet. Its belly lifts. The young contract beneath its plates, responding to the tightening of the chamber around them.

It leaves the overhang before the first grazers begin to stream.

Climbing with its burden takes time. At the corner of the shelter it encounters a fold already spreading upward, and for several moments its good forefoot rests on living flesh. Then it finds the stone beyond. It climbs onto the lintel, crosses the grooves, and takes the narrow route toward the roof’s outer edge.

There it turns its back to the heat.

The plates overlap more tightly on that side. On the side facing the entrance, they part by a fraction. Cold air passes across the exposed seams, carrying away some warmth before the parent closes them again.

Around it, the shelter disappears beneath bodies.

A grazer works at the healed stump of its forefoot. It dislodges the grazer. A suspended hunter lowers a hook and tests the edge of one plate. The parent flattens so abruptly that the hook slides off. Through all this, the young remain sealed inside.

One is ready to leave.

It has been ready since before the pulse, but there has been no suitable surface. Now it pushes against the rear of the chamber, where the plates close less firmly. The parent hooks its tail beneath the roof slab and lifts the tip.

The young animal emerges backward into the sheltered gap.

For a while it stays attached by its hind feet. Its new plates are darkening, their edges still faintly translucent. It touches the stone with one forefoot, withdraws it, and touches again. Beneath it, thousands of grazers are climbing toward cooler ground.

Eventually it lets go of the parent.

There is no open space waiting for it. It must press between two shells, lift the edge of a third, and wedge its body underneath. For an instant only its tail remains visible.

The parent lowers its own tail and closes the chamber. Inside, the remaining young shift into the room their sibling has left.
