The first sign of the larger animal is a place where the shrimp will not settle.

They crowd the underside of a tray, even hanging from one another where the ceramic offers too little purchase, but leave a narrow strip empty along its back edge. Nothing visibly occupies the strip. The darkness there is a little smoother than the darkness inside the adjoining pipe. Once, two shrimp rise together, turn, and come down farther apart.

Something has drawn a foot beneath itself.

The animal rests between the tray and the wall, with its head in a recess left by a missing block. Most of its length is hidden. What can be seen looks less like a body than a fold in the mineral coating: pale brown, blunt, dusted with sediment. There are no scales to catch the light. A small snail has been grazing across its flank for some time.

When it moves, it leaves the snail behind.

It comes out backward. First the folded hind legs appear, then the long middle of the body, then the head, which turns sideways to clear the tray’s short support. The tail has already found the next gap. It is flattened from side to side, but used here less as a fin than as another surface with which to feel. Its tip passes around a fragment of jar before the rest of the animal follows.

Fully extended, it is somewhat longer than a human forearm. It rarely extends fully. The chamber offers few places where that would be useful.

Its head is wide and low, with a mouth that reaches almost to the attachment of the neck. The eyes are small but not absent. Each lies beneath a cloudy patch of skin, and in the gray light from the slit a dark center can sometimes be distinguished. Around the jaw are rows of shallow pits. Silt collects in them while the animal rests; a quick shiver of the head clears them.

The forefeet have four separate fingers. These are soft, without nails, and very sensitive along their undersides. The animal places them against a surface before putting weight on it. On sound ceramic the fingers spread. On a thick deposit of loose mineral they curl, searching through it for something firm.

Its belly is almost white. The back is the color of wet mortar, except along the spine, where repeated contact with low arches has left a narrow, darker thickening of skin.

There are several of its kind in the chamber.

They do not gather at the richest inlet. That place is too exposed, and the small fish stationed around it are quick. Instead they occupy the awkward approaches: beneath a sill, behind a jar, inside a passage whose entrance has narrowed as its walls acquired crust. An adult can remain in such a place with only its chin projecting into the current.

It waits for more than prey. Through the skin of the jaw it feels the small pulses made by animals moving nearby. A shrimp crossing the floor produces a brief, leggy disturbance. A snail produces almost none. Another adult, pushing between two partitions, sends a slow displacement through the whole adjoining aisle.

At that, the waiting animal lifts its chin.

Encounters are quiet. One animal may place its broad head against the shoulder of another and hold it there. Their feet brace against opposite walls. Nothing happens for a long time. Then one yields, folding its limbs close and allowing itself to be pushed backward until there is room to turn.

Old individuals carry pale crescents on their shoulders where these encounters have gone further. The wounds heal slowly in the warm water. Tiny grazers sometimes work along their edges.

Their ordinary food is the white snail.

An animal finds one by passing its chin over a rim or ledge. It may examine several before choosing. Then the mouth opens, the head makes a short sideways movement, and the snail disappears with a little rush of water.

Inside the jaws are low, crowded teeth, better at holding and crushing than cutting. The shell breaks with a sound like a grain of grit caught between stones. For several breaths the throat works. Splinters emerge at the corners of the mouth.

The heaviest snails survive because they are difficult to detach. Those in narrow crevices survive because the broad head cannot reach them. On an occupied feeding route, the visible rims of jars become unusually clean while their undersides remain crowded.

This is part of how the animal alters its shelter. It removes the grazers that would rasp certain surfaces away, but its own movements loosen others. A shoulder presses the same swollen partition night after night. A foot pulls against the same imperfect joint. Eventually a small piece comes free.

The animal startles at the change. It may abandon that route for days.

When it returns, it explores the new opening with its chin and fingers. If the body fits, the opening becomes a passage.

The largest one lives near the fallen partition. Its left hind foot lacks two fingers, and its tail has healed with a slight bend. In the open eddy it looks cumbersome. Among the fallen slabs it moves easily, knowing which gaps admit its head and which require it to roll partway onto its side.

It sometimes takes the long-bodied fish that breed there. More often it takes their eggs.

The fish attack its face in quick, ineffectual darts. It closes its eyes and continues searching beneath the slabs. Yet many eggs lie beyond its reach, attached deep within cracks that its own weight has helped hold open.

Its young are much less like it than their size alone would suggest.

They hatch with fine external gills and tails edged by transparent fins. At first they stay among the pores and holes of the broken screen, where an adult’s snout cannot follow. Their bodies are clear enough for a swallowed speck to show through the belly. They eat the minute animals that graze the bacterial film, then the smallest shrimp.

A current that barely troubles an adult can carry one away.

The surviving young learn shelter before they learn distance. They move along the downstream faces of objects, stop in the dim hollow behind a jar rim, and cross an exposed gap in a sudden wriggle. Many vanish into the slower channels. A few return months later, thicker, darker, their gills shrinking into soft folds behind the head.

By then they begin visiting the air pocket.

An adult can go a long time without doing so. Much of what it needs passes through its skin, provided the water keeps moving. But after a meal, or when the inlets weaken, it climbs toward the worn bowl beneath the vault.

There is no hurried ascent. It goes by surfaces, hooking its forefeet over ledges, pressing its belly to the side of a pillar. Beneath the pocket it pauses, nose just short of the water’s skin.

Then the nostrils emerge.

The breath is small. A bubble slips from the mouth, spreads against the ceiling, and joins the stale air already there. The throat expands twice. The animal lowers itself again without showing its eyes.

This shelf is the only place where all the adults regularly pass. Their bellies have kept its shallow central hollow free of crust. In the oldest deposits around its edges are thin, dark bands of shed skin, buried between layers of mineral.

In dry intervals they come more often. They wait beneath one another without fighting, loosely braced against the stone. The smallest must sometimes reach between the feet of a larger animal to breathe.

Near the light slit, their behavior changes.

Even an old one that moves confidently through the room approaches the thinner masonry by degrees. Its fingers search the last joints. Its head rises into the cooler water, and the small eyes turn toward the brightness.

It can feel the outward movement there. Beyond the wall there is nothing against which to place a foot.

The animal remains for a while with its chin on the lower edge. Clouds pass close enough to alter the light across its face. Then it withdraws backward, slowly, until its tail touches a pillar.

At that contact the limbs loosen. The body turns. It descends between the familiar walls, stirring a little silt into water that has scarcely cooled, and finds its way beneath the tray.
