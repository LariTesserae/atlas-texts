The animal’s head is the end that hesitates.

The two ends are otherwise much alike: narrow, blunt, furnished with short feelers that lie flat along the stone. It can walk backward without turning. When a current strikes it from the side, its joints fold in sequence, bringing its body down between its feet until there is scarcely enough of it standing above the surface to cast a shadow. The dark fork remains visible. Everything else takes on the color of whatever lies beneath.

It has six pairs of feet. Each foot ends in two soft pads with a hard point between them. On the old ceiling, the pads hold. On the forming floor, the points enter the mineral before it has finished becoming solid.

This requires exact timing. A foot placed too early goes through. A foot placed too late finds an edge on which there is no purchase. Occasionally a point is caught, and the animal must wait for that part of the mountain to reconsider.

It can wait a long time.

The feelers at its head are unequal. One is short and thick, with a pale knob at the end; the other is so fine that it becomes visible only when it crosses a shadow. The thick feeler rests on stone. The fine one stands a little way into the water. Between them, the animal receives two versions of every impact.

Before a ridge appears, it will sometimes lift three feet.

Before a ridge vanishes, it presses its whole underside against it.

There are changes too small for a person to see that evidently matter very much to the animal. It may spend the entire passage of the morning sun negotiating a distance smaller than its own head, testing the same spot repeatedly, withdrawing, approaching from another angle. Then, during an impact that makes the bone gate jump, it crosses the hollow in a few quick steps.

Its mouth is underneath. When it feeds, a little fan of transparent parts opens against the floor.

There is almost nothing to eat. The green strands lose a cell occasionally. Something pale gathers at the margins of the brown cups. Meltwater brings matter to the entrance, though very little of it passes the trembling face. The animal finds what arrives. It also works its mouth over new mineral, following the cloudy line where water is acquiring edges.

Afterward, a faint yellow thread can sometimes be seen inside it.

The dark fork is elsewhere, higher in the body. Its branches attach near the first pair of legs, and its stem runs almost to the tail. During the heaviest shocks the branches draw together. Slowly, afterward, they spread.

When the animal is young, this structure is nearly colorless. The darkening comes with use.

There are young, though the visitors have seldom distinguished them from moving grains.

They begin beneath the animal’s back, in a shallow space between shell and body. Usually there are two. For a time their feet touch the same floor as the adult’s feet, reaching down on either side of it, so that the creature appears to have acquired an extra fringe. They learn to hold before they leave.

The adult carries them onto the oldest stone.

This is the only journey it regularly makes to the ceiling. The ascent takes several loud intervals, with long withdrawals into seams. Beneath the roof it stops in a particular junction of facets. There the young step away, one at a time, into an angle too small to contain the parent.

For several days afterward, the adult returns to the place and opens its mouth against the stone just below them.

A person looking through the entrance sees nothing of this unless the light is exactly right. Even then it resembles a flaw in the mineral.

The animal molts in the hole behind the rib.

First it fastens all twelve feet. Then the shell opens along the underside, and the animal draws itself out through the split, leaving the old feet attached. For a little while there seem to be two animals occupying almost the same place. The new one is softer, wider, and entirely clear except for the fork. It remains beneath the empty shell while its joints become firm.

The opaque flecks in the old shell loosen slowly. Some disappear into the water. Others are taken back into the floor. A few stay suspended in the cast until a current tears it free.

These are the grains that sometimes cross the hollow too quickly to follow.

The bone gate has altered its life.

Behind the three teeth, the floor is dependable enough to cross without testing every step. Food settles there. The animal visits the crosspiece’s shadow, especially after someone has taken a flake. It searches the shallow scar, putting its fine feeler into the corners.

It does not climb onto the bone.

Once, when the gate was being repaired, it approached a finger held motionless in the water. It touched the nail with its thick feeler. For several seconds it remained there, front feet raised, while the person outside lay rigid on the slope.

Then the finger moved with the person’s pulse.

The animal withdrew so violently that it lost a foot.

The foot remained on the nail: a speck with two transparent lobes. The person brought it down the mountain in the fold of a leaf. By evening there was nothing to find.

The animal grew another at its next molt. Until then, its journeys were slower. It avoided a certain open patch near the entrance, although the finger never returned.

There may be several adults in the mountain. There may be only one accessible to sight at any time. The hole behind the rib deepens when the hollow contracts, and sometimes a feeler appears from much farther inside than the visible floor ought to allow.

When the roaring stops, the animal goes there.

Quiet leaves it without warning of what the stone will do next. It folds its legs beneath itself, wedges its back against the upper surface, and holds the two feelers apart: one on the floor, one in the water.

At the first deep impact, the fork in its body closes.

Its feet unfold.

Well before the new edge becomes visible, it has found somewhere to stand.
