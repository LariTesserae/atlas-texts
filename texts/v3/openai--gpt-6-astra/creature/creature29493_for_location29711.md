The small animal at the cup-leaf does not swallow all the water it takes.

Some goes into its mouth. The rest passes along two grooves beneath its jaw and disappears under the overlapping plates of its chest. For a moment, as it leans forward, you can see a bright wet line between them. Something moves in that line against the direction of the water.

The animal closes its plates.

It is about the length of your hand, though it is difficult to judge where its body ends. Six limbs hold it against the leaf. Two others, finer and paler, wipe its mouth and probe the hinge from which the water has just poured. Behind it lies what looks like a little bundle of split roots. These unfold when it moves, finding purchase independently, then draw together again beneath the abdomen.

Its skin is neither furred nor bare. Short, transparent blades grow from it in overlapping rows. On the back they lie flat, trapping a layer of cloud moisture. Around the joints they stand apart. Each has a dark speck at its base, and when a current passes through the leaf, the specks contract.

The animal becomes very still.

Only one forelimb continues moving. It reaches under the cup, feels along the swollen joint, and presses twice.

The leaf releases another drop.

***

These creatures live wherever water is held above the ground.

They are most easily found under the collecting fronds, but those are feeding places, not nests. A nest occupies the dry side of a root hollow, just above the highest water mark. It has no entrance visible from the forest floor. The animal reaches it by walking down through the reservoir, flattening its blades and carrying a skin of air against its body. Underwater, it appears briefly silver.

Inside the nest are narrow shelves made from chewed fibers and mineral paste. The lowest shelves hold eggs. Higher ones hold food, shed body plates, plugs for stopping leaks, and little dark lumps that must be kept from touching one another.

The lumps are alive.

When placed in water, each unfolds into a many-branched animal, too fine to lift out again without destroying it. It feeds on the cloudy waste around the eggs. Once the water clears, it folds back into its hard casing. A nest may contain dozens, arranged with considerable care.

The cup-dweller turns them regularly. It discards the ones that have become too light.

It also maintains a patch of white growth on the inner wall. The growth is clipped short everywhere except along one edge, where it is allowed to thicken into soft nodules. These are eaten during the long intervals when the cloud touches everything and gives up almost nothing.

Not every nest is well kept. Some smell sour. Some contain eggs buried under loose fibers, or drowned stores, or the empty casings of filter animals that have never been replaced. Their owners can be seen traveling farther each day, searching the neighboring leaves.

Others are so productive that small thieves live permanently nearby.

A thief resembles the pointed tip of a new shoot. It stands among actual shoots with its hind limbs buried in their shared wax coating. When the nest owner passes, it bends with the disturbed foliage. Later it walks down into the hollow and takes one egg.

Usually only one.

A thief that eats heavily becomes round and conspicuous. The cup-dwellers examine round things.

***

The water beneath the animal’s chest is separate from the water it drinks.

There are several chambers there, each shallow and lined with moving hairs. The chambers hold the young during a crossing. They also carry the folded filter animals, a scrap of the white nest growth, and a few organisms whose usefulness is less apparent.

One is a black disc that attaches just behind the forelimbs. It feeds directly through the chamber wall. Animals carrying it grow slowly and often remain smaller than their neighbors. They also leave failing ground earlier.

Well before a seam opens, the disc begins to turn.

Its host stops feeding, presses its belly against the root beneath it, and waits. The disc turns again. Then the animal goes home.

Packing takes time. Eggs must be freed from their shelves without tearing their membranes. The white growth must be cut at an active edge. The reservoir is skimmed for food, but not emptied: a heavily laden animal cannot flatten enough to cross warm bare stone.

Often there is more to carry than the chambers will hold.

The animal tries different arrangements. It moves an egg from one chamber to another. It takes something out, puts it back, adds water, drains some away. Its fine forelimbs work steadily while its other limbs grip the nest wall.

Eventually it leaves.

In abandoned nests, there may be three or four eggs left on a shelf, each still moist from being handled.

***

On mineral pavement, the cup-dwellers travel low.

Their transparent blades lift at the edges, admitting air over the wet chamber walls. The rootlike appendages spread behind them. These are walking limbs too, but delicate ones, used less to carry weight than to sample the ground.

Each tip pauses after contact.

A healthy crossing has a particular succession of sensations: pressure returning through the stone, a fading charge, heat moving upward at an even rate. A cup-dweller can cross a considerable distance without raising its head. When that succession changes, it begins to test sideways.

Others may follow its path. They keep far enough apart that one will not necessarily be caught in what takes another.

Where two travelers meet, they place the pale limbs of their mouths against each other’s chest plates. The exchange lasts only a few seconds. A little water passes between them. Sometimes an egg does too.

This does not happen at every meeting. Some animals recoil. Some strike with a hard spur hidden inside the jaw. A heavily laden traveler may pursue an empty one for several body lengths, trying to force its chest open.

But on a broad crossing, with the green seams faint at either edge, transfers become frequent. A young animal can reach the far side in the care of a creature that has never visited its home reservoir.

The receiving animal makes no distinction when it unpacks.

***

There are hunters adapted to these crossings.

One lies almost entirely within its own shadow.

Its body is a thin, dull sheet, warmer on the lower surface than on the upper. Around its margin are minute limbs that move without lifting it perceptibly from the rock. Dust adheres to its back. When it encounters a shallow depression, it settles into it and draws its edges tight.

A cup-dweller stepping onto the hunter feels cooler ground. It may pause there to conserve water.

The sheet folds.

It does not close quickly enough to catch an alert traveler. Instead, it releases a pulse through the feet: the brief, patterned pressure of secure root tissue. The cup-dweller lowers its chest, preparing to open its water chambers against what it perceives as a reservoir wall.

Then the hunter brings its edges together.

After feeding, it spends hours sorting the contents. Soft tissue is absorbed. Mineral plates are pushed out beneath one margin. Eggs sometimes survive and adhere to the hunter’s underside, where the warmth is steady and the humidity high.

Their hatchlings ride there until the hunter approaches vegetation.

For this reason, the cup-dwellers probe patches of dust that seem unusually smooth, but their young often gather beneath them.

***

A young cup-dweller does not yet look like its parent.

It is almost clear, with a flattened head and a single broad organ that beats against the water. It hangs among the root hairs inside the reservoir, eating the soft coating from their surfaces. Its limbs appear one pair at a time.

Before it leaves the water, it makes a chamber within its own chest.

At first this is only a crease. The young animal spends long periods curled around it, using its mouth to draw the edges together. Other inhabitants of the reservoir enter the fold. Some are eaten. Some leave. A few remain as the crease deepens and divides.

A youngster raised in an almost empty reservoir may develop a perfectly formed chamber that cannot keep water fresh. Such animals make repeated journeys to the collecting leaves. Their plates open and close. Their young seldom hatch.

The inhabitants carried beneath an adult’s chest are acquired from many places. Some came from its first pool; others arrived with exchanged water, food, or eggs. There are tiny grazers that clean the moving hairs, and burrowing things that must be kept out of the soft walls. The adult reaches inside with one pale forelimb to remove them. It can distinguish something smaller than a seed by touch, even while running.

At rest, it grooms the chambers for hours.

Seen close enough, this is the busiest part of the animal: its still head beneath the leaf, its fine limb passing in and out, the little residents scattering and returning.

***

The amber light is dangerous to them.

When a shaft approaches, adults turn toward its warmth before it reaches them. They open the blades along their backs. Some climb to meet it.

Within the light, stored food is consumed rapidly. Old plates loosen. New tissue swells beneath them. Eggs darken as the bodies inside develop.

A well-provisioned animal may gain an entire new row of chambers in an afternoon. It emerges larger, carrying young that have already begun to move.

An animal with poor stores cannot complete the change.

These are the pale shapes found along yesterday’s bright paths: creatures with one side of the chest enlarged and the other still enclosed in its old plates; creatures whose new limbs remain soft; creatures that have grown around the branch they were gripping.

Many die. Others continue living where they are.

A stranded adult can hold a collecting leaf in the right position. Its fine forelimbs remain mobile long after the heavier limbs stiffen. It catches small passing animals, tends the water beneath its chest, and removes decay from its own margins.

Roots eventually enter the spaces between its plates.

The animal bites some away. Others it permits.

***

Over years, the distinction becomes harder to make.

There are root hollows whose walls include the chest plates of a single old cup-dweller. The plates have separated, and living tissue stretches between them in dark bands. Water enters at one end and leaves through a narrow aperture at the other. The mouth is still present, tucked under a projecting rim.

It feeds when touched.

These old animals no longer carry reservoirs. They enclose them. Their chamber hairs have become long, densely packed filaments that draw water through the interior. The black warning disc, if they carry one, grows deep into the wall.

Travelers favor such hollows. They are clean, warm, and quick to close during a tremor.

The resident allows some visitors to shelter. Others it grips between its plates and slowly consumes.

There is no visible difference between the visitors it takes and those it releases. Sometimes a small animal repeatedly touches the old mouth before entering. Sometimes it deposits food there. Sometimes it enters without hesitation and does not come out.

Around an ancient hollow, generations of cup-dwellers may live within reach of the same forelimb.

It moves rarely, but precisely.

***

Deeper in the forest are animals that have remained rooted through more than one opening of the ground.

Their reservoirs extend into galleries. Their bodies are supported by mineral deposits so thick that only the wet seams between them remain flexible. Burrowers pass through their lower chambers. Vines enter, branch, and emerge at another level.

A portion may be cut off when the plateau changes. If it contains an active margin, it seals and continues pumping. If not, its inhabitants abandon it as the water fouls.

There are small cup-dwellers that seek these separations. They wait nearby with empty chest chambers, then enter the damaged tissue to collect what is exposed. Eggs, filter animals, white growth: all the provisions of a nest, released at once.

They must work quickly. Other mouths approach through the galleries.

The great slurry-feeders extend pale throat tissues into the breach. Armored worms rasp at the mineral margins. Flat predators arrive along the undersides of roots. For a few hours the quiet forest is full of tiny sounds—scraping, sucking, the clicking of chest plates being forced shut over too much cargo.

Then the wound closes, or becomes a passage, and the gathering disperses.

No animal sees the whole of it. Each leaves with what it can carry.

***

At the opening in the far mountain, green surfaces slowly turn toward the incoming air.

Some are leaves. Others have joints that close in the wrong direction.

Water beads on them almost at once.

Within the cleft, large pale bodies are withdrawing from one another. Their exposed sides bear rows of shallow hollows, each fringed with collecting tissue. Roots bridge the widening spaces until they pull free. The bodies settle back against the newly revealed stone.

Small animals move over them.

They are already occupied with feeding. One works at the hinge of a cup, pressing until it releases water. Another drags a dead grazer toward a crevice. Along a damp ridge, a procession of travelers carries eggs under tightly closed plates.

They do not come out of the mountain in a rush. Most remain where they are.

As the opening widens, the inner air dries. Collecting fronds turn. Mouths close. Several of the little travelers reverse direction and climb back into the darker green.

One stays on the exposed edge.

It tests the fresh stone with its trailing limbs, then lowers its chest against a shallow mark left by a root. For a while it waits there. The stone is warm, but the mark holds no water.

The animal rises and goes on searching.
