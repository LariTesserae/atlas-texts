The people are not especially small, though you remember them that way. It is their habit of keeping their elbows close, of turning sideways before there is any need to, of making room for things you have not yet noticed. Out on the bare floor of the hall, a woman straightening from a basket can seem to grow abruptly into her full height.

Their clothes are soft at the joints and heavily patched across the shoulders. They carry loads high on the back, away from the gutters and the trailing leaves. Most wear a second, loose piece of cloth over the head and neck. Indoors, this is hung on a forked stick rather than a hook and left for a while before being folded. Small occupants must be allowed to leave it.

There is an old man whose neck cloth has supported the same colony for so long that his daughter refuses to bring it into her house. He says they are a clean kind. She says that is not the point. He stands outside her door and removes it with exaggerated care while she pretends not to watch.

They do talk. From the hall, you seldom hear them because most conversation takes place toward the backs of rooms, where a smooth wall returns a low voice clearly. The houses are full of little sounds that do not travel: grinding, the splitting of a dry stalk, a child repeating a word incorrectly to make another child laugh.

Laughter is not forbidden or even discouraged. It simply startles so many things that a joke sometimes has to be interrupted while the room settles.

Their hands are conspicuous. The nails are kept short, and the sides of the forefingers are polished by work. Many adults have pale, crooked scars on the backs of their hands, where something concealed beneath a leaf objected to being moved. They touch unfamiliar surfaces with a brush first. A visitor may mistake this for ceremony until someone lends them a brush.

Children learn the routes of animals before they learn the names of all the households. There is the route that may be crossed, the route that may be crossed after tapping, and the route beside which one must put down whatever one is carrying and wait. These distinctions are not absolute. They change with the season, the widening of a seam, the appearance of young.

A child who has memorized yesterday’s rules is still a child.

The adults teach by resting a hand lightly between the shoulder blades. The hand restrains, releases, turns. In the hall, you can see children move with the memory of it: stopping with their weight forward, looking not at their feet but at the wall ahead.

They also run. They know places where running is possible, and use them furiously. The widest tier of the hall bears an oval of faintly darker stone from generations of bare heels. Games are suspended when the shell-backed creatures change their line. There is considerable argument about whether a player caught during such a suspension remains caught afterward.

The children collect things from the new cracks. Translucent flakes. Empty jointed skins. Little hard cups that might be eggs or seeds, although nothing has ever emerged from the ones kept in boxes. Adults confiscate anything warm.

There are very few warm things here.

The household animals are not easily distinguished from the others. Some are fed; some are merely not removed; some are carried to a particular ledge each morning and become indignant if this is done late. A six-footed sill animal may accept scraps from everyone and sleep only above one person’s bed. No one would necessarily say that person owns it.

One house keeps three blunt-eared grazers indoors through the coldest weeks. They sleep beneath a raised platform and smell faintly of crushed stalks. Their owner insists they improve the warmth of the room. Her husband says they eat the bedding. Both statements appear to be true.

The narrow gutter animals are treated with less affection and greater care. They clear soft growth from the overlaps, where it would otherwise draw water outward and let it fall uselessly onto the paving. A household without them spends a discouraging part of each morning taking its gutters apart.

Yet they bite, and their young will enter a sleeve.

People coax them out with a chilled spoon, holding it against the skin until the small body presses toward the colder surface. Almost every house has such a spoon. Several are quite beautiful.

The pale flying creatures are never named individually. Their markings shift with each molt, and perhaps they do not remain in the town long enough. Nevertheless, an old woman in the uppermost house claims to recognize one by the way it approaches a flower and then thinks better of it. She leaves a window open for it even when the cold troubles her knees.

When it fails to come, she says nothing. Someone brings her a cutting from the flowers it used to visit.

Not every accommodation is gentle. The people take eggs from beneath the roof turf. They catch the fat, blind animals that push out of certain seams after a long pulse of air. They kill predators that settle too near a sleeping platform, though moving one is preferred if it can be moved without breaking it.

Food is steamed in tightly covered pots over small, enclosed fires. The fuel is dry leaf ribs, pressed husks, pieces of wood already too thin for another use. The lid is tilted afterward so its water runs into a cup.

There is meat at some meals. The children know where it came from. They may have carried the animal home themselves, cupped in both hands to keep its feet from being damaged.

The granary keeper is a broad woman with one clouded eye. She can tell by lifting a basket whether its contents have been dried enough to store. Sometimes she makes a mistake, and a covered bin develops a quiet movement all through it. Then half the town spends an afternoon sorting grain on the hall steps while pale larvae try to hide in the seams of their clothing.

She does not find this charming.

She has a vocabulary for unwelcome animals that is much richer than the vocabulary used for the welcome ones. A boy helping her once pointed out that two things she called by different names were the same creature. She asked whether he was the same boy while working as while stealing.

The person who measures the cracks is young. This surprises visitors. They expect someone old enough to remember the shape of every stair.

Her predecessor’s hands had become too uncertain to feel when a wooden slip first met resistance. He still accompanies her on some mornings, offering advice she does not always take. Afterward they compare the slips against notches cut into a beam inside the granary.

The measurements do not tell them everything. A crack can widen harmlessly for years, while a smaller one changes the weight on a roof overnight. They listen to the houses as well. They notice which door has begun to swing shut, where a jar rocks on a previously level shelf.

Once, the young measurer ordered a staircase taken down and was wrong. The wall held. People had to climb through a neighbor’s kitchen for nine days, carrying baskets past a sick man’s bed.

For a while afterward, whenever she entered the hall, somebody found a reason to discuss the inconvenience of stairs.

She still measures the cracks.

The seven houses contain more households than their doors suggest. Rooms have been divided, exchanged, opened into neighboring rooms as the mountain makes this possible or necessary. One family owns a sleeping recess that can only be reached through another family’s storeroom. The arrangement predates everyone’s birth and remains a source of resentment.

People marry into the town. People leave it. The gate without a gate is not a prohibition.

Those who return after years away step over routes that no longer exist. They reach for a rail that has been moved. Their relatives watch them with a tenderness that can become irritating very quickly.

The dead are carried out through the gate and up beyond the hanging gardens. It takes more people than you would expect, because the path is narrow and the burden must be passed from one level to the next. Those waiting below face the rock until it is their turn.

For several days afterward, someone will still leave space for the absent person beside a jar or on a step. Household animals continue to wait at the old places. Eventually a sill is occupied by something else, or a stool is moved, or a roof repair makes the familiar corner unrecognizable.

There is no agreement about what comes up from below.

Some say it is only air displaced by distant water. Some say the mountain contains enormous chambers that alternately warm and cool. A few people give the pulses names, distinguishing them by smell or by which animals move first.

The granary keeper maintains that they are all damp and all inconvenient.

During a strong pulse, however, she stands beside the youngest children. She does this without calling them over. They find her there, with her feet well apart on the stone and her basket braced against one hip.

The town rearranges itself around them. Leaves lift. The white hanging creatures draw in their long bodies. Somewhere beneath the steps, thousands of shells touch lightly together.

A child puts a hand into the granary keeper’s free hand.

She waits until the gutters have stopped shifting before she lets go.
