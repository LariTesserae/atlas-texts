The power noticed the log after they had gone.

Under Sela’s weight it had scarcely moved. Under her father’s it had pressed a little deeper into its support. By morning someone would have wedged it with a broken brick, and for a while this would have been enough.

The power held it steady through the night.

It did not hold every loose thing still. Some movement was how the town learned what lay beneath it. But it kept this crossing from turning under a foot, and it listened to the water gathering below the houses.

Water had no wish to enter a sleeping room. It went where there was room to go.

The power followed it through the clay, out to the gate, and beyond the gate into the river. It followed the river past places the people in the hollow had never seen: a bend where nets hung from forked poles, a low pasture, a ferry landing with a worn approach. There were houses downstream, too.

For a long time, though no part of the night was lost, it considered the basin.

***

In the morning there was a woman beside the southern shelter.

She wore a traveling cloak with mud along its hem. Sela’s father, coming off watch, took her for someone seeking a missing basket.

“I have no basket here,” she said. “I would like to see the water marks.”

He showed her the marks on the stake. She asked which had been made when the gate was shut, and which when it was open. She asked how long the river sometimes stayed high.

That question took more time.

The other watcher joined them. They disagreed about one autumn, then discovered they were remembering different years.

The woman waited.

“What are you building?” Sela’s father asked at last.

“Nothing yet.”

“Who sent you?”

“No one who owns land here.”

This did not answer everything. He was too tired to pursue it.

She looked at the basket under his arm.

“Will you be back tonight?”

“Tomorrow night.”

“And when do you sleep?”

He gave her the look people gave a question whose answer was already walking beside them.

She stepped out of his way.

***

She paid for her breakfast at the grain seller’s stall, and for a place to sleep, and for the work she asked people to do. When anyone asked where her money came from, she said she had brought it for this purpose.

She did not bargain down a price because someone needed the work.

By the fourth day, several people were willing to tell her things they had not thought worth saying at the shelter. The woman with the vibrating bench took her inside. The man with the catching door showed her his seed. The reed sorter made her sit down and learn the difference between two stems.

The widow refused to have a trench opened beside her wall.

The woman looked at the cramped room, the hanging pot, the mat from which its owner could reach the lintel.

“We will find another place to look,” she said.

That evening the widow told her about three households who could not afford to stop building, even if stopping was what the ground required.

The woman listened to that as carefully as she had listened at the gate.

***

The first work was above the town.

People found this wasteful. They needed gravel below, not men walking the rim with measuring cords.

But there was water entering the hollow that need never pass beneath a house. There were old channels hidden by cultivation, and a depression in the southern fields through which a second way out might be made.

The woman brought the farmers to look. She brought people from downstream. She paid for the grain that would be lost where the work crossed a field, and would not let the town’s overseer describe an occupied strip as unused merely because its owner was absent.

There were arguments. One lasted all morning.

She did not make the arguments pleasant by entering anyone’s mind.

At noon she bought food, so that being hungry would not decide who could remain.

Eventually, there were cords on the upper ground as well as below.

***

Some work she did without witnesses.

Far beneath the southern bank lay a bed of coarse gravel, sealed between layers of clay. No well reached it; no spring depended on it. She made room there for water to wait when the river was high.

She did not open a hole beneath the town. She left the bearing ground beneath the houses undisturbed. The passage into that deep place ran from the new collecting pool, away from foundations, and its mouth was broad enough to be seen and cleared.

While people slept, she shaped the hidden banks against collapse. Where the stored water would return to the river, she gave it a slow passage through stone.

She could have made a drain that swallowed anything.

Instead, she made one that would leave a dropped cup within reach.

In the morning the workers found that the cut they had expected to struggle with for weeks had reached gravel already. Water lowered in the trench. They stood watching, tools in hand.

The woman was there with them.

“Will it always do that?” one asked.

“No. We must still watch the river.”

They put a measuring stake beside it.

***

The unsafe houses did not become safe merely because the water had somewhere else to go.

Three were emptied. A fourth needed only to lose the stack of bricks someone had stored against its wall. The woman paid for carrying them away.

For the families who had to move, she found rooms before asking them to leave. When there were not enough rooms, she helped build more on the rim.

The widow went up to inspect hers.

“Who has the right to come in?”

“You do.”

“Who else?”

“Anyone you invite.”

She tried the door twice. Then she asked whether the grinding stone would be carried up whole.

It was.

Not everyone chose to remain above. Some returned when their foundations had been rebuilt. Others discovered that they had liked the town’s company more than its damp, and came down each morning to work.

The woman did not require anyone to call either place home.

***

At the gate, there were soon four people who knew the watch.

The new bench was long enough for a person to lie down. The old warped board became a shelf. Under it hung the smooth piece of wood, still passed from hand to hand; a written roster did not replace the certainty of feeling someone else take its weight.

The watcher with poor hearing helped place the new water marks. Sela’s father taught the others the different sounds at the sill.

Neither surrendered his opinion of the other’s method.

Both slept more.

***

The central pool was not filled.

The old fisherman had feared it would be. When the woman asked him where its deeper part lay, he pointed, then looked around for his sister.

She was already shaking her head.

Together they found it.

A portion of the shore was left to reeds, with a firm approach elsewhere for people who needed access. The fish had deeper water through the dry weeks. The willows’ exposed roots were covered properly. The little trays of onions did not turn overnight into gardens, but good earth began arriving with the gravel, and some of it went to households with no relatives in the fields.

The dog acquired a second bowl.

The widow said the first had been perfectly adequate.

***

Sela eventually asked the woman whether she had done something to the log.

They were at the crossing. It had proper supports now, and space for two people to pass if one turned sideways.

“What makes you ask?”

“I came back that night. I forgot my cord at the shelter.”

“You should have woken someone.”

“I know. The log moved before my foot touched it. Then it stopped.”

“Yes,” the woman said. “I held it.”

Sela crouched to look underneath.

There was only shadow, a little running water, and the familiar underside of the wood.

“Can you hold other things?”

“Some.”

“All the houses?”

“For a time.”

“Why didn’t you?”

The woman sat on the edge of the walkway.

“I have been holding two of them. The ones beside the drying ground. Until the people inside had somewhere to go.”

Sela looked toward the roofs.

“Do they know?”

“Yes.”

“Are you tired?”

“No.”

Sela considered this. Then she leaned against the woman’s shoulder for a moment, experimentally, with a child’s considerable weight.

The woman stayed quite still.

***

By the time the fine autumn rains came, the town was less crowded.

This was not how everyone would have chosen to describe its good fortune. The grain seller missed having customers pressed close to her doorway. One of the brickmaking brothers complained about the distance to the new building plots, although he liked the ground when he got there.

There was a lane that had been kept open and had actually remained open. There was money set aside for the gate watchers’ grain, held where more than one person could count it. There were roofs whose owners had been allowed to wait.

On the first wet evening, people came out to watch the gutters.

The water ran milky, then clear. At the southern shelter, the watcher checked both stakes. Beyond the bank the river was high, and the gate stayed shut.

No one had to stand all night in the rain wondering how much longer the houses could bear it.

Sela’s father was at home, mending a basket by the doorway. She sat beside him sorting her pieces of cord. Farther up the lane someone was singing badly enough that another person called for mercy.

The woman passed them carrying a pot she had borrowed.

Sela lifted a hand. Her father looked up and nodded.

Under the houses, the ground was still settling. It would do so for years. There would be work tomorrow, and disagreements about how to do it.

For tonight, the bowl stayed dry beneath the sleeping mat. The door opened without scraping.

The power returned the pot, and stayed for supper.
