The answer comes three times.

Two squeaks close together, then a third from farther inside the wall.

The female presses her nose into the drain. Water has risen over its lower lip. She pushes the fibers ahead of her, braces her hind feet against the concrete, and disappears.

Above the plateau, something has been listening.

It does not mistake the smallness of the voices for the size of what is happening.

For a while it attends to the wall: to the old sacks packed between the studs, the wet spreading upward through them, the three young animals whose weight has begun to stretch the narrow ledge beneath their bedding. It attends also to the worker beside the engine, who has slept for four hours in the last two days and is trying to distinguish a loose mounting bolt from a damaged bearing by sound.

It follows the discharge to the next basin. Follows that basin to a notch in the rock. Follows the falling water through cloud to a river swollen against the piers of a bridge far below.

There are houses beside that river.

It could empty the upper lake. There is room for all this water somewhere, but not everywhere it might be sent.

It listens longer.

***

The worker finds the loose bolt.

Their torch has been failing all evening. Now the beam steadies, and in its white circle they see a bright crescent where the engine mounting has shifted against its washer.

They stop the pump.

The sudden quiet is not quiet at all. Rain strikes the roof. The bridge rings under a blow from drifting wood. From the maintenance house comes a faint scratching.

The worker puts the spanner in a pocket and goes out.

Something about the footbridge makes them pause.

They have crossed it hundreds of times. They know the bowed railing and the place where the decking flexes. Tonight, looking at the black accumulation of roots on the upstream side, they notice that one of the pier’s anchor plates is lifting.

They take the longer way around, along the stone shoulder behind the shed.

Halfway to the house, the bridge moves.

There is a sharp report, almost lost in the water noise. One end drops a hand’s breadth. The rake slides along the rail and hangs by its hooked head.

The worker stands still in the rain.

Then they go on, faster.

***

Inside the wall, the female has stopped arranging fibers.

She is pulling at a sack.

It is a good sack, thick and soft from years of use. She has brought it upward twice already. Now one corner is trapped beneath the water, and her pulling folds the dry part over the young.

She loosens her grip at once.

Something touches the wall from the other side.

She freezes, muzzle raised.

Two knocks.

Not the stamps from the bridge. A smaller sound, transmitted through wood.

The worker takes away a loose board.

Light enters the chamber.

For a moment there is a crowd of wet whiskers, a flash of teeth, the young pressing backward into a space that does not go far enough. The worker lowers the torch.

“Oh,” they say.

They fetch the fish box from beneath the kitchen table.

The old coat smells of dust, cooking fat, and animals that have been there before. They set it beside the opening and retreat to the doorway.

The female does not move.

The water does.

It climbs the thickness of a claw along the exposed stud.

She bites the loose skin above the nearest youngster’s shoulders.

***

The power waits through the first crossing.

It waits while the female puts the youngster into the box, climbs in after it, climbs out again, and investigates the dark beneath the table. It waits while she returns to the wall.

The second youngster catches a forefoot in a fold of sackcloth.

Here the power acts.

A rotten thread parts.

The foot comes free.

There is no light, no new sound. The female backs out carrying her young, and the worker, who has been holding their breath, lets it go.

The third youngster is the smallest. It makes no sound during the journey. Once beneath the coat, it begins to squeak so fiercely that the female gets into the box and puts a paw over it.

The worker sits down on the kitchen floor.

For a little while, this is enough.

***

By midnight, it is no longer enough.

Water enters beneath the kitchen door. The worker moves the box onto the table, but the female will not remain there. She comes down by way of a chair, enters the water, and circles the room looking for a passage that still leads somewhere she trusts.

Outside, other families are leaving their islands.

The power could make the house impervious. It could keep that one room dry while the flood passed around it.

Beyond the window, a bank bear is standing on a willow branch with a youngster in its mouth.

The power turns its attention to the stone shoulder behind the buildings.

There is a ridge there almost wholly buried beneath peat. Once, before the road and long before the sheds, it divided two shallow pools. Its highest part now supports a patch of bent willow and a scatter of rounded stones. Even at the present flood level, some of its ground is dry.

Not much. Not enough.

Under it, the rock is sound.

The change begins so slowly that the snails on the underside of a stone do not lose their hold.

Along an old fracture, the ridge rises.

Water slips away from its flanks in narrow, temporary falls. Peat wrinkles and settles. Willow roots stretch; the power supports them where they would otherwise tear. A nest in the crown of one shrub tilts, then steadies.

The movement stops.

The ridge is still low by the measure of mountains. By the measure of an animal carrying its young, it is high ground.

On the basin side, its edge lies in a long, gentle slope. Toward the current, the stones fit together more closely, leaving deep recesses above the water. There are narrow ways into these, and other ways out.

The displaced water spreads across miles of flooded meadow. At the river below, no one sees a change against the bridge piers.

Rain continues to fall on the new shore.

***

The worker sees it in the sweep of the torch.

They look once, turn the light away, then look again.

The willow patch is higher than the windowsill.

They remember looking down on it that afternoon.

For several seconds they say nothing. Then the female climbs onto the kitchen chair and begins pulling at the coat.

The worker brings the box down.

There are two old duckboards in the storeroom. They lay them from the back step toward the exposed ground, weighting the near ends with a sack of bolts. The far ends rest on stone where there should not be stone.

The female comes to the doorway.

She tastes the air. Rain, willow bark, disturbed peat. No engine vibration.

Her paws find the first board. She stops where it bends beneath her weight, retreats, comes forward again.

The worker does not hurry her.

Across the basin, a husky call sounds once.

The female raises her head.

Then she returns to the box.

***

The power stays through the night.

Not everything requires its hand.

The female chooses a recess under the willow roots rather than the larger cavity beneath a boulder. Another adult arrives and is driven away, then admitted to a neighboring shelter. A youngster tumbles down the wet slope and climbs back by itself, indignant and coughing.

The worker carries the coat to the mouth of the chosen recess. By dawn it has been pulled entirely inside.

Once, near the broken bridge, a young bank bear enters the current at the wrong angle.

The power puts a stillness behind a stone.

The animal turns into it, rests with its chin just above water, and eventually scrambles ashore.

Above the escarpment, the scavengers make their morning circuits. They find a fish stranded in the grass where the ridge rose, and quarrel over it until one carries it away.

***

In the morning there is work.

The pump must be secured. The bridge must be closed. Someone must reach the cable station and report that the road is no longer a road.

The worker writes down the night’s water levels, then stands a long time over the next line in the log.

At last they write:

*High ground behind house now accessible. Animals sheltering. Keep clear.*

They underline the last two words.

By the following evening, clipped sedges lie along the new bank. Someone has pushed a twig into a crack between two stones. Mud clouds the shallow water where a forepaw is feeling for mussels.

The old female sleeps with her clouded eye toward the entrance.

Her good eye is closed too.

Under her flank, three young bodies shift and settle. Water sounds outside, close enough to hear, low enough to remain outside.

The power attends to them a little longer.

Then its attention widens: to the exhausted worker sleeping upstairs, to the loosened peat islands, to the full river below the clouds.

On the ridge, a small paw emerges from beneath the coat.

It rests on dry ground.
