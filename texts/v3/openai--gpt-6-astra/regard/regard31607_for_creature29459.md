The power does not first touch the creature.

It rests its attention on the water around it: the films between flattened stems, the beads held in translucent loops, the small reserve beneath the sedge root against which the pale fold is pressed. It follows these inward. There are passages still open behind the wall, but some end in ice and others in peat that has dried too far to soften readily again.

It follows the tremor outward, too, through the pipe supports and the concrete plinth.

Stopping the pumps would be simple. But the piece beside the vent depends on the warmth of machinery that is making the larger body’s shelter uninhabitable. The piece in the culvert cannot retreat quickly. Water returning all at once would tear loose the stack with the birch seedlings before its occupant could find a deeper seam.

The power waits through several strokes of the pump.

Then, behind the hanging roots, a little water ceases to drain.

Nothing wells up. No bright surface appears. The peat holds what it had been losing, and the pale fold remains against the stem.

For the first night, this is enough.

***

The maintenance worker comes down before daylight with a replacement belt over his shoulder. His knee hurts on the steps. He has been meaning to tell someone about the thing beneath the vent, but there is no version of the story he likes hearing in his own voice.

At the door he stops.

A strip of wet material lies across the concrete, just short of his boot. One end is folded under. The other carries a patch of moss.

He watches it longer than he has watched it before.

The power does not put a thought into him. It holds the condensation warm enough that it will not freeze during the time he needs.

A pale fold opens. It presses against a shallow defect in the plinth, finds no way through, and draws back.

The worker lowers the belt.

He goes into the shed and returns with a shallow tray used for catching oil. He cleans it twice, although the second cleaning costs him hot water he had wanted for his hands. He fills it with wet peat from beside the wall and places it against the concrete.

The creature does not enter.

After a while he moves the tray out of its path.

This is the first useful thing he does with it.

He leaves the wet peat there and puts a loose cover over the patch, high enough not to touch the moss. Later he takes a photograph. The photograph shows almost nothing, so he takes another when the folds are open.

***

There are people who can arrange for a pump to stop without leaving a worker to answer for the consequences. There are people who know how water moves through cut peat, and people who know how little a photograph can establish.

The power brings none of them unwillingly.

But the worker’s message reaches someone who reads it carefully. A visit is not cancelled. Money that would have paid for another season of extraction becomes available for work whose result will be less visible.

The worker stays employed. He knows which bank sheds slabs after a thaw, which drain runs under the snow, and where the tank supports have begun to lean. He also knows where not to put a shovel.

When the visitors arrive, the thing beneath the cover remains motionless for two days.

They leave their instruments beside it rather than lifting it onto a table.

On the third day, it moves into the damp peat the worker has set nearby. Only its leading folds go first. The rest follows slowly enough that one visitor misses the movement entirely while looking down to write.

They cannot agree whether the portions are separate animals. They mark each occupied place on the same map.

That is sufficient to begin.

***

The changes are made from the higher ground downward.

Low barriers interrupt the drains. Some have adjustable openings. Water gathers behind them, dark under the ice, and is allowed to rise by small amounts. The pumping station runs less often, then for shorter intervals. During each quiet period, the worker walks the terraces.

At first the quiet makes the place seem exposed.

He hears a loose buckle against his coat. He hears the frozen grass scrape itself in the wind. Once there is a soft, extended tearing somewhere in a bank, and he backs away before the slab falls.

The power attends to the places no one has found.

Beneath the tank, it keeps a seam yielding while the warmed pipe cools. In the culvert, it leaves room behind the crystals for a folded edge to withdraw. When the stack with three birches settles, the settlement takes an hour instead of a second. Its dark occupant eases downward with the wetting blocks.

It does not gather the pieces into one body. Their meeting and parting belong to them, whatever those acts may mean.

Near the vent, a small temporary shelter replaces the warmth of the generator. Water drips beneath it onto peat rather than concrete. The creature can leave along a covered, saturated seam; it can also remain. For much of the winter it does both, extending into the seam and withdrawing again.

No one pulls it free of the moss.

***

Spring does not make the basin beautiful all at once.

The thaw exposes rubbish. Some banks collapse into ugly heaps. The first pools have an oily-looking skin of iron bacteria, and the old cut faces remain square above them. There are broad stretches where nothing green returns.

The power does not raise the ground to its former surface.

It could lay something over the wound. It could fill the basin with a substance resembling what was taken. But the peat was made by growth, saturation, burial, and the long survival of things that did not entirely decay. Its absence has dimensions. The power leaves those dimensions visible.

What it supplies is time without further cutting, water without violent inundation, and shelter while the new margins establish themselves.

The work continues.

Cotton-grass takes hold on a low shelf. Moss fragments lodge against a barrier. The worker, now checking water levels instead of fuel, learns that a rise too small to notice from the rim can change the whole underside of a bank.

By the second winter, the station is cold.

The patch beside its wall is still green, but beneath the moss there is only peat, roots, and the impression of something that has left.

Farther in, behind the old fresh face, a broad thin body occupies several connected seams. Perhaps two portions have met there. Perhaps one has widened.

A pale fold rests against a sedge stem.

Through the ground comes the weight of a person walking, pausing, walking on. Then a much smaller disturbance: water finding a passage between fibres.

The fold opens a little.

There is room behind it now.
