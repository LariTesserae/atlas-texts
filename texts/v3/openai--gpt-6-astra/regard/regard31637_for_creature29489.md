The power noticed her hand around the cup.

It could have noticed the plateau first. There was enough of it to occupy a lesser attention for centuries: the ancient folded surfaces, the pressure returning by routes that no longer lay entirely in the present, the weight of a city distributed among its possible positions.

But Sere’s hand had tightened before the floor moved.

She knew the adjustment was coming. Her knee knew. The cupboard gave a small click, and she reached for the cup while her son was still turning toward the window.

The power waited until the movement had passed.

Then it looked carefully at what she had been holding steady.

***

For some time, nothing happened that anyone could have distinguished from maintenance.

A drain ramp settled more securely into its socket. A window tie, worn almost through on its hidden side, held until Sere reached it the following morning. Deep beneath the northern mill, a split in a pressure vessel stopped short of the surface.

The split remained visible. When the vessel was inspected, the fitter found it, swore, and called for a replacement.

The power did not make the vessel seem sound.

It was learning the city.

It followed water through the basins and heat through the mills. It listened to the things that came before their sounds and the sounds that had outlived the things that made them. It went below the service shafts, where the maps became descriptions of bodily movements: crouch here, turn the left shoulder, do not breathe out until through.

There were indeed inhabited rooms.

Some belonged to people who considered Sere’s city a troublesome district overhead. Some had doors opening onto mornings that had not occurred on the surface for a very long time. Some were occupied only during adjustments, by families who had learned to keep everything necessary within reach of the table.

The uncharted supply lines supplied them.

The power left those lines alone.

It also knelt beside one of the gray animals.

For a considerable interval, neither moved.

Afterward, the animal carried its white strand under a sill. The power knew rather more than before, but there was still no reason to pick it up.

***

On Wednesday, someone came to the dining room.

She wore a plain coat with cuffs that had stopped growing. Her shoes were clean in the manner of shoes recently cleaned, rather than shoes never worn. She carried a covered bowl.

Sere found her reading the notice by the door.

“Wednesdays,” Sere said.

“Yes.”

“People keep asking whether we can change it.”

“I can come on Wednesdays.”

The woman looked through the doorway at the table.

“Is that the place?”

Sere nodded toward Olan’s chair.

“You’ll have to sit properly back. The weight needs to go near the wall.”

The woman sat properly back.

The meal in her bowl was warm. It contained storage kernels cooked until they had split, with something green and sharp folded through them. There was enough for six, including the man who always said he had already eaten and then accepted a substantial portion.

For the first half hour, the conversation concerned a change to the freight timetable.

The woman listened. When the turbine fitter spoke too softly, she did not answer before the others could hear him. She waited while he repeated himself.

After the dishes had been moved aside, Sere asked what work she did.

“I can sometimes make a burden easier to carry.”

“We’ve all got one of those.”

“Yes,” the woman said.

“Can you do knees?”

“I can help with yours, if you want.”

Sere looked at her across the table.

“Are you selling something?”

“No.”

This required a longer conversation.

***

Sere allowed the woman to examine her knee in the kitchen, with the door open.

She did not wake the next morning young.

The joint was still thickened. The foot-wrapping still required an awkward reach. But she stood before remembering to brace herself, and remained standing.

She walked to the basin, came home, and sat down.

Then she walked to the basin again.

Her son found her inspecting the little wheeled frame.

“You won’t need that now?”

“I’ll need it for the weights.”

He put his hands in his pockets.

“And when you’re tired,” he said.

Sere rubbed the pale handle with her thumb.

“Yes.”

The next Wednesday she had questions ready.

There were seven written on the back of an old route amendment. By the end of the evening she had added four more.

***

Word spread unevenly.

Some people asked for things that could be given at once. A pain relieved. A cataract cleared. A child brought safely out of a street that had ceased to have a far end.

Others asked for the mills to stop, or the dead to return, or a house to remain exactly as it had been during the best summer of their lives.

The power did not answer these requests as though they were all the same.

Before touching the mills, it spent several shifts with the workers. It learned which vibrations told them that a bearing was failing. It learned how much of the city’s warning system passed through feet, teeth, and bowls standing on shelves.

A quieter mill could kill people if quietness arrived without preparation.

The first altered turbine therefore kept its old warning beat. Around it, the ordinary pounding softened.

The workers were given instruments that showed what they had previously endured in their skulls. There were trials, disagreements, a damaged rotor caught early, and one furious fitter who wanted his old machine restored.

His was restored.

Three weeks later he asked to try again.

Away from the mills, people began hearing the beadfall for longer in the mornings.

No one had realized how far that small sound could travel.

***

The dead were more difficult.

The power could tell the difference between a person living on another folded surface and the trace left by someone who was gone. The inhabitants often could not, and the city had taught them to be afraid of deciding too soon.

It established a room near a continuous street, with water, warm clothing, and doors that could be opened from either side.

People who returned were brought there.

They were not required to remember the correct year before being given breakfast. Their families were asked whether they wished to be contacted. Sometimes the answer was complicated.

The power did not fill Olan’s chair with a convincing likeness.

When Sere eventually asked about him, it told her what it knew.

She stood at the kitchen counter for a while afterward.

Then she said, “He used to bring that awful paste.”

“I’ve heard.”

“He knew we didn’t like it.”

Outside, a freight warning sounded.

Sere took six cups from the cupboard, counted them, and put one back. Then, remembering the woman in the dining room, she took it out again.

***

The houses took longer.

Sere brought the power along her route. She showed it where a staircase had begun to shorten, where an unused pantry pressed against a bedroom, where the wall answered with the pressure of someone else’s palms.

Here the power asked her to wait.

It went around.

The journey could not have been made by going through the neighboring house. It involved a lower leaf, a room with laundry hanging beside a warm conduit, and a doorway whose threshold remembered being vertical.

On the other side of Sere’s wall lived a man who had been maintaining an empty room for reasons much like hers.

He had heard her complaints about the bread.

He had recommendations, none accessible from her side.

The power asked his permission too.

Eventually, a narrow recess appeared in each room. Inside it was a small shutter. Opening one shutter did not open the other. Either occupant could leave theirs closed.

The first exchange consisted of names, followed by a disagreement about the weather.

Sere came home late.

***

She wanted the houses kept open.

All forty-three.

Then, when she understood that the offer was serious, she began adding others.

The power walked with her through rooms whose windows had not admitted a face in a century. It listened while she explained which walls were original and which doors had once led somewhere useful.

At the thirty-first house, it asked, “If this were allowed to close, who would lose something?”

Sere gave the former owner’s name.

“Is anyone waiting for them here?”

“I don’t know.”

They left that house undecided.

Others were easier. A room used for shelter during interrupted journeys. A hall where children played in bad weather. A house someone still hoped to occupy. Places that mattered for reasons not entered in municipal records.

These stayed.

For the rest, there was time to ask. Notices went up. Cupboards were checked. The power could wait through a disputed inheritance, a late reply, a person standing in a doorway unable to say what they wanted.

Where rooms were released, their closing was gradual. Useful fittings were removed. Addresses were recorded. The ground thickened into low, sheltered commons with sitting places along the veins.

Sere visited the first one three times.

On the fourth visit, she brought her lunch.

***

The greatest change was beneath the city.

There were layers carrying more than their share of its weight. Whenever they yielded, the strain passed upward until someone steadied a table, a stair broke, or a street slipped into an old arrangement.

The power began taking some of that weight.

It did so cautiously, leaving the living plateau room to contract, feed, and turn toward warmth in the slow ways still available to it. It did not pull the buried leaves apart. Too many lives had grown between them.

Instead, deep supports began to form in places where there had only been compression.

The crews found them first: broad, pale structures, warm to the touch, with passages left around every supply line. Their surfaces bore marks showing where inspection was safe.

The city still moved.

But there was warning now, and somewhere for the movement to go.

***

Sere’s route became shorter.

This was not accomplished without an argument.

The municipality took on more attendants. The power provided the means, but the attendants negotiated their own hours, and several had strong opinions about carrying chairs. Overnight work was paid. No building of historical importance was given precedence over an occupied house becoming unsafe.

Sere kept seventeen addresses and the Wednesday dining room.

Sometimes she missed one.

Nothing dreadful happened.

It took her longer to trust this than to trust her knee.

***

One evening, well into the next cold season, Sere sat by her kitchen window while her son brought in the washing.

Button stood beneath the cupboard. Its head was turned toward the pipes.

Far away, the northern mill struck six times.

The scraping vibration followed, quieter than it had once been. A seam opened along the avenue, and the floor began its familiar adjustment.

Sere reached toward the table.

Beneath the city, something received the weight.

Her cup trembled once in its saucer and was still.

She left her hand beside it.

After a little while, she went on drinking her tea.
