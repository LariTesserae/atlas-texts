The power sees that the knot will hold.

It also sees the place, nine millimeters farther along the cord, where a strand has been cut by the edge of the chair’s frame. That will hold too, for a while.

It could mend the strand without being noticed. Instead it lets the copper light remain a little longer.

The person turns the chair back. Their thumb finds the roughness.

“Ah,” they say.

They unwind three careful turns.

The power waits. It has no objection to work taking time. But while it waits, it attends to the city.

***

There is a great deal to attend to.

The canopy is neither an enemy nor a wound. Lifting it would cool the courts, stop several kinds of rain, and kill the white growth on the deepest cultivation plates. Within a season the people would be hungry under a beautiful sky.

The translucent skin in the drains is alive. In small quantities it takes something harmful from the water. In large quantities it puts something else back.

The municipal notice is approximately right.

The sleepers are sleeping.

The power follows the water through their beds, through the fine passages of their bodies, and finds processes too slow to be mistaken for life by anything in a hurry. Some are healing. Some are changing without healing. One is in danger because a feeder pipe has narrowed behind a wall, although the wetness of the bed still looks sufficient.

Here the power acts at once.

It holds the flow steady. Then it strikes the tending-room rail with the signal used in that district for a fault requiring inspection.

When the attendant arrives, the inspection window is clear enough to reveal the obstruction.

Elsewhere, it listens.

There are people who want the city restored to its former population. There are people who are relieved that their nearest neighbors live three courts away. A woman in a southern gallery wishes the lower gates would stop requiring two workers for a task one worker used to manage. Her apprentice wishes she would stop attempting it alone.

The power does not find a single wish large enough to stand for all of them.

***

Beside the doorway, the person finishes the knot.

By then the power has made a place for itself in the evening: a voice nearby, and a small interruption in the damp on the paving, no larger than the mat.

The pale animal lowers its belly to the stone.

The person looks at the interruption.

“Are you with the visitor?”

“No.”

“Are you the weather?”

“Not that either.”

The person puts the chair down, presses against its back, and tests the cord.

“What do you want?”

“To offer help.”

This is not an unfamiliar claim. The person examines it with the same attention they gave the damaged strand.

“What sort?”

“What is making your life difficult?”

A long pause follows.

Beyond the court, a gutter begins to fill. The person watches until the first water has crossed its upward bend.

“Does it have to be an important thing?”

“No.”

“My friend lives below the fifth exchange. I can get there. Coming back is troublesome.”

The power traces the journey.

There is a passage that closes before the return route opens. Between the two lies a wait of nearly seven hours, in a gallery with nowhere suitable to rest. The person has made the journey before. On the last occasion, their joints remained pale and sore for several days.

Their friend knows this. It is part of why no invitation has come.

“There used to be a waiting room,” the person says. “They shut it when the bed cracked.”

“Would you show me?”

“Not tonight.”

“Tomorrow?”

They glance at the chair.

“After the short cycle.”

***

The waiting room contains three broken rakes, a carrier without a lid, and a good door that has been stored badly.

The cracked bed stands beneath a painted notice. Someone has crossed out the old maximum occupancy and written a smaller number. Later someone else has crossed that out too.

The power could make the bed whole.

First it asks who looks after the room.

This takes longer than the walk.

The room belongs to one district, the feeder pipe to another, and the person who holds the key believes—with some justification—that reopening it will result in her being expected to clean it.

“I can repair stone,” the power says. “I can maintain the feed and clear the vents. I can clean it too.”

“For how long?”

They discuss this.

The keyholder has several questions that no one has thought to ask a benevolent power before. What will happen to belongings left behind? Can the room be closed if there is a pressure fault? Will it begin answering people who talk in their sleep?

They arrive at an arrangement.

The power repairs the bed. It leaves the old crack visible as a narrow line, so the next inspector can find where the work was done. It restores the water supply, adds an accessible shutoff, and submits to having the shutoff tested six times.

The keyholder discovers that it has cleaned behind the stored door.

She becomes somewhat more willing to believe it.

***

News travels by railing, ledger, and complaint.

Soon there are requests.

Some are simple. A handrail has been loose for eleven years. A cook wants a cupboard in which crisp food remains crisp. Two households ask for help moving a sleeper without waking him.

Some require long conversations.

The power sits with the physicians. It accompanies workers into the older channels. It gives explanations where it can, demonstrations where explanations are insufficient, and warning where a demonstration would be dangerous. In places where its help will have to continue, it says so plainly.

The maintenance ledgers acquire a new mark.

It means that the ordinary mechanism is not, for the moment, doing all the work. The mark is important. A worker should not have to stake their life on an inaccurate account of a pipe.

There are disagreements about which repairs should come first. The power makes its reasons available. Sometimes it changes its mind.

It also makes more waiting rooms.

***

The person visits their friend eleven days after the chair is repaired.

They take the chair, which makes the outward journey considerably more difficult than necessary.

At the fifth exchange they have to turn it sideways. At the receiving steps they meet someone going the other way with two food carriers, and there is a prolonged negotiation involving the chair’s ceramic foot.

Their friend opens the door.

For a moment neither speaks.

Then the friend says, “You brought that?”

“It’s yours.”

“I left it.”

“You used to complain when anyone else sat in it.”

The friend stands aside.

The power does not accompany them into the room.

It attends instead to the waiting place, where a traveler has just found that the bed is warm, the feeder channel is clear, and there are two hooks for clothes. She puts a hand against the stone before trusting her weight to it.

Farther down, behind the closed door, the visit goes imperfectly.

There is an old grievance. There is food that has been kept warm too long. The chair is put in three different places before both people are satisfied. Once, the visitor considers leaving early.

They stay.

When it is time to depart, their friend asks about the return journey.

“There’s somewhere to wait now.”

“Properly?”

“Yes.”

The friend touches the new cord along the chair’s back.

“Well,” they say. “Then next time you needn’t bring anything.”

***

Much later, the power finds the person resting at the exchange.

Water darkens the bed beneath their heels. Their cup stands on a shelf whose underside has been painted a deep, durable blue.

“Was it a good visit?” the power asks.

The person moves one hand uncertainly.

“A visit.”

The power accepts the distinction.

After a while the person says, “I’m going again.”

Beyond the wall, the first float drops. There is still plenty of time.
