The power noticed the difficulty with which she knelt.

It also noticed the door, which was closing correctly, and the bird, which needed water. These were not equal matters. They were simply all present.

It had come a long way without crossing a distance. In other places it had been perceived as weather, as a voice from an empty vessel, as a sudden understanding shared by people who had previously been unable to agree. Here, where the weather stood in walls and vessels often spoke without assistance, it chose to be visible.

Mera saw someone sitting on the lowest stair.

“Residents aren’t to leave things on the landing,” she said.

The visitor looked at the small bag beside its feet and put it on its lap.

“Thank you,” said Mera.

She worked the strip of paper under the door. It passed without catching. When she tried to stand, her knee stopped her halfway.

“May I help?” the visitor asked.

She held out her hand.

It did not pull her up. It supported exactly the weight she needed supported, through the whole awkward business of getting her foot beneath her.

By the time she was standing, she knew it was not a person.

She kept hold of its hand a moment longer.

***

They went to her room because she did not want this conversation in the entrance hall.

The power carried the cage. Mera carried her tools.

On the second landing, she asked, “Have you come about the building?”

“Partly.”

“Is it safe?”

“For tonight, yes.”

She stopped.

“And after tonight?”

“There are things that should be done.”

“There usually are.”

“Yes.”

This answer pleased her more than reassurance would have.

***

In her room, the power set the bird on the table, out of the draft. Mera filled a saucer and put it through the cage door.

She did not offer tea immediately. There was one clean cup, and she wanted to know whether the visitor drank before washing another.

“What do you want?” she asked.

“To make some things easier.”

“Which things?”

“I would like to begin with your knee.”

Mera sat down.

Outside, a freight elevator passed the window. Its platform carried three empty bins and a man eating something from paper. They watched his boots rise out of sight.

“Will I still feel when I’ve put my foot wrong?”

“Yes.”

“Will I need time off?”

“No.”

She put her hand over the knee, as if the visitor might otherwise mistake which one.

“All right.”

There was no light. For a moment she felt the precise shape of the injury: not as pain, but as something comprehensible, an old displacement around which her body had been making arrangements for years.

Then that sensation was gone.

She bent her leg.

She straightened it and bent it again. The joint made a small, ordinary noise.

For some time she said nothing.

The power waited while she went down the stairs and came back up.

When she returned, she was angry.

“Eleven years,” she said.

“Yes.”

She sat on the bed. She was thinking of things she had declined to do, and things she had done badly, and a particular winter afternoon when she had snapped at Tavi because he had left a book on the floor.

The visitor did not tell her to be grateful.

Eventually she washed the second cup.

***

The power did not ask Mera to name everyone who deserved help.

She would have made a conscientious list. She would also have forgotten someone, and remembered them during the night.

Instead, it asked whom it should speak to about the building, and whether it might return.

She gave it six names. Two were people she disliked.

Then she added the woman who cleaned the public hall.

“She knows what happens when we’re all sent over there. The committee only knows what it costs.”

***

Over the following weeks, the city acquired a visitor who could be found without being summoned by a ceremony.

It kept hours in an unused room beside the municipal survey office. When those hours proved unsuitable for night workers, it kept others as well. It visited people who could not come.

There were requests it refused, and requests it misunderstood until they were explained again.

One man wanted the grazer herds removed from the tram approaches. A driver wanted them kept away from the points. These turned out to be different requests. Warm resting places were established beyond the tracks, and the animals were allowed to discover them.

A reservoir engineer asked for a basin that would never need cleaning.

The power went down into an empty basin with her. Beneath the last shallow puddle, channel creatures had gathered around an escape board whose lower end no longer reached the floor.

They spent the first part of the visit extending the board.

The basin still needed cleaning afterward. It needed it less often, and its new shallow ledges gave small hands somewhere to take hold.

***

The ground was more difficult.

The power listened beneath the five shelves. It followed heat through stone that had not decided how heavy to become. It went to the unfinished peaks and returned with pale dust on its bag.

The surveyors asked whether it could stop the rising.

“Yes,” it said. “But not safely.”

They were disappointed. Several had brought plans.

It spread the plans on a table and examined them.

What it offered instead was a way to measure the pressure before it reached a wall.

The first instruments were not elegant. They resembled shallow bowls filled with dark water. Their surfaces tilted toward strains that the existing survey needles would not register for months.

The power taught the instrument makers how to build them. Then it let them improve the housings, which leaked when carried on trams.

Tests were made beneath an empty warehouse. There were arguments about what the readings meant. The visitor stayed for those.

By the next autumn, crews could distinguish between a foundation that needed adjustment and one that needed to be left alone. They could also tell when they did not yet know.

That last improvement caused difficulties in the department before it prevented an accident.

***

Mera’s employment took longer.

Neither authority objected to paying her a full wage. Each objected to paying the portion it believed belonged to the other.

The power attended a meeting.

It did not make the officials kinder. It asked who was responsible for inspecting a fire door when a change in the ground had brought it into contact with a heating pipe.

The discussion lasted until lunch.

Afterward, there was a single maintenance budget, two new positions, and a provision allowing Mera to keep her room if she stopped working.

She read that provision several times.

“Does this mean you expect me to stop?”

“No,” said the clerk.

“People say no when they mean eventually.”

“It means you can.”

She took the document home.

For the first time, the notice for the rooms on the southern shelf could lie on her table without also being a notice that she would have no income.

***

Senn asked whether the answering sounds could be silenced.

The power listened with him through an evening.

Just after nine, a hammer sounded below the newest cliff. Senn’s hand stopped above his bowl.

The answer came seven seconds later.

“I can keep the sound out of your room,” the power said. “I cannot yet tell you everything that makes it.”

Senn looked at Mera. She waited for him to decide.

They tried quiet for three nights.

On the fourth, he asked to have the ordinary sounds back: the freight lifts, the water, the late tram braking beyond the laundry. He wanted to hear his pupils coming up the stairs.

The deep answers no longer entered his room.

Elsewhere they continued, and people who wished to investigate them were given instruments, safe places to stand, and an honest account of what remained unknown.

***

Not everything changed at once.

Tavi still left complaints about breakfast. The entrance hall remained gray. The creature with the broken shell acquired a piece of red cord and refused a washer of excellent weight.

Mera’s new assistant was thirty-eight, formerly a pump fitter, and inclined to plane things before looking at the hinges.

She taught him.

When he could manage a morning alone, she took the tram to the southern shelf with Senn. They inspected the rooms from the notice. His table would fit, but only if the cupboard came out, and neither liked the view.

They went home by a longer route.

At one of the bare intervals between buildings, Mera got off the tram.

“What is it?” Senn asked.

“Nothing.”

She wanted to walk the rest of the way.

The streetlights had begun to come on. Between their concrete bases, the new stone held the last of the daylight. A group of grazers crossed ahead of them, making their small, distinct sounds.

Halfway home, she noticed that she had not been watching the pavement.

She continued walking.
