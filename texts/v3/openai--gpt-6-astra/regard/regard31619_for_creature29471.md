The water catches the folded end of the tail and opens it.

Usually this helps. The animal turns with the pull, lets its flank pass the narrowest part of the opening, and braces one forefoot inside. This time the concrete has moved. Not far: a grain’s width at the upper edge, rather more below. Enough to close the space through which its shoulder must follow.

It pushes. Its pads spread against the stone.

Behind it, the pump takes another stroke.

The power that has noticed the hollow feels this through the same concrete. It feels, too, the little fans of cartilage bending inside the animal’s toes, and the old break in the foundation, and the water entering that break under pressure. Farther in, a bearing has worn unevenly. The shaft now lifts on each turn before falling into its accustomed groove. The lift is being carried through a bracket to the floor.

There are many things the power could move.

It does not move the hunter.

Instead, it supports the weight of the broken concrete.

No light comes into the hollow. The fog does not open. Beneath the floor, where two rough faces of a crack have begun to grind together, they cease approaching.

The animal pushes again. Its shoulder passes. Skin scrapes from one fold along its throat, but the forefoot inside finds purchase, and the rest of the body follows in a quick, awkward rush.

It lies beyond the opening with its mouth slightly parted.

The water goes on moving.

For several strokes the power holds the foundation exactly where it is. This is not difficult for it. Knowing where to leave the foundation is more difficult.

There are eggs on the underside of the slab. There are tubes in the fracture, and a pale swimmer wedged in a pocket that has only just become connected to running water. A strand of bacterial film extends across the place where the concrete ought, by the building’s original design, to be solid.

The power does not put the building back as it was.

It attends to what is there now.

Under the broken slab, beyond the egg cavity, lies a thickness of gravel. Some grains carry living coatings. Others have remained pressed together in darkness since the concrete was poured. Beneath these is sound shale.

The weight passes down through the buried grains. They settle against one another, very slightly, until there is a support where there was only loose material. No passage closes. In the cavity above, one suspended egg trembles and becomes still.

The power eases its hold.

The slab remains.

Under it, the hunter touches the scraped place on its throat with a forefoot. Then it turns and presses its jaw to the nearest egg.

—

The bearing cannot be treated in quite the same way.

Its unevenness has entered the life of the hollow. A small hesitation before every fourth stroke gives the valve-dwellers time to fold their egg ribbons. The looseness around the shaft admits a seep that feeds the white comb-shaped animals. What was once waste is now a source of food. What would once have counted as a smooth-running machine would crush some of the things keeping it running.

The power follows a complete cycle.

Then another.

During the long return of water through the rock, it follows the water instead. There are deep channels under the eastern bank, a compressed layer of clay beneath the western pools, a pocket of trapped air that grows smaller when the machine is working. The hollow has more room inside it than its surface suggests.

It watches the small hunter move through three of those rooms. It watches the northern animal turn its damaged foot outward to brace against the pipe. It watches a grazer lose its hold and disappear into a threadlike mouth.

Nothing is stopped for that death.

The pump-house hunter eats, too, once the soreness in its throat permits it. It catches a swimmer near the intake and holds still afterward, while the hidden slits behind its head release water.

The power remains through the feeding and the long intervals between.

By the next cycle, it has found the part of the bearing that can be strengthened without sealing a crevice. During the unloaded portion of each turn, it restores a little of the missing support. Not the whole worn surface. Not the seep. The shaft’s damaging upward knock diminishes, while its pause remains.

It takes several days.

On the first day, the hunter withdraws earlier than it needs to.

On the second, it comes out again before the pump has finished.

By the fourth, the animals in the valve recess fold at their usual signal, and the concrete no longer strains beneath the hunter’s feet.

—

There is another place to attend to under the foundation.

When the egg chamber drains, its ceiling becomes dry before the strongest current through the exit has weakened. The young must release themselves within a brief interval. Some will miss it. They have broad feet and very little experience of water moving with force.

Beside the chamber is an old air pocket, separated from it by a plug of silt. Water already passes through, but slowly.

During successive quiet intervals, the power lets a little more of that silt travel out. It goes as silt ordinarily goes: a faint cloud, investigated by mouths, settling over stone. A hollow opens behind the plug. Its entrance is narrow, its floor uneven, its water sheltered from the direct pull of the intake.

The first creature to enter is a grazer.

The second is a swimmer, which stays until the hunter puts its face to the opening.

The refuge is not made inaccessible to hunger. But when the eggs hatch, there will be somewhere nearby for a young animal to lose its grip without being lost beneath the floor.

The parent explores it once, pushing its broad head as far as it will go. Later it leaves a mouthful of grit at the entrance. Water carries the grit away.

—

The hollow does not become gentler in every respect.

The air remains cold. Shells close over empty chambers. Mites find the eggs, and the parent takes down one that has softened. The northern hunter continues to walk on its incomplete foot.

There is still iron wearing inside the pump.

But its wear no longer drives the foundation apart, and the power has learned where to listen. It need not keep every stone in place to remain attentive.

Toward morning, the large hunter comes out onto the concrete. The scrape beneath its throat has closed to a pale seam. It steps around the shells and drinks.

Below it, the bearing begins to take the load.

The animal presses one forefoot flat. Through the five black pads comes the familiar gathering movement: the pause, the slight turn, the water waiting to be lifted.

It withdraws, cheek down against the polished opening.

This time there is room.
