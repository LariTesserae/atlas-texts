The power first noticed the hollow by its sound.

There were larger sounds in the mountains: glaciers shifting in their beds, rivers moving boulders under water. But this particular note came and went with the cloud, and had done so for a long time without repeating itself exactly.

The power listened.

While it listened, Essa came out of the shelter and put a stone on a piece of cloth. Then she put a larger stone on the first stone.

Something about this delayed the power’s departure.

It remained through the afternoon. It watched Meret separate two sorts of seed that looked identical until they were turned sideways. It watched a snail travel beneath the lip of a basin, carrying on its shell a bright bubble that gradually diminished. When the brown hunter entered the root mats, the power felt the sudden attention of all the small bodies there.

It did not interrupt the hunt.

That evening, Essa found it sitting near the upright slab.

She stopped well short of it. There had been no footsteps in the cleft, and no one could have come up the rope: it lay coiled beside her bedding.

The visitor had taken a shape that could sit on a stone. It had been less successful with its shadow, which fell toward the remaining sunlight.

“Meret,” she said.

He came to the doorway carrying the bowl.

The visitor looked down, noticed the shadow, and corrected it.

“That doesn’t improve matters,” Essa said.

“No,” said the power. “I suppose it doesn’t.”

It moved away from the slab so that it was not between them and the descending path.

“I have been listening to the mountain,” it said. “And watching you. I would like to do something useful here, if you are willing.”

Meret put the bowl inside the shelter.

“What sort of useful?”

“I can mend things. Change them. Provide things you lack.”

“Rain?” Essa asked.

“Yes.”

“Don’t.”

The power waited.

“We’ve had enough,” she said. “And if you stop it altogether, we’ll have something worse.”

“I won’t change the rain.”

Meret lowered himself onto the threshold. His knee caught halfway down. He paused, adjusted his foot, and finished the movement with an expression of exaggerated leisure.

Essa glanced at him, then back at the visitor.

“Will you still be here in the morning?”

“Yes.”

“Then we’ll think.”

Before going inside, she brought in the drying trays. The power offered to carry one.

“Both hands,” she told it. “And don’t tip it toward yourself.”

It carried the tray exactly as instructed.

***

In the morning, they had questions.

Would a thing mended remain mended?

Would there be a payment later?

Could something be changed back?

Did the power understand roofs?

The answers to the first three were satisfactory. At the fourth, the power looked at the shelter for some time.

“I understand this one now.”

“Well, come round the back,” Essa said.

She showed it the place where water followed a roof pole inward. In hard rain, it gathered above Meret’s bedding and fell only after they had settled for the night.

“We tried packing it,” she said. “That made it come down farther along.”

The power could have made the entire hollow dry. Instead, it followed the water backward through the overlap of the slabs. There was a small stone there, put in to steady a larger one. The stone divided the flow badly.

Moving it would divert water onto a cushion of flowering plants.

The power examined another route.

Meret watched it with growing approval.

“People generally tell us we need a steeper roof.”

“You do,” said the power. “But not a great deal steeper.”

They took everything out of the shelter. Meret insisted on lifting the cooking stone himself, in daylight. Essa removed a spider from one of the bindings on a twig and put it beneath a ledge, where it remained motionless, perhaps inconvenienced beyond immediate action.

The work took less than an hour.

The roof was raised at the back by the width of Essa’s hand. The split poles became sound, though they kept their crookedness and the marks of the small blade. One slab acquired a shallow groove on its upper surface. Water poured onto it from the visitor’s cupped hand, ran outward, and fell into the existing runnel.

Essa went inside and inspected the joints.

“You’ve made it higher.”

“A little.”

She stood in the place where she usually dressed, and discovered she could straighten her neck.

Outside, Meret was touching one of the poles.

“Could you do that to a knee?”

“Yes.”

Essa stayed inside.

The power sat down beside him.

“Would you like me to?”

“Would it make me young?”

“Only if you wanted that too.”

He considered this with a seriousness the power respected.

“No,” he said eventually. “I’ve arranged quite a few things on the understanding that I’m not.”

He pulled his trouser leg above the joint.

It was thickened on one side. The power could see the old injury and the smaller injuries made in accommodating it: the strained hip, the sore foot, the muscles that never quite rested.

It explained what it could repair.

Meret asked whether it would hurt. He asked how soon he could put weight on it. Then he looked toward the doorway.

“Essa?”

“It’s your knee.”

“I know whose knee it is.”

She came out and sat beside him.

The power laid a hand against the joint. Meret felt warmth, then a peculiar loosening, as though something he had been bracing against had quietly moved away.

For a while afterward he did not stand.

When he did, he kept his weight on the other leg.

“Go on,” Essa said.

He bent the knee.

Then he bent it farther.

He walked to the runnel and back, looking annoyed.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

He walked the distance again.

***

The copper pot was discussed after breakfast.

By then Essa had decided that neglecting to mention it would be foolish.

She described the trader’s pot in detail, including two things she disliked about it. The power asked what she cooked. Meret raised the question of taste.

“A lining,” the power said. “And it will need renewing eventually.”

“By you?” Essa asked.

“A tinsmith can do it.”

She liked that answer.

The pot it made had a broad enough base for the cooking stone, a lid that fitted closely, and a handle that could be moved without trapping fingers against hot metal. It was not so large that they would resent carrying it down.

Essa lifted it empty, then half full. She poured from it.

“Good,” she said.

After a moment she added, “Very good.”

The power showed them how to care for the lining. Meret listened particularly closely to the part about not scraping it with a knife.

Neither mentioned the bowl.

When they put their things back inside, Essa placed the new pot beside it.

***

There were other things the power might have done.

It saw the loosened rock above the western cleft and settled it securely into the mountain. First it waited for the birds nesting behind it to leave on their morning flight; afterward, their entrance remained the same.

With permission, it strengthened the worn place inside the slab’s hole without roughening the surface. It did not make the rope imperishable. Essa had asked whether the rest of it would then wear differently, and after some discussion they preferred a new rope, made so that Meret could splice it with the skills he already had.

It offered to improve the cliff path.

They went down together to look.

What Essa wanted was not a stair, nor a railing all the way down, but room to turn one foot at the blind corner while carrying a basket. Meret pointed out a handhold that filled with ice late in the season.

The power made those changes. The chips of stone it removed were set in the rubble below, not dropped through the nesting air.

By afternoon the cloud had risen.

The hollow hummed. Under the repaired roof, three figures sat with their knees near the cooking stone. Water heated in the copper pot.

“Can you make people pay what things are worth?” Meret asked.

Essa laughed before the power could answer.

They talked about that for some time.

***

The power stayed three days.

It learned that Essa’s hands hurt most after washing, and offered to heal the splits. She accepted. It learned that neither of them wanted the brown hunter driven away, though Meret wished it would hunt somewhere less conspicuous.

It left the green seam alone.

On the last morning, it asked whether they wanted it to return.

“In summer,” Essa said. “We won’t be here once that freezes.”

She pointed behind the shelter.

“I can find you below.”

“Yes, but there are more people below. You’ll be asked to look at everything.”

“I don’t mind being asked.”

“Wait until you’ve met my son-in-law.”

Meret was already sorting the baskets. He had begun, cautiously, to trust his leg, but still chose where to set his feet.

They had work beyond the western cleft. A seed head beside the shelter had opened during the night.

The power moved out of their way.

Essa came back once. She checked that the pot was out of the sun, then took its lid, considered it, and secured it beneath a stone.

“It fits,” the power said.

“I know it fits.”

She tightened the basket strap across her shoulder.

“If you’re here when the cloud comes, bring the trays in.”

Then she followed her brother through the cleft.

The power remained.

Toward noon, the opposite summits lost their bases. The hanging ferns stirred, and the first damp wind moved inward across the floor.

The power brought in the trays, using both hands.
