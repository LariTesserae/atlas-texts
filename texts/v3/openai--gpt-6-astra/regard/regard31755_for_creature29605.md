The power did not mistake the town’s care for an absence of need.

It saw the hand resting between a child’s shoulder blades. It saw, also, how often that hand was needed; how little a person could think of anything else while carrying a pot across the hall. It admired the gutters without admiring thirst. The beautiful spoons did not persuade it that there ought to be things biting inside sleeves.

For a while it attended to the mountain.

Its attention passed through the fresh pink stone, down beyond the older crossings and the beam wrapped in roots. There were things living there that had never approached an opening. There were weights supported by places no wider than a fingernail, and slow movements whose consequences had not yet reached the houses.

It could have stopped the cleft from widening.

But the green sheen followed the opening stone. The animals followed the green, and other animals followed them. The roots needed new passages. Far below, something blind and very old was turning toward a change in pressure.

Stopping the mountain would not be a simple kindness.

The power attended longer.

Then, one morning, the young measurer found someone sitting beside the granary, leaving the hooks unobstructed.

The stranger had brought no bundle. A small pale creature was investigating the edge of one sleeve.

“Are you waiting for someone?” she asked.

“For you, if you have time.”

“I have to do the cracks.”

“May I come?”

She handed it a brush.

At the first crack, the stranger watched her clear the mouth of the seam and select a slip. At the second, she explained why she did not push harder. At the third, she said nothing, and the stranger did not require her to.

Afterward, beside the notched beam, it asked what happened when a measurement was wrong.

“That depends which way.”

She put the slips away.

“Sometimes you take down a stair you needed.”

“And sometimes?”

“You don’t.”

The stranger looked at the beam for some time.

“I can help with that,” it said.

She glanced toward the door. She had heard offers before from people who had not carried a dismantled staircase anywhere.

“Come back when everyone is here.”

***

They met in the hall. Not all at once: someone had to finish a feeding, someone else was asleep after sitting through the night with a feverish child. The old man arrived without his neck cloth and was asked by his daughter where he had left it.

The power waited through these matters.

Then it explained what it could do.

It could make sudden movements of the inhabited rock slow enough to escape. A slab might still settle, a wall might still have to come down, but neither would fall on a sleeper or a person carrying grain. Where the town’s weight rested badly, it could bear that weight while repairs were made.

“The roots go behind the walls,” someone said.

“Yes.”

“And below the paving.”

“Yes.”

It showed them, in a shallow bowl of water, what it meant. They watched the houses become clear, their fitted slabs and bindings visible through one another. The tiny movements of the mountain continued. Here and there something took a load, gently, until a loose support could be replaced.

The predecessor leaned so close that his breath disturbed the picture.

“Would we still measure?”

“If you want to know where you can build. And where repairs are needed.”

“Who would maintain this?” the granary keeper asked.

“I would.”

“For how long?”

“For as long as it is useful to you.”

She considered that answer with the expression she used for grain offered in an unfamiliar sack.

“And if you go away?”

“It will not depend on my remembering to stay.”

There were more questions. The power answered them. Where an answer was difficult to understand, it found another way to show them.

They asked for the protection to extend along the path above the gate, where the dead were carried and the living sometimes slipped.

It agreed.

They asked what it wanted in return.

“Nothing.”

This did not end the discussion. People wished to know what *nothing* included.

By evening, they had agreed to try it at the broken stair.

***

The first alteration was almost disappointing.

The slab remained a slab. The dark slit beneath it still breathed. Droplets gathered on the rope rail.

But when the young measurer placed a hand beneath the crossing, she felt something holding it that was neither stone nor wood. It yielded slightly against her palm, as a broad strap might yield under tension.

She brought her predecessor. They tested it with weights, then with people, then with a degree of ingenuity the power found pleasing.

The granary keeper would not permit children to join the testing.

After six days, they asked for the rest.

***

Water took longer.

The power’s first proposal put a clean spring beside the hall. The inhabitants rejected the position immediately.

“That is where they turn,” a child said, pointing to an apparently unoccupied piece of wall.

The spring was moved in the plans.

The second position would have required a woman carrying two jars to pass beneath a leaf where no sensible person carried anything that needed both hands.

At the third, the granary keeper asked whether it could simply come out inside a cupboard.

It could.

Each house received a small cold outlet, closed with a handle large enough for uncertain hands. The water came from no reservoir the roots depended on. It did not diminish the beads on the cliff or alter the pulses from below.

At first people filled only one jar.

Then they filled two.

Someone washed a blanket that had been waiting until rain. Someone spilled a cup and stood looking at the paving before remembering that this was no longer a household misfortune.

The gutters remained. There was no reason to throw away good water, and the narrow animals still needed their routes. But a damaged overlap could now wait until after breakfast.

This was the change the power had hoped for, though it had not known the exact shape it would take: a woman sitting down to eat while a repair remained undone.

***

There were other requests.

Some were easy. The old woman’s knees could be helped without warming the wall where the moss animals slept.

Some required discussion. One household wanted every animal removed from its rooms; another feared where those animals would go. The power helped them make a boundary at the first household’s thresholds and places elsewhere for the displaced creatures. It did not ask the people inside to become more affectionate.

The flying creature the old woman remembered did not return.

The power did not send a likeness to her window.

It sat with her once while she described the peculiar hesitation before the flower. She laughed at herself for explaining a movement with so much movement of her own hands.

“No, not like that,” she said when it tried to understand.

She showed it again.

***

The young measurer continued her work.

For several weeks she checked the cracks more often than before, suspicious of how much less fear attended them. Once she asked the power whether the staircase she had ordered dismantled would really have held.

“It would have held that season,” it said.

She rubbed her thumb along the blunt edge of a slip.

“So I was wrong.”

“Yes.”

It did not add that the inconvenience had been secretly necessary. It did not invent a future accident to make her judgment flawless.

Later, when a different wall began to lean, she called for help. They propped it, emptied the room, and took it apart over three unhurried days. The family slept in their own house throughout.

Her predecessor learned to recognize the new supports by touch. With these beneath a failing section, he could work there safely despite his uncertain hands. He proved very good at freeing pegs that younger people were inclined to cut.

***

The power remained available, though it ceased to occupy a place beside the granary every morning.

People disagreed about it. One thought it should have put a roof over the hall. Another thought a roof would be intolerable. The granary keeper said that anyone wishing to roof the hall should first explain where she was meant to dry things.

The town continued to widen.

There were new gardens. A window had to be moved. Children discovered a game involving the faint yielding beneath the old crossing, and were forbidden to play it until someone had checked whether their invention was dangerous.

During a strong pulse, the granary keeper still stood beside the youngest children. Leaves turned; wings opened under the eaves. A child found her free hand.

This time she was carrying a cup of water in it.

She drank the last mouthful, put the cup on a step, and took his hand.
