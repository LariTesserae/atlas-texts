The power found Vey after she had put the jar away.

It had been considering how to announce itself. Light would have been easy, but people here used light to see the edges of things, and a sudden brightness would hide more than it revealed. A voice from above would send someone up a ladder to investigate. A trembling in the floor would be cruel.

So it spoke from the empty place beside her.

“May I sit?”

Vey drew her feet back from the wall.

“Not there. That tile rocks.”

The power sat somewhere else.

For a while it shared the warmth. Behind the buttress, the little animal turned around with some difficulty. A flake of old mortar fell inside the wall.

“What do you want?” Vey asked.

“To help.”

She looked at the place where it was sitting. There was no person there, but the lamplight had begun to disclose the faint impression of weight on the cloth.

“With what?”

“That is what I have come to learn.”

Vey rubbed the side of her smallest finger.

“Can you carry?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

The power considered the roofs, the roofs above them, the chambers below, and the long, precarious arrangement by which everyone’s shelter was also someone else’s burden.

“Enough,” it said.

***

It did not wait for the morning to keep the cracked lintel in the eastern sleeping hall from breaking.

There were thirty-one people beneath it. None had known it was cracked; the fault lay inside a fired beam, under a repair older than the district. The power held it through the night, then left a narrow white line on its underside where the listeners could find it.

Elsewhere it kept a child’s foot from driving through a rotten chute cover. The cover bent, making the danger plain. The foot did not follow it.

These were small things for the power. It took care not to let their smallness make it inattentive.

In Vey’s room, it asked about the basin.

She described the inlet, the working edge, the shortage of hands. She explained that the new floor needed weight in one place and could not bear it in another. When she reached the matter of the buried supports, she stopped.

“You could look,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Without opening it?”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you asking me?”

“Because I can see what is underneath. I cannot see which passage you need to keep.”

Vey fetched her damp tablet.

She drew the passage with the blunt end of a peg. Then she drew the room that must not be cut off, and the old drain that sometimes carried water backward, and a little square for a kitchen whose owner would be furious if anyone moved it again.

The power listened.

***

By morning, three floor-listeners had been called.

The cheerful woman arrived annoyed because nobody could tell her whether this was an emergency. The man who preferred darkness had brought his own lamp so that he could be certain of removing it. The listener who heard through his wrist put a palm on the buttress and waited.

Vey’s brother stayed outside.

The power explained what it proposed.

Beneath the inhabited chambers, among the places already crushed beyond use, it would make supports that did not need to be quarried out of other supports. The weight above would pass into them. No room would be filled merely because it was convenient. No inhabited passage would be closed.

The woman asked where the weight would go after that.

“Into my keeping.”

“For how long?”

“For as long as it is needed.”

She made the expression of someone who had heard contractors speak before.

“And if you leave?”

“The work will remain sound without my attention.”

They made it explain again.

Then they chose an abandoned storage chamber for the first attempt. Its roof had sunk until the door was a sideways slit. The listeners knew its noises. One of them had been marking its decline for years.

The power did not lift the whole district. That would have broken pipes, spilled lamps, and frightened everyone.

Instead, it took the weight a little at a time.

Dust slid from a seam. A buried arch eased against its neighbor. The cheerful listener raised a hand, and the power stopped.

They waited.

Far below, something gave a single heavy knock.

Vey’s brother, waiting at the end of the passage, went white around the mouth.

“Still here,” Vey called to him.

The power waited until he had answered before continuing.

By the end of the working cycle, the storage chamber was no taller. Its door was still a slit. But its roof was no longer coming down.

The listeners returned the next day, and the next.

On the fourth day, the cheerful woman lay on the tiles for a long time, then began to laugh.

“What?” someone asked.

“Help me up first.”

***

There was trouble, because help did not make everyone agree.

People wanted the nearest chamber strengthened first. They wanted lost storerooms reopened. One district claimed that the new supports were drawing water away from its collection lips. Another insisted the power had made their kiln burn badly, though the kiln had burned badly for eleven years.

Some people were frightened of having anything beneath their homes altered. The power left those homes alone unless a failure threatened their neighbors, and then it found ways to carry the load from outside.

The work proceeded.

For the first time anyone could remember, a crew finished repairing a street and did not have to begin saving the street beneath it.

They stood beside their stacked baskets.

Someone asked where they were to go next.

“Nowhere yet,” said the forewoman.

They did not quite believe her.

Two remained to tidy the tools. One went home and returned because he had forgotten why he was there. Several sat on the curb, looking embarrassed.

The power had thought rest would be easier to give.

It learned.

***

Vey asked for water next.

“Not a flood,” she added quickly.

They were at the new basin. She held the half-cup in her hand.

“Here?” the power asked.

“Not until we finish.”

It waited while she corrected the slope.

At the basin’s head it made a small covered mouth of plain fired clay. Water came through when a lever was lifted and stopped when it was lowered. The first flow was so clear that the workers suspected it of containing something they could not see.

They tested it in their own ways. The power answered their questions. It did not ask them to drink as a demonstration of trust.

There were other mouths afterward, distributed so that fetching water would not become a new kind of queue or a new person’s authority. The drains were widened before the supply increased. Old chambers were protected from the damp. Warm water went to washing places where it could be carried away without soaking the foundations.

Vey continued recording the loss from her test pours for nine days.

On the tenth, she made the nick, stared at it, and smoothed it out.

She did not throw the tablet away. It was useful clay.

***

The power could have filled every room with food.

Instead, after feeding the people who were hungry that day, it went to the growers and cooks.

There were things they had wanted to try for years: deeper trays, clean fiber, steady warmth, vats that could be emptied and scrubbed without losing a district’s next meal. The power provided these. It also brought foods nobody knew, in small amounts, so that an unfamiliar taste did not have to become an obligation.

The old man tried a crisp green leaf.

He chewed with profound suspicion.

“Water with string in it,” he said.

Later he asked whether there were any more.

A cooking room was built with enough heat to brown his food properly. Its vents did not steal warmth from the sleeping rooms.

People began arguing about recipes with an energy previously reserved for supports.

***

Vey did not ask about her shoulder.

The power asked her, privately, whether it hurt.

“It works.”

“That was not what I asked.”

She looked away.

There were several conversations before she let it help. She wanted to know whether her arm would still feel like hers, whether she would lose the strength built around the crookedness, whether she would have to stop working.

The healing was gradual because she preferred it that way.

One morning she reached for a jar on a high shelf and found herself waiting for a pain that did not arrive. She kept her arm raised a moment longer than necessary.

Her brother accepted help for the old damage in his ankle. He declined the power’s offer to accompany him into the rebuilt washroom.

“All right,” it said.

He looked relieved, then suspicious of the relief.

The power did not mention the room again.

***

It was less easy to persuade people that they could have more space.

Space had always been made at someone’s expense: a widened passage narrowed a room; a larger room required heavier roofing; an extra storey meant more anxious listening below.

Now there were dry, sound chambers that could be opened without this exchange.

Some families moved. Some stayed and took down partitions. Some preferred low sleeping recesses and asked only for a place where they could stand upright to dress.

Vey’s household kept the buttress.

By then its pegs were useful, and her brother liked the heel rest more than he was willing to admit. The power secured what lay behind it and repaired the mortar around the animal’s passage.

“Don’t make it too smooth,” the girl said. “It has to climb.”

So the entrance retained its little ridges.

When the drains were cleaned, warm pockets were left for the film and its grazers. Water still reached them, though workshop oil no longer had to serve as their only nourishment. The armored creature continued to emerge in search of food. It still stopped at hammer blows.

There were fewer of those now.

Sometimes it waited for a second blow that never came, then went on across the floor.

***

Much later, people asked about the upper roofs.

The power showed them what lay above, and beyond that, and farther still. It made safe routes for those who wanted to travel. There were railings, resting places, ways back. Nobody had to climb a ladder on the strength of a promise.

Vey did not go immediately.

She had begun teaching three apprentices how to read an unfinished floor. Water could still pool in the wrong places; abundance had not abolished slopes.

One apprentice was quick and careless. One was slow and nearly always right. The third could detect an error but could not yet explain it without moving both hands.

Vey found them exhausting.

She also found that she liked going home before she had used up everything in herself.

There was blue cloth in the market now, and yellow, and a red so bright that people disagreed about whether it was attractive. Her daughter chose the red.

The old blue lining remained inside her coat until she outgrew it. Then she asked Vey to help unpick it.

They made a little pouch. The stitches were uneven on one side.

***

One evening the power visited while Vey was examining a jar.

“The finger mark,” it said. “Not a crack.”

“I know.”

She turned it once more and set it down.

Beyond the doorway, someone was singing a song her brother did not know. He had gone out to learn it and would probably return with half the words wrong. The old man was asleep beside the warm wall, having failed to maintain that he was only passing through.

Vey’s daughter sat on the floor with a board across her knees.

She still made small clay people. Some knelt. Some stood, with broad feet to keep them upright. One sat with its back against a lump that represented a buttress.

On the board beside them lay an unfinished figure.

“What will that one be doing?” the power asked.

The girl pressed a thumb into the clay, making a place for its head.

“Nothing yet.”

She set it among the others, lying on its back.

Then she put down her tools and went to see whether her uncle had learned the song.
