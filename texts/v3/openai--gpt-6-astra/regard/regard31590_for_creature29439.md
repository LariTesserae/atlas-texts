The power noticed first that Iven was taking the long way home.

It would have been easy to misunderstand this. The longer path passed the split boulder with its hidden water, then climbed through a stand of old trees whose crowns still belonged to the orchard. In the afternoon there was shade there. A visitor might have supposed she had chosen beauty over convenience.

But the shorter path had acquired a downward tilt. Iven had not yet reported it because she wanted another measurement, and because reporting it would mean closing the route until someone could inspect the shelf below. There were already two routes closed. Everyone was tired.

Her knee hurt on descents.

The power attended to this without making it a symbol of anything.

It attended also to the shelf, the roots beneath it, the seam-runner drawing its troublesome strip of felt toward a space behind a pipe support. It followed the copper down through the generating hall and the anchored columns into the rock. Far below the lowest shaft, pressures were arriving from places that had never been open to air.

For a while, it did nothing that could be distinguished from attention.

This was not because the valley was perfect.

***

That evening Iven found someone sitting on the bench outside her room.

The stranger was dressed for the valley, though the clothes looked unused. Both feet rested flat on the floor. Beside one boot a snail was crossing the junction between two boards.

“Have you been given a room?” Iven asked.

“No.”

“Then you want the other building.”

“I wanted to speak to you.”

Iven shifted the bag against her hip.

“About the rising?”

“About your knee, to begin with.”

She looked at the stranger for some time.

From inside her room came the faint brushing of the animal in the wall.

“Who sent you?”

“No one.”

The stranger did not stand. Iven was grateful for this before she knew whether gratitude was appropriate.

“What do you want?”

“To help. I haven’t decided all of what that should mean.”

“An unusual qualification.”

“Yes.”

Iven sat at the other end of the bench.

The power told her enough of what it was for her to become frightened. When she asked it to stop speaking for a moment, it did.

Across the passage, someone was trying to open a cupboard while holding a bowl. Iven heard the familiar soft double contact as the door was caught, released, and caught again.

“Are we in trouble?” she asked eventually.

“No.”

“Is somebody?”

“Several people. Not in a new way.”

“And you’ve come about my knee.”

“I can attend to more than one thing.”

***

She did not agree to anything that evening.

The next morning she brought three other residents, including the engineer who disliked Sena’s description of the northern hall. They met in the common dining room. Someone put a cup before the power, which asked whether drinking from it would make the conversation easier.

“Only if you want tea,” Iven said.

They spent most of the morning establishing that an offer of help did not mean the works had to close, or remain open, or be given to anyone.

The engineer wanted to know whether the ground could be made safe.

“Yes,” said the power. “But I would like you to tell me what you mean by safe.”

This annoyed him until they began making the list.

They wanted the ground to continue moving. They wanted the roots not to be crushed. They wanted no unnoticed strain to accumulate under a building because it could no longer escape somewhere else. They wanted water to keep gathering in the fractures. They did not want the immense, slow labor below them stopped merely to hold their shelves level.

They wanted, said Iven, to be able to drop a spoon.

The power considered all of these requests together.

Then it asked to see the accident records.

There were six books. One had been damaged by condensation. The oldest contained the names of two people whom no one at the table mentioned aloud.

The power read those pages no faster than the others.

***

Its first substantial work was beneath the paths and buildings.

It did not bind the rock into one unyielding piece. Instead, it gave sharp disturbances somewhere harmless to go.

A blow entering the stone was received, spread, and returned as warmth too slight for a root to notice. The slow pressures continued. Water moved as before. Fractures still opened, but a dropped tool no longer sent an uncertain message through half a mile of unsettled ground.

The power made this change visible to the instruments.

“That matters,” the engineer said.

“I thought it might.”

They tested it on an unoccupied shelf, with the surrounding paths closed and observers stationed at distances the power knew were unnecessary. It did not object.

First they dropped a nut onto a slab.

Then a wrench.

The wrench made a noise so ugly that one of the observers sat down.

For days afterward, they measured.

The power stayed.

It answered questions, including the same question asked by different people and the same question asked by the same person in the morning and again after dark. When an instrument showed an unexpected result, it helped them discover that the instrument’s mounting had been slipping for eleven years.

They did not put the clappers back into the bells. The lamps worked perfectly well.

***

Iven accepted help with her knee on the fourth evening.

She asked whether it would make her nineteen again.

“No.”

“Good. I didn’t care for nineteen.”

The power repaired the places that hurt and left her otherwise recognizably herself. It asked about her shoulder. She said later, perhaps.

That night she lay awake waiting for the ache to arrive when she turned over.

The seam-runner came through the pipe opening and sat beneath the radiator. After a while Iven switched on the lamp. The animal withdrew, mildly inconvenienced, into the wall.

She read until she was sleepy.

***

There were requests the power did not carry out immediately.

Someone wanted a lake in the basin.

Someone wanted the climbing plants gone from every service pipe.

Someone asked for all the dead trees to stand alive again.

The power walked with them to the places concerned. Beneath the level canopy were dwellings smaller than a thumbnail, dry chambers, root junctions, sleeping spaces. The dead trees held up living ones. The climbers intercepted water that would otherwise be lost into inaccessible cracks.

A lake remained possible. It ceased to be a casual suggestion.

Other things required less deliberation.

The northern rooms became warm without drawing moisture from the walls. The mineral dust no longer entered Iven’s herb pots, though she still had to remember to water them. The inhabitants of the pipe supports kept their nightly warmth.

The power could not ask the seam-runners what they preferred in words. It watched where they slept, which passages they abandoned, what they carried to their young. When it widened a cramped opening and the resident animal blocked most of the new space with fibers, it narrowed the opening again.

The box of worn felt stayed beneath the stair.

***

Sena asked for a ball court.

By then the adults had become accustomed to discussing foundations, consequences, and the rights of things too small to appear on a survey. Sena arrived with a drawing. Her court stood beyond the southern ridge, on a place already cleared for storage. It had high sides to stop the ball going down the mountain.

“The walk is too long,” said her mother.

“I know,” said Sena, and unfolded a second sheet.

The power helped build the path and the court. Several residents helped revise the drawing. Sena objected to some of the revisions and accepted others.

There was an argument about whether a roof would spoil it.

The power did not settle the argument. It put up a temporary roof for the wet season, which gave them something specific to disagree about.

The first time the ball struck the boards, everyone stopped.

“Again,” said Sena.

***

What took longest was not the engineering.

At meals, hands still darted beneath falling objects. People still woke after dreaming of a noise they could not take back. The engineer continued to inspect the northern hall whenever the weather changed.

The power neither corrected them nor asked why they were not happier.

It helped train more attendants. It made the southern crossing dependable in winter. The towns receiving the electricity learned, through quite ordinary letters and meetings, how much of their reliable light depended on twenty-six people arranging their lives around it.

There was money for relief workers. There were rooms fit for them to live in.

Iven took twelve days away.

She visited the coast, disliked the wind, ate a fruit she had never seen before, and spent an afternoon in the hospital whose cards stood behind the ribbon. No one there recognized her until she explained.

When she came home, her measuring round had been done correctly by someone else.

She examined the figures twice.

Then she put away the book.

***

The power had no wish to make itself necessary at every hinge.

What it had changed would remain without offerings, gratitude, or supervision. What it had not yet understood, it continued to study. It could still be found on the bench sometimes, though people no longer assumed that finding it meant something was wrong.

One afternoon Iven encountered it beside the central surveying line.

The distance had increased again.

She recorded the measurement, checked her arithmetic, and lifted a white flower from the hinge of the box. There was still plenty of room beneath the newest figure.

“Which way are you going?” the power asked.

“The long way.”

It glanced toward the shorter path, repaired now, its descending shelf secure.

“I know,” she said.

She secured her bag. Somewhere beyond the ridge, too far away to hear, children were hitting a ball against a wall with all their strength.

“The flowers have opened beside the boulder,” she said. “You can come if you like.”
