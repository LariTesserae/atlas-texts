The power would come without light. There was already enough light for those who needed it, and for others the dark was part of the shelter.

It would take time to learn the place.

Not because anything was hidden from it, but because seeing the whole recess at once was not the same as knowing what happened at a particular crossing, when a particular ant arrived carrying more than it could easily turn around with. It would attend to the hollow under the jar rim, and to the chambers behind the mortar, and to the difference between a wall that held a gecko and a wall that merely looked rough.

The gecko would remain where it was. No great presence would enter its awareness. Its throat would move. The power would not ask it to look up.

There were things to do.

Beneath a leaf skirt, a beetle had become trapped between two flakes of pottery. It had entered while the upper flake rested on a root; the root had shifted under the passage of ants. Now the beetle could move its legs but not its body. Its efforts had polished a little crescent in the dust.

The power would lift the flake scarcely at all.

The beetle would push forward with the same effort it had been making for hours, and this time the effort would work. It would stop beneath the next leaf. For a while, nothing more would happen.

Farther back, one of the wall joints was beginning to fail. The failure would not arrive tonight. Water had entered it in winter, frozen, and left a space that subsequent dust had concealed. In another season, perhaps two, a stone would turn outward. There were roots behind it, and an ant chamber, and above it part of the pigeons’ floor.

The power would settle the weight into the sound masonry on either side. It would leave the joint open. A root was using it.

It would not make the whole building permanent. People still needed to alter their houses. The pigeon chambers would need cleaning; the roof would need repair. Brown stone could not simply be exempted from weather without changing the lives that depended on its weathering.

But when someone next climbed up to mend the beam, the dangerous place would be visible before they put their weight on it. They would have enough sound timber, enough mortar, enough time to do the work without haste. Kneeling beside the fallen lintel, they would notice the little plants.

The recess could remain. It was not in the way.

For the gecko, the power would attend first to the tail.

The lost part was no longer hurting it. The new growth was serviceable, and there was no need to seize the animal in an invisible hand and remake it into an earlier version of itself. But the power would find the small remaining tenderness where the replacement joined the body, and ease it.

The gecko would shift its hind foot. Its belly would settle more fully against the wall.

Later it would hunt.

The power’s attention would not leave the moth when the gecko noticed it. Nor would it leave the gecko because it needed to eat. There would be no declaration that everything happening here was good merely because it belonged to the life of the recess.

There would be care for the frightened animal, too: no added terror, no prolonged injury where a swift ending was possible. And after the meal, the gecko’s hunger would quiet.

Near dawn, a little moisture would gather deep in a crack where the roots could reach it. Not enough to flood the ant chambers or soften the wasps’ sealed doors. On the underside of a projecting stone, one small drop would hold.

The gecko, returning from the entrance, would encounter it. Its tongue would touch the stone.

Outside, someone would lift a latch. The pigeons would begin their heavy walking. An ant would discover the broken seed and depart with news of it.

The power would stay through the ordinary morning, learning what else could be helped.
