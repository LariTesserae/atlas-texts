The power first noticed the knot.

Not because it was beautifully made, though it was. It noticed how little of the wall’s weight the knot could bear, and how much depended on its bearing that little until the root grew strong.

Then it noticed the hands that had tied it.

For some time it attended to the town without making itself known. It followed the guide ropes. It listened in the close air beneath the roofs. It learned the difference between a room that had been abandoned and a room whose owner was waiting for someone else to repair the entrance.

It did not mistake the white stone for an invitation.

Nor did it mistake the drowned town for a town that wished to be dry.

***

Eda met it on the mountain path.

She had gone up because her brother had brought back the wrong bundle of willow twice, and because on certain days being grateful for an errand was more tiring than doing it herself.

She was resting where the path turned above the lake. Her bad leg lay straight before her, though not quite straight. Below, the roofs crowded together in the afternoon light. Someone was beating a mat. The sound reached her after each small movement of the arms.

There was a person sitting on the uphill side of the path.

Eda was reasonably sure no one had been there when she sat down.

“Are you going past?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then you could have chosen somewhere wider.”

The person moved.

After a while it said, “Your knee hurts.”

“It has been doing that longer than you’ve been sitting there.”

“I could help it.”

Eda looked at the person’s hands. They were empty.

“With what?”

The answer took some time. Not because the power was evasive, but because it had to distinguish what it was from several things Eda already knew: a healer, a spirit belonging to a particular mountain, a dead relative, a trader with an unusual opening to his negotiations.

When it had finished, she looked down at the roofs again.

“Why my knee?”

“Because you are here, and it hurts. Not because it is the only thing.”

“What would I owe?”

“Nothing.”

“My mother would want to hear you say that.”

“I can say it to her.”

Eda considered this.

“Don’t make me sleep.”

“I won’t.”

“And tell me before you begin.”

***

The healing was not warm. That surprised her afterward.

There was a sensation like the small release when a cord that has been pulling across a finger is lifted away. Then nothing.

She bent her knee. She straightened it.

For a moment her face became angry.

The power waited.

She put her foot beneath her and stood. She sat down again, deliberately, without reaching for the rock beside her.

“I had a way of doing that,” she said.

“Yes.”

She remained seated for a long time. Then she got up to fetch the willow.

On the descent she still planted her foot sideways at the difficult turn.

***

Eda’s mother asked more questions than Eda had.

Several concerned matters the power had not expected anyone to put first. Could a healed joint become injured again? Would a person’s children inherit anything unusual? If someone declined, would they be asked repeatedly? Did the offer apply to the settlements farther down the lake?

The power answered.

Eda’s mother felt along the rope in her lap, found a bad strand, and cut it out.

“All right,” she said. “You can sit there. Not on that bundle.”

Word spread badly, as word does.

By evening, someone believed Eda had acquired a new leg. Someone else believed the town would have to surrender its iron. A boy arrived carrying a dead crayfish and was distressed to learn that the power’s presence did not mean every loss could simply be taken back.

It sat with him for a while.

There were people who came immediately, and people who watched those people for several days. One woman asked for relief from pain but did not want the old shape of her hand changed. A man who had not heard clearly in years wanted to try hearing first, somewhere away from the waterfall.

The power went with him up the mountain.

It did not require witnesses.

***

The town had suggestions.

Some were urgent. Some contradicted other suggestions. Several concerned the willow between the western ladders.

“Ask it whose tree it is,” someone said.

“It isn’t going to know whose tree it is.”

“It knew about Eda’s knee.”

“My knee was attached to me,” Eda said.

The power did not settle the ownership of the willow. It did inspect the roots, with Senn and the girl with narrow wrists. When she withdrew her hand from a dark joint, it waited for her account before speaking.

There was rot behind the sound outer wood.

They discussed three ways of supporting the wall. The power could have held it upright without any support at all, but the household wanted something they could examine.

So it helped them grow an arch.

Not in a night. They wanted time to move the soaking wood, clear a passage for the fish, and decide where the arch should meet the floor. When it was finished, the girl put her arm into the joint again.

“Hard behind,” she reported. “Nothing moved.”

Senn asked whether he could have a piece of the rotten root.

His wife, overhead, said he could not.

***

It was Eda who eventually asked about drowning.

They were beneath her house. She had brought the power down to look at the fish, which was indeed larger than she remembered. Her brother had not yet been told this.

Eda touched the toggled rope but did not ascend.

The power understood her without speech.

*Could you?*

*Yes.*

They went up into the little air chamber.

“Not just ours,” she said.

“No.”

“And not only children.”

“No.”

She wiped water from her mouth.

“Then why are we talking about walls?”

The power accepted the rebuke. It had been learning how to enter their lives without taking possession of them. Here was something it need not have waited to be asked.

That evening, it told the town what it would do.

There were questions again. What would happen to someone working beneath a beam? What about a person caught in a net? Would the water know the difference between a diver resting and a diver who had ceased to struggle?

The power answered carefully. It would not merely lift bodies. It would attend.

No one had to make a promise in return.

***

The first person brought up was a visitor.

He had stepped between two rafts at dusk with a sack over his shoulder. He struck his head on a mooring pole. Before anyone reached the opening, the water held him at the surface, face clear, as steadily as a pair of hands.

Afterward he insisted he had caught hold of something.

There had been nothing to catch.

For weeks the adults watched the children more closely, not less. One boy attempted to test the promise and was brought home furious, having been deposited on the wrong roof.

The power spoke with him. So did his grandmother, for considerably longer.

Work changed.

People still became cold. Hands still lost their accuracy. Air chambers remained useful places to confer, and no one enjoyed having an unfinished repair interrupted by being carried upward. But a dropped knife became only a dropped knife.

The first child who returned without one was praised convincingly.

There were arguments about whether some of the harnesses could come off. There were new swimming lessons for adults who had previously pretended not to want them.

And one afternoon Eda found her mother sitting with her feet through the floor opening.

“Eleven years,” her mother said.

Eda fetched a second belt.

They went down together, slowly.

At the old tree, her mother laid a hand on the white stone. Then she examined the niche around it, running one finger along its shallow edge.

“There were two,” she said.

Eda did not argue.

Above them the roofs shifted against their moorings. Someone lowered a basket too quickly, spilling crumbs through the weave. Fish rose to meet it.

Her mother turned toward the western street, where there were things she wanted to see.

Eda followed without taking her arm.
