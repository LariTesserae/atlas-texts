The power does not begin by opening the clamps.

It watches the resident test the frame. It watches where the tissue thickens under the weight, which plates find purchase, how much of the body must leave the pressure diaphragms to make the attempt.

Then it looks at the terrace the resident has cleared.

The terrace will bear the frame. The bracket beneath it will not. There is a flaw deep in the casting, older than the enamel, where two fronts of cooling metal failed to become one.

During the next inspection, a thin brown stain appears over the flaw. A worker scrapes it, taps the bracket, and calls another worker down to listen.

The replacement takes most of a shift. The resident stays near the vine while they work.

Nothing is lifted for it.

***

There are things the power could do quickly.

It could make the water independent of the plants. It could make every bearing turn forever. It could remove the mast and leave the chamber standing on warm ground, with a door opening into a garden.

But the roots incline toward the new water. The resident listens to bearings. No one has asked it whether it wants ground.

The power attends instead to the storage tanks.

Their stated capacity is correct. Their usable capacity is not. Sediment has gathered behind a baffle where the gauges cannot register it. During an ordinary repair, this would make no difference. During two failures together, it would matter very much.

The next maintenance draw brings a little of the sediment into a sample bottle.

After that there are work orders, delays, a disagreement over the replacement seals. The power does not need to conduct every kindness itself. People arrive with the right seals. Someone makes an access panel large enough for the baffle to be cleaned again.

On the wall beside the tanks, the old figure is painted out and a new one written in.

For the first time since the chamber was filled, the reserve can sustain it through a long repair without anyone having to choose which beds receive water.

***

The coupling with the green pencil mark troubles the power.

Not the small amount taken from it. The dependence on a gasket left incorrectly seated.

At the next shutdown, the worker responsible for that line finds an unused sampling branch behind the insulation. It is inconveniently placed, but it can be extended. She fits a short transparent reservoir with a wide, soft-edged outlet beside the old seam.

For several days the resident ignores it.

The old coupling remains untightened.

Brown liquor enters the reservoir whenever the processing line runs. Nothing closes over the outlet when tissue touches it. Nothing grips. The worker demonstrates the little shutoff twice, then leaves it alone.

Eventually the resident lays one of its three tabs inside.

Later, a fringe.

Only after it has fed there for several weeks does anyone repair the gasket. The green pencil mark is moved to the new pipe.

The worker adds a note to the maintenance book. She crosses out the first version and writes another:

*Supply must remain available during servicing.*

***

The power cannot tell whether the old pump shaft is a keepsake, a tool, or something for which the workers have no word.

It leaves the shaft where it is.

It also leaves the marks on the governor casing, including one almost hidden beneath a reddish deposit. There is no revelation in which the scratches become letters. When the resident makes another, the power watches the angle of the plate and the brief tightening that follows.

Then it turns its attention to the woman who wore white gloves.

She is alive in a town below the cloud line. Her knees have made the mast inaccessible. She still receives occasional news of the chamber, usually when something has gone wrong.

This time she receives a photograph of the ring.

She asks for a better photograph.

Then she asks how large the young one is.

The mountain has other installations with difficult access. A service lift has been proposed before, costed before, postponed before. The power finds no wisdom in making one woman young so that an unsafe climb can remain necessary.

The lift takes months.

When it is finished, the woman comes up with two inspectors, a crate of filters, and lunch she will not eat until she is back in dry air. She brings white gloves, but does not put them on immediately.

At the doorway she waits for the worker who is to fit her service frame.

Inside, the resident touches her tool bag.

Then the rim of the frame.

She offers her wrist.

The part that takes it is broad and clumsy at first. Several cords gather. The grip adjusts.

For a long while she reads no gauge at all.

***

High above them, the vine continues to thicken around its joined place.

The water within the ring has needs the chamber’s instruments were not built to measure. Warmth crosses the boundary readily. Other things pass unevenly. Some remain gathered along an edge that has no equivalent outside it.

The young body is growing close to that edge.

The power could hold the little water steady by its own attention. But an arrangement that fails when attention turns elsewhere is a poor gift to leave with a parent.

Instead, it makes the boundary legible.

Not transparent—it already is—but capable of showing its strain. Under the next change of light, a faint amber line appears along the vine where the two waters press differently. The line fades when the small body moves away from it.

The resident touches that place from outside.

The amber returns.

It withdraws.

For three days it does little else with the narrow extension on the cable. Pressing, waiting, releasing. Below, portions of its body return to tasks and leave them again.

The workers bring their instruments. Most show nothing useful. One records a slight change when held crosswise rather than upright.

They mark the orientation on its handle.

The isolation frame is unpacked, examined, and repacked. It was designed to close an uncertain opening. No one is yet sure it can keep one safely open.

A different order is placed.

***

Meanwhile, the spare frame remains in its rack.

The power gives it neither wings nor obedience. It does something less remarkable: along the route from the rack to the cleared terrace, it ensures there is room to work.

A pipe scheduled for replacement is rerouted. A corroded stair tread comes away to reveal sound mounting points for a small hoist. The hoist arrives with a broad sling, soft enough not to score brass or living tissue.

The resident watches the workers demonstrate it.

One worker lowers a glass block onto the terrace, raises it, lowers it again. She leaves the control hanging near the floor and disconnects nothing.

For a week, the resident touches only the sling.

Then it moves the control a little.

The empty hook rises.

A contraction passes through so much of its body that a worker on the upper stair turns to look behind her.

The hook stops.

It descends.

***

The moving of the frame is not graceful.

There is a root everyone has missed. The sling slips against a corner. Twice the resident returns the weight to the rack and withdraws behind the tanks.

No one takes this as a refusal forever.

On the third attempt, it permits a worker to steady the lower edge.

By then the new frame has arrived for the vine: adjustable, instrumented, capable of supporting the living ring without cutting it. The workers have tested it around a service opening for twenty days. Its fastenings can be released from either side.

The resident examines those particularly.

What it intends is still not entirely clear. The cleared terrace might be a nursery. It might be a place from which to work on the smaller opening. The large frame might be meant to receive the vine, or to make another boundary altogether.

The power does not settle the question by putting an answer into the animal.

It keeps the chamber supplied. It keeps the hoist from hiding a fault. It lets the workers discover that the young water can be fed through a narrow, properly oriented passage without losing its different up.

The rest takes time.

***

Eventually there are two openings on the cleared terrace.

One is small and green and living. The other is brass. Between them, water occupies a shape that makes the stair behind it look gently folded.

The young one has more than a wedge within reach now. There is a porcelain surface, a length of loose cord, a root that can be touched without being damaged. There is also space containing nothing at all.

It spends considerable time there.

The adult reaches into the new water only a little way. Its entering margin turns at an unfamiliar angle. The young one approaches, rests against it, and moves off again.

Below, a leaf catches on a comb.

A worker sees it and lifts it free.

The adult does not leave the terrace.

For once, nothing elsewhere in the chamber requires it to.
