The second foot arrives before the power does anything.

It comes through the roots at an awkward angle, sole turned inward. Several seedlings must be eased aside. The large seed slips, catches beneath a runner, and remains there while both feet search the place where it was.

The power could move it.

Instead, it attends to the search.

One foot is stronger. The other has a patch along its inner edge that feels pressure only after a delay. Between them, the seed is discovered three times before the discoveries become one event.

Then the soles turn toward each other.

For a while nothing moves except a swimmer beneath the lowest transparent leaf.

The seed rises.

It is held no higher than the thickness of a nail, and only briefly. When it falls, both feet follow it downward. Their weight sends a single deep knock through the floor.

The animals answer.

The power remains through the answering.

***

To be present here without doing harm requires some care.

Warmth would be an intrusion. So would perfect stillness, if it prevented a root from growing into an occupied space. Even attention has weight in a place that is learning to join distant things.

The power makes its presence small and local.

It takes no water from the pool. It puts no unfamiliar substance into the mouth. When a root approaches, it moves aside at the speed at which the root can notice.

For its first winter, it inhabits the hollow as a slight resistance beneath one broad runner near the entrance. The runner rests against it. A furred animal finds the junction a little more secure and moves its sleeping young there.

The power supports their weight.

It listens to the drumming until it can distinguish feeding from questions, and questions from the little aimless knocks animals make when they are comfortable.

It listens longer before answering.

***

The first repair is outside.

A thaw comes too early. Water passes through the meadow soil, reaches the pale swelling, and freezes when the weather turns. Above the seam between the roots, a wedge of ice begins pressing downward.

The hollow cannot feel the danger clearly. Its outer roots report little beyond cold. Inside, the ceiling leaves loosen to accommodate a shoulder, unaware of the weight gathering over them.

The power does not warm the ice.

It opens a narrow course through the soil beside it, following an old worm passage. Water drains downhill. The wedge stops growing.

In spring, the passage will carry meltwater to a stand of sedges.

Below, the shoulder continues its descent. No one wakes.

***

There are things the power does not mend.

It leaves the little frozen hand beneath its mat of leaves. Seedling roots have entered the spaces between the bones. Nothing living reaches from it toward the body now.

It leaves the unfamiliar crescent of plants beside the pool.

It leaves the seed too large for either foot.

But when a new vessel begins to grow along the route that carried the first hand beyond the boundary, the power attends closely.

The root enclosing the vessel is following a gradient of sugar. Another root crosses nearby, carrying water inward. They almost touch.

The power rests against the first, lightly enough that its growing tip can turn away.

It takes nineteen days.

The roots meet. Their tissues join. The vessel follows the inward curve, and a winter later the new hand receives blood through a route the hollow can sustain.

The power withdraws its pressure. The joined roots hold.

***

Eventually, the foot near the entrance discovers the slight resistance beneath the runner.

It presses down.

The power yields a little, then presses back.

The foot withdraws so quickly that syrup wells up between the roots.

For eleven days it does not return.

On the twelfth, one toe appears.

The power waits.

The translucent nail touches the runner once, then again. Beneath it comes an answering pressure, weaker than the foot’s own.

Soon they have something they can repeat.

A touch. An answer.

Sometimes the foot touches and the power remains still. Sometimes the answer comes only after the toe has begun to withdraw. The inhabitant learns to wait without pressing harder.

Once, the toe curls sharply and pulls away.

The power stops.

It does not answer again until the foot returns.

***

The difficult winter arrives late.

By then the flank has come free of the wall, and the shoulder hangs low enough that the white grazers must take another route. The body lies across the floor in a posture that cannot become rest. One knee has arrived too close to the ribs. The throat is continuous now, but swallowing still tightens tissue along the back.

The mouth drinks poorly.

For several days the swimmers keep their distance from the membrane. The furred animals gather against the exposed skin, fastening their green filaments into the leaves beneath it.

The hollow is working everywhere.

That is part of the difficulty. Roots thicken under both hips at once. Three ceiling folds try to open into the same space. The cold-regulating leaves send heat to a bud in a spring that has not yet become possible.

Their margins turn silver.

The power could hold the body upright. It could draw the head from its coverings and place every limb where a finished limb should go.

Instead it returns to the runner.

A touch reaches the sole.

The foot answers faintly.

Beneath the trapped knee, the power offers the same yielding resistance. The inhabitant presses against it. A root shifts. The knee moves a little away from the ribs.

Then they stop.

Water passes through the throat.

They do it again after the mouth has drunk.

Over the following weeks, the power lends support in places the body can learn to use. It bears weight without choosing the movement. When a foot finds a sound tangle of roots, the support there diminishes. When the shoulder begins to fall faster than the elbow can bend, it steadies the descent.

The leaves have time to withdraw.

One furred animal refuses to leave its nest against the hip. The power waits while the new hand, moving with extraordinary slowness, cups the nest and carries it six inches to the left.

By the end of winter, the inhabitant is lying on its side.

Both knees can bend.

The silver blemishes along the dark leaves begin to narrow.

***

Much later, one eye opens.

For several days it looks at the underside of a leaf.

The power remains beneath the runner, where it can still be found by touch. There is no need to become another thing the eye must learn.

Above, the meadow unfolds.

The seam between the roots is still narrow. Beyond it lie distances the body may never be able to cross. The power keeps the drainage passage clear, but does not widen the entrance.

Inside, the inhabitant has begun lifting its head.

Sometimes its jaw fringe opens toward the air descending from the meadow. Sometimes it closes again, and the face turns toward the pool.

There is time to discover what these movements mean.

One day the large seed is brought to the entrance.

It has a short stem now and a hard, unopened pair of leaves. The new hand carries it badly, with two fingers beneath it and the others folded out of the way.

The hand lowers the seed onto the runner.

The power gives the familiar answering pressure.

The fingers do not release it at first.

Then, carefully, they do.

The power supports the seed while the hand finds a better place beneath it.

Together they turn it toward the light.
