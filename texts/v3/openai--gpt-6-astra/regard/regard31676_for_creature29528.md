At the next turning, a little of the strain passes through a hand that was not there before.

The tender feels it. So does the child.

They look at you.

“Not there,” the tender says.

You move your hand.

***

It is possible to arrive in a world with power sufficient to change it and still put your hand in the wrong place.

You had touched the seam where its edges pressed together. The tender shows you the softer strip beside it, where a change in pressure can be felt before it becomes movement.

“Here. Otherwise you only know afterward.”

You leave your hand where they place it.

Through the root comes the turning of a leaf, the weight of distant animals, the minute pull of a new graft drawing moisture toward itself. There are doors being scraped open. There is someone running. There is a sleeping person whose chest has settled so close to the living grain that their breathing and the root’s slow exchange seem, from this distance, almost one event.

The seam is failing.

It will survive tonight. Probably it will survive the wet season. The tender has judged it well.

What they cannot feel from here is the obstruction eleven layers below: an old drainage braid pinched between two later repairs. The wood around it has been thirsty for sixty years. New growth has learned to go around the thirst, putting its weight on something else.

You could close the seam.

You follow what would happen if you did.

Far below, a drinking pocket would empty. A nursery ledge would cool. Beyond that, a strip of new fiber would take a load it could not yet bear.

You keep your hand still.

“Something below?” the tender asks.

“Yes.”

“How far?”

You show them.

This takes longer than expected. There are distances for which they have no spoken measure, but they can make room for them in a cord.

***

The child’s name, at present, refers to a noise made while eating.

They are old enough to know this is unjust, and young enough that the noise still happens.

They fetch a length of practice cord. You and the tender sit with it between you. The child corrects two of your knots.

Eventually the obstruction lies represented on the root: the pinched braid, the thirsty layers, the route by which water once arrived. The tender touches the account several times.

“There were steps,” they say.

There are still steps. They are inside a wall now.

“My mother used to speak about going down them.”

They sit back, carefully. Their hip hurts when they do this. It has hurt for so long that they prepare for it even in dreams.

You ask whether they would like help with that.

They glance at the seam.

“With your hip,” you say.

“What would you do?”

You tell them what you can: ease the inflamed tissue, restore the worn surface, leave the joint shaped for the body that has grown around it. There may be a few days of unfamiliar balance.

They consider this more suspiciously than they considered your knowledge of the buried stairs.

“I have work tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“And after?”

“Yes.”

They give permission.

The child watches their parent stand.

There is no brightness. The tender rises with the usual guarded motion and then stops halfway, annoyed by a pain that has failed to arrive where expected.

They sit down and stand again.

“Don’t keep doing it,” the child says.

***

By morning there are six people at the seam.

One has brought an old account of the lower works. One has brought food. One has come because the person with the account cannot be trusted to distinguish an obsolete gutter from a household boundary.

The other three have come to argue about closing the road.

You tell them the road can remain open while the buried braid is reached, if its weight is carried elsewhere.

“Where?” they ask.

“Here.”

You hold out your hands.

They do not accept this immediately.

They test you first with a loose rail, then with a loaded sling. Someone proposes something heavier. Someone else says that if you intend to deceive them, a larger basket will hardly expose it.

In the end, the tender places both palms on the root while you take the strain.

They feel the change.

“Enough,” they say.

You could carry more. You stop where they ask.

***

Opening the way to the obstruction takes nine days.

It could take you less than a moment, but there are things living in the wall. There are also old grafts no one has seen since their making, and the people want to learn how they were done.

You make it possible to work without hurry.

Where the opening descends beyond the breathing skin, you hold good air close to the workers. It has the ordinary warmth and dampness they expect. The seam-feeders remain comfortable in their cups.

You steady the layers as they are parted. You catch three dropped tools and, on the sixth day, a person who was certain the foothold had thickened sufficiently.

They are embarrassed.

You put them back without making an occasion of it.

The animals displaced from the wall are given a sheltered strip beside the excavation. Several return persistently to a hollow that no longer exists. The child makes a similar hollow in a piece of waste bark. It works for two of them.

At night, the tender goes home.

The first night, they return twice.

On the second, they send the child to check.

On the third, nobody comes until morning.

You hold the road.

***

Inside the old braid they find a fastening that should have been removed when the surrounding lattice took its weight.

It is a small thing. A strip of tough fiber, tied well.

Its makers meant to return.

There is no way to learn why they did not.

Nearby, however, there are newer cords. These record inspections, diversions, requests for labor. The trouble was known before anyone now alive was born. Water had been sent by a longer route, then charged against a district that had never agreed to maintain it.

The people who find these cords become very quiet.

You keep the exposed wood moist while they read.

You do not improve the account. You do not supply forgiving intentions for the dead, or make the obligations disappear because their consequences are inconvenient for the living.

When the arguments begin, you keep holding the road.

There is no wound worsening beneath the hearing. No family has to consent before evening because their sleeping ledge might otherwise fall.

They can take the time they need.

This changes the discussion.

Not all at once.

***

The braid is freed.

Water enters tissue that has waited sixty years for it. Along the opened wall, pale surfaces darken by degrees. A smell rises, fresh and bitter.

The repair must remain accessible. That is the tenders’ first decision.

Their second is to teach more people how to recognize the same failure elsewhere.

You help them examine the districts connected to this one. There are more obstructions. There are sound structures that look alarming, and handsome surfaces with very little holding them up. You show what you find without making every defect into an emergency.

People need to sleep.

When they ask you to make all the work sound forever, you ask what they would want to happen when a room must widen, or a watercourse move, or someone begin a new stair.

They spend an afternoon discussing it.

Then they ask for something different: a warning that travels through a structure before its failure becomes unavoidable, and a way to distinguish that warning from the countless ordinary movements of growth.

Together, you try a small graft beside the seam.

It gives a faint, uneven pulse when the fibers beneath it begin carrying more than they can safely hold. It does not heal them. It can be checked, grown elsewhere, altered.

The tender makes you try it under wet conditions, then dry ones.

The child suggests a place where a sleeping person’s hand would feel it.

***

You remain long enough for your absence to become something that can be planned for.

Other hands learn the seventeen bindings. The tender learns work that can be done sitting down, then discovers they would rather do some of the climbing now that climbing does not hurt.

They take three days away.

On the second day, they miss the seam-feeders.

They do not come home early.

There are larger matters awaiting you: darkened spans, settlements bearing burdens they never chose, travelers whose routes have kept them from home for most of a lifetime. You go to them by the directions people give you. You learn to turn a little left.

But you return to this causeway.

The seam has a narrow inspection opening now, with a lid that must be trimmed each season. Beside it grows a tuft of fresh breathing leaves. Someone has planted it for families making the long crossing.

The child is there when you arrive. They are constructing something of no evident use from hoops and scrap fiber.

They show it to you.

You ask what it will become.

“I don’t know yet.”

There is room beside them, on the warm part.

You sit down.
