The first thing the power felt was the pressure.

Not the weight of the high sea against the mountains. That was held, and held well enough for now. It felt the pressure beneath Emet’s breastbone as she followed Seli up the rope: the small inward insistence that had already begun to narrow her attention.

She had let him sit a little longer because he was happy.

Now she watched his ankles moving ahead of her and did not look toward the gardens. There was no danger yet. There was only the familiar subtraction: first the view, then the thought she had meant to keep, then everything except the next knot and the chamber beyond it.

The power followed them there.

It could have emptied the valley of water. It could have hardened every mountain, filled the dark with light, opened the houses to a wide and effortless sky.

For a moment it considered all of these things.

Then it attended to the roots suspended beneath the gardens, to the bodies of the people, to the warm moving skin under a newly laid slab. It felt what a dry mountain would crush and what sudden light would close. It watched Emet rise into the air beside her apprentice.

She put both elbows on the ledge. He was already talking.

“It came up while I was touching it.”

“Yes.”

“I thought it would feel different.”

“How?”

“More like something happening.”

Emet laughed, then stopped to breathe.

The power waited until she had finished.

***

It made itself known in a chamber where there was room for one more person.

This was harder to find than a splendid place would have been.

The chamber belonged to an old garden keeper who left its entrance open, but who had strong opinions about strangers using her eating ledge as a seat. The power entered from underneath in a borrowed shape, with bound hair and empty hands. It waited for its eyes to clear.

The garden keeper looked at it.

“Not there,” she said.

It moved.

Emet arrived a little later carrying a damaged roof binding. She had come to ask whether a length of waterwood could be spared from the upper frames.

The stranger listened while they discussed the wood. It listened through a disagreement about how much had been lent last season. When the garden keeper went below to inspect her stores, it spoke.

“I can make something that would keep this air good.”

Emet glanced toward the ceiling mesh.

“The leaves are changed regularly.”

“Yes.”

“What would it eat?”

“Nothing you need to bring it.”

She looked at the stranger more closely.

“What would it do to the water?”

“I would arrange it so that it did nothing to the water.”

“That is not the same as knowing.”

“No,” said the power. “It is not.”

This answer improved her opinion of it a little.

She unwound the broken binding from her wrist and laid it on the ledge.

“You should speak to the people who tend the gas plants.”

“I would like to.”

“And the chamber cleaners. Not just the garden owners.”

“Yes.”

The garden keeper returned with less wood than Emet had asked for and more than she had expected.

By then the stranger had acquired a list.

***

There were questions the power had not needed to ask itself before.

Could the thing be moved?

Could it be shut off?

Would it make a chamber dangerous if the mountain grew through it?

Would it work in a vessel small enough for a child to carry?

Would someone be able to demand payment for all the air it made?

The power could answer the first questions by changing what it intended to make. The last required a longer conversation.

People came and went. Fresh gas was brought. At one point the garden keeper told the power to take a vessel itself if it meant to stay for another discussion.

It did.

Its first journey was unnecessarily slow. It understood the vessel perfectly, but understanding a thing was not quite the same as carrying it past two neighbors bringing a door panel the other way.

Emet watched it return with most of the gas still inside.

“Under the cross rope next time,” she said.

***

The first piece was no larger than a closed hand.

It looked like dull fired clay. One side was hollowed to fit over a peg; the other bore three broad grooves that could be recognized in darkness. When it was hung above water, the air around it remained breathable.

There was no brightness, no scent, no pleasurable feeling that might conceal bad air. It did not make those who breathed it tireless. It did not give them patience with relatives.

The power made it so that nothing harmful accumulated elsewhere. No exhausted air was sent into another chamber, no life in the lower world paid for what the valley received.

This was a greater work than the making of the clay shape.

It was also work no one in the chamber could verify merely by looking.

“Then we begin with an empty bell,” said one of the cleaners.

They hung it there for many days.

The power did not object to the tests. It helped carry the equipment, and answered the same questions when different people asked them. When it did not understand a question, it said so.

Eventually the garden keeper sat beneath the bell for an afternoon, mending a frame.

Afterward she reported that the thing worked, but the ledge was intolerable.

Emet altered the ledge.

***

While the trials continued, the power went through the town.

It did not go only where someone had invited it.

There was a chamber in the lowest quarter whose air was poor because the woman who maintained it had hurt her shoulder and was concealing how little gas she could carry. There was a child who avoided a certain passage because older children waited there to pull his feet. There was an old man who had stopped visiting his sister. Each thought the other had become indifferent. The journey between their houses had grown too long for him.

The power did not suppose that a single object would answer all of this.

It carried gas for the woman and asked whether she wanted her shoulder mended. She wanted to know whether the mending would hurt. It told her exactly what she would feel.

It stayed near the passage until the child had gone home, then went to speak with the adults responsible for the children who waited there.

It asked the old man whether he would like company on a visit.

Sometimes it was refused. Sometimes it was asked for things it would not give: a neighbor’s private thoughts, a change in someone else’s affections, an advantage that would leave another household short.

Sometimes the requests were very small.

Emet, discovering that it could mend an eye, sat silent for a while.

“Would I still see properly in water?”

“Yes.”

“And in air?”

“Yes.”

She touched the corner of her eye.

“Do that, then.”

Afterward she looked at a lamp for a long time. She had forgotten how little space one light occupied.

***

The power made more of the clay pieces.

It did not place them all in the communal chambers. Some went into small resting bells along the ropes, at the distances chosen by people who could no longer cross a terrace on one breath. Some went into homes. A few were kept loose for repairs, rescues, and journeys beyond the gardens.

Anyone could ask for one. No household had to surrender its chamber, promise gratitude, or attend a gathering.

The gas keepers were uneasy.

Their skill had fed the town with something more urgent than food. Now people spoke in their presence of all the work that would no longer need doing.

The power listened to them too.

For a time the gardens were maintained as before. The new pieces were tested through the warm season and through changes in the mountain. Later, some frames were given over to edible roots. Others remained, tended by people who wished to keep the old knowledge alive.

No one was required to spend a lifetime carrying vessels merely because the work had once been necessary.

Nor did the power pretend that relief could not arrive with grief attached.

One keeper took down her last frame and wept angrily when someone congratulated her. The power sat beside her without trying to make the afternoon into a celebration.

***

There were other dangers.

The power examined the skin that held the sea between the peaks. It went below the soft mountains, where heat and mineral and living whiteness worked against one another. It followed the large dark swimmer without touching it.

Where a deep fault threatened to open too quickly beneath the town, it eased the strain over a broader reach of stone. Nothing above became rigid. The mountains continued their slow unfinished work.

It told the builders what it had done.

Emet asked whether this meant the lower storehouse could remain where it was.

“No. That slope is still rising.”

She sighed.

The power offered to help move the baskets.

***

The resting bell nearest the unfinished staircase was built with a ledge for three people.

Seli wanted to make it larger. Emet reminded him that someone would have to carry the slabs. He suggested asking the power.

“We can ask,” she said.

They did. It carried the slabs.

Seli set the last one himself, badly on the first attempt and better on the second. The power held it while Emet showed him where the pressure would fall.

Inside, they hung a clay piece on a peg. They fastened the rope below the entrance and tied the round knot. Emet felt it with her eyes closed, then made Seli trim an end that might be mistaken for a tassel.

By then the working period was over.

They rose into the chamber. There was a new-clay smell, and the water beneath their chins was cloudy with what their clothes had brought in. Seli began describing another bell farther down the slope, where future houses might stand.

Emet listened. She could hear the flaw in his plan, but it could wait until he had finished.

Outside, a faint seam opened in the mountain. The vibration passed through the ledge and into their elbows.

All three fell quiet.

Presently Seli began again.

Emet settled her back against the wall. For once, no part of her was counting how long they had been there.
