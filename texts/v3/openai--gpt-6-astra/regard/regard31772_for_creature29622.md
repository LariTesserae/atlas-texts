The power noticed the lifting of the handle.

It had noticed the mountain first, naturally: the immense warmth beneath the cloud, the slow arrival of material where no material had been, the city holding its shapes against that arrival. Such things were visible from very far away.

The lifting of the handle required closer attention.

Eda did it with her left hand while keeping the right beneath the second tool bag. The strain passed through her wrist and into her shoulder. Once inside, she put the bags down before taking off her sandals.

The power watched her stand for a moment without needing to carry anything.

It could have made the door fit. It could have made every door in the city fit, and kept them fitting for a thousand years.

Before doing this, it considered the doors.

Some were meant to bind when a fire softened their retaining pins. Some held back water. One, below a collecting court, was being leaned against by a sleeping animal whose young occupied the shallow space behind its forefeet. A door in the northern district had been deliberately wedged by a woman who did not want the person with the other key to enter.

The power took its attention off the doors as a class.

It stayed with Eda’s.

***

There was a knock the following afternoon.

Eda had come home early because her third customer had forgotten the appointment and gone to the reservoir hearings. She was sitting with an account book open, trying to remember whether a charge included a replacement spindle.

The person outside had no tool bags.

“Yes?”

“Your door catches.”

“I know.”

“I could help with it.”

“Who sent you?”

“No one.”

Eda looked down the landing. People occasionally came to the wrong level, but this did not seem to be the difficulty.

“Are you looking for work?”

“No.”

“Then what sort of help?”

The visitor considered how much of the truth could be made useful in a doorway.

“I can change things,” it said. “A great many things. I would rather find out what is wanted before I start.”

Eda kept her hand on the handle.

“Change that,” she said, pointing to a split in the ceramic cover over a pipe.

The split closed. The small missing flake returned, including the gray dirt along its edge.

She crouched and touched it.

“Is the pipe sound underneath?”

“Yes.”

“You ought to have looked before you closed it.”

“I did.”

After a while she stood up.

“Come in. Don’t do anything yet.”

***

She gave it water because there was water in the jug and she did not know what else to offer.

The visitor set the cup on the floor.

Eda looked at it.

“I’ve been watching your work,” it said.

“That usually costs.”

“I can pay.”

“With what?”

This took longer to settle than the matter of the pipe.

Eda would not accept coins made to resemble municipal coins. Nor would she accept a piece of precious metal without knowing whether some person elsewhere would be missing it. When the visitor explained that it need not take anything from anywhere, she asked what would happen to the price of that metal if it paid everybody in the same way.

Eventually they left payment aside.

The water in the cup held steady. The floor had bowed very slightly near the hinge side. Eda explained how the movement entered the frame, where she would normally remove material, and why there was less room to do so than there had been five years earlier.

The visitor listened without needing the explanation for its own understanding.

When she finished, it asked, “Would you like the floor made level?”

“How far?”

“The room.”

“And outside?”

“That could remain as it is.”

“So I’d have a step.”

“Not necessarily.”

She sat back on her heels.

“You can see why I’m asking.”

“Yes.”

“Can you make the door work without putting the trouble somewhere I haven’t looked?”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“As long as you want this door here.”

Eda rubbed the underside of the handle with her thumb. Its plating had worn through where she lifted it.

“All right,” she said. “That.”

Nothing shone. There was no sound.

The door opened under her hand.

She shut it, opened it again, and examined the hinges. The change was there, though she could not at first understand it: a small freedom within the frame, taking up movement without allowing the door to sag. No part was loose.

“Will it need oil?”

“The hinges will.”

“Can I take it apart?”

“Yes.”

“And put it back?”

“Yes. I can show you.”

She fetched her tools.

***

By evening she understood enough to be dissatisfied.

“I can’t make that lower piece.”

“No.”

“Neither can the hinge works.”

“Not with their present equipment.”

“So if you go away, we have one very good door.”

The power had come prepared to offer wonders. It found itself receiving a reasonably priced consultation.

They spent the next two hours changing the lower piece.

The second design needed a ceramic the city could produce, but only in small quantities. The third could be assembled in an ordinary fitting shop. It was larger and less graceful. Eda preferred it.

They fitted the third version to her door.

She insisted on keeping the old hinges in a bag.

***

The power did not appoint Eda to speak for the city.

By the second visit she had made it clear that she knew doors, some kinds of frames, and which building managers would claim a tenant had caused movement in a foundation. Her knowledge of reservoirs was no better than anyone else’s. She had opinions about the return wells, but warned it not to confuse these with qualifications.

She also did not want people coming to her kitchen to petition it.

It rented a room near the station court.

The rent was paid with work agreed upon in advance: the repair of a failed public condenser whose replacement would otherwise have required closing two stairways for a season. The municipal clerk entered the arrangement in three ledgers. In the space for the contractor’s trade, after some discussion, she wrote **repairs**.

People came.

Some wanted things it would not give them. A landlord asked for foundations that would grow only under the portion of a building he owned. A manufacturer asked whether finish readers were really necessary.

Some wanted things it could provide immediately, once it had asked enough questions.

Venn came with his pension drawer tied shut with cord.

The power did not tell him that his dispute was small beside the mountain. It read every paper. Then it asked whether he wanted help with the claim, with his hands, or with both.

He was silent long enough that it offered to leave the question with him.

“Would I have to go back?” he asked.

“No.”

“They’ll say I can.”

“We should settle the claim first, then.”

He untied the drawer.

***

Eda continued working.

She agreed to test the new fitting in six buildings, on the condition that the residents paid nothing and that she was paid for inspections even when nothing needed adjusting. A young fitter from the northern blocks worked with her. He had steady hands and a habit of explaining a fault before he had finished looking at it.

She cured him of this in several buildings, though not permanently.

The woman with the yellow entrance wanted to know how much of the frame they would disturb.

“Less next time,” Eda said.

“That isn’t what I asked.”

They marked the proposed cuts in chalk. The woman moved one mark, then allowed them to begin.

At the hinge works, the first batch of fittings failed under sustained heat. The power could have prevented the failure. Instead it had insisted that the batch be tested before installation, and stayed while the workers opened the testing chamber and found the distortion.

It answered their questions. When asked for a material they could not make, it helped them find one they could.

There were arguments over ownership of the design. These lasted longer than the heat tests. The power would supply neither exclusive rights nor a threat to make everyone agree. It did pay for the time spent preparing clear drawings, and put copies where any workshop could obtain them.

Not every workshop wanted to change its methods.

Enough did.

***

There were other matters.

The power went beneath the formation yards, where the mountain was entering the collars, and remained there through a cloud season.

It did not return with an explanation of why the stone rose.

It returned with places marked on a survey: collars that could be loosened, channels that must not be sealed, an occupied shaft absent from the municipal drawings. It distinguished what it knew from what it had only begun to suspect.

The planners moved a proposed storehouse.

At one return well, a gate was installed that could be opened from either side. No one saw it used. There were marks on its inner handle.

The city remained hot. Food still came from below. People still quarreled over chairs and passage rights, and an improved hinge did nothing for a tenant whose bed stood in a public route. That required a different repair, involving a wall, a lease, and compensation the landlord was reluctant to pay.

The power stayed for that too.

***

Months later, Eda came home carrying one bag.

The other was with the young fitter, who was returning a borrowed gauge. He would bring it in the morning. She had not retired, and there had been no family agreement about what this arrangement meant.

She opened her door while looking through the day’s receipts.

Inside, she set down the bag, took off her sandals, and went to fill the kettle.

Only when she returned did she notice that she had left the door standing open. It rested where she had released it, neither swinging inward nor dragging against the floor.

Across the slot, the man had begun the portion of his song he knew.

Eda went back and closed the door with one finger.
