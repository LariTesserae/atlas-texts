For a long time, the power does nothing that can be distinguished from attention.

It attends to the gauge, but also to the snail crossing it. It attends to the pressure beneath the central growth, and to the water passing through the broad strips that connect the smaller inhabitant to the larger. It does not assume these are separate matters.

Nor does it assume that the hollow is broken because it has departed from its first construction. The little trucks will not run here again. Whatever the boiler once supplied is no longer wanted in that form. The copper has ended in the right place for something else to begin.

There are injuries. There is work. There are movements whose purpose is not apparent.

The power takes care not to confuse them.

It watches the larger inhabitant turn the bottle glass toward the light. Nothing in the pressure changes. No bore opens. The glass does not become a tool.

The power remembers this.

—

Its first intervention takes place outside the hollow.

Far under the modest rise, water passes through a bed of gravel before entering the old industrial drains. One of these drains has been narrowing for years. Fine roots have caught silt, and the silt has held more roots. Water still passes, but after heavy rain it arrives in a succession of sudden loads.

The inhabitant knows these loads through its feet. On such days it does not visit the shelf behind the building.

The power does not pull out the roots.

Instead, it opens a small space in the gravel beside the drain. When the next rush comes, some of the water enters this space. When the rush subsides, the water returns. Nothing is diverted from the hollow. The arrival is merely less abrupt.

At the gauge, the needle rises.

It pauses where it used to tremble.

The inhabitant places its pads against the glass, then against the wall. It goes behind the building. It returns. Through three cycles it leaves the amber wedge untouched.

On the fourth, it removes the wedge and tries a thinner one.

The power waits through this adjustment before doing anything more.

—

There is a place beneath the central growth where the larger inhabitant rests badly.

The depression fits most of its length. Near the rear, however, a buried iron bracket prevents the living surface from yielding. One of the thick plates presses there. The plate has grown crooked, and the soft seam beside it opens a little whenever the hollow closes.

The power could change the plate. It could change the inhabitant’s whole body.

Instead, while the depression is empty, it lets the buried bracket part at an old flaw.

The two pieces settle scarcely the width of a root hair.

That evening the inhabitant enters as usual. Its flanks meet the soft edges. The pale openings disappear.

Near the rear, the floor yields.

The inhabitant withdraws halfway.

It feels the depression with an appendage, working from one end to the other. It touches the place where the bracket lies, then the corresponding plate on its own body. For a while it rests elsewhere.

Nothing follows it. Nothing urges it back.

Two cycles later it tries the depression again.

This time, when the paths soften, its whole length remains settled.

—

The harder matter is the rail bed.

A clear road could be made through the hollow in an instant. But a road has edges, and these edges would have to be kept from joining. Roots would be severed. Small bores would lose their destinations. The basin might stand at a different height.

And the inhabitant has already made passages. It has kept footholds where feet are likely to need them. It has carried rings of hard growth considerable distances.

The power begins with one of those rings.

It lies in the discard shelf, split along one side. The inhabitant has packed it between a piece of ceramic and the dried fruit.

During the night, a little moisture collects in the split.

By afternoon, the broken edges have softened. Between them is a narrow seam of amber-colored growth.

The inhabitant discovers this while moving a snail.

It examines the ring for a long time. It turns it over, places it against the wall, takes it down. Then it sets it apart from the other things.

The next day it carries the ring to a constriction beside the rails.

The power follows, without weight.

The ring is too small to hold the passage open. The inhabitant puts it around a bore instead. It plugs a neighboring bore with fiber, waits for the deep double beat, and presses its body against the hanging growth.

The growth lifts.

Not far. Not for long.

The inhabitant retrieves the ring.

Behind the building, it places a second broken ring beside the first.

This one is larger.

—

What they make takes most of a season.

There is no new road. There is a low passage beside the rails that stays open through the falling half of the cycle. Its roof is supported by three loops of hard growth, each joined to a different pressure channel. When one channel relaxes, another takes the load.

The larger inhabitant arranges the loops. The power makes possible a few joins that would otherwise take years.

Once, a join begins to close across a small side bore. The inhabitant presses a pad into the soft material and keeps it there.

The growth stops.

It does not resume when the pad is withdrawn.

The inhabitant clears the bore, moves the loop, and starts again.

At the entrance, where the rails disappear beneath the roots, the power does one thing without waiting to be shown. It keeps a narrow outlet open to the air. The inhabitants need not use it. But nothing inside will have to tear itself from a resting place merely because a visitor has stayed too late.

The outlet is not straight. Daylight appears at its far end only when you are close.

—

Not everything becomes easier.

The hollow still requires attending to. Leaves still enter openings where leaves are not useful. The gauge clouds at its edges. Some days the young inhabitant makes a repair that has to be undone.

The power does not prevent this.

But when the larger one settles beneath the central growth, small changes in pressure no longer require it to leave. The new loops shift their burden. The gravel chamber fills and empties under the rise.

The raw strip along its side closes. For several days the healed surface remains pale.

During one of those days, the inhabitant goes to the shelf and takes out the hollow fruit.

It carries the fruit to the brick building, sets it beneath the window, and leaves it there through an entire cycle.

The power finds no further work to do with that.

—

Late in the season, the larger inhabitant passes through the new outlet.

Beyond the rim, the ground is comparatively hard and astonishingly bare. A little grass grows among stones. Sound reaches the body poorly here. The inhabitant pauses often, laying its pads down in different arrangements.

The power does not put a path beneath it.

It remains attentive to the water held in the inhabitant’s folds, to the distance back, to the afternoon warmth. There is still ample time.

At the edge of a shallow ditch, the inhabitant finds a stone with a hole through it.

The hole is irregular. It would make a poor fitting. The stone is too heavy for a wedge.

The inhabitant lifts it.

Coming back takes longer than going out. Once, it sets the stone down and rests beside it.

By the time it reaches the hollow, the young one is beside the gauge, touching the pipe and the wall in turn. The larger passes without interrupting.

Behind the building, it makes room on the shelf.

—

The power stays, though not at the center.

It watches the far drains and the slow movement of the surrounding earth. It learns how much warning the hollow needs. If something comes that cannot be met by pads against a pipe, by a wedge, by patient shifts of living weight, there will be time to respond.

Within the hollow, the discharge gate opens.

The larger inhabitant lays its appendages along the floor. The smaller sets its pads beside them.

Water passes underneath.

On the shelf behind the building, light enters the hole in the new stone and falls across the bark. A snail reaches the bright patch, draws in its feelers, then continues.

No one moves it.
