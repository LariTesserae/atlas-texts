The fiber holds.

The animal braces its hind feet and pulls again. It is not a valuable fiber, except that it is dry, and long, and already beginning to separate into the fine strands that make a nest warm.

The power which has noticed the shelf notices this also.

For a moment it considers loosening the stone. Then it attends to the stone’s other work: the woman’s hand upon it, the gravel it retains, the small dark chamber beneath its lower face.

It parts the fiber where the stone has pinched it.

The animal falls backward, recovers, and carries away what it can use.

***

It finds the woman well below the shelf, at a place where the descent requires her to lower herself from one bent leg onto the other.

She has done this many times. Today she sits first and lets her pack down on its cord. There is nobody to see the awkwardness, which is a relief until she hears someone say, from farther along the path:

“Would you like a hand?”

She looks for the speaker’s feet before looking at the face. The feet are on sound ground.

“Yes. Stand there. Not on the yellow piece.”

The stranger stands where she directs.

Its hand is warm. With its help she reaches the lower step without the usual sharp catch in her knee. Then she draws her hand away and takes up her pack.

“Are you going above?”

“If I may.”

“It is not mine.”

“The shelter?”

“That is.”

“May I use it?”

She considers the stranger’s empty shoulders.

“You have no fuel.”

“No.”

“Then come down with me first.”

It does.

***

In the settlement she gives it a task before she gives it food. There are fuel cakes under an eave which must be turned, and a basket with a broken carrying loop.

The power turns the cakes. It repairs the basket with a strip of hide she provides. Its first repair is stronger than the surrounding wicker, and it pauses, undoes the binding, and distributes the strain over a wider section.

She watches this from the doorway.

“You have mended things before.”

“Some.”

“Not baskets.”

“Not this kind.”

At supper she asks what it wants above the ice.

“To see what is happening.”

This is an answer she understands. She does not ask again.

***

They climb six days later.

The stranger carries fuel, a little food, and a piece of tightly woven cloth. It does not offer to carry her whole pack. At the steep place it waits until she asks.

On the shelf, she shows it where to put its feet.

“There are holes under that.”

“I see.”

“You see the leaves.”

It looks again.

She takes it around the basin, pointing with the antler. The power could follow each fracture into the mountain. It could distinguish the roots of one plant from another in the crowded dark. But it listens to her account of the pale vein and the shading shard, because these tell it things the mountain cannot: where she stood, what she expected, what has surprised her.

At the shelter she checks the roof.

Then she checks it again.

“What?” the stranger asks.

“That one.”

The middle slab has moved very slightly. A patch of stone that was sheltered from the light is exposed along one edge.

She kneels with difficulty. The stranger kneels beside her.

“Can it be reset?”

“Yes,” it says. “But not safely while the upper weight is on it.”

They unload the roof.

It takes most of the afternoon. She directs the placing of the smaller stones, keeping them away from the drying places beneath the overhangs. When the slabs are exposed, they find a moth pressed into a shallow hollow, wings laid along the grain.

They leave that slab until the sun reaches it.

The woman is tired before the work is finished. She sits on her pack and becomes angry at the wasted light.

The stranger does not tell her to rest. It brings the next stone within reach, so she can inspect its bearing surface without standing.

At last there is one slab left to lift, too heavy for either of the bodies they appear to possess.

“Wait,” she says. “We shall need a lever.”

But the stranger has already raised it.

It holds the slab upright with one hand. With the other, it shades the moth, which has not yet flown.

For a while the woman says nothing.

Then she puts down the cord.

“You might have told me.”

“Yes.”

“Put it there. No—turn it first.”

***

That evening she asks questions.

Some the power answers. Some it cannot answer in a way that is useful to her, and these it does not dress up as answers.

She asks whether the shelf will last.

“Not in this shape.”

“The shelter?”

“Less long than the shelf.”

“How much less?”

It tells her what it can distinguish: a seam behind the recess, the course of water in spring, the uncertainty of a block still held by ice. She listens closely.

“Show me tomorrow.”

“I will.”

After a while she stretches her bad leg toward the hearth.

“Could you do anything about this?”

“May I touch it?”

She pulls the cloth above her knee.

The power attends to the old injury, the joint worn unevenly around it, the muscles which have spent years protecting it. It does not return her leg to the shape it had in childhood. Much of her strength has been built around what happened afterward.

It eases the swollen tissue. It gives the roughened surfaces room to move. The deeper repair proceeds slowly enough that she can feel where her weight belongs.

She bends the knee a little.

“More?” it asks.

She tries again.

“Enough for tonight.”

It takes its hand away.

***

In the morning they examine the split bank.

The woman crouches beside it more easily than before, and is distracted for a moment by this.

“Could you hold it up?”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“As long as I remained.”

She looks at the white roots stretched across the opening.

“That seems a poor occupation for you.”

“There are worse.”

Under the hanging turf, a trickle has been washing fine material from a narrow bed of grit. They trace its entrance without lifting the bank. A stone could divert it, but the water would then pass through a mammal’s store.

They leave the water alone.

Farther down, beneath the bank but clear of its roots, there is a bare notch in the rock. The power holds the turf’s weight while she fits two thin stones into the notch. Behind them she works a little loose gravel. It makes a small catching place, nothing more.

When the power lets go, several grains fall. The roots remain taut.

“It may still come away,” it says.

“I know.”

They move back. By noon, a beetle has entered the new space.

***

The cloth becomes a lining beneath the shelter roof. They hang it with a slope toward the doorway, leaving the rear passage open. Condensation gathers on it and travels away from the bedding.

The shelter is still low. Smoke still blows into it from the hearth when the wind turns. But on the next wet night the woman sleeps without a bowl beside her face.

Before that night comes, she asks for help with the marks.

They spend hours inside, shoulder to shoulder. The power holds a light that gives off no heat. She copies the incisions onto thin boards, adding the landmarks she remembers. Where she is uncertain, she leaves a space.

Twice she asks it what an old mark meant.

It tells her what happened to the stone at that time.

“That does not mean it was what I noticed,” she says.

“No.”

The space remains.

***

When it is time to descend, she stands beside the hearth with her pack ready.

“Will you come back?”

“If you want me to.”

“I cannot promise to be here.”

“I know.”

She looks across the basin. A flower stalk is shaking. Near the lip, a pale bird watches them from its usual stone.

“If you arrive first, check above the roof. Before you go inside.”

“I will.”

“And don’t make the place easy to reach.”

The power waits.

She tightens one of her pack straps.

“Unless I need bringing down.”

They leave while the gravel is still frozen.

At the steep step, she gives the stranger her hand. The knee bends farther now, but she is not yet used to trusting it.

Above them, the shelf resumes its small sounds. Beneath the repaired roof, a clipped stem passes through the space left open behind the bedding. The cloth stirs in the draft without touching it.
