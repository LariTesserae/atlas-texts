The power noticed the second pressure gauge.

There were larger things to notice: the weight of the country resting on its hot chambers, the cloud ascending every afternoon with water in its hands. But the second gauge required an explanation, and the explanation was eggs.

It stayed a while.

It watched Nera scrape a condenser gutter, stopping twice to straighten her back. It watched the carriers take possession of what she scraped out. In the evening it followed the injured grazer up a path which, from below, appeared not to exist.

By the end of a week it understood that cooling the basin would kill most of what lived there. A river, besides being an extravagance, would drown several kinds of life that existed nowhere beyond a few warm inches of stone. Even mending the leaking pump flange would require care. There were generations of small mammals whose knowledge of water began beneath it.

Nera was not the only being here who had arranged a life around something faulty.

On the eighth morning, after breakfast, someone knocked on the turbine-house door.

They wore a gray coat and boots already dusty enough not to offend her. They carried no luggage except a bag with a shoulder strap.

“The lower station didn’t telephone,” Nera said.

“No.”

“Then you’ve walked.”

“Yes.”

She looked at their boots again.

“Sit down before you fall down. Not that chair. The other one.”

***

They said they had come to offer assistance.

“With what?”

“That is what I need to find out.”

“Inspection,” Nera said.

“No.”

She gave them a length of blocked pipe and a scraper. This was not a test she acknowledged as a test. The pipe needed clearing.

They worked in the shade until noon. Once, they reached toward a bucket.

“Not that water.”

Their hand stopped.

“Why?” they asked.

She explained. They listened to the whole explanation, including the part about the labels and the inspector’s socks.

Afterward she felt obliged to add that he had been a decent enough man in other respects.

***

The visitor learned the water first.

They learned that the condenser nearest the ridge ran cloudy before the others, and that the valve marked CLOSED had to be left a quarter-turn open. They learned why a square appeared on certain pages of the ledger.

Nera took them to the patch of ground that rang after rain.

“Never carry anything heavy across here.”

They looked down through the mineral crust, through a chamber full of scalding darkness, into the narrow place where pressure was worrying at a seam.

“All right,” they said.

It would have been easy to hold the seam shut. But the pressure would then have gone elsewhere, and elsewhere included the foundations of the sleeping room.

That afternoon they asked to see her oldest ledgers.

She brought out six, tied in pairs with cord.

They read the records of rain, pump failures, hot patches, changes in the vents. Then they walked the old workers’ paths with her. At three places they stopped and asked questions so exact that she fetched the field glass, although there was nothing distant to look at.

Together they marked a route for a new vent pipe.

“Will that do it?” she asked.

“For this chamber.”

“For how long?”

They told her what they knew, and what would still have to be watched.

She preferred this to the inspector’s manner of answering, which had involved the word *permanent*.

***

On their twelfth evening, she found them beneath the pipe racks, considering the leaking flange.

“If you fix that,” she said, “leave a drip somewhere farther out.”

“Yes.”

“Not onto the post footings.”

“No.”

“And it needs to be somewhere I can clean.”

They shifted aside to show her the shallow stone channel they had laid out, not yet connected. It ended beside the hollow where she emptied her cup. A small valve would let her drain it, brush it, or stop the supply entirely.

She crouched with some difficulty and ran a finger along the channel.

“Too smooth coming out.”

They roughened one edge for the animals’ feet.

She said it would do.

***

There was nothing spectacular about the visitor until the cable bearing failed.

Nera heard the change before the wheel stopped: a thin, uneven note beneath the ordinary clack. She was reaching for the brake when the support arm cracked.

A loaded bucket swung sideways.

The visitor put up a hand.

The bucket stopped. So did the fallen section of the arm, with its sharp end hanging a little above the roof. Along the wire, each of the other buckets settled from its swing.

For a moment Nera could hear the turbine distinctly.

“Brake first,” the visitor said.

She set it.

Then they lowered the broken iron onto clear ground, slowly enough for the lizards to move.

Nera stood with both hands still on the lever.

“You might have mentioned.”

“Yes,” they said. “I should have.”

***

She had questions. Most came out as accusations.

Could they have cleared the pipe without scraping it?

Yes.

Had they needed to eat the flatbread?

No, but they had liked it.

Was something going to be required of her?

No.

“People say that before they tell you what.”

The visitor waited.

“Do you want me to stay here?”

“Not unless you want to.”

“And if I leave?”

“Then the station will need another keeper.”

She looked away from them, toward the repair tools laid out beside the broken support.

“That’s what I keep saying.”

“I know.”

“No. You know it needs one. You don’t know what they send.”

***

The power could turn a bearing true. It could draw a buried fracture together without disturbing the nesting holes above it. There were repairs which took only an afternoon once Nera had explained why she had never been able to shut down both pumps at once.

A keeper took longer.

With Nera’s permission, a notice went down the line. It offered full pay during training, proper leave, and a return fare if the work proved unsuitable. The visitor supplied the money for the first year and made the arrangements plainly, without a debt attached to Nera’s name.

Three people came up.

One left after a night. One wanted to enclose the damp terraces and increase salt production.

The third was a woman who maintained municipal fountains. She had never worked a turbine. She carried a notebook and asked where she could empty her wash water.

Nera did not immediately like her.

This turned out not to prevent anything.

Her name was Seli. She learned slowly where haste would be dangerous and quickly where Nera wished she would ask another question. She moved a tool hook without consulting anyone. Nera moved it back. They discussed this more fiercely than either discussed the new bypass.

One afternoon Seli found cloudiness against the white enamel strip before Nera mentioned it.

That night Nera opened the flour tin.

The dress smelled of paper. She unfolded it on the bed and discovered that she still disliked the buttons.

***

The visitor did not find her a perfect room.

They brought descriptions of four rooms she could afford. One had a bath but no tree. One had both, but the landlord lived below and objected to late footsteps. Another overlooked a delivery yard and a plane tree, with a bathroom shared by two tenants.

Nera asked about the fourth.

“Above a bakery.”

“Too hot.”

“Yes.”

She chose to visit the third.

Only visit. She said this to Seli, to the lower station, and finally to the visitor, who had not disputed it the first time.

The morning she left, she checked the receiving tank after Seli had checked it. Then she checked the brake.

At the door she hesitated.

“The cup—”

“I know,” Seli said.

“It doesn’t need tea in it. Just water.”

“I know.”

Nera put on the boots fit to leave in. They were stiff across the toes.

The visitor walked with her as far as the basin’s edge. Below them the road entered cloud. Somewhere beneath it were streets, trees, deliveries, people whose habits she had not yet had cause to resent.

“What will you do now?” she asked.

“Finish the vent.”

“And then?”

“See what it changes.”

She nodded. That was necessary. A repair was not finished merely because the tools had been put away.

She started down, testing each step.

Behind her, the basin remained hot and densely occupied. Seli opened the cable line. A bucket crossed the first support wheel with a dry clack.

At the new water channel, a dust-colored animal stood upright for a long time before approaching.
