The power first notices the collar around the root.

There are larger things to notice: the water moving uphill, the machinery that draws it out of noon, the animal spread so thinly over stone that a moth’s foot can trouble several inches of its body. But the collar is smooth. The root has thickened since entering the chamber, and the animal has widened the passage around it.

Elsewhere, against the buried upright, its flesh has had no such allowance.

The power stays through a collection of dew.

In the closed chambers, bright grains move. Some strike the scarred walls and stop; others pass into channels too narrow to see from above. The wings of nearby insects falter. Beneath the center, the animal draws its smallest companions close against the low roof.

The power stays through the next collection, and the next.

It learns that the upright is not drawing water from the animal. It learns, too, that this is not enough to make the arrangement harmless. Each collection requires a brief alteration in the chambers. Old tissue resists. Living tissue takes up the strain. What appears aboveground as a loose hair turning toward the black surface is, belowground, a pulling that travels through almost the whole animal.

Once, the animal begins to form a head beside the fern. The stalks rise halfway. The upright starts its work, and the unfinished head subsides.

The power could break the upright.

It could lift the animal out through the stone without tearing a sensory hair.

Instead, for a little while, it does neither.

It follows the moisture into the seams. It finds the eggs that require the dry interval between collections, the fungus that drowns if a certain chamber fills, the soft-skinned young beneath the fern. It follows the warmth. It follows the food carried toward the pits and the food carried away.

In one place, a crack that looks empty holds a hundred sleeping bodies.

In another, there is an actual emptiness: a chamber beneath the upright, sealed long ago by a fallen grain of its own pale lining. Nothing has entered it for so long that its inner surface retains a finish absent everywhere else.

Here the power begins.

It moves the grain.

The change aboveground is very small. A bead of water forms on the upright and hangs longer than usual before disappearing.

Below, the empty chamber takes part of the load.

The animal tightens, waits, and loosens again.

For the rest of that afternoon it keeps a narrow strip of flesh pressed against the newly opened passage. It sends no mouth into it. The sensory hairs touch the inner surface repeatedly, each tip swelling with a clear bead.

That night, it eats as usual.

Over the following days, the power makes a second lining inside the upright.

It takes nothing from the pale fragments, under whose surfaces small animals are walking. It does not use the silver thread in the root. The material it supplies has the dull, close-grained appearance of an old seed. It can hold water, yield under pressure, and return to its former shape. Nothing in the hollow finds it appetizing.

At first the lining carries only the strain of one chamber.

Then two.

The power works slowly enough to learn when it has been wrong. A passage that ought to remain full must briefly empty. A tremor that appears wasteful is how the animal knows the water is coming. These are retained.

When the new lining can sustain the whole collection, the power leaves the animal attached.

The scars contain living passages. Some have become useful in ways neither the original makers nor the animal could have intended. To sever everything would be another kind of carelessness.

Instead, the hard ridges against its flesh become flexible. Along one side of the attachment, a seam opens that can close again.

The animal discovers it before sunset.

For several minutes nothing visible happens.

Then, beneath the vine, one blackened chamber draws back from the upright.

It draws back only the width of a root hair. Water enters the new lining. The soil beside the fern darkens on time.

The chamber presses forward again.

The animal repeats this eleven times that night.

***

The first rain comes much later.

By then the power has made one other change.

Outside the western lip, just beyond where the animal’s head has reached on previous occasions, there is a shallow overhang. It has always been there. Wind has kept its underside dry; the stone behind it is cool even at noon.

A narrow groove now connects it to the hollow.

Very little water goes that way. Enough to moisten a sheltered seam, not enough to green the plateau. The overflow reaches it only after the inner seams have received their share. At the edge of the old boundary, where the buried workings used to turn living bodies back, there is a passage no wider than the overhang.

The power has left the country beyond unchanged. It is a dry country. A creature may still find it unsuitable.

Under the overhang, however, there is somewhere to pause.

When rain strikes the hollow, the animal gathers itself.

Ropes of flesh rise through the vine. The temporary head thickens, and the dark tips divide into shining points. Leaves turn under the weight.

At the base of the upright, the old chambers begin to contract.

Then the new lining takes the pressure.

The animal climbs another inch.

Its head clears the lip. Behind it, a thick fold slips free of the roots with a sound like a wet leaf being pulled from another leaf.

It remains in that position for a long time.

Rain runs down the stalks. On the exposed plateau, drops strike hard enough to rebound as smaller drops. The animal touches one with its hairs and withdraws. It touches the stone. It pushes a lobe across the lip, where the rain has cooled the surface far below the warmth of its floor.

Below, dew gathers on the upright despite the rain.

The fern receives its water.

The animal brings more of itself over.

It is awkward. The power does not arrange its limbs for it. A gripping fold slips, finds a roughness, holds. Part of the body drains into a shape too narrow to support the head, and has to thicken again. The vine, relieved of some of its burden, lifts several leaves against the underside.

Eventually, the animal rests beneath the overhang.

Most of it is outside the hollow.

The cold makes its internal structures draw close together. For a while its skin imitates the rain-dark stone, then gives up the attempt and remains smoke-colored.

Its head turns.

From here the plateau is visible in long shelves, with rain passing over some and sunlight lying on others. There is another low rise nearby. Beyond that, the land descends a little, out of sight.

The animal stays until the rain weakens.

Then it returns.

Its passage back is quicker. It knows which roughness will hold. At the upright it presses two chambers against the flexible lining, leaves the others folded loosely beneath itself, and spreads toward the center.

That evening, larvae bring food.

The animal accepts it.

For three days it does not approach the lip.

***

The power remains long enough to see a smaller animal find the passage.

It travels at night, carrying a yellow body near its center. At the overhang it flattens itself beneath a chip of stone and stays until morning. Nothing summons it back.

Two nights later, it returns without the yellow body.

The larger animal begins visiting the overhang in dry weather. Not often. Sometimes it raises a head there; sometimes it merely lies against the cool rock. Once, it brings almost its entire body outside and leaves a narrow cord trailing through the groove. Before dawn the cord thickens, and the body follows it home.

There is still hunting in the hollow. The lizard loses another small piece of its tail and eats two of the translucent jumpers. A fold closes over a larva. Moths wear the fine dust from their wings against the stone.

The power attends to what it has changed. It makes sure the outer seam does not drain the inner ones. It makes sure the new lining can repair its own small injuries. Then it removes the need for its attention.

Nothing is placed on the rim to mark the work.

The upright remains black and waist-high beneath the vine. Its beads appear in sunlight. The hollow keeps its difficult abundance, its intervals of clean-looking rock.

One afternoon, the animal raises a head beside the fern.

While it watches the lizard, the upright begins to gather dew.

The head remains.
