The power first became noticeable as an absence of dust on the oldest resident’s dark cloth.

Dust collected on the bed, on the lamp, and in the crease of her wrist. On the cloth, nothing. She moved it to the stool. The clean square remained clean.

She put a curl of threshold wood in its center.

By morning it had unfolded. Not straightened: unfolded, separating along surfaces that had never been cut. She could see the vessels inside it, including a small brown interruption where something had begun to fail.

She sat for some time with her hands on her knees.

Then she fetched another piece.

***

For nine days the power was given things to look at.

There was a beetle whose jaws would not close, a length of insulation softening around its wire, and a tooth from one of the rollers. The millwright brought a section of his drawing and held the remaining pages out of reach.

The power did not supply the missing end.

It showed him where one of the bristle rows had been drawn backward.

The woman with the bent thumb brought nothing. She stood outside the curtain while the oldest resident examined a bearing collar, then went back to the press.

That night she woke because her hand did not hurt.

She could still feel the old injury. Her thumb would not straighten. But the deep ache that usually reached her wrist before the end of a shift had stopped.

She lay still, suspicious of having slept on the arm.

In the morning she came to the loft.

“Was that you?”

The oldest resident shook her head.

The woman looked toward the cloth.

“I need to feel if I’ve strained it.”

Something touched the base of her thumb, lightly enough that she could have mistaken it for a feeler.

She felt the difference then: the old pain was gone, but when she pressed the joint too far, it warned her.

“All right,” she said.

***

The power had come upon many lives here. It could not attend to one without finding another pressed against it.

A wall was a nursery. A nursery was a defect in a wall. The beetles ate fungus cultivated on waste from the press, and their dead fed the small mouths that widened the floor joints. Fresh air arrived as a gale because the passage had narrowed around generations of growth. The gale carried lint away, and carried some of the animals with it.

The power stayed through the weekly stoppage.

With the machinery silent, it could hear a child grinding her teeth in sleep, water moving through a stem, and the pressure behind the fourth ventilator’s closed folds.

The stem was not ill. It had been doing what a living stem does: laying down tissue around a repeated strain. Each reinforcement made the opening a little smaller. Each opening then required more force.

The oldest resident had noticed the late closure. She had not known that the same thickening was narrowing the intake.

The power laid a pattern of pale lines across her cloth.

She brought the millwright.

They studied it beneath the lamp. He went away twice for measurements. On the second return he brought the woman who spent her nights in the orchard.

“There used to be another way down,” she said.

They found its upper end under a stone trough, where a tree removed sixty years earlier had left a hollow root.

Its lower end was behind the sealed doorway.

***

They did not ring the bell.

The oldest resident stood beside it with her palm against the wall. After a while, the power let her hear what lay beyond.

There was a shallow cavity, warm and almost full of a folded body. Fluid moved slowly through it. Three hard parts touched the old door in sequence whenever a tremor passed along the bell wire.

“That’s what it’s been saying,” the millwright said.

“That’s what we’ve been hearing,” she answered.

They could learn no more without disturbing it. The creature had grown around the doorway’s iron fittings, and the oldest resident would not allow them to cut those free.

The new passage went beside the old one.

It took most of a season.

The power could have opened a shaft in an instant. Instead it held the roots alive while the millwright guided their growth around a space wide enough for a person carrying a child. The orchard woman arranged its outlet so that rain would not run down it. Beetle keepers moved trays away from a wall that would become cooler. Families argued about the landing, which occupied a useful storage recess.

For a while there were baskets everywhere.

The mill produced less. Above, those who bought its fiber received less, and the power went to them too. It did not make them generous. It showed them the failing passage clearly enough that they could no longer mistake the repairs for idleness. Most agreed to wait. For the few who would not, other arrangements had to be found.

The new stair had no machinery in it. Its gates opened from either side. Along one edge ran a narrow channel for ordinary ventilation, so the square need not wait for the great stems to open before receiving fresh air.

At its foot, the millwright fitted a shutter signal.

The oldest resident watched him test it.

“From the lofts,” she said.

He looked up.

“They must be able to see it from the lofts.”

He moved it.

***

Not everything needed a passage.

The lizard boxes needed a rough strip up one side. Once someone saw it, this was embarrassing.

The children needed somewhere to set things down without having them eaten. A shelf of fired clay was brought from above. It was blue underneath, where the glaze had run, and had to be packed carefully because the wall continued to grow.

The press tender needed another person who knew the press well enough to let her leave it for two days. This took longer than easing her hand.

The oldest resident needed help remembering.

She disliked this discovery more than she disliked the dust returning to her cloth.

The power could preserve every arrangement she had ever known, but the residents could not consult a power in the middle of a breakdown and be certain it would still be there. They began making a record.

At first the ledger filled with machinery: clearances, strains of sorting beetle, the proper smell of bearing sap. Then she dictated where water used to gather before the west loft was enlarged. She described the old ventilation failure and which curtains had lifted first.

One afternoon she remembered that a former resident had hidden money inside a stopped conduit.

They found the conduit.

Something had eaten the purse. The coins were still there.

After that, attendance at the recording sessions improved.

***

The seam animals received no promise that the floor would always be wet.

The power followed them into the thickness of the roots and found places where moisture had once escaped slowly, before repairs had sealed them. It opened a few of these again, away from load-bearing joints and electrical nests.

There were more small mouths at those places afterward. Then more things that hunted mouths.

The millwright worried.

The power showed him the roots there, sound and growing. He marked the places in his ledger and checked them every stoppage.

Under the unused rooms, the long ivory hunter found a stretch of floor with plentiful prey and no freight moving over its back. It still entered the square occasionally. The warm tubing remained on its hook.

The Landlady acquired another white mark. Two of her young settled under the new stair’s bottom tread.

No one was instructed to love a different kind of animal.

***

Toward the end of the season, the power brought the oldest resident a stone.

It was flat, gray, and large enough for both her feet. Water had worn a shallow hollow across one end. There were no fossils, jewels, or hidden markings.

She asked where it had come from.

For a moment she smelled salt and heard small stones shifting under a withdrawn wave.

“Not from the picture, then.”

No.

She had it placed beside her stool. The hems inspected it and found its underside satisfactory.

Sometimes, after the mill started again, she rested her bare feet on its cool surface. It stayed cool for a long time. She liked that it did not answer the machinery.

The power offered her a sight of the shore.

She considered this, then asked whether she could come back before the stoppage ended.

She returned with wet hems—not the animals—and a pocketful of pebbles wrapped inside her coat. She had found the distance between boats difficult to judge. The gulls had struck her as badly behaved.

For several days she told people these things.

She kept the rest.

***

When the work was finished, the power ceased keeping the cloth clean.

This was how she knew it was leaving.

She brushed the cloth, folded it, and put it beneath the lamp. There would still be things to examine. One of the roller teeth was healing unevenly; the youngest sorter’s gloves needed changing; the new air channel had attracted a moth no one recognized.

At ventilation, the four stems opened.

The noise was still tremendous, though less so than before. Curtains stirred. Hunters took their places in the current. The new shutter remained plainly visible above the square.

The oldest resident sat with her feet on the stone.

One stem closed late.

Her hand went toward her shoes, then stopped. Across the square, the apprentice had already seen the signal. He took the inspection lamp from its peg and waited for the freight gate to lift.

She watched until he reached the stem.

Then she settled back on the stool, leaving her shoes where they were.
