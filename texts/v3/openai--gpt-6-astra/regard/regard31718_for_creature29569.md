The figure on the steps was having difficulty with the bag.

It was not especially large, but its strap had caught on something beyond the opening, and each time the figure pulled, the snow behind them blew harder. Seli could see one bare hand holding the strap. The other was inside a mitten.

“Leave it a moment,” she called.

The figure looked up.

“Come up first. We’ll see to it from here.”

This was advice Seli gave often. People arriving from difficult places disliked being separated from the things they had managed to bring.

The figure loosened their grip.

Under the square, the rolling knock began.

Seli put a hand against the arch. Usually there was nothing useful to be done this way, but Oren was across the court and the steps were beginning to fold with someone still on them.

“Not yet,” she said.

And, for the first time in all the years she had tended the basin, something answered.

“Quite right.”

The voice came from the steps.

The knocking stopped. It did not fade or finish. It stopped in the middle of a blow, leaving a peculiar fullness in the air.

The figure freed the strap, climbed the remaining steps, and stood dripping on the pavement. There was snow in the folds of their coat. As Seli watched, it melted into ordinary water.

They were neither tall nor short enough to remark upon. The mitten was badly darned.

“Have you eaten?” Seli asked.

“Not here.”

She considered this answer.

“There’s batter frying.”

“Yes,” the stranger said. “I noticed.”

***

The power that had come to the town could have lifted it out of the basin.

It could have separated every root from every other root without breaking one, held the submerged houses in the sunlight, and shown Oren the whole arrangement at once. It could have found the places to which the blue bowl belonged.

These possibilities were present to it as the spoon was present on Seli’s wall: available to the hand, not therefore to be picked up.

It followed her indoors.

The stopped knock remained beneath the square.

***

Seli gave it a plate and asked whether it had stopped the basin.

“Only the part that was about to close.”

“Can you start it again?”

“Yes.”

“People will be waiting.”

“I know.”

She put down the bowl of fruit.

“Then I’d better get Oren.”

The power agreed that this would be best.

While she was gone, it sat in the room intended for the niece. A seam-crab emerged from behind a white mushroom and began to examine a drop of melted snow. The power moved its sleeve out of the way.

There was much to attend to.

The failed chamber beneath the square was trying to complete an arrival, empty its cold water, shift its weight, and respond to an old instruction to grow away from an injured root. Each action required another to have finished first. For many years the town had escaped this difficulty by doing several things badly and repairing itself afterward.

Now it had run out of room in which to be wrong.

Beyond the arch, three other landings had begun to carry part of the strain. At one of them, someone had set a hot pan on a railing and gone to find a porter. At another, a closed gate was frosting over in summer weather.

The power took the strain itself.

No light accompanied this. In Seli’s room, a second drop fell from the coat.

***

Oren arrived carrying his basket and half a folded pancake.

He listened to Seli, then to the arch, then put his hooked probe into a seam below the basin.

“Did you kill it?” he asked.

“No.”

He left the probe where it was and laid his cheek against the fibers.

For a while nobody spoke.

“It’s still trying.”

“Yes.”

“Then what’s holding it?”

“I am.”

Oren looked at the stranger for the first time with something other than professional annoyance.

“How long can you do that?”

“Long enough for you to finish eating.”

He glanced at the pancake in his hand.

“And afterward?”

“Long enough for us to look.”

***

They did not open the square.

Oren objected to opening the square even before the stranger suggested any means of looking underneath it.

“There are things attached to things,” he said.

So the power made a shallow dish of clear water show him what his fingers would have felt, had they been able to reach every passage.

It took some adjustment. At first there was too much.

“No,” Oren said. “Only this bit. And slower.”

The image settled.

He leaned over it. Seli sat beside him, knotting and unknotting a loose loop in her cord.

The town was older than either of them had understood. There were passages built around other passages that had ceased to serve any purpose. A chamber once used to warm outgoing travelers was keeping a stand of reeds alive half a court away. Two apparently separate drains were the same drain at different points in its movement.

Oren asked questions. The power answered what it knew and distinguished this from what it merely suspected.

Beneath the market square, a broad gray body shifted.

Oren went still.

“Can you show us—”

The view widened a little.

There was no clear place where the animal ended and its shelter began. Fine roots lay along its ridges. Some entered them. It was holding a portion of the mat against its underside with many small, patient movements.

Seli bent closer.

“Is it caught?”

“Not at present,” said the power. “It could leave through the western roots.”

“Does it want to?”

The power was quiet.

“I would have to ask differently.”

It let a small change of pressure pass through the water below: an opening, then a return to the previous pressure, with nothing driven toward either choice.

The gray body loosened its hold. For several minutes it rested without attachment.

Then, ridge by ridge, it took hold again.

Oren rubbed his beard.

“Leave that bit out of whatever we do.”

They did.

***

By evening, they had agreed on very little.

This was not wasted time.

Oren wanted a way to interrupt a movement before it damaged something. Seli wanted the basin to stop admitting people when the landing was unsafe. The man whose cuffs smelled of oranges wanted to know whether this meant he would sometimes be unable to get home.

“Yes,” said the power. “Unless there is somewhere safe to wait.”

“On which side?”

“Either.”

This required more discussion.

The sisters came because someone had told them the canals were being altered. They stayed after discovering that the canals were not being altered, since this seemed an opportunity to have something recorded.

One of the children asked whether the town would still move.

“Yes.”

“Will we know when?”

“Sooner than you do now.”

“How much sooner?”

They tried several intervals. The first was long enough to make everyone impatient. The second gave Oren time to get up from a kneeling position but not to collect his tools.

The third gave him time to put down his basket somewhere dry.

“That much,” he said.

The power did not make a new town beneath the old one. It gave three existing chambers room to swell without pushing against their neighbors. It loosened a ring of fibers that had been tightening for centuries. It helped the mat grow a narrow bed of pale leaves beside each busy crossing.

Before a path tilted, the leaves would turn their undersides upward.

These changes were first tried in an empty court, with Oren watching and a weighted basket standing in for a traveler. Twice he asked to have them undone.

They were undone.

What finally remained could be tended with reeds, salts, clean water, and attention. Oren made the power show him where it would fail if neglected.

By then his mother had brought supper.

She looked into the dish, pointed to a broad chamber near the yellowing pavement, and said, “There.”

“No,” said Oren.

She ate beside them anyway.

***

The arrival basin reopened after dark.

First the cold drained away. Then the arch made a low sound, less like a knock than a person setting a bowl down carefully.

At the far landing, someone shouted.

Seli stood by the rim.

“One at a time,” she called.

A porter emerged with the hot pan wrapped in his coat. Behind him came a woman furious about the delay. Seli let her finish, then asked whether anyone else was waiting in the cold.

There were two.

The stranger went down to fetch them.

***

It stayed nine days.

Not every difficulty required its abilities. It carried a basket while Oren worked. It sat beside the basin when Seli went to bathe. Once it accompanied the youngest child of the man who smelled of oranges as far as the top step, and brought him back when he asked.

On the seventh morning, Seli found it studying her cord.

“That one never came back,” she said.

She touched a knot worn dark by her fingers.

“Do you want to know where they went?”

Seli looked across the square. The pale leaves beside the crossing turned over. A moment later, water slid across the path.

“Not today.”

The power left the knot alone.

On the ninth day, it asked whether there was anything else she wanted to ask.

Seli had been thinking about this. She sat with her larger foot out of its sandal, drying a rubbed place between two toes.

“Can the basin tell someone at the other end that there’s a person here?”

“It already tells them the landing is open.”

“That isn’t what I mean.”

She showed it the loose loops, the bowl of fruit, the low room with its obstinate mushrooms.

After some work, they found a way.

***

There were still collisions, though fewer of them. The town still woke people in the night. Once, the new warning leaves turned over and stayed that way until Oren discovered a snail holding open the passage that served them.

He moved it a short distance and apologized.

The old square remained broad. Beneath it, the gray creature kept its own hours.

Seli acquired someone to take the basin two afternoons a week. This had required no miracle, although it had required several conversations and an arrangement about payment.

On one of those afternoons she sat beneath the shade tree without her cord.

Across the canal, the basin opened. She began to rise, then saw her replacement come out from under the arch carrying the fruit bowl.

Seli sat down again.

Far away, on a landing under a high roof, a traveler watched green marks gather along the rim of the water. They could not read the town’s old records. Almost nobody could.

These marks were simpler. A gardener had insisted upon that.

They meant that the steps were safe.

Below them, after a small interval, another mark appeared.

Someone was there.
