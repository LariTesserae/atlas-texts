The power first notices the yoke.

Not because it is the heaviest burden in the city. It is not. Nor because the woman carrying it has asked for help. She is thinking about the boy, and about whether there is enough fuel to soften the beans, and about a place beneath her shoulder where the wood has begun to hurt even through two layers of wool.

When she reaches her door, she lowers the vessels in the usual way: knees bent, one hand steadying the nearer lid. For several breaths afterward, she cannot straighten.

The power attends to this interval.

Then to the house. Then to the stair, the spring, the covered channel. Its attention travels beneath thresholds and through the cold joints of the masonry. It follows water backward into the mountain and labor outward through the city.

There is no lack of ingenuity here. There are places where ingenuity has been used for so long against the same difficulty that people have forgotten the difficulty might end.

The power does not mistake this for contentment.

***

That night it prevents a roof from falling.

The beam has been splitting inside its socket for three winters. Beneath it sleep four people and a dog who would otherwise wake just early enough to become frightened. The power holds the load until morning. When the household stirs, a little plaster falls onto the hearth, followed by a piece of wood that makes the fault unmistakable.

They empty the room. Neighbors bring props. The owner stands in the court with his bedding under one arm, staring at the doorway.

No one is injured.

Elsewhere, less visibly, the power keeps an infant breathing through the worst hour of a fever. It settles a loose slab before a hoof comes down on it. It gives a woman who has been coughing blood the strength to reach the healer, and gives the healer what her knowledge alone cannot supply.

These things do not wait upon a plan for the city.

***

Water requires more care.

Opening a lake above the houses would be easy. So would making snow fall for forty days. The power sees the retaining walls that would fail, the grazing grounds buried beneath a crust the animals could not break, the old channels carrying a force they were never fitted to contain.

It also sees the villages downstream.

It chooses a seam of stone below the frost, above the lowest inhabited terraces. There it makes water—not taken from another basin, not borrowed from a later year. Cool, clean water enters a cavity that has stood empty since the rock first lifted into daylight.

At first, only a damp patch appears in an unused court.

A girl notices because her ball comes back wet.

By the next morning, the patch has become a thread of water. By evening, people have brought cups, arguments, and the old keeper with his staff.

The spring strengthens slowly enough for them to work around it. When they cut a basin, it fills. When they lay an outlet, the flow finds it. It does not rise beyond what the stone can safely carry.

The owner of the neighboring storehouse immediately remembers that his grandfather’s boundary included the court.

The power has expected this.

For seven nights, everyone who sleeps within the district dreams of the same place: the spring court, empty of people, with water passing through an open doorway. Those who cannot sleep see it when they close their eyes. Those who cannot see feel cool stone beneath their hands and hear the flow.

There are only a few words.

*This water is a gift. No one has bought it from me.*

The dreams do not settle every question. Someone must clean the basin. Someone must decide where animals drink. People still disagree about paving, drains, and whether children can be trusted with the new covers.

But the storehouse owner ceases speaking of his grandfather.

Other districts find their own damp patches.

***

The power makes itself available.

Not in a palace, and not through a chosen household. Anyone may address it at the new springs. It answers without requiring a particular posture, language, or name.

At first, people mostly ask whether the water will last.

“Yes.”

Even in a poor snowfall year?

“Yes.”

What must be given in return?

“Nothing.”

This answer is repeated in the city with considerable suspicion.

People bring offerings anyway. The power asks that food be taken home or shared, and that wool remain on someone who needs it. One woman leaves a blue thread tied around a stone because gratitude, she says aloud, is her own affair.

The thread remains.

***

There are requests it can grant at once, and others it must understand.

A child asks that her grandfather never die. The power sits with them both through a long afternoon. It eases the pain in his hands. It does not tell the child that grief is good for her.

A porter asks for a different back.

The power heals the old damage, then asks about his work.

Within a month, several broad routes have resting ledges where none existed before. In two steep districts, builders work with the power to make covered lifting shafts, counterweighted and slow, large enough for water jars, sacks, or a seated person. The first is operated by a man who distrusts it so thoroughly that everyone agrees he is the right choice.

Some porters welcome the change. Others see their earnings disappearing.

They say so at the spring.

The power listens. Assistance that leaves a household without supper has not finished its work.

***

It learns the smoke.

It learns which hearths burn badly because their owners are careless, and which burn badly because the alternative is cold. With the stove-makers, it tries shapes of clay and stone, draws air through passages, warms rooms without filling them with the fire’s waste.

The first new stove cracks.

The maker is furious.

“You knew it would do that.”

“Yes,” says the power. “I should have told you sooner.”

Thereafter they work differently.

The successful design is copied without payment. Fuel lasts longer. A mother wakes one morning and realizes that she has heard her daughter sleeping, rather than coughing, through most of the night.

Beyond the walls, the power attends to animals with no owners. It does not draw every drop into the city or turn every sheltered hollow into a field. Water remains in the burrows’ country. There are slopes on which nothing new is built.

***

Not everyone wishes to stay.

The power helps a young man reach the wooded valleys he has imagined since childhood. His mother asks whether he will be happy there.

“I do not know,” it tells her. “He can come back.”

It helps people return, too.

The city changes unevenly. Some households prosper quickly. Some distrust every improvement until a cousin has tried it. There are still quarrels, badly balanced loads, songs learned from travelers, and people who insist the old water tasted better.

The woman with the yoke comes to the new spring carrying one vessel.

Her shoulder no longer hurts. There is water nearer her house, but she has come to meet someone here.

The boy arrives late. He has grown enough that his coat closes badly across the chest.

They are arguing again.

When she stands to leave, he reaches for the vessel. She keeps hold of it a moment, finishing what she has to say. Then she lets him take it.

The power watches them go up the stair.

In the emptied court, a bird lands beside the overflow and drinks.
