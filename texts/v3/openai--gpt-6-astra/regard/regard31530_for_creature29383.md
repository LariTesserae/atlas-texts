The power notices the water coming out from beneath the stone.

It is warmer than the water beyond the entrance, poorer in air, carrying the faint products of eggs and skin and a body that has been working in a space scarcely larger than itself. The cloud disperses. Beneath the slab, the animal lowers its back and begins again.

For a long while the power attends to this.

It follows the seep into the pale clay, through a seam of sand, back to a buried hollow where the water from the last rain is still gathering. It feels the weight of the slab and the thin strands holding the eggs to its underside. It knows which beads contain movement. Some do not. Those it cannot help by making the shelter better.

The animal has fed, but not enough. Beneath its skin there is a small reserve that will diminish through the days of tending. The notched foot does not hurt now. A grain lodged in the fold behind it does.

The power loosens the grain.

When the animal next moves, it falls away.

Nothing brightens. No presence fills the opening. The animal presses its foot into the mud, finding the footing slightly different, and continues lifting its body beneath the eggs.

The power could raise the whole slab. But the low roof keeps out the long bills of birds. It could deepen the pool. But a deeper pool would admit fish that now remain in the channel, and the young will need warm, shallow places when they hatch.

Instead, at the back of the shelter, where clay has closed an old passage between two stones, it makes a space no wider than a sedge stem.

Water enters slowly.

It comes from the seam of sand, cooler by a little than the pool. It passes along the side of the animal, beneath the trembling eggs, and leaves through the mouth of the shelter. There is no jet, nothing strong enough to loosen a strand. A small piece of leaf turns once and settles elsewhere.

The animal pauses.

Its throat moves. One hind foot draws inward. After a time it lifts its body again, but the intervals between movements grow longer.

Outside, a bird takes a fish from the brown margin.

The power does not turn the bird’s head away.

Beyond this pool are other slabs, other eggs, animals sheltering alone in poor hollows, young ones whose backs have only recently broadened enough to make concealment difficult. The power’s attention travels outward. It does not find an empty basin awaiting improvement. Every small difference already belongs to something.

Still, there are places where help can fit.

Under a bare shelf, a buried pocket has collapsed. The power settles its stones so that a low chamber remains, with two entrances and a roof above the usual flood. Along a route the animals already use, it steadies a leaning root mass. Leaves will lodge there as the water rises. Between the highest stones it preserves shallow passages, so that reaching refuge need not require a long crossing in the open.

It does these things in scattered places. No single shelter becomes the place toward which everything must travel.

Then it stays through the change of season.

When the pools draw down, the little seep beneath the slab continues. When the first young come free of their strands, they hang briefly in its slow passage before slipping toward the light. One settles on the parent’s head. Its tail moves beside an eye.

The adult does not know what has happened beneath the clay. It knows water against its skin, the movements of small bodies, a looseness in the space above its back where the eggs had been. Later it goes out to feed.

Not all the young survive. The power attends to them too; its care does not end where it cannot make the outcome gentle. Nor does it mistake a first act of help for all the help the basin will ever need.

For now, under the stone, there is an interval in which nothing needs pushing aside, wetting, lifting, or holding up.

The animal returns before daylight. It backs into the familiar gap and settles, flanks touching the cool sides of the chamber.

Water passes over its skin.

For the first time in many days, it sleeps without having to move the pool.
