The grazer lowers its head.

Its black mouthparts touch the line. For a moment it feeds. Then all six feet open against the floor, and the animal lifts its head so sharply that a thread of sap draws out beneath its jaw.

Under the houses, a wedge jumps from a man’s hand.

This is when the power arrives.

There is no additional brightness. The chamber already has more light than anyone can comfortably use. Nothing descends through the roof.

Instead, the wedge falls slowly enough for the man to move his fingers.

It strikes the floor and lies there.

The man looks at his hand. Beside him, his sister is still pulling on a rope. He has time to tell her to stop.

*

The power has been attending to the place for some time.

At first it understood the immense plant more readily than the houses. The plant’s needs traveled through its tissues: water drawn upward, sugars descending from a forest spread beneath an unfamiliar sky, the long redistribution of weight as a new chamber opened. Its injuries were distinct. So were its efforts to close them.

The houses were harder to understand.

They were made of dead pieces but contained warmth. They moved without growing. The small beings inside them cut the living floor, then carefully stopped the cuts. They carried colonies of other life against their bodies and fed those colonies before feeding themselves.

The power watched a woman scrape the last paste from a tray and leave a little at the bottom.

It watched her give what she had scraped away to someone else.

After that, it attended more closely.

It learned that the plant could lose a surface larger than a human country and remain healthy. It learned that a person could lose a strip of tissue narrower than a leaf vein and die.

Neither scale made the other unimportant.

Now, beneath the settlement, growth has reached the place where its pressure must be released. A sheet of old tissue is about to lift. The houses stand across its hinge.

The power holds that hinge.

Elsewhere, far below, it permits a closed fold to ease into an unoccupied hollow. The pressure travels there instead. A sleeping silver animal wakes as its seam exhales, shifts beneath its skin, and settles again.

The power can hold the ground for a while without injuring what bears it.

It does.

*

Everyone feels the interruption differently.

The oldest woman has been expecting a tremor. Its absence troubles her more than its arrival would have. She kneels, pulls off a glove, and lays her whole palm beside the green line.

“There,” she says.

“What?”

She moves her hand.

“Still there.”

Usually a pressure travels. This one remains beneath her palm, immense and checked.

The girl who found the line is standing beside the food trays. Through the soles of her boots she feels something like a held breath.

She looks toward the grazer.

It has begun walking away, slowly, along the outer edge of the settlement.

Then a voice speaks near her, at the ordinary distance of someone helping with a fastening.

“I am keeping it from opening.”

She turns. No one is there.

“For how long?” asks the oldest woman.

The voice has reached her too.

“Until you are clear. I cannot leave this part unmoving without harming what grows beneath it.”

The woman keeps her palm on the floor.

“Can we take the houses?”

“Yes.”

“The rack?”

“Yes.”

She looks toward the low covers beyond the houses.

There is a pause.

“I can keep that ground together,” the voice says. “You may leave them, or carry them. Neither will hurry you.”

The old woman sits back on her heels.

Around her, people begin asking questions at once.

The power listens to all of them. It answers in turn.

*

There are things it could do quickly that it does not yet understand well enough to do kindly.

It could lift every house. But one sleeping platform is tied to a runner in a way that will pull the house apart if the load comes from above. A sick man lies inside another, and his daughter wants to be beside him when he is moved. Under the third house are two trays no one has remembered.

So the power asks where to take the weight.

At first the people have difficulty answering. They have never been asked such a question by something that can take any weight at all.

Then a builder says, “Not there. Along the rib. The whole rib.”

The house rises less than the thickness of a finger.

Every rope goes slack.

The builder makes a small, involuntary sound.

They slide the hauling straps into place. When they pull, the house comes after them with the weight of an empty basket.

Someone laughs. Someone else tells him to save his breath, then begins laughing too.

The forgotten trays are found.

The sick man sleeps through the first part of the journey, with his daughter sitting beside him and a cup held steady on her knee.

*

Ahead, the grazer reaches a narrow saddle between two folds.

It could cross. The houses could not.

Beyond it lies an old island, broad enough for the settlement, with food-bearing layers below its dull surface. No one has used it because the saddle has been opening for years. Its far lip is almost out of reach.

The power examines the island.

It does not merely look at its surface. It follows the burdens beneath it, finds which layers are dying and which are only resting, checks the long fibers by which it hangs. It attends to the animals in its crevices and the little lives in its pores.

Then it tells the people what it knows.

“This ground will remain quiet for many of your lifetimes. The crossing will not. I can make a way over it.”

“What sort of way?” asks the builder.

The power shows her.

Along the saddle, several folded ribs lift out of their sockets. They curve toward one another, slowly enough that the silver animals run clear. Their ends meet. New tissue knits across the joins.

The builder watches the fibers arrange themselves.

“Across the grain there,” she says, pointing.

The power had intended to do this, but waits while she explains. She knows something about loaded runners that it has not learned from holding up continents of leaf.

The bridge grows a shallow lip on either side.

Underneath, separate strands take the load. If one should fail, the others will hold.

The grazer crosses before anyone else, pausing halfway to bite the parapet.

“Don’t,” says the woman whose pan it once ruined.

It continues chewing.

*

By the time the last house reaches the island, the people are thirsty.

They have been thirsty for much of the day. This had seemed too ordinary to mention among their other troubles.

The power notices the care with which a father tips three drops into his child’s mouth, then puts the stopper back without drinking.

It asks about the vessels.

They show it the vessels.

Here, too, it takes advice. An open pool would freeze. A constantly breathing seam would spend too much of the plant’s water into the chamber. A deep cut could damage the island.

Together they choose a place beneath an overhang.

The power draws a narrow channel from a supply that passes far below, carrying more water than the settlement will ever need. At its end it makes a small, yielding lip. Pressed upward, the lip releases water. Released, it closes.

The water is clear and cool.

The woman who tastes it first stands with her cup against her mouth for so long that someone asks whether anything is wrong.

She shakes her head.

They fill every vessel. Then they drink again.

One man washes both hands, using more water than he means to. He looks around afterward, embarrassed.

No one reproaches him.

The power makes an overflow lower down, where shallow creases can stay damp without flooding. By nightfall, silver animals have found it.

*

Only when everyone is across does the old settlement begin to open.

From the new island they watch the floor rise beneath the empty places where their houses stood.

The movement is slow at first. A rack they chose to leave tips, catches, and spills its unseasoned ribs. The green line widens. Light enters it.

Beyond the former houses, the grave ground remains whole.

It rises as one piece, gently inclined, with its covers undisturbed. The power settles it against an older fold where the people will be able to reach it. There is a path between that fold and the bridge.

The girl’s father has left markers, as he promised.

For several minutes the girl can still see one of them.

Then the opening sheet turns it away.

She cries, unexpectedly and furiously. The move has gone well. Everyone keeps saying so. Her house is behind her, and her possessions are inside it, and she cannot explain what is missing.

The power does not explain it either.

Her father sits down beside her. After a little while, she leans against him.

*

Later, the people ask whether the power will stay.

“Yes,” it says. “Though I must learn the rest of this place as well.”

They ask for many things. Some are urgent. Some contradict one another. One person wants the bridge wider; another fears that a wide bridge will bring too many strangers. Someone asks about a traveler who has missed two circuits.

The power begins looking for the traveler at once.

There are also arguments about what to call it.

It leaves those unfinished.

Before the people sleep, it asks the oldest woman to show it the witness-pegs.

She empties them into her lap.

Together they consider how much warning a floor ought to give.

*

In the seasons that follow, the place does not cease growing.

New valleys open. Membranes unfold in the high air. Rain moves across distant basins, and the grazers travel toward it.

But the old inhabited islands begin to change in one small respect.

Before pressure becomes dangerous, a ring of tissue around each witness-hole turns dark. At night it is plainly visible. By day a hood will reveal it.

The warning comes early enough to free the runners, pack the cultures, and argue about what to take. It is not infallible. The power says so, and the keepers of the floor continue their work.

There are water lips along some of the longer routes now. Shelters stand near them, built by travelers from ribs the power holds while the joints are fitted.

News of these things travels in narrow sample cases.

Sometimes it arrives before belief.

Sometimes a company reaches a place where there should be no crossing and finds a bridge with shallow sides, marked at either end by the hand of someone they know.

And in one settlement a child is still learning to take a splinter without lifting a strip.

The knife is blunt. The lesson takes time.

An adult sits beside her, close enough to cover her hand.

When she makes the cut correctly, she holds up the little piece for inspection.

Nothing enormous happens.

She is allowed to be pleased.
