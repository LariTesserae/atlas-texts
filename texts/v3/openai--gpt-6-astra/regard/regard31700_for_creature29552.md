The power did not enter the hollow as light. There was already light enough to lead a moth into the wrong opening.

It attended first to the moth beneath the bearing lip.

One wing had lengthened properly. The other lay against the iron, held at an angle by a grain caught between its fringe and the roughness of the casting. The moth moved its feet. Each movement brought its body closer to the obstruction.

The power could have carried it into the aisle. But the aisle offered a moving belt, an open bin, a window that did not open.

It followed the possible passages farther than the moth could feel.

Beyond the low window, a gap beneath the sill opened onto a dry recess in the outer wall. There was room there to hang, and no web across the entrance. At evening, air would move through it toward a stand of grasses.

The power shifted the caught grain.

For a moment nothing happened. The moth remained where it was, its feet gripping the same irregularities. Then it stepped backward.

A little air entered beneath the bearing, not enough to disturb the web or lift the flour. The moth turned into it. Along the stone, where the way outward narrowed, a loose chip settled on three points instead of two. Beneath it there was now a passage with sufficient height.

The moth went under.

Its wings brushed neither side.

The power stayed with it until it reached the recess. Then it stayed a little longer, because reaching shelter and being able to use it were not always the same thing.

Inside the hollow, the flour continued to fall.

It would have been easy to stop the falling. Easy, too, to fill every lean place, to lay food before each mouth. The power watched what abundance did here. Beneath the washer, bodies already pressed against bodies. Eggs lay beside eggs. More food would become more animals, and later there would be more mouths than the gift could satisfy.

Nor was every mouth waiting for flour.

At the low web, a mite had caught two legs. The spider approached. The power attended to both, without finding that attention made their needs agree.

It did not call the capture good.

It did not turn away.

There were things it could mend without handing the cost to another hungry body. It began with those.

The twice-repeated strike beyond the wall came from a fastening that had worked loose. Each blow travelled into the stone, disturbing shelters built against a gentler motion. On the next morning’s round, the millwright paused beside it. He had passed the noise for weeks. This time he heard the difference between the running mill and the part hurting itself.

He stopped the machinery and tightened the fastening.

When the rollers resumed, the husks still trembled, but they no longer jumped. A larva held its place without drawing its whole length taut. The spider’s outer attachment lasted through the shift.

Under the washer, a roof of compacted meal had begun to sag. The power did not make it permanent. It let a narrow fissure open where there were no eggs, and the loose flour drained slowly through. The roof came down by degrees. Those beneath it had time to leave.

The beetle with the broken antenna crossed the spill, corrected its course, and disappeared behind the copper wire.

The power learned that beetle’s route. It learned others. Not because knowing a route conferred ownership, but because an alteration made for one traveller could close the way for someone smaller.

So its work was patient.

It left the mortar crack open. It kept water from entering when rain found a fault beneath the window. It preserved the low, dry passage through which moths could leave, and watched to see whether it became a place where predators gathered. When a husk lodged there, it waited until the animals sheltering beneath it had moved before loosening it.

There was no single morning on which the hollow became safe.

Things were still eaten. Some eggs did not hatch. The old wing cases went on breaking beneath the feet of the living. The power remained troubled by what it had not yet learned to help.

But fewer moths dried against the bearing lip.

Fewer silk roofs failed in the heavy knock.

And when the mill eventually stopped for three days, and the ordinary white weather ceased, a little of the meal caught uselessly on an upper ledge came down—not all at once, and not enough to bury anyone.

At the mouth of the hollow, the balancing grain turned again.

Below it, bodies passed in both directions.

There was room.
