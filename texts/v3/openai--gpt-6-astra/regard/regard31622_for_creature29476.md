The power does not open the hatch.

For a long time its attention is no more present in the room than the daylight before anything crosses the glass. The animal feeds. A softened fiber catches between its front limbs and is turned, released, caught again. Water passes through the hollow beside its flank.

There is much to learn before changing anything.

The roar, for instance, is not an injury merely because another creature would find it unbearable. The bare middle of the floor is not a failure of vegetation. The animal’s long pauses are not necessarily fear, and the room’s smallness is measured partly by the size of what lives there.

The power watches through several rounds of feeding and withdrawal. It follows the water backward through the pressure-relief joint, into passages where the warmth and dissolved air acquire their particular balance. It follows the waste heat through the wall. It attends to the film on the ceramic, the animals in the moss, the small exchanges by which the ledge remains a ledge rather than becoming either a heap or a clean shelf.

It also attends to the injured foot.

The edge is healed. There is no soreness left in it. The animal braces with three fingers, adjusts its weight, and reaches what it was reaching for. The power leaves the foot alone.

What concerns it is elsewhere.

In an office above the pumping floor, a maintenance schedule includes the old service shaft. The wired glass is to be replaced by an opaque cover. This is sensible work by the standards of the office: the shaft serves nothing, and the glass has begun to separate from its frame.

The power makes no one foolish or forgetful. When the frame is inspected, the vegetation below is noticed. A light is lowered, briefly. Someone says that a clear replacement would cost very little more.

The new glass admits much the same light as the old. Its fittings will last longer.

There are other such provisions. The hatch is marked against sealing. The small continuous discharge is recorded rather than mistaken for a leak. None of this requires the animal to be caught, photographed on a ruler, or given a name before its water is allowed to continue.

The power is not satisfied with protecting a dependence on machinery that will eventually stop.

It looks beneath the works, where water moves without impellers. There is a route by which a very small part of that water can reach the old joint. Not enough to alter the room now. Enough to sustain its shallow passage when the pumps are idle. The route needs little opening, but the opening must be made slowly; mineral content matters, and so does temperature.

Over many months, the water changes by less than the difference between one ordinary day and another.

The animal continues to feed. The moss retains its inhabitants. At the margins, where the power’s first calculation proves slightly wrong, it waits and adjusts. Being able to do a thing does not spare it the work of noticing what the thing does.

Beyond the plugged socket there is another small hollow, dry and inaccessible. The power lets a seam connect it to the current. Silt begins to lodge there. Later, a film grows on the wetted surface.

It does not move the animal into this additional shelter. For some time the animal does not enter it at all.

Then, during a pause in feeding, one of the fine hind limbs finds the seam. The animal turns. Its head passes into the opening, then withdraws. It rests outside with its underside in the water.

That is enough for that day.

There is no blessing laid upon its back, no new understanding placed behind its small eyes. It does not become grateful. It remains capable of drawing away.

The power keeps its attention where the animal cannot put its own: on the decisions upstairs, on the slow failure of materials, on the difference between a stable refuge and a trap whose conditions have merely not changed yet.

Beneath the ledge, the front limbs stir.

A grain is turned and found inedible. The animal lets it go. The current takes it toward the slot beneath the hatch, and nothing prevents it from leaving.
