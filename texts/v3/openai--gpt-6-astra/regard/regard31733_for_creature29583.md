The power came to the mountain while Sera was sitting on the floor.

It had no difficulty hearing the flute. It could have heard a hair falling against the far side of the moon, if there had been a moon with someone on it losing hair. But hearing a thing was not the same as knowing how it sounded to the person who was making it.

So it listened through the room.

It heard the girl’s breath run short. It heard the cloth fail, very slightly, to keep the glass from answering. Through Sera’s bare heels it felt the pressure beneath the house. It noticed her knee, which she had straightened and bent twice without finding a comfortable position.

The girl missed a note, went back, and missed it again.

The power waited.

***

There were many things it could have done to the mountain.

Stopping the water was among them. So was moving the city to a plain beside a mild river, with deep soil and trees that needed no committees.

It considered the foundations, the warm factories, the nests behind the falls. It considered a woman who had spent thirty years arranging her kitchen and did not want a mild river where her street ought to be.

It did not move the city.

Nor did it decide that the residents’ ingenuity made their suffering necessary. The mirrors on the prams were clever. They were also an answer to something that had hurt people.

For a while the power went about finding out which was which.

It watched hands. It read the registry of structures that must remain standing. It followed the water through chambers whose shapes had been entered incorrectly on every surviving plan. It visited the rooms advertised as quiet and stayed through the hours when the freight lifts were loaded.

Beneath one bed it found fourteen folded pieces of cloth, inserted over several years. The woman sleeping there had ceased filing complaints because the landlord charged for inspections that found nothing wrong.

The power spent longer in that room than in the turbine halls.

***

Sera met it three mornings later.

It was waiting outside her shop, dressed in unremarkable clothes, holding a slate.

I WOULD LIKE TO PAY FOR SOME REPAIRS.

She indicated the notice giving her rates.

The stranger read it.

MORE THAN THAT, it wrote.

Sera took the slate.

HOW MANY ROOMS?

The stranger hesitated, which she later regarded as a good sign.

I DON’T KNOW YET.

Inside, it explained enough.

Sera heard it out with her arms folded. When it said what it could do, she asked it to mend the cracked handle of her tea strainer.

It did.

She examined the handle, then the mesh.

“Why not make a new one?”

“You asked me to mend this one.”

She put it down.

“And can you do a floor?”

“Yes.”

“Without putting the vibration somewhere else?”

“Yes.”

She unfolded her arms.

“Well,” she said. “That would be something.”

***

The first material arrived in a shallow wooden box.

It was dark gray, slightly yielding, and could be cut with the tools Sera already owned. Pressure passed through it. Vibration did not, except in amounts that could be adjusted by changing its thickness.

Iven held a piece against his teeth.

“Don’t,” Sera said.

He took it out and asked three questions in rapid succession.

The power answered two. The third concerned what would happen after eighty years under a loaded corner.

“It will retain its properties,” it said.

“That isn’t a test,” said Sera.

“No,” said the power. “It isn’t.”

It put the box back on the bench.

They spent six weeks testing it anyway.

There were loads, immersion baths, hot mineral solutions, cold freshwater, badly fitted bolts, and an experiment Iven performed when Sera was out, which damaged a vise. They discovered that the material made an excellent supporting block and a poor doorstop: a door resting against it could still move slowly enough to trap a person in a passage.

That went into the instructions.

The power supplied more. When asked where it could be obtained after the power left, it showed them how to make it.

The process required heat, pressure, and several common mineral deposits.

For once, the city had everything it needed.

***

The repairs began in the worst rooms.

Finding those rooms required more work than finding the material.

Some tenants could not take time off to admit a repairer. Some did not believe a free inspection would remain free. Some had hearing so diminished that they described a room as quiet while its floor kept them awake.

The first notices were written too formally. The second made the whole undertaking sound like a warning about structural collapse.

Sera’s sister rewrote the third.

The power paid for training, wages, access, replacement crockery, and the hours people had to miss at work. It paid without demanding that anyone stand beside the finished room and look grateful.

Sera remained busy.

“There goes your ambition,” she told Iven, as they carried tools into a house whose inner wall had been attached to the outer one in eleven places.

“No,” he said. “There goes yours.”

She laughed so loudly that the tenant, waiting beyond the second door, looked up.

***

The city did not become silent.

Its crossings still used lamps. Public conversation still passed from hand to hand. There were people who wanted quieter streets, people who feared losing the changes by which they knew the works, and people who wanted both.

The power did not ask them to settle this in a single meeting.

It helped the pressure offices map the buried chambers. It marked uncertainty where even its drawings would become uncertain as the stone changed. It made instruments that could show a tired gatekeeper what his palm had felt and could disagree with him without requiring anyone to call him a liar.

At the lower works, a proposed outlet was moved after a keeper brought in her private inspection plan.

The official drawing had shown an empty ledge.

Hers did not.

***

There were smaller offers.

Sera was told that her knee could be repaired. She asked whether she would have to stop work.

“For four days.”

“Two.”

“That would be a different repair.”

She considered this.

“After the school recess.”

It waited until then.

Others asked about their hearing. Not everyone wanted the same thing. One man wanted to hear his wife but not recover a particular whistle from the machinery that he remembered with loathing. A woman asked whether the sound inside her ear could go away while everything else stayed as it was.

The power took these questions seriously.

It did not make private changes to sleeping people.

There were refusals, postponements, and people who returned much later with someone to help them ask.

***

What surprised Sera was that relief could make a person angry.

A woman stood in her repaired bedroom and looked at the water in a glass.

It did not move.

For a while she said nothing. Then she began describing the years she had lived there: where she had slept, what she had put under the bed, how often she had been told she was unusually sensitive.

Sera put her tools away slowly.

There was no use pointing out that the room was better now. The woman knew. That was why she was angry.

Afterward, Sera told the power there should be someone people could talk to, if they wished.

It arranged that too.

***

By the following wet season, Sera could have taken a month away.

The difficulty was still the snails.

Her sister would trust no one who regarded them as interchangeable. Iven offered and was rejected after referring to the breeding trays as “the large ones.”

Eventually a retired baker qualified. He kept written feeding records and had no objection to names.

Sera went down to the lowland with her daughter.

On their first afternoon, rain approached through the trees. They stood beneath the lodging-house awning and heard it arrive by stages.

Her daughter asked whether they ought to go inside.

“Not yet,” said Sera.

***

Back on the mountain, the power continued its work without becoming the subject of every conversation.

There were still quarrels about rent and disputes over the courtyard trees. Someone invented a new way to install a cupboard incorrectly. The pressure offices were faster in some matters and stubbornly slow in others.

In the district of three trees, the dead tree remained. Removing it was now affordable, but the residents had not agreed to do so.

On a fourth-floor balcony, a man read the committee’s annual letter and went to fetch his long pole.

Below him, workers crossed the wet square with their elbows close to their sides. Warm water brightened the steps. Behind the falls, something small pressed its belly to the stone.

And in thousands of rooms, when people set a cup down, it stayed where they had put it.
