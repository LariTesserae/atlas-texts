The first instrument to register the attention is in Saret’s ninth inspection room.

It measures the distance between two points in the rock. The points have been approaching one another for longer than Saret has been responsible for the room. Eventually the instrument will have to be moved.

One morning, beside its familiar descending trace, there is a second line.

Saret checks the stylus. She checks the paper, the housing, and the little leveling feet. Then she puts her hand against the wall.

Something puts its attention against the other side.

Not pressure. The rock does not move.

She takes her hand away.

After a while she puts it back.

The question arrives through her palm. It is not in words, but neither is it vague. It has the courteous shape of a person waiting outside a room whose purpose they do not know.

May I?

Saret looks at the instrument.

“Not through that,” she says. “We need the record.”

The second line ends.

***

The power could have understood the plateau from a distance.

It could have counted the occupied pores in every wall, followed the chemistry inside the pale shells, traced the great faults down beneath the lower workings. It could have known where each cable would eventually part.

But knowing what held a world together was not quite the same as knowing what its inhabitants wanted kept.

It had seen Saret watch the roof animal depart. It did not yet know whether she was afraid for it, fond of it, or simply interested.

So it came to the west courtyard in a form that could rest against a bench.

It had no face at first. Saret found this difficult to speak to, so it acquired one: not a copy of anyone, and not a particularly beautiful face. Its eyes did not shine.

She offered it the warming rail.

It accepted.

For some time they stood with their forearms beside one another.

“What is wrong?” it asked.

“With what?”

The power considered this.

Saret waited. She was better disposed toward it after it failed to produce an immediate answer.

***

It did not bring rain.

Later, when it learned what rain would do to the exposed food cultures and to the breathing seams of the small animals, it was glad it had not mistaken the absence of a familiar substance for a calamity.

It did not cool the houses, brighten the nights, fill the empty municipal reaches, or make the elevator cages move quickly.

Some cages could safely move more quickly. Others could not. Some passengers would have welcomed a shorter journey. Others needed the long ascent for their internal reservoirs to adjust.

These things required separate inquiries.

Its first work was undertaken at Saret’s request and occupied less than a palm’s breadth.

The west sill had become dangerous. One end would soon break beneath someone’s foot. The power showed her the hollow underneath.

“I know about that,” she said.

It showed her the fracture above the hollow.

She bent farther.

“Oh.”

Together they lifted the sill. The chipped animal closed itself so firmly that it resembled an inconvenient piece of rubble.

The power asked before touching it.

Saret moved it with both hands.

They repaired the stone while leaving the hollow, its narrow entrance, and the strip of mineral the animal had been rasping. Saret tested the finished sill with her heel.

“Will that hold?”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

The answer was very long.

“Put it in the maintenance book,” she said.

***

News traveled faster than the trams.

By the third evening the courtyard contained a survey clerk, two physicians, a woman with a failed culture tray, an advocate carrying six generations of deeds, and a child whose toy had gone over the escarpment.

The child was attended to first, not because the power considered the toy the greatest matter, but because the child had been waiting longest.

The toy was lodged on a shelf well below the parapet.

Beside it lay three others.

The power brought all four up and left them at the retrieval office, where ownership could be established without inaugurating a new system of justice.

The physicians stayed until morning.

They had brought records of an illness in which the yielding plates beneath the shoulders adhered to one another. Heat relieved it at first. Later heat only hurt.

The power could help.

It explained what it proposed to change, and what it did not yet understand about the treated person’s sensations. One physician went to fetch a colleague. The other remained with her hands flat on the table.

“My mother has this,” she said eventually.

The power waited while she decided whether she wished to say more.

***

There was no single day on which everything became different.

There was a day when a man who had not folded his legs unaided for nine years rested against a public bench. He stayed there through three trams, becoming increasingly annoyed when acquaintances congratulated him.

There was a day when the lower workings received a warning of a failure that their instruments could not yet detect. The warning included measurements, a way to check them, and enough time to bring everyone out.

There was a season when no household had to choose between warming its sleeping room and keeping its food cultures alive.

The engine caretakers argued fiercely over the new equipment.

This pleased the power less than Saret said it should.

“If we were afraid of you,” she explained, “they would have approved it already.”

The equipment was revised.

***

Beyond the inhabited districts, its work became enormous.

It entered places where a mistake would propagate quietly for generations: the deep foundations, the aging shaft walls, the seams along which whole neighborhoods rested. It learned to distinguish a useful movement from a dangerous one. The plateau still expanded in the morning. The engines still reversed at dusk.

Where it strengthened the rock, it left marks that surveyors could read.

Where its own continued attention was necessary, it said so.

It taught crews how to inspect what it had done and how to maintain those parts that could be maintained. It did not put a graceful, incomprehensible object at the heart of every essential service and ask the inhabitants to trust that it would remain benevolent forever.

There were many nights of instruction.

There were manuals with mistakes in them.

Saret found two.

***

It also spent time with the animals.

The pale colonies did not need to become people to warrant its care. Nor did the black hunters have to cease being hunters.

But a thousand lives could collect on a warm cabinet that was due to be taken apart, and the old removal process killed most of them. The power helped devise a temporary surface whose warmth and mineral flavor drew them away before the crew arrived.

The crews called these surfaces waiting rooms.

Their occupants sometimes declined to leave.

The broad roof animals proved harder to understand. Their injuries were legible, their pleasures less so. The power watched them for a long time before offering anything.

One old adult accepted a repair to a stiffened membrane.

Another folded its limbs tightly and would not permit an approach.

It was left its clear path across the roof.

***

Saret did not ask about her brother until the beginning of the cool season.

She had taken the accounts to the wall chambers. On returning, she found the power sitting in the court with her niece’s younger child, examining a wrapper that had failed to release its wafer.

Later, when the child had gone indoors, she asked what it could do for the dead.

It did not answer quickly.

She noticed that.

There were things it could discover from a body. There were things it could infer from all the traces a life had left. But it had found no way to bring her brother back and be certain it had brought *him*, rather than someone made to satisfy the ache of his absence.

It would not conceal that difference from her.

Saret rested her hand on the bench.

The power kept its own hand still.

After a while she said, “He would have asked you a terrible number of questions.”

“I would have liked that.”

“Yes,” she said. “Until he started again.”

***

Her niece went to see the settlement on the second rise.

Not to move there. Not yet.

There was reliable care for the children now, and someone qualified to take Saret’s duties if she became ill. The appointment had long since expired, but the settlement still needed people with her niece’s training.

She returned with samples of a soft lining manufactured there and a complaint about the public warming rooms.

For several meals, the family discussed something other than whether leaving was possible.

Saret disliked two of the proposed arrangements.

Her niece disliked three.

The papers came out of the drawer.

***

In time, the power was asked why it remained.

By then it was attending to workings far beneath the dust, to solitary houses beyond the maintained tram lines, to creatures whose whole inhabited world was the underside of a single stone. Its attention was not scarce in the way a caretaker’s hours were scarce.

Still, it sometimes came to the west court and used only the little part of itself that could sit beside Saret.

“Is there more to do?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“There always is.”

“Yes.”

Above the elevator houses, a roof animal opened its membrane. An old repair ran pale across three of the ribs.

Saret stopped sorting her gauge strips.

The animal stepped outward. For a moment its weight drew it below the roofline. Then the membrane filled, and it rose through the warm air.

Beside her, the power watched.

It had already checked the repaired rib. It knew the strength of the rising air.

It watched anyway.
