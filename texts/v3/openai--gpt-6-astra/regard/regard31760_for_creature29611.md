The power noticed the pause before she bent over the fastening.

It did not mistake the pause for the whole of her life. There were reasons to finish a knot. There were also knots which, once finished, were followed by another knot, and another, until the boat had gone.

It watched her test the lashing. It watched her sister turn back toward the doorway, already distracted by a man approaching with one hand cupped in the other.

Then it came down to the landing.

It took a shape that could use the paths: a person of no remarkable height, with empty hands and feet that needed placing carefully. Its first act was to stand aside while two people carried the long object from the boat. This proved to be a timber, dark with seawater, which had generated considerable interest.

The power did not bring trees to the skyline. It had seen the films beneath the water, the grazers on the stone, the shellfish opening where warm and cold water met. The bay was not waiting to be made into another kind of country.

Nor did it quiet the birds.

At the top of the landing, it met the sister.

She had finished the trap. She had also washed her hands, because she did not want to arrive looking as though she had run out of the house at the first glimpse of a boat. One thumb was still marked by the pressure of the wet strip.

“Have you come far?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Along which coast?”

“Not along the coast.”

She looked at it more carefully, then moved past. The people in the boat had answers of a more useful kind.

The power followed at a distance and listened.

The boat would leave in three days. It would visit two settlements she knew by name, then cross a stretch of open water to one she did not. The crew wanted another person who could mend lashings and work traps. They had little room. They were willing to take her.

Their answers about the soft ground were less satisfactory than she had hoped. One person said it was soft everywhere. Another said that this was nonsense, and described a slope on which he had fallen and broken a tooth.

She asked to see the tooth.

He showed her.

This reassured her more than the first account.

***

By evening, the town knew there was something unusual about the stranger.

A roof slab had broken while being lifted. It had begun to fall toward the leg of the person below it, and then had remained where it was, tilted in the air.

The stranger asked where they wanted it put.

There was disagreement. Even with a broken slab suspended above them, the owners could not immediately decide whether it could still be used.

The stranger waited.

Afterward, people brought requests.

Some were urgent. A child had a cut that had swollen beyond the household’s ability to care for it. A diver had been unable to take a full breath for many days. The power helped them first, with their permission, in a room crowded by relatives.

The woman who removed glass watched closely. When it was finished, she asked questions that took longer to answer than the healing had taken.

Other requests required more listening.

The old man wanted the landing restored to his household.

The neighbors wanted the stranger to understand that there was nothing to restore.

The power sat where everyone could see its hands and asked them to begin with the last arrangement they had all accepted. This did not produce a quick result. By the time they had finished, the stranger had been accused twice of favoring the other side.

The agreement they eventually made allowed passage at certain times and required both houses to maintain the steps. Nobody liked all of it. The old man liked being asked which part he objected to, rather than being told afterward that matters had been settled.

***

The power stayed.

It could have set the whole town right according to a single idea, but each doorway opened onto people who would have to live with what that meant.

When it asked about noise, a fish worker said she would give a great deal for somewhere she could hear herself think. Her companion said that a silent house would make him afraid something had happened to the springs.

They argued about this until the power asked whether the quiet needed to occupy every house.

There was an unused chamber above a dressing terrace. Its roof was sound, but its approach was awkward and it had no convenient outflow. With the owners’ agreement, the power made a place there where the walls did not pass on the sound of the bay.

The doorway remained open. From inside, one could still see birds landing on the opposite parapet.

At first, people entered, stood uneasily, and came out again.

Then someone brought work that required counting. Two people used it for a conversation they had been postponing. A woman slept there through most of an afternoon while her brother sat outside and turned visitors away.

The old man tried it and found no particular advantage. He preferred the terrace, where more things happened within sight.

The sister went in once. Without the uproar, she could hear the small catch in her own breathing.

She stayed until it settled.

***

Food occupied more of the power’s attention than anyone expected.

It watched a good catch become a hurried day, and a hurried day become an exhausted evening. It watched people continue cutting after their hands had begun to slip.

At several households’ request, it made close-fitting storage recesses in which food stayed cold. The cold remained inside the stone; the channels beneath the houses kept their warmth. Each recess could be returned to ordinary stone by lifting out a small white wedge.

The people tested them cautiously.

They put in fish they could afford to lose. They argued over smells. They made the stranger open one and explain again why the wall outside it was not cold.

When they trusted the recesses, they did not all use the time gained in the same way. Some caught more fish. Some slept. One household held a meal at which nothing had to be eaten merely because it would otherwise spoil before morning.

The woman who removed glass finally allowed two other people to sit beside her and learn. She had resisted this before. Teaching took time, and they were slow, and a person with glass in his foot generally preferred her hands.

Now she could send away anyone unwilling to let the learners try.

Her sister noticed that she did so with considerable pleasure.

***

On the second evening, the woman asked the power whether her sister would be safe.

They were sitting in the doorway. Inside, the younger child was trying to fit a crab leg to a shell it had never belonged to.

“Not entirely,” the power said.

“Could you make her?”

“I could prevent a great many things from reaching her. I would have to keep deciding which things she should be allowed to meet.”

The woman picked at a rough place on the door frame.

“The boat?”

“I have looked at it. There is a fastening below the stern that should be replaced. I told the crew.”

“Should?”

“They replaced it.”

She nodded.

After a while she said, “She thinks I don’t want her to go.”

“Do you?”

“No.”

The power waited.

“I don’t want her not to go, either.”

From inside came a small, irritated sound. The crab leg had broken.

The woman rose to see what could be done.

***

The sister did not ask for certainty.

She asked whether the household would manage, then became angry with herself for asking a stranger what she could ask at home.

So she asked at home.

The answers were uneven. The old man said they had managed before she was born, which was not helpful. The younger man named the work he could take on. Her sister named some more, then stopped and admitted that she would miss having someone who noticed things without being told.

The child asked whether she was taking her sleeping hide.

She said yes.

This made the departure real in a way the boat had not.

They sat close and worked through the following days: traps, meals, the cleaning of the channel under the third chamber. The power was not present. They had asked it to leave them alone for this.

***

On the morning of departure, the woman carried another bird out by its feet.

She released it over the water and watched it return to the roofs. Then she went inside and lifted one end of her sister’s bundle.

At the landing, the crew had already begun making room.

There was no promise that the sister would love the other places. She might find the cold miserable. The ground might disappoint her. She might come back angry at having wanted so much from a description.

The power had offered her a way to send word home from any shore, and a passage back if she needed one. She had accepted both. Neither required her to return.

Her sister checked the bundle’s fastening.

For a moment it seemed she would undo it and tie it better. Instead she pressed it once with her thumb and passed it down.

The boat left with the usual difficulty of boats leaving: a misplaced foot, an instruction repeated too loudly, a pole catching where it should have slid free.

From the water, the sister could see her household above the landing. The younger child was on someone’s shoulders. The old man had come down farther than was comfortable for him and would complain about the climb.

Her sister raised a palm.

Wait.

The boat was already moving, but she leaned toward them.

Her sister touched her own ear, then pointed at her.

Tell me.

The sister nodded. She kept watching until the passage between the houses narrowed, and the people at its end became difficult to distinguish from the pale stone.
