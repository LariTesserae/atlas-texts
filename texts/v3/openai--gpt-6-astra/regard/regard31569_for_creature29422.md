The power notices the shrimp going back into the water.

It notices the woman’s wrist turning the bucket, the brief interruption in her work. It notices that one shrimp catches for a moment on the rim and that she waits.

It does not take this as evidence that the place needs no help.

By nine, the room beside the pump house will be too hot. The air conditioner has been losing refrigerant for months. The woman has reported it twice. On her days off she sometimes wakes at the hour when the intake usually clogs and lies listening to plumbing in the wall of her apartment.

Under the stair, the crab has a different difficulty. A flake of rust has lifted from the reinforcing rod inside its shelter. Behind the flake, the metal is expanding into the concrete. Sometime in the coming weeks, a piece will break loose. Depending on where the crab is resting, the crack will become either a larger entrance or a closed one.

The power can see both possibilities.

For a while it attends to the whole district: the warm water, the buried cables, the woman’s swollen knuckles, the larvae drifting toward a gate. It follows the outgoing tide beyond the tanks. Beneath one of them, a seal has begun to fail. Nothing has reached the canal yet.

There is much it could do badly.

Fresh water poured generously into the salt pans would kill what lived there. Trees planted across the mud would shade some bodies and displace others. Turning off the pumps would restore one movement of water while drowning several places where animals now took refuge. Keeping the heron from every fish would leave a hungry bird beside a crowded outlet.

It does not need the district to look innocent.

It begins beneath the tank.

The failing seal holds until it can be replaced. At the next inspection, the defect is plain enough to be found without an accident. The replacement part is available. The crew has enough time to empty the line correctly.

This requires alterations well beyond the tank yard. The power makes them too. It is not interested only in the last inch through which harm enters a body.

The woman’s third repair request is answered. A technician arrives during the afternoon shift, so she does not have to stay awake to let him in. He clears the drain, repairs the leak, and checks the unit under load. The damp patch beneath it dries over two days.

Nothing about this makes her love her work. She has other things she would rather do. But when she returns to the office after checking the screens, the air cools the sweat at the back of her neck. There is another trained worker available when she needs leave.

She uses some of it.

Beneath the lowest stair, the power waits until the crab comes out.

The crab advances sideways, stops, and scrapes at a dark patch on the concrete. Its smaller claw works almost continuously. When a neighboring crab approaches, it lifts the larger one and holds it there.

Behind it, deep in the split, the pressure in the rod eases. A loose grain falls into the water. The portion of concrete that would have dropped instead remains lodged against the stair’s side.

The shelter is still rough and narrow. Water still reaches its back. The power leaves the entrance where the crab has made it.

When the crab returns, it pauses as usual, then withdraws into the damp.

By noon the exposed apron shimmers. Beneath a slab, shrimp press into a diminishing band of cooler water. The power follows the heat downward and finds an old passage clogged with silt. It opens enough of it for a slow exchange with the deeper canal. No jet sweeps the shrimp out. The water under the slab cools by a little.

Elsewhere, it does not cool the ponds. The creatures in the strong brine have their own tolerances. It learns these closely, body by body where it must, rather than treating the visible ones as representatives of all the rest.

Over the following months, the work spreads.

A corroded gate is replaced before it jams. The work is done in sections, with water moving through another channel. Some of the old masonry is left submerged because mussels occupy it. Where a dangerous wall must come down, there are already shaded seams nearby, washed by the same tide.

There are mistakes that would once have been called unavoidable. The power makes them avoidable, then makes the means ordinary: time to inspect, money to repair, room to change a plan, someone authorized to stop the work.

It does not ask the woman to become the guardian of every small life along the canal. She can miss a shrimp in a bucket without the whole weight of the district falling on that moment.

Nor does it finish its attention after the repairs.

One evening, weeks later, the crab beneath the stair begins to molt. It has stopped feeding. In the back of the split its old shell loosens, and the long work of withdrawing from it begins.

The power stays near.

Outside, the heron feeds at another outlet. A truck passes on the raised road. The woman, at home, sleeps through the first part of the tide.

The crab emerges soft, its legs scarcely able to support it. Water touches the floor of the crack and recedes. Nothing enters while its new shell firms.

Before dawn, it comes to the entrance.

There is film on the stair, fresh matter caught in the roughness, a small current passing below. The crab sets its feet against the concrete. It reaches down.

The power attends to what happens next.
