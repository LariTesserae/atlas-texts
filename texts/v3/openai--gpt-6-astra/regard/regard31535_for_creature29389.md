The power does not clear the water.

For a long time it attends to what happens beneath the front edge: the lobe extending, the paired knobs meeting a fragment of shell, the pause before the fragment is turned. On its underside is a little flesh. The animal separates this from the shell and draws it inward. The empty piece falls back into the mud.

The next thing it touches is not food. Nor is the next.

There is no need to make every touch successful. There is need enough that the searching body should sometimes find what sustains it.

The power follows the water entering the basin. It attends to the worms in their narrow passages, the crustaceans among the grains, the living mouths of the dust-covered sponges. What feeds one body may be another body. It cannot care for this animal by making everything within reach helpless.

Nor does it mistake stillness for safety. Water must pass across the back. Food must arrive. The mud beneath the animal must exchange something with the water above.

It waits through a tide.

As the flow strengthens, the feeding lobe withdraws. The underside presses down. Hooks enter the firmer layer, and for a while the animal holds.

Then that layer begins to move.

The failure starts several yards away, where water has worked between two beds of clay. A shallow sheet loosens. Nothing opens above it; there is no visible crack. The whole surface, animal and shell fragments and the little raised rims of burrows, travels slowly downslope.

The animal grips harder.

The power feels the strain gather along its damaged margin.

It does not hold the whole acre in place. Beneath the animal, it parts a little mud around the hooks that have caught most deeply. The purchase fails before the flesh does.

The body lifts.

Its margins close in their accustomed way. Sand passes over the embedded plates. It turns once, twice, carried within the slipping water.

The power goes with it.

There is a low rock back nearby, almost buried. Along one side, old gravel supports a bed of clay. The currents reach this place from different directions, but none meets it with the full force of the sill. Several worms live there. A sponge has survived on its exposed end for longer than the animal has been alive.

The turning body passes above it.

Here the power makes a small change in the water, no stronger than the eddy a larger stone might have caused. One margin touches down. It opens. The hooks find mud that is not moving.

The other margins follow.

For a while, nothing visible happens.

The power does not take this pause as permission to do more. Underneath, the knobs press down in sequence. The animal tests its new ground for itself.

At last the front edge loosens.

It feeds.

Only then does the power attend to the old injury. The pale tissue is closed, but a fold beneath it pulls whenever the animal advances. There is a narrow place where each contraction brings damaged surfaces hard against one another.

Over the next several days, that place softens. Tissue grows where growth has long since stopped. The plates retain their irregular spacing; the missing margin does not return all at once. But the fold ceases to pinch.

The animal moves farther between pauses.

Nothing arrives for it to worship. No brightness enters the sediment. The faint chemical trail ahead remains more interesting than anything overhead.

When it reaches the edge of the sheltered ground, the power lets it continue.

Beyond lies ordinary mud, with food scattered through it and dangers the animal has ways of meeting. The power keeps its attention there without making a boundary. This place is useful because the animal can rest in it, feed in it, leave it.

Later, another individual settles along the opposite flank of the rock. Farther down, a young one has outgrown its shell. A current turns the shell over, exposing the transparent back.

The power is there also.

It learns where a small interruption will spare a body, where repair is possible, where interference would merely carry harm elsewhere. Its care extends into the basin without requiring the basin to become something else.

Above, a storm crosses the polar sea.

Below, sediment rises from the plain and travels through the dark. The animal draws in its feeding lobe. Along the healed edge, the plates slide easily over one another as it settles against the ground.

The water presses.

This time, the ground holds.
