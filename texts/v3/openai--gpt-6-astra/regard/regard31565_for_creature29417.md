For three evenings the power sat on the oldest stair, leaving room for people to pass.

This was not quite as easy as becoming a star or moving a hill. There was very little room, and the cat wanted some of it.

On the first evening Sefa took it for someone waiting for the grain seller. On the second, she asked it to move its foot. On the third, it caught a jar that fell from the gallery above.

It caught the jar at the height of Sefa’s shoulder, although it was still sitting on the stair, seven paces away.

Sefa put down the cord she was splicing.

“Can you do that with anything?”

“Some things.”

“Come here, then.”

She took it to the basin.

One of the wheel’s supports had begun to move inside the wall. It moved so little that most people blamed the wheel itself. Sefa laid the power’s hand against the plaster and asked a boy to pedal.

“There.”

“Yes.”

“I’ve told them.”

Behind the plaster, an old timber ended in a pocket of damp brick. Beneath the brick was the crown of a forgotten doorway. The weight above had been finding its way around that doorway for a hundred and seventy years. Recently it had begun to find a different way.

“Can you mend it?” she asked.

“Yes. But this wall is also carrying part of your neighbour’s floor. I should speak to her.”

“She’ll say the floor is perfectly good.”

“Is she home?”

***

The power could have made the court new.

It could have lifted every roof, unthreaded the roots from the drains, and replaced the crooked stairs with broad, identical flights. It could have brought daylight to every threshold.

But daylight in one room would have taken away the room above it. A wider stair would occupy the space where the kiln worker slept. The figs were already swelling on the branches whose roots had broken the drain.

So it went from door to door.

This did not mean everyone agreed. The woman whose floor rested on the failing wall insisted that the trouble came from the wheel being pedalled too hard. The grain seller wanted to know whether repairs would interrupt deliveries. Sefa’s mother wanted someone to admit that she had mentioned the wall before Sefa was born.

The power listened, then placed a small bowl on the paving.

In the bowl they could see the timber, wet at its end; the brick beneath it; the darkness of the buried doorway. When someone turned the wheel, fine dust loosened inside the image.

Sefa’s mother leaned forward.

“That,” she said, “is where the old passage went.”

They arranged to begin in the morning.

***

For the next nine days, nobody had to choose between helping with the repairs and earning enough to eat.

Food arrived first, from sellers in the district above, paid for without bargaining them down. There were wages for the people who worked, and food also for those who could not. When the grain seller asked how the baby should be counted, the power said, “As someone who eats,” and waited while he corrected the measure.

The work was strange but not incomprehensible.

A mason put her hand into a wall as though reaching through a hanging cloth. She could feel every brick without disturbing it. The power held the weight while she and her brother rebuilt the failed support. They argued over the bonding, and the power did not settle the argument for them. It showed them what each arrangement would bear.

Under the lowest paving they found a crushed drain, three earlier drains, and a little flight of steps that went nowhere anyone remembered.

The power opened a route for floodwater beneath them all. At its mouth it made a settling chamber with a cover that two people could lift. The cover had handles. Sefa was firm about the handles.

“Everything here is made as if hands never get old,” she said.

They put a second rail beside the water steps, lower than the first. They roughened the slippery treads without taking away the hollows where feet expected them.

The bats were left asleep behind the flue. Work near that wall began after dusk.

Not every nest could stay. Where a cavity had to be closed, the power showed the pigeon keeper how to open another before the birds returned. He was suspicious of its advice until it admitted that one pair would probably refuse the new hole out of sheer preference.

“They will,” he said, pleased to be understood.

***

Water took longer.

There was no hidden river immediately beneath the court, and the power did not steal one from a place it could not see.

It followed the water far enough to learn where it came from, who waited for it, and who went without when the upper district filled its cisterns. Then it went uphill.

What it said there was not heard in the court. But a week later, people from three adjoining streets came down to inspect a new supply pipe, and the agreement governing it was read aloud in the galleries as well as written down. No household in the court could sell another household’s share. The reserve belonged to those who lived there, including tenants and people too young to carry a jar.

There was more water, though not an endless amount.

The wheel remained. Sefa liked being able to see how it worked. Its axle turned more easily now, and it could be driven from a seated treadle. The header jar had a lid that opened for cleaning without requiring someone to balance on the narrow ledge.

The gutter still received water. A drinking dish for the animals stood apart from the washing place. Rats could no longer enter the grain bins, which displeased them considerably.

The mint put out such a quantity of leaves that its owner began giving them away before anyone could accuse her of charging.

***

On the last afternoon of the work, the power asked Sefa what she wanted for herself.

She named a better drying rack.

“For yourself,” it repeated, then stopped. “I’m sorry. That may be for yourself.”

“It is.”

They discussed the rack.

Afterward she asked whether it could mend her hearing.

“Some of it,” it said. “May I look?”

She sat on the threshold while it held its hands near her ears. There was no light, and no sudden voice telling her anything important.

Then she turned sharply toward her room.

“What?”

“Your mother is folding cloth.”

Sefa listened.

She had not known that she no longer heard it.

The power could also loosen the old injury in her finger. It explained what would change and what might ache afterward. She considered this for a while.

“Not today. I have cups to finish.”

“Another day, if you want.”

It left her a way to call that did not require climbing stairs, making an offering, or saying the right words.

She tried it once while it was still there.

“Yes?” it said.

“Nothing. I wanted to see.”

***

Her daughter’s request was different.

She wanted to know what had withdrawn from her hand behind the drying racks.

Together they moved the lowest rack. The power waited while she decided whether to look.

There was a narrow run through the broken backing of the wall. In it lay an old scrap of sacking, several kernels, and a tuft of brown fur.

“A rat?”

“Probably.”

“Was it frightened?”

“Yes.”

“Of me?”

“Yes.”

She considered this reversal with interest.

They did not make her put her hand into the gap. They made a hook for retrieving fallen things, with a handle long enough that she could remain outside.

She charged her uncle for the hook’s use.

***

Before leaving, the power paid for another inspection after the rains, and another the following year. It made sure the money could not be diverted to anyone’s roof without the others knowing.

There were further offers: help for the mother’s hearing, a place with higher ceilings if Sefa wanted to move, lessons outside the court for any child who wished to attend. Some answers came at once. Others did not.

On its final evening, Sefa brought down one of the five heavy cups.

She had not ground the base. Instead she had filled it with mint water.

The power accepted it in both hands. Above them, someone was complaining about washing across the light shaft. The wheel began to turn, and Sefa paused in the middle of a sentence.

One cup struck the header jar.

Then the next.

She listened through a whole circuit before she went on speaking.
