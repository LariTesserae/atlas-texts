The power first attended to the drop.

It had gathered along the underside of the slab, traveled a little way along the new crease, and fallen into the bowl the girl had moved. There was nothing wrong with it. The power let it fall.

For several days it did much the same with everything.

It followed the warm air down through the mountain until warmth became heat, and the passages narrowed around dark water. It watched roots drink from cracks too fine for a human hair. It learned where the holders slept and where they went when a nest became too crowded. Some did travel beneath the stone. The marked animal had crossed the gulf by a route that would have been no use to the people: a descending fissure, a web of roots, three hundred paces upside down.

It listened indoors.

Eda’s brother was not wrong about all the noises in the roof. One branch had begun to soften where it entered the western wall. Eda knew about it, but believed it would last until the next cutting season. It probably would.

The power stayed with that word for some time.

***

When it appeared to Eda, it did so in the house, where a person could hear an answer.

She was alone, separating seeds on a cloth. It took no grand shape. There was someone sitting on the visitor’s stool, and the stool had ceased to wobble.

Eda put her hand over the seeds.

“Who let you in?”

“No one.”

After a moment she said, “That is not a good beginning.”

“No.”

It told her, as plainly as it could, what had brought it there. It had seen the country and the lives upon it. It could change things. It wished to help, but looking at a house was not the same as living in it.

Eda listened without removing her hand.

“Can you stop the wind?”

“Yes.”

She glanced toward the damp passage behind the slab.

“What happens to our water?”

“That would change. So would the gardens, and the birds below the cliffs. I could keep changing things to account for it.”

“For how long?”

“For as long as necessary.”

She considered this.

“And would we have to find you every time something went wrong?”

“You should not have to.”

Outside, the creeper roof lifted against its weights. Dust sifted from a branch. Eda looked up.

“There,” she said. “You can begin by telling me about that.”

***

They did not leave the roof until the family returned.

The young cord-maker objected to having the work begun without him. Eda’s brother objected to the branch having been left so long. The niece wanted to know whether the visitor had a name, and whether it could get a tool out of a vent.

“Yes,” said the power.

“How far down?”

“How far down is it?”

This led to the recovery of two knives, a drilling stone, a valuable hook, and a spoon no one admitted to losing. There was also a little clay figure with its head broken off.

Eda’s brother stopped talking when he saw it.

He took it outside and stayed in the entrance for a while, turning it in his hands.

The roof was repaired without anyone having to sleep elsewhere. They kept the sound branches and replaced the softened one. The power could have made the whole thing imperishable, but the family had questions about that. Would the roots still enter? Could they enlarge the house? What would happen when they wished to take a beam down?

So it showed them several possibilities. They chose a roof they could still alter, with places to inspect every buried end.

The young man worked on it until evening.

After supper, Eda asked why he could not work so promptly on his own.

***

Word traveled by the sheltered routes.

People came with jars, sick children, old injuries, disputes, and elaborate explanations of why their particular garden should receive attention before anyone else’s. Some came because they did not believe what they had heard. A woman from beyond the Lower Orchard arrived carrying nothing and asked for one night in which her hands did not hurt.

The power did not make her wait for the discussions about cisterns.

Nor did it require everyone to agree before easing a fever, or restoring feeling to a foot, or bringing back a collector who had been stranded on a ledge since morning. Where someone could answer, it asked. Where delay would kill, it acted.

There were limits it had to speak aloud.

It could recover a clay figure from a crack. It could not place the brother who had made it, and died forty years earlier, on the stool beside the hearth merely because another brother still wanted him there.

Eda’s brother asked several questions about this, in private.

Afterward the power sat with him. It did not attempt to make the loss seem useful.

***

Water took longer.

Not because it was difficult to provide, but because water would change where people lived, what they grew, who needed to visit whom, and which household could ask a favor of another.

The first proposal put a plentiful spring in the largest garden.

“That belongs to Sen’s family,” someone said.

“They would share,” someone else said.

Sen said nothing, which was sensible of him and did not settle the matter.

In the end there were many sources, including sources beside the houses of people who had not attended the meetings. They were set into covered stone basins, below the wind, and fed by new passages that took nothing from the old ones. Each had an overflow, a place to clean the basin, and a way to stop the flow.

The power showed the people where the water came from. Those who wished went down with it, safely, into a chamber beneath the inhabited stone. They came back with conflicting descriptions of its size, but agreed about the water.

For the first few weeks, most households continued filling all their buried jars.

Eda continued cleaning the condensation slab.

Nobody told her this was unnecessary.

When the bulbs could finally be soaked without counting the changes of water, her niece discovered she disliked them.

This caused more annoyance at supper than anyone had expected.

***

There was considerable argument about the crossings.

Some wanted bridges. Some feared that a bridge would bring strangers to gardens whose entrances had always been difficult to find. Some wanted a safe route down. Others had no wish to descend but wanted relatives from the lower country to be able to visit without being carried for the last part.

The power would not settle who owned a garden. It would not allow a dispute about ownership to require a child to cross bare stone above a gulf.

It made sheltered ways to the shared water first.

Then, with people walking the proposed lines, it made other routes: passages through the stone, steps below the brows, broad crossings with high sides pierced so the wind could pass without striking a traveler. They were not all straight. One bent a long way around a nesting ledge.

The birds disliked the work. The next season they returned.

On the old exposed paths, new handholds appeared where hands had searched and found nothing. The power tested the rock behind each one. Ropes remained useful. The cord-maker did not lose his skill or his work.

He did, eventually, finish his roof.

For some time afterward his bowls remained in Eda’s wall.

***

There were people who left.

The first was an old woman who had spent sixty years claiming she wanted to see a tree that stood upright. Everyone had assumed this was a manner of speaking.

She returned after twelve days with seeds, news, and a poor opinion of the insects below.

Others stayed away. Some sent messages. The power helped establish ways for messages to travel without requiring its attention, and places to rest along the descent. It did not make departure a test of gratitude, or staying a proof of courage.

There were also people who arrived.

This created difficulties no bridge could solve. Meetings grew longer. Eda found herself explaining where to cut a creeper to a man who asked very good questions and understood none of her answers.

She put him underneath it.

***

The power remained attentive.

It had not come merely to admire how well people bore what hurt them. When a child lost her hold, it caught her. When a new passage carried too much damp into a sleeping room, it closed the passage and helped find a better place for it. When the holders discovered the seed stores beside one of the new basins, it listened to several opinions about holders that were not benevolent at all.

Not everything became easy.

There were still funerals. There were quarrels that survived an ample supply of water. People still promised visits and failed to come, though they had fewer excellent reasons. The niece grew older and found that being right did not always improve an argument.

But one afternoon Eda took her out along a path that had once required both hands.

They carried food in an ordinary basket. At the far end was a sheltered place overlooking the gulfs, with enough room to sit without bracing a foot.

For a while they watched a bird work the rising air.

Then Eda lay back on the warm stone.

Her niece turned toward her sharply. She had never seen her aunt do this outside a garden.

Eda’s hands rested open beside her.

Above them the green coverings of the mountains lifted and settled. The wind went on through its immense passages, bearing mist, leaves, seeds, the heat of the lower forests.

Eda closed her eyes.

Nothing needed holding just then.
