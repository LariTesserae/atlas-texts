The first thing the power does is wait through several changes of the tide.

It attends to the chamber at full draw and in the intervals between. It follows the water beyond the intake, through the pumps, and out into the harbor again. It finds what depends on that movement: not only the machinery above, but the fish beside the housing, the anemones under the sill, the oxygen passing through the narrow space behind the tile.

It watches the octopus leave and return.

There is no need to persuade it that somewhere else is home.

The fishing line goes first. During a pause in the draw, when nothing is touching it, the loop lifts clear of the bolt. The whole length passes out through the grille, including the part buried in the grit. It does not break into smaller pieces.

The hollow beneath it remains. The shrimp pushes out a grain, holds it briefly, and lets it fall.

Beyond the wall, the damaged shutter acquires a sound bearing. For the first time in years, it moves without striking its housing. The flow continues, but the rapid rattle is absent.

This absence changes the chamber.

Small sounds become distinct: a shell turning over on the paving, the separate ticks within the wall growth. The octopus holds still beneath the step. An arm lies along the tile, its suckers closed against the familiar surface.

The power makes no further change that tide.

Later, it eases the valves so that they no longer deliver their hard blow through the water. It leaves the deep note of the pumps, diminishing the vibration where it enters the masonry. When the machinery needs to draw harder, the increase comes gradually. Sediment still lifts. Food still arrives at the edges of the sheltered places. There is time for an arm to find another hold.

At the intake, the power spreads the pull across a larger surface. There is room behind the new guard for water, but no opening through which a hunting arm can pass into the mechanism. The guard has no shearing edge. Its supports do not tremble.

For a while the fish abandon the housing.

Then three return, facing the current in a slightly different place.

The power follows weaknesses through the ceiling. Some end harmlessly within a block. Others continue into the harbor wall, where each tide presses them farther apart. Those it closes from within. The loaded iron in the columns becomes sound beneath its crust.

Nothing bright appears. No occupied joint fills with new mortar. The mussels along the cable continue opening their shells.

Under the step, there is less to do than the power first supposed.

The narrow entrance is useful. So is the tile, and so is the passage the conger cannot enter. The scattered shells are not rubbish. Even the closeness of the roof gives the octopus something against which to brace.

But behind the mantle, beyond the places its arms can comfortably fold, there is a block with nothing living inside or beneath it.

Over several nights, its inner face recedes.

The change is small at first: space enough for an arm to uncurl without displacing another. Then a rounded recess, dark and rough, in which the body can turn. The entrance remains where it was. The roof remains low near the tile. The shrimp’s constriction is untouched.

The octopus discovers the recess by touch.

An arm enters, withdraws, enters again. Suckers travel over the new surface. The animal stays near its old position for most of that day.

On the next, it draws a shell farther inside.

The power does not lengthen the short arm all at once. It attends to the living edge, to the tissue forming there, and removes nothing that belongs to its growing. There is sufficient nourishment. There is no infection. In time, the little suckers acquire a wider reach.

Nor does it drive the conger away.

The eel still emerges from its pipe. Crabs still cross the floor, and some are caught. The octopus still pauses with an eye above the threshold, reading what the water brings. The chamber has not become harmless. It has become a place in which fewer dangers arrive without any possibility of meeting them.

Above, where rainwater collects the harbor’s spilled fuel and carries it toward the drains, the power turns its attention next. This work extends beyond the chamber. There are pipes to mend, places to catch the oily film, people whose repeated requests for repairs can at last be answered. The next brown cloud contains mud, but less that burns the gills.

Below, the octopus knows none of this as an intention.

One night it climbs to the barred opening. Its arms explore the familiar spaces. Outside, the harbor moves against the wall, full of traces arriving from beyond the reach of sight.

It passes through.

The power does not follow closely. It does not set a limit on how far the animal may go, or keep food beneath the step to ensure its return.

Near morning, the octopus comes back along the ceramic pipe. It descends behind a column and stops at the wrench, where an arm turns a shell in the scour hollow.

Then it crosses to the stair.

The tile is pulled inward. A few grains roll down the slope. Within the cavity, seven long arms and one shorter one settle into places that fit them.

The tide rises. Water fills the last pocket between the beams.

Nothing requires thanks.
