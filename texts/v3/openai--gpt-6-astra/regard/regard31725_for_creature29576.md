For a while, the power listened.

This was not because it believed suffering became good if it continued long enough. Beneath the meeting house, a loaded stone had begun to part along a seam that no tap of a hammer could reach. The power held it together before anyone entered the room that morning.

Then it listened.

It heard the woman who remembered agreements explain that a drain could not be moved because the right to cross a sleeping room had been exchanged for the right to empty a jar. It heard a child ask whether this meant the jar could go through the sleeping room. It heard the answer, which was no, and the further explanation, which made the matter less clear.

It listened to the water. There were several waters under the town, traveling at different depths, and the white stone joined some of them that had previously been separate. To stop the rising damp altogether would send pressure elsewhere. To lower every channel would dry the places where the little fish laid their eggs.

These were difficulties, not reasons to leave things as they were.

On the fourth morning, a stranger sat on the wall beside the landing. Their clothes were plain, their feet muddy. When a grain boat arrived, they helped unload it. They carried the sacks where they were told and did not mistake the first open doorway for the destination.

By noon, people had noticed that the stranger’s hands were uncut.

By evening, someone had asked for help with a threshold.

The stranger knelt beside the householder, who showed them where to place the wedge.

“Not there. You’ll take the corner off.”

“Here?”

“A little lower.”

Together they lifted the stone. Underneath lay a black channel almost closed by white growth.

The stranger put one finger into it. The hard lining loosened and came away in a single curved piece. Water passed under the doorway with a sound like someone drawing breath.

The householder looked at the piece, then at the finger.

“There are others,” she said.

“Yes,” said the stranger. “Will you show me?”

* * *

Their first public meeting was badly attended because it was held while the grain was being measured. Their second went better.

The stranger explained the stone beneath the meeting house. Several people immediately disagreed about who had last inspected it.

“It is safe now,” the stranger said.

“How long?”

“As long as the house stands. But I would like you to see where it failed.”

They made a small model from clay, with separate pieces that could be lifted out. The conduit worker turned it over in his hands. Then he fetched his apprentice.

Other things took longer.

People wanted dry rooms, but not at the cost of the wells. They wanted wider passages, though nobody volunteered the wall of their own kitchen. The mender wanted the smoke to rise out of the hollow instead of coming through her windows. Someone wanted his brother’s roof lowered. His brother wanted him to mind his own affairs.

The stranger listened to all of it, including the requests people made afterward, separately.

One woman asked whether she could have a door that closed properly.

She said it lightly, as though it were a small matter.

The stranger went with her to look.

* * *

Under the town, they made room for the water.

There were no tremors. A bowl filled to its brim did not spill. Deep in the clay, passages opened with roofs that would bear the weight above them. Excess water could reach the marsh without forcing its way through every inhabited stone.

The white slabs still darkened during dry weather. The exposed tops still shone. But the damp stopped short of most bedding, and the growth in the gutters became slower.

At the places where the channels needed tending, the stranger made openings wide enough for a person’s shoulders. Each had a ledge on which to set a lamp and a place to brace both feet.

The conduit worker inspected one for a long time.

“You could have made it never need clearing,” he said.

“I could.”

“Then why didn’t you?”

“I wanted to ask what you needed to keep.”

He looked into the passage. A snail moved along the old waterline.

“Those can stay. The cutting can go.”

So the stranger changed it again.

The crust would form in shallow removable beds. It could be lifted out while still soft, without a wedge. The potters asked to keep some. The rest could be returned to the marsh.

There remained plenty of work in the town. Nobody found this difficult to arrange.

* * *

The stranger cleaned one western well of the sickness that sometimes entered it from below. They showed the townspeople which water was safe to drink and left marks that would change if it ceased to be safe.

They eased the smoke upward.

They mended the cracks in people’s hands, where people offered their hands. The mender watched her own skin close beside the nail of her thumb.

“Will it open again?”

“Not from this work.”

She rubbed the place once and picked up her needle.

Later she put it down.

“My youngest wheezes when he sleeps.”

That evening the stranger came upstairs.

There were other visits. Some were spoken of afterward. Some were protected by pot lids, conversations with children, and the quiet attentiveness of people who knew how not to hear.

At the landing, the stranger set a broad stone step that stayed just above the water, rising and falling with it. Boats no longer had to be unloaded by someone standing thigh-deep in cold mud.

They also made a dry place outside the wall, large enough for stores, a work shelter, or houses.

The town argued about it for eleven days.

During those eleven days, children used it for running.

* * *

The old woman brought down her pot.

She held it out but did not release it.

“This earth,” she said.

The stranger waited.

“Not better earth.”

“No.”

“Can anything grow in it?”

“Yes. Would you like something from the place it came from?”

She considered this. Then she asked for a small flowering plant that the stranger had not heard anyone in the town name.

It grew slowly enough that she could watch it happen. First there was a pale division in the soil, then a bent stem carrying its first closed leaves.

She took the pot upstairs herself.

The stranger did not ask her to tell a story.

* * *

By the time they left, there was a cupboard near the cistern containing things that could be borrowed: good blades, needles, lamps, a few tools whose uses had required demonstrations. Beside it hung the clay model of the foundations, remade in pieces that would not crumble.

There was also a place on the wall where anyone could lay a palm and ask for the stranger.

“Will you always come?” the agreement-keeper asked.

“I will always hear. If I cannot come at once, you will know when.”

“What do we owe?”

“Nothing.”

She made them repeat this before witnesses.

The apprentice’s elaborate spillway was still unfinished. For several days he had been uncertain what to do with it, now that the wall beside the mender’s stair no longer wept.

At last he carried it up anyway.

Her eldest daughter turned it in the light.

“What is it for?”

“It could hold a little water,” he said. “For a bird.”

She looked at him, then made a space on the outer sill.

Below them, someone lifted a paving stone and found that the channel was clear. He stayed on his knees a moment, holding the crooked stick he had brought.

Then he put the stone back.

It was not yet noon. He washed his hands, went upstairs, and asked whether anyone wanted to walk out beyond the wall.
