The power does not touch the hand.

It attends first to the concrete beneath it: the small grains loosening at the surface, the rust swelling around a buried rod, the load carried down through water and new mud into a layer that has not moved for centuries. It follows the pile to its footing. Then it follows the others.

One has begun to lean.

Not enough for a person on the platform to notice. Enough that, when the pumps start together, a narrow crack opens beneath the beam that carries the nest. Water enters it and does not entirely leave.

The power holds the footing still.

Above, the animal withdraws its hand. It turns twice in the passage, pushing past a sleeping flank, and settles with its chin on the back of another animal. Nothing in its experience requires it to know what has happened.

For a while, the power does no more.

It watches a feeding patch become too crowded. It watches an animal driven away from an overflow find shrimp beneath a floating sheet of bark. It watches a worker strike a casing, wait, and strike again because the first animal to emerge has turned back for something.

What comes out next is a young one, already too large to be carried conveniently. Its hind feet knock against the pipe. The worker keeps the spanner raised until both have dropped clear.

The power attends to the worker as closely as to the animals.

There is water inside his left boot. He has been putting off replacing it. The pump that he is about to start has a fault which will be blamed on his shift, though it began before he was employed here. Beyond the retaining bank are houses that depend on the pumping, and beyond those are fields whose drains enter the river. Stopping the machinery would bring a kind of quiet. It would also bring water through doorways in the night.

The power leaves the pumps running.

Before the worker reaches the switch, the worn coupling parts at rest. There is a sharp, contained sound. A piece of metal falls onto the platform where he can see it.

He lowers the spanner.

Later there will be an argument about the repair. There will not be a body beside the switch.

Rain begins over the basin.

The power follows it into every hollow. It learns which pools join first and which remain separate; where fish wait against the current; where the smallest animals are drawn toward the intakes. Beneath the platform, an adult descends into the lowest nursery and emerges with a young one hanging from its mouth.

There are four young in that chamber.

The power could lift them all. Instead it slows the water entering through a seam in the bank. The adult has time to return. The receiving animal has time to pull the bedding clear of the upper doorway. The last youngster is brought out before the floor goes under.

Only then does the water rise at its former rate.

By morning, the nest is crowded but dry. An animal carrying a strip of sack finds a new place to fasten it: a roughness in the concrete where there had been only a smooth face. It hooks two fingers over the edge, tests its weight, and climbs.

The power has begun to understand what the basin will become.

On the survey board, the future is a level surface. The stakes stand at unequal heights, but their painted tops describe a single intention. Eventually the pipes will be moved. The last pools will drain. Roads will be laid across the buried channels, and the buildings will cease to look as though they are standing in water.

There will be grass, perhaps trees. Much that cannot live here now will arrive.

The animals beneath the platforms will have nowhere to feed.

A deeper pool would not be enough. Neither would a patch of reeds left between roads. They live along the moving edge where sediment settles out of water, where buried things work upward, where a ridge briefly holds and then gives way. Their abundance belongs to a process.

So the power looks beyond the basin.

It follows the river through its high banks, past the last pumping station, to an old inlet closed by a stone sill. Behind it lies a narrow tract of sour ground. Water reaches it through a broken culvert at the highest tides, then remains until it smells. Reeds grow there, but little moves between their stems. The sill was built for a works demolished years ago.

The power loosens no stone yet.

It examines the houses nearby, the road, the buried cables, the winter height of the river. It finds where an opening could admit silt-bearing water without admitting a flood. It finds higher ground for refuge, and firm ground beneath it.

Then, during the inspection already due that autumn, rain washes the face of the old culvert clean. The engineer sees the full width of the blockage. Her sounding rod passes through what had been recorded as solid masonry.

There is more room here than the drawings show.

The power cannot make her thoughtful by making her agree. It can give her something worth examining. It keeps the rain light while she takes her measurements.

What follows takes time.

There are revised drawings. There is a proposal too expensive to build, then a cheaper one that would leave the water stagnant. There are people who know how the river used to enter the inlet, and people who know what is buried beside the road. Their knowledge reaches the same table.

The power remains attentive during the delays.

At the basin, it holds the damaged footing until the platform can be repaired. The crack becomes visible from the service ladder. The nest, discovered during the inspection, is left in place while a separate brace is installed.

Elsewhere, small shelves are fixed beneath structures that will remain after the pipes are gone. They are above the expected water and reachable from below. Some remain empty. Birds occupy one. At another, an animal tears out the lining provided for it and replaces it with wet string.

The power does not correct this.

When the inlet is opened, the first incoming water spreads over an old surface and finds very little to eat.

It is not yet a home.

Silt gathers behind the new low ribs. Worms arrive. Fine roots loosen the edges of the higher ground. The gates are adjusted because the first arrangement drains one bed too quickly and leaves another without enough exchange. Workers learn the inlet’s noises, which differ from those of the pumps.

For two more seasons, water still passes through the older basin. The animals have time to discover the connecting ditch.

One goes only as far as the culvert and returns.

Another stays away through a night of rain.

Eventually, beneath a small inspection bridge, reed stems begin to collect against a beam. Most are carried off by the next tide. A strip of sack remains caught around a bolt. More stems accumulate behind it.

The power watches without securing every strand.

There must be room for the nest to be built where its builders find it workable, not merely where shelter has been offered.

Years later, part of the basin is firm enough to carry a road. The old platform stands beside a drainage channel, its legs buried almost to the crossbeams. No animals sleep beneath it now. Dry fragments of the nest remain in a corner, too tightly plastered to fall.

At the inlet, the beds are different ages.

One is open water. One bears the delicate marks of feeding mouths. On another, reeds have become thick enough to slow the current, and a narrow channel has begun to cut around them. Water enters through the gates with a low, uneven rush.

Under the inspection bridge, a young animal is trying to climb.

Its hands spread against the concrete. One foot slips. It hangs for a moment, indignant and silent, before finding the lower edge of the beam.

The power is near.

The beam is sound. The water beneath is shallow. An adult waits inside the entrance, blocking it with a wet shoulder.

The young animal tries again.

This time, the power leaves everything as it is.
