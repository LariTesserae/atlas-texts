The power first noticed the lip because it was waiting.

A foot had come down on the stair. The clear beads beside the hood-maker’s mouth had flattened, and cold was moving through a newly opened doorway. Beyond it, the fine ridges were forming. There was food between them.

But a runner stood on the extended lip with one leg held clear of the surface.

The hood-maker waited until it put that leg down.

The power, which had arrived with several large intentions, waited also.

For a while it attended to the underside of the step. In that interval, the hollow beneath became crowded. It learned to turn away from one place without abandoning it. Some of the animals would not emerge under any other kind of attention.

It watched three drops fall.

After the first, it considered making the water more frequent. After the second, it saw the occupied burrows left by the retreating pool. After the third, it found small swimmers waiting in a dryness that was not empty.

It left the water alone.

It considered taking the weight off the stair. Then the hood-maker opened its soft pits, and the power felt, through them, something of the season arriving overhead.

So the people in the garden continued to use the steps.

The bristled predator continued to hunt. Once it caught a runner beside the arcade. The power felt the brief distress of the runner and the easing of something drawn tight within the predator. Neither feeling was small because the bodies were small. It stayed through both.

In the oldest hood, the split nail caught against a pellet.

The animal pulled. A rib of skin tightened along its side. The pellet turned, but the nail did not release.

Here the power came very close.

It could have made the nail new. Instead it steadied the pellet with a pressure no greater than that of another grain. The hood-maker changed its grip. The caught edge came free.

The animal held still for a long time.

Then it rolled the pellet once more and carried it to a doorway.

The power withdrew its pressure.

That evening, above the chamber, someone stopped on the stair and looked down at the black seam.

A finger pushed into the damp earth beside the stone. Some soil crumbled away. Through the roof came the muffled sound of a voice.

“Needs packing underneath.”

The hood-maker’s pits opened. It turned toward a plugged entrance, took the pellet inward, and waited for the cold.

The power followed the person into the garden shed.

There was mortar on a shelf, hardening in a folded sack. There was a narrow trowel. There were pieces of broken tile in a bucket, and beneath them a flat offcut of stone.

It did not make the person forget the loose step. The step was loose. One end had begun to rock, and someday an ankle might turn on it.

Instead, when the person shifted the bucket, the offcut slipped against its side with a clean, useful sound.

They took it out.

The next morning, the stone was fitted beneath the outer corner of the stair, well away from the seam. It took several attempts. Each time the step was lifted, the chamber opened into an unaccustomed brightness. Animals clung, buried themselves, vanished into their separate moments.

The power held nothing still that needed to move.

But it kept one loose chip of limestone from falling through the roof.

The person tested the stair, heel first. The new support held. The middle of the step still yielded by an amount no human foot could distinguish.

Inside, the season passed through.

The hood-maker fed.

Afterward, the seam was still there. A little earth had fallen across one end, and a transparent blade leaned against it, its brown mouth closed.

The power did not clear the entrance. Before night, something from outside pressed through the fallen earth and ate part of the blade. Something from inside ate part of that thing. The remaining earth acquired passages.

There was one more intervention.

A young hood-maker had begun its first wall beneath a place where a drop would strike. Its awkward tail could not yet grip. Twice it pressed a grain into position; twice the grain slipped into brightness.

The power laid a fragment of the fallen limestone nearby.

Not beneath the wall. Not over the animal.

Nearby.

The youngster touched it with its mouth, went around it, and continued building in the same place.

The power waited.

By morning, the wall had failed. The youngster sheltered beneath the fragment with its six front limbs drawn close together. Along its tail, the first travelling ribs had appeared.

When it began again, it built against the stone.

The power stayed through another drop, and then through a long dry interval in which very little seemed to happen.

At the oldest hood, the split edge of the nail wore smooth. The lower lip grew rougher. An accepted pellet waited just behind a closed doorway.

Beyond the new support, rain entered the soil at its old slow rate.

The power no longer occupied any particular part of the chamber. There was room for the animals that appeared only when attention shifted, and room for the weather, and for whatever the hood-maker kept folded in the cold hollow of its body.

A runner came across the lip.

This time it did not stop.

The lip drew inward, carrying a pale grain. Behind it, in the dark, six worn nails began their work.
