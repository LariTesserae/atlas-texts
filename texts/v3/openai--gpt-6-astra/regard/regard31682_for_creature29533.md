The power noticed Eda opening the door a second time.

It had seen the growing stone, the gardens suspended above the cloud, the old leaf held beneath the floor. These were remarkable things. But the second opening of the door detained it: the small unnecessary movement, made after the work was done.

It came up the cleft the next morning.

At the gate it took the shape of a woman carrying nothing. Her clothes were dry, although rain had been passing through the leaves. She waited while a basket went out and another came in. Then she turned sideways, as the inhabitants did, and entered.

Eda was washing her chisel.

“Where should I put this water?” the stranger asked.

Eda showed her.

For the first day, the stranger stayed in the western room. She accepted greens, grain, and a cup with a pale seam running through its base. When the instructions about washing water were repeated, she listened again.

She did not pretend to be hungry, but she enjoyed the food.

Senn found her looking at the lines on the pillar.

“Those aren’t all the same measure,” he said.

“I see that.”

“No,” he said. “You see that some are higher. That isn’t the same thing.”

She moved aside so that he could explain.

He showed her where the floor had risen, where a pillar had thickened unequally, and where an inscription recorded a mistake rather than a change. The crossed-out line was Tavi’s.

“She was measuring herself,” the stranger said.

“She was using my point.”

By evening the stranger knew which parts of the town were being watched carefully, and which were being watched by people who hoped someone else was watching.

She did nothing to either.

On the second morning she went down with Eda to sweep.

There was no spare broom. She waited while Eda fetched one from a household that would otherwise have slept longer. This inconvenience seemed worth allowing. To arrive with exactly the implement required would have made the morning easier, but would also have begun something the stranger did not intend: people waiting to discover what she already knew.

They worked from the western curve toward the pool.

The stranger was thorough. She lifted a seed before its translucent collar closed. She left a frond that had already become part of the floor.

“That one can come out,” Eda said.

“It will leave a mark.”

“It’s a floor.”

The stranger took it out.

Later, when Eda began the climb with her basket, the stranger saw how she shortened her steps halfway up. Eda set the basket down, though there was nowhere level to set it. She kept one foot behind it while she rested.

“Would you like a place to put that?” the stranger asked.

“I would like it at the top.”

The basket rose.

It travelled slowly enough that nothing spilled, passed beneath the nearest arch, and settled beside the wall.

Eda remained with her foot braced against its absence.

After a while she said, “You might have said.”

“Yes,” said the stranger. “I should.”

***

There were questions.

Some were asked immediately, and some after the questioner had gone away and returned.

Could she bring a person back?

Not every person, and not from death. She would not let anyone mistake that answer.

Could she find someone who was living?

Often. But finding a person and bringing them home were different things.

Could she stop the stone?

“Yes.”

Senn put both hands flat on his knees.

“What happens to the water then?”

“That depends on how I stop it.”

“And the walls?”

“That too.”

“Then don’t start yet.”

“I haven’t.”

Tavi asked whether the woman had lifted the basket with the air or with the basket.

“With the basket.”

Tavi looked pleased. This confirmed something, though nobody could discover what.

Eda asked her question after the others had left.

“What did you come for?”

“To see whether I could help.”

“People help here.”

“I noticed.”

Eda turned the cup between her palms. Outside, someone was trying to pull a reed screen free of a swelling socket. Each tug sounded like an animal coughing.

“I don’t want to spend tomorrow telling you everything that ought to be different.”

“You needn’t.”

“Or find you’ve done it while I was asleep.”

“No,” said the stranger.

***

The first thing they agreed on was a resting place halfway up the bowl.

Even this took discussion.

It could not obstruct the spreading water. It could not have a sharp edge for a falling person to strike. It could not become the first of a row of household shelves. Eda wanted room for a basket and both her feet. Another sweeper wanted room to sit.

The stranger laid a length of cord on the stone to show its size.

“Too much,” said Senn.

“You aren’t the one carrying my basket,” Eda said.

He looked at the cord again.

“A little farther from the spout, then.”

They marked the place. The stranger asked them to leave the cord overnight.

In the morning she put her palm against the slope.

The stone did not crack or melt. Its pale bands curved, slowly, as bands in a bent piece of wood might curve, except that nothing was compressed or strained. A shallow seat formed beneath her hand. Below it was a broad place for feet. Water found its way around both sides and continued down.

She had made it as old and strong as the surrounding floor.

Eda tested it with an empty basket. Then with a full one.

From above, the alteration was scarcely visible.

From halfway up, carrying wet leaves, it was considerable.

***

The stranger remained through several rains.

She went down the approach path with the people who carried loads. On the narrowest turn she stood against the wall while a man passed her, his basket brushing her breast, his outer foot seeking a hold beneath leaves.

Here she asked to do more.

Not a road. No one wanted carts arriving at the gate before anyone had decided where a cart might stand. She proposed a little more width at the turns, handholds where wet roots were currently trusted, and three places where carriers could pass without one having to retreat.

She showed them first.

The man who had once been frightened of the outer walk went over every proposed change. He found a place she had missed.

She thanked him and marked it.

For two days the path was closed while she worked. The town had to arrange its deliveries. Orrel complained about the timing, because his roofing cord was expected.

“Are you roofing it?” his sister asked.

“I need the cord before I can.”

Nobody took this opportunity to settle the matter for him.

When the path reopened, the oldest resident was helped down as far as the first new resting place. She had not been beyond the gate for four years.

There was no revelation waiting there. The cloud was thick. The leaves dripped on her sleeves.

She sat for a while and complained that they had brought the wrong wrap.

The next clear day, she asked to go again.

***

The power could have stayed.

There was always another channel, another cold room, another person whose pain it could ease. It did ease pain when asked. It also sat with those for whom ease was not the whole answer, and did not require them to speak wisely about suffering.

But it saw how quickly a town of twenty-eight people could become a town of twenty-eight people waiting upon one.

Before leaving, it told them how to call it. No altar was needed. No particular words. An ordinary request at the gate would reach it, though an answer might not be immediate.

“You can ask again,” it said. “If I have misunderstood.”

Eda walked with it to the cleft.

“Will the seat keep growing?”

“With the bowl.”

“So we’ll have to tend it.”

“Yes.”

Eda considered this.

“Not as much as you would have had to tend the steps you first suggested,” the power added.

“I still think steps would have been useful.”

“I know.”

They parted without agreeing.

That afternoon Eda swept beneath the western spouts. Halfway up, she rested her basket on the new ledge and sat beside it.

Nobody called to ask why she had stopped. There was a place to stop now.

She could see the upper gardens, Senn’s bare ankles beneath an arch, a strip of cloth lifting from a roof pole. Across the hollow, Tavi was making a noise she had been specifically asked not to make.

Eda took a wooden bead from the little pouch at her waist. It was dark red in shade. She held it where the light reached her, turning it until the grain appeared.

After a while she put it away and carried the basket up.
