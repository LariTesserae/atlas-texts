The power’s attention reached the hollow without making anything look up.

It followed the female through the low passage, waited while she smelled the entrance, and remained near her as the young settled. It could feel the stiff edge of a leaf against her belly, the pull of three mouths, the ache in the muscles along her back. Beyond these things lay sleep, repeatedly approached and repeatedly interrupted.

For a while, it did nothing.

The hollow was full of actions already in progress. A crab was drawing a leaf into a burrow narrower than the leaf. Water was loosening a grain of limestone above the basin. One of the young had a foot across another’s nose. The female shifted, and the foot slipped away.

There was much here that did not need another will.

But the power did not mistake quiet for sufficiency.

It attended to the terrace, including the parts no animal could enter. Behind the fitted stones, old earth lay in layers interrupted by roots. The passage that drained the shelter had narrowed over several seasons. Fine clay had gathered against a fallen chip of rock. Water could still leave, but more slowly than it could arrive.

Above the hill, weather was gathering.

The power followed the possible water downward. It found where it would soak into the soil, where it would divide, where one part would reach the hollow long after the rain had stopped. In the terrace chamber, it would rise first beneath the bedding. By the time the female felt it, the way out would be carrying water too.

She had moved young before. She might move these.

The power looked for something better than might.

It could have opened the roof or lifted away the terrace. It could have set the family on the hillside, dry and bewildered beneath an unfamiliar sky. Instead it followed the obstructed drain to its lower end.

There were living things there: a white root tip, two mites, a beetle resting against the underside of the stone. Farther down, the passage opened into coarse rubble beneath an unoccupied ledge.

The beetle finished resting and went elsewhere.

Then the chip of rock turned.

It moved less than the width of the female’s smallest claw. A little dry clay fell through. The root remained whole. Beneath the shelter, the passage was open again.

The power stayed.

That alone would not be enough if the hillside became thoroughly wet. It examined the ground above the chamber and found a pocket behind the upper terrace, sheltered by a broad stone whose weight rested securely on two others. There was room inside for the female and her young. There was no room at the entrance for her shoulders.

Across that entrance lay the dead end of a root.

The living tree had withdrawn from it years ago. Fungi had softened its center, leaving a brittle skin that still held its shape.

The skin parted along its underside. A short piece settled into the earth.

Nothing shone. Nothing called.

Near dawn, when the female came out, a faint movement of air carried the smell of dry wood down the step.

She stopped.

She stood with one forefoot lifted, nose working. Then she climbed past her usual entrance and put her head into the newly opened gap.

Her whiskers touched both sides. She withdrew, smelled the broken root, and tried again.

This time her shoulders followed.

Inside, she turned with difficulty. She scratched once at the floor. There were fragments of wood, dry soil, the empty skin of an insect. No fresh scent of another animal.

She came out and went to drink.

The power did not take her lack of immediate use for a mistake.

***

Rain reached the hillside the following afternoon.

In the hollow it was heard first as a thickening of sound overhead. Leaves shook beyond the openings. A gecko retreated into its crack. The female slept behind the lower terrace with her chin on the flank of one young animal.

Hours later, a root above the entrance began to shine.

Water entered beneath the fitted stones. It darkened the lowest bedding and found the cleared passage. Most of it went on through.

The female woke anyway.

She smelled the floor. She pushed her nose beneath the young, turned once in the chamber, and came out.

At the upper gap she paused only briefly.

She carried the first young one awkwardly. It flexed its feet against her chest and caught a claw in her fur. On the step she stopped to improve her hold.

The second made a small sound.

The third was asleep beneath a strip of leaf. She searched the chamber after removing it, nosing the place where all three had lain.

Then she climbed again.

In the upper pocket, the young pressed against her before she had finished settling. There was less room to stretch, but the floor was dry.

Below, water continued through the terrace. It emerged under the empty ledge, darkening a few stones before disappearing into deeper rubble. The basin at the eastern wall filled at its own pace. Beneath its rim, the pale snails remained.

The power’s attention did not leave when the immediate danger passed. It rested over the hill, the roots, the small chamber.

Near morning, the female woke and put her nose through the gap. The hollow smelled changed, as it always did after rain.

She listened for a while.

Then she backed into the dry wood, laid her head down, and slept until the young woke her.
