The power noticed the cord first.

It lay along the new stone beside the steps, already dark with water. Someone had used a good length of it, more than would be needed to mark the dangerous edge. The excess had been tucked into a joint where a later worker could find it.

The power followed it to Ilen’s hands.

There were small injuries on nearly every finger. Some had closed badly because they were always being opened again. Beneath her thumbnail, a thin line of mineral had grown into the wound.

It attended also to the fault, the water behind it, the animals pressing forward between her boots. Far below, the mountain was shifting a weight for which the city had several names and no complete measure.

It could have stopped that movement.

Before doing so, it followed the warmth.

***

By the time Ilen stood up, her knees had stiffened. Her son offered an arm. She used it, then pretended she had only needed somewhere to put her hand while adjusting her bag.

“There’s someone behind you,” he said.

A person stood on the steps. Their coat was unfamiliar, but not impractical. They had left room for others to pass.

“Do you need this opening checked?” Ilen asked.

“No.”

“Then move back. That edge hasn’t been covered.”

The person moved.

Ilen began winding the remaining cord around a second exposed section.

“May I take the water out of the cut in your thumb?” they asked.

She stopped.

“What?”

“The saltwater. And the stone that has begun forming there.”

Ilen put her thumb inside her fist.

“Are you a physician?”

“Not one trained here.”

Her son had moved closer.

The stranger waited. They did not reach for her.

At last Ilen held out her hand, palm downward, with the reluctance of someone showing a badly maintained tool.

“Only that,” she said.

The pressure under the nail disappeared.

She brought her hand toward the amber marker beside the steps. Nothing was glowing. There was no new scar. When she pressed the nail, it hurt only as a bruise hurts.

Her son said something sharp.

Ilen made him be quiet.

For a while she tested the web between finger and thumb against the nearest opening field. The resistance was even. Her hand told her what it had always told her.

“What else can you do?” she asked.

“A great deal.”

“Can you tell where that water goes?”

“Yes.”

“Then start with that.”

***

The first meeting took place in a pressure office with an animal asleep under the table.

There were nine people present, then thirteen, then eleven after two went to fetch instruments. The stranger did not ask for a larger room.

They put a map on the table.

It was a map that could be touched. Channels ran beneath its surface. A hand laid over a reservoir felt a faint pressure; a finger following a warm seam grew warmer. Where the city’s surveys ended, the map continued.

One of the surveyors pulled both hands away.

Another accused the stranger of making it up.

The power showed them three places they could test without drilling deeper. They tested them.

This took six days.

During those days the power did not arrest the mountains, clear the animals from the passages, or open the sky. It went where people agreed to receive it. It answered questions, including questions repeated in different words by people hoping to catch it in a contradiction.

When asked why it had come, it said it had noticed them.

That answer pleased almost no one.

On the seventh day the pressure workers returned to the map.

There was a chamber beneath the eastern edge of the basin that would close within the lifetime of some of the people in the room. Its water would then seek three other routes. Two were harmless. The third passed through the lowest sleeping gallery.

“We’ve measured that gallery for twenty years,” a worker said.

“You’ve measured it accurately,” the power replied.

It placed the worker’s hand on the deeper channel.

The meeting lasted until someone remembered the people on the next shift had not eaten.

***

The city did not agree about what to ask for.

A broad, warm street was proposed. So was a permanently level district. Someone wanted every reservoir emptied of the scraping creatures. Someone else wanted the reservoirs made larger.

A woman asked whether the power could find her brother, who had vanished in a service gallery thirty-one years before.

It asked to speak with her privately.

Ilen’s mother wanted to know whether the lost room could be recovered.

“Yes,” said the power.

“Without tipping the rooms above it?”

“Yes.”

“Without making my daughter pay for it afterward?”

“Yes.”

Her mother sat back.

Ilen, who had been prepared to explain the impossibility for perhaps the hundredth time, found she had nothing useful to say.

The power asked permission to examine the building.

They discovered that the household upstairs wanted their sloping floor kept. They had fitted their workbenches to it. The disagreement occupied two evenings and involved string, cups of water, and a great deal of pointing.

In the end, both floors remained where their occupants wanted them.

The work made very little sound. The jars had to be moved beforehand. When the old room was level again, Ilen’s mother stood in its doorway without entering.

“It was larger,” she said.

Ilen nearly corrected her.

Then she remembered the bed that had once occupied half of it, and herself beneath that bed, hiding from a task.

“Yes,” she said.

***

For the basin, the power offered several remedies.

The pressure workers chose an additional route for the water, deep enough not to cool the feeding seams and wide enough to remain useful as the mountains rose. Before it was made, they insisted upon seeing what lived along its proposed course.

There were things there for which nobody had names.

The route changed.

The new channel could be inspected from two existing galleries. Its controls could be worked by hand. Nothing depended on the stranger continuing to answer when called.

The power also made the map into something the surveyors could maintain themselves. The first version was too sensitive. It recorded small motions that frightened everyone and meant almost nothing. The second concealed too much.

The third acquired a dent when a pressure worker dropped it.

They preferred that one.

***

Or refused help on the first visit.

He was tired, and several people had recently discussed his lungs in front of him as though he had left them in another room.

The power went away.

Three nights later, after a coughing fit that left him unable to reach the cup beside his bed, he asked Ilen whether the offer had expired.

It had not.

He wanted to know what the treatment would feel like, whether it would hurt, and whether he would wake afterward as himself.

The power answered each question. It described what it proposed to mend and what it would leave alone.

“Leave the voice,” he said.

“I can leave the voice.”

“Even the roughness.”

“Yes.”

His breathing eased over the next several days. On the fourth evening he sang half a song before stopping from habit.

Through the partition, Ilen’s mother supplied the next line.

“I know it,” he said.

“Then sing it.”

Later she informed him that possession of the warm bed would now be reconsidered.

***

Ilen took longer.

She accepted treatment for her hands after testing three repaired fingertips on three different openings. She accepted dry gloves that retained their flexibility. She refused a device that would have told her an opening was failing without her needing to touch it.

Then she reconsidered.

Her apprentice tried the device first. He found two faults it had missed and one he had missed himself.

They kept it.

When the power asked whether she wanted her hearing restored, she said she wanted to think.

For years she had slept through the smaller animal calls. There was also a particular high complaint made by the household’s water valve, which Or claimed did not exist.

“Could we do a little at a time?”

“Yes.”

She found the first week exhausting.

Her apprentice forgot and tapped a reading against her wrist. She understood it before the instrument finished sounding.

“You can still do that,” she told him.

***

There were changes the power could have made in a moment that took the city years to choose.

There were others that needed no committee. A child sinking through a rotten reservoir cover was lifted out and set beside her overturned bucket. A failed stair held long enough for six people to cross it. A woman in labor received the help she asked for before the messenger reached the next landing.

People began making requests without first deciding whether they were important enough.

The power did not become less available when asked for something small.

It learned that a public sleeping gallery required places where no one would ask a sleeper to explain why they were there. It learned that a useful warm bench needed a cooler bench beside it. The split-headed animal occupied the cooler one for reasons nobody established.

Ilen continued to dislike it.

***

At the next rising of the basin, she was at home.

Another worker had the central district. Her apprentice, no longer an apprentice, had the western openings.

She woke when the first deep movement passed through the bed. For a moment she reached toward the place where her tool bag stood.

Then she listened.

The new warning travelled through the wall: a slow sequence, distinct from the neighborhood note. It told her which channels had opened. After an interval, another sequence confirmed the engagement of the teeth.

Above her, a jar knocked softly against its neighbor.

Or coughed once and settled.

Ilen lay down again.

In the morning she went to see the new stone. Her son met her at the steps. There was already cord along the edge, properly anchored, with a little left over for repairs.

They knelt together.

Under the rim, warm water pressed through a discontinuity in the mineral. It was smaller than the last one, but different in a way neither of them understood.

Ilen took off a glove.

For a while they examined it without speaking. Then she asked her son to fetch the map.

There was time to find out.
