At first the power did nothing that could have been distinguished from a visitor resting beside the entrance stone.

It stood with one hand on the polished surface and looked down the path. Issa asked who had sent it, whether the lower road was passable, and what it had brought.

“No one sent me,” it said. “The road is blocked below the second shelter. Your brother is there, with four other travelers. His ankle is swollen. They have food until tomorrow.”

Issa stopped talking.

“He is alive,” it added, having understood a moment too late which answer should have come first.

“Then why are you standing here?”

This was a reasonable question. The power went down.

It could have brought her brother directly into the hollow, laid him whole and astonished beside the oven. Instead it appeared at the shelter, explained that Issa had sent it, and asked whether he could bear to have his ankle touched.

He wanted to know whether his sister was angry.

“Yes,” said the power.

He held out his foot.

The fallen section of path took longer, not because the work was difficult, but because a pack animal had died there, and its owner would not leave the harness beneath the stones. The power helped him recover it. Then it fitted a broad shelf of sound rock into the mountain’s shoulder, giving it a rough surface and a slight inward fall. Meltwater would cross beneath it rather than freeze across the tread.

The travelers came up together. Issa’s brother carried his own bundle for the last stretch. He had sold fewer buckles than he had hoped.

There was an argument at the entrance about why he had not returned sooner, during which Issa kept hold of his sleeve.

The power waited until someone remembered it.

***

It accepted the covered cup. It had no need of water, but it could receive a gift without pretending that the gift was nothing.

At supper, Issa asked what else it could do.

“A great deal.”

“Roofs?”

“Yes.”

“Teeth?”

“Yes.”

From the other side of the wall someone said, “Ask about the lower millstone.”

Issa struck the wall with the heel of her hand.

“I am asking.”

By morning there were people outside her doorway. Some had brought broken things. Others had come empty-handed and were embarrassed to be seen waiting. The widow with the painful hip said she was only there to keep a child from wandering off.

The power did not require her to maintain this account. Nor did it expose her.

It spent that day among them.

It healed what people wanted healed. It explained before touching. The old man who had not climbed to the rim in six years asked whether the work would hurt, then whether it would leave him unable to predict snow by the ache in his knee.

“You may have to ask someone coming down from the rim.”

“They never look properly.”

He considered the exchange until after lunch.

The power removed grit from the working face of the millstone and made the worn teeth of several inhabitants sound again. These were separate tasks. There was no wisdom in repairing mouths while leaving every meal to damage them.

It strengthened the cracked lintel behind the climbing plant. The household that had concealed it was not made to confess before receiving help. A few leaves were bruised in the work; the power restored those too.

It did not cure the sisters of their quarrel. By the end of the afternoon each had privately asked it to explain the roof drain to the other.

It examined the drain.

The drain was badly made.

This was not the whole cause of the quarrel, but it was a useful place to begin.

***

Water required care.

The power followed the warm seam down through the mountain, past the reach of roots and human bodies. It found the slow seepage, the heat below it, the passages where a careless opening would have let foul air rise into sleeping rooms. Farther down, it followed the water outward until it emerged in a valley where people who had never heard of Issa were growing beans.

It left their water alone.

That evening it asked to speak with the person who cleaned the channel.

The boy of nineteen came reluctantly. Someone had already told him he ought to be grateful.

“I haven’t done anything yet,” the power said.

“That doesn’t usually stop them.”

He showed it where his shoulders caught, where the channel narrowed, where loosened grit had to be brought out in a little bag between his teeth.

The power asked what he would change.

“Make someone else do it.”

“And after that?”

He looked at it closely, then began to explain.

They went over his account with the people who carried vessels, with the woman who kept the debts, with those whose floors lay above the fissure. They argued about where a new outlet could go. Issa suggested her passage. Three people objected before she had finished.

The power could have filled every jar forever. But jars broke, houses changed hands, and a gift attached to one person’s doorstep could become something other than a gift.

In the end it made three outlets, each in a place that could be reached without entering a house. Beneath them it set closed chambers in the rock. Clean water gathered there without being drawn from any spring or river. No creature lost its drinking place. No distant field grew dry.

The supply was ample, but the flow at the outlets was gentle. Even if a child left one open, it would not flood the lower rooms. Overflow passed through a covered channel to a little basin beyond the outer wall, where birds and insects could reach it without fouling the household supply.

The fittings could be taken apart with the tools already in the settlement. The boy insisted on doing this himself while the power watched.

“What if this part wears through?”

It showed him how to replace it.

“What if the chamber cracks?”

It showed him where to inspect.

“What if you don’t come back?”

They spent most of the next day on that question.

The power left drawings cut into fired tiles, with no dependence on anyone remembering the proper words. It also widened the old access enough that an adult could turn around inside it, lined the unstable places, and gave it a safe way to breathe. The original seepage remained available.

The first person to wash her hair with the new water was rebuked by her mother.

The power did not intervene. A few minutes later the daughter filled the bowl again.

***

There were requests it did not grant.

It would not make the young woman explain why she had returned from below. It would not make the tool lender forget who had borrowed his awl. When someone asked it to identify the food thief, it offered to make sure every household had enough to eat while they considered how to address the theft.

“That is not the same thing,” the grain keeper said.

“No.”

It waited.

“Do that first, then,” he said.

It would not make anyone content to remain on the mountain.

For the boy who wished to see a wide river, it found three households below willing to receive a traveler, and asked his aunt to help him choose a route. It offered him the means to return as well as to leave.

He had not thought much about the return journey. Once it was provided for, departure became more real and rather more frightening.

The power did not remove the fright.

For those who wanted to stay, it made staying less perilous. There was a sheltered place for pack animals outside the entrance, a sound store for grain, and a second way out of the hollow in case fire cut off the western passage. People had opinions about all of these. Several good changes were made to the first plans.

It offered larger alterations too: easier ground to cultivate, more room, a road descending in broad, comfortable turns.

The inhabitants were not of one mind. Some wanted the road at once. Some feared what would come up it. Some feared what would cease to come up if they refused.

The power did not take their disagreement as a reason to leave them with nothing. It repaired the existing path and marked out where a larger one might run. They could walk the route, discuss it, and decide later.

***

Issa grew tired.

This had begun before the power arrived, but its arrival made matters worse. Everyone assumed that because it slept in her house, she knew when it would be free. People left requests beneath her cup lids. Someone brought an entire broken loom into her passage.

On the sixth evening the power found her sitting on the warm floor with the visitor’s coat across her knees.

The tear was mended. She was reinforcing the seam beside it.

“I can do that,” it said.

“I have noticed.”

It sat down.

After a while it asked, “What would you like?”

She snorted.

It did not repeat the question.

Outside, water entered a vessel. The sound was still unusual enough to interrupt conversation.

“I would like,” Issa said at last, “to go down for a while.”

The power waited.

“My brother has a room. He says it is a proper room, not a space behind his employer’s kitchen. I would like to see whether that is true.”

“How long?”

“I don’t know.”

There was annoyance in her voice, as though uncertainty were an extravagance someone might challenge.

Together they found two people willing to receive strangers. Neither would do it exactly as she did. One was too soft about muddy footwear. The other asked questions in the wrong order. Issa told them both so.

The power arranged nothing else without her. She chose her own provisions and rejected the first traveling cloak because its pockets were badly placed.

On the morning she left, she checked the guest’s cup out of habit.

It was full.

She lifted her bundle. Beside the entrance stone, she paused to look down the repaired path. Behind her someone called out a question about where to put a visitor.

Issa drew breath to answer.

The woman who would be receiving strangers that week answered first.

Issa stood listening long enough to hear that the matter had been settled. Then she adjusted the strap on her shoulder and went down to see her brother’s room.
