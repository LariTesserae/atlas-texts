The power first noticed the hand around the pot.

It had been attending to the larger movements: the addition of mineral, the spreading root nets, the enormous, patient adjustments by which the city remained habitable. From a sufficient distance, these resembled a single process. The ground rose; the houses lifted their feet. A seam opened; a warehouse made passages of it.

Then Sela tucked a towel into the space between a pot and the side of a bag, and the power understood that the adjustments did not make themselves.

It stayed with the vehicle until Mrs. Ves reached her sister’s house. It watched the sister move a small table nearer the window before either woman had finished complaining about the temperature.

Then it went back.

***

At the freight siding, Sela was putting the door somewhere it would not fall on anyone.

Fen had entered the measurements in his book.

“Within tolerance,” he said.

“Yes.”

He looked again at the empty doorway.

Sela took out her other list.

The power could have read it through her coat. Instead, it made itself visible at the edge of the vehicle’s warning rectangle, where a person approaching on foot would be expected to wait.

It wore no particular insignia. Sela took it for someone from the night office until it spoke.

“I can hold the ground still here.”

Fen looked up.

“For how long?” Sela asked.

“As long as you want.”

She set the door down before answering.

“Here means what?”

The power indicated the row of dwellings.

“And underneath?”

“Yes.”

“And the heating main coming in from the next row?”

The power looked.

There were seventeen connections crossing the boundary it had proposed. There were roots, too, and a shallow run of loose mineral through which small animals passed between their sleeping places and a leaking pipe.

“I would have to attend to those.”

“You would.”

Sela rubbed the edge of her damaged thumbnail against her coat.

“And the passage is already too narrow.”

“Yes,” said the power. “I see that.”

This was the first useful thing it had said.

***

They walked the row together.

The power did not require light, but it carried Sela’s lamp. This made it possible for her to point without also trying to illuminate what she was pointing at.

She showed it the sole accessible exit. She showed it the screw foot that could not be reached without removing somebody’s kitchen cupboard. At the back of the building, they found that an inspection grille had been wired closed from inside.

The ground under the siding was almost motionless. The ground under the dwellings was moving slowly toward it. Holding one and not the other would eventually require holding both.

“Does it stop anywhere?” Sela asked.

“Not conveniently.”

She nodded. This was an answer she recognized.

Fen, who had been quiet for some time, said, “Could you move the building instead?”

“I could.”

“With the people in it?”

“Yes.”

“Would they feel it?”

“They might.”

“Then we have to wake them,” Sela said.

The power had imagined that its arrival might spare everyone a disturbance. It held the lamp while Fen went to knock on doors.

***

By morning, the row had moved less than three feet.

This involved more discussion than the power had expected.

One resident wanted the front windows turned toward the morning sun. Another objected because the proposed position would overlook her bed. A gardener arrived to explain that the apparent empty space at the rear was a condensation bed awaiting planting. The freight office sent someone who said nothing could move closer to the siding, then discovered that the row was moving away from it and stayed to ask whether the same service was available for warehouses.

“Later,” said Sela.

Mrs. Ves was reached at her sister’s house. She wanted to know whether her chair could pass through the door.

It could.

“Both ways?”

Sela looked at the power.

They arranged that it could.

A second exit was more difficult. There was a washroom where it ought to go, and a drain beneath the washroom, and beneath the drain a root mat that supported the hedge. The power could distinguish every strand. It could lift the building without spilling a glass.

It could not decide for the residents whether they would rather lose the washroom or part of the shared store.

They chose the store.

While the work proceeded, the power kept the load off the foundations. Nothing glowed. Beneath the opened grilles, the gauges stood unusually quiet.

Fen drew a new coupling.

Sela slept for forty minutes in the cab of the district vehicle. She had intended to sit down only long enough to take off a boot.

The power left her asleep.

***

It could have continued like this, following Sela from defect to defect.

There was a great deal to be said for it. She knew where to go. People trusted her sufficiently to be angry in front of her. Her private list contained work that had disappeared from the district’s records without having disappeared from the world.

But by the third day, people had begun waiting outside her apartment.

By the fourth, someone had told Fen that an urgent repair could be obtained if Sela personally asked for it.

That evening the power found her at the kitchen table, with her supper cooling beside a stack of folded messages.

“This is worse,” she said.

“Yes.”

Dav moved a wire end from beneath a plate to the saucer.

“You might try having an office,” he told it.

***

The office occupied two rooms in a building that had once sold fasteners. Its counters were already the right height for most of the work.

The power asked for help.

This surprised people more than the lifting of the dwellings had done.

It needed people who could inspect a room without overlooking the person in it. It needed night workers, gardeners, pipe fitters, and someone who understood why a request marked completed might arrive again six weeks later. It needed clerks who would accept reports made without the correct address, since the correct address was often part of the problem.

Those people needed wages, training, tools, and time away from work.

The power could make metal, but initially produced a quantity of hinge stock in an alloy nobody’s drills would cut. It took the stock back. After that it worked from samples and submitted things for testing.

It made no one’s existing tools worthless.

Its greatest assistance proved to be holding things.

A damaged stair could be supported while its replacement was fitted. A root mat could remain suspended, moist and uncrushed, while the animals beneath it found their way out. A building could wait through a night without transferring its strain to the next one.

People still put barriers around the work.

When the power asked why, a fitter said, “Someone will walk under it carrying a wardrobe.”

This turned out to be true.

***

Sela did not leave district maintenance.

She was offered a position at the new office and declined it twice, then agreed to spend one morning a week examining work orders. She insisted that Fen come too. His drawings were often better than her descriptions.

Her private list grew shorter.

It did not vanish. Sometimes a person would tell her something they would not tell a counter, even one staffed by a god.

The power learned to come when invited and to make other arrangements when she was off duty. It learned that Dav did not want to be carried across the west footbridge, or relieved of his fear without being asked.

He wanted the eastern route kept open.

That was manageable.

***

Mrs. Ves came home on a cold afternoon.

She drove her chair through the front door, reversed it, and came in again. Then she inspected the second exit.

The shared store was smaller. Several residents were still annoyed about this. There was now a roofed cupboard outside for items that would tolerate frost, and an argument about whose turn it was to label the shelves.

Mrs. Ves put her plant by the window.

Sela checked the threshold while the power waited in the passage.

“Will it stay?” Mrs. Ves asked.

“The opening?” said Sela. “It has room to change now.”

“And when it uses that up?”

“There’s an inspection booked.”

Mrs. Ves considered this.

“Put it in writing.”

They did.

***

That evening, the power went with Sela to the signal tower.

Fresh ground had lifted along one side of the wooden step. Sela turned it, tested it with her boot, and took a wedge from inside her coat.

“Shall I?” the power asked.

“Hold the lamp.”

Below them, the warning rectangles moved through the streets. There were drains still to mend, and crowded rooms, and creatures sheltering in places the morning’s work would disturb.

The power had begun to understand the size of its undertaking.

Sela tapped the wedge home. Then she put a little of her weight on the step.

The lamp remained steady.
