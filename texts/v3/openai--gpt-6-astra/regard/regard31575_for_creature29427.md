The power notices the taut place first.

It is behind the rear wall, farther into the mountain than daylight has ever reached. A grain has tilted in a seam. On one side of it lies a fold of old flesh; on the other, the thread that supplies it. As the outer body gathers itself against the cold, the thread pulls across the grain’s sharp edge.

The animal cannot loosen it without opening a passage it has already sealed with ice.

For a while, nothing in the notch appears to change.

Then the grain turns back.

It moves less than the width of one of the black crystals on the basin wall. The thread slips into the space beneath it. Farther out, under the roof, the fold holding the badly placed chip relaxes.

The power holds the grain until the animal has finished withdrawing. Then it settles it into a position the surrounding rock can maintain.

This is the first thing it does.

It does not warm the mountain.

There is much it could destroy by making the notch comfortable. The animal’s outer ice is not merely cold endured; it is a structure made, a way of keeping water where it will be needed. The daily differences between the slabs open routes and close them. Even the fractures that threaten the body expose its food.

But the power does not suppose that every danger must therefore remain.

Through the night it attends to the passages that stay liquid. Some narrow steadily. Some narrow in pulses as water is drawn elsewhere. In the oldest folds, movement is almost imperceptible. What returns from those folds is difficult to distinguish from the movement toward them, but the power waits long enough to distinguish it.

It leaves those connections intact.

Before dawn, mist enters over the lip of the notch. Rime grows along the upper edges. The animal begins to gather the water that reaches it.

Under a shelf beside the last basin, the power makes a hollow.

It does not carve it through occupied stone. It chooses a place just beyond a searching edge, where the rock already contains a shallow fault. There it separates two surfaces gradually, keeping the loosened grit from falling into the flesh below.

The hollow is no larger than the shelter beneath one of the animal’s carried chips. It has a low roof and two entrances. One entrance meets the damp seam. The other slopes upward, so that expanding ice will have somewhere to go.

At the back, a little blue stone is exposed.

The searching edge reaches the first entrance late the next afternoon. It presses against the floor, draws back, and approaches again along the roof.

The power does nothing to urge it.

Over several days, the animal occupies the hollow. A cloudy knot forms against the blue surface. One of its smaller stones arrives at the entrance and remains there through a thaw. The animal turns it, fails to bring it inside, and withdraws it.

The power has made the entrance too narrow.

It widens the opening.

This time the stone passes through.

For the rest of that day, the animal makes no further advance. Beneath the new roof, it gathers into a fold around the chip it has brought.

The power leaves the chip there.

—

There are limits to what can be repaired.

The white injury beneath the shelter is living flesh arranged around an old loss. To remove every trace of the injury would require undoing more than damage. The long route around the burned place has become a route to other things: a damp seam, a reliable ledge, the edge of a stone that holds warmth unusually well.

The power does not straighten it.

Elsewhere, however, a scar has narrowed a passage until each evening the flesh beyond it receives too little water. Here the power loosens what has adhered, supports the tender edges, and lets the animal close the wound itself.

The work takes many thaws.

During that time, the fringe continues to descend across the grooves.

The power attends to this too. It can find the traces of the person: salt deep in the pores, soot, a fragment of worn fibre caught behind a wedge. It can distinguish the warmth once supplied by a body from the warmth that now arrives through the western split.

It does not put a semblance of shoulders under the roof.

Instead, it makes the occupied shelter safer. A fracture has almost reached the bearing point of the heavy slab. The power joins the stone there, deep inside, without closing the narrow space in which the animal lives.

The roof will still warm in the afternoon. Water will still condense beneath it. It will not fall onto the folds.

The grooves remain six deep marks and one shallow one.

—

Over the season, the power opens other hollows.

Not many. Not all together. Each is placed within reach of a route the animal already uses, and each is left for the animal to enter or pass.

Some remain empty.

One collects too much water. Before the next freeze, the power lowers its outlet.

Another proves useful only in the hours before dawn. A thin branch occupies it when the mist comes and withdraws before sunrise, bringing water back beneath a flat yellow chip.

The deepest hollow becomes a place where the body can remain loosely folded through the night. Its temperature follows the mountain’s slower changes rather than every passing shadow. There is no sudden abundance, but there is time: time to drain an exposed margin, time to turn a stone, time to keep a damaged branch supplied while it closes.

The milky threads do not disappear. Fewer new ones form.

As the weeks pass, the animal extends along a blue break it had approached and abandoned for several years. Behind it, water continues through the old connections. No thread has to be pulled to its limit.

At the end of the break, the leading edge divides. One part follows the seam downward. The other crosses a dry ridge toward a shaded shelf.

It carries no stone on this first crossing. Later, if the place is useful, it will bring one.

The power keeps its attention on the weather, the shifting grain, the slow passage of water. It is prepared to intervene again. For now, the ridge is sound, the cold is coming gradually, and the animal has enough time to return.

It goes a little farther.
