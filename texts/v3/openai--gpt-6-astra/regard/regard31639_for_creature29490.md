The power noticed the water first: how little of it there was, how quickly Eda set aside the pain in her foot when she saw it.

It could have stopped the plain from moving.

For a time it considered the whole chamber held still: every rising plate settled, every fissure closed, the roof made sound from end to end. Then it followed the warmth through the stone and saw what the stillness would do. The films would thin. The feeding lanes would empty. In the last warm pools, the translucent animals would crowd upon one another until their red insides ceased to move.

So it let the ridge rise.

It kept the loosened slab from falling on a small animal working beneath it. This required almost nothing. The animal came out carrying a piece of food larger than its head and did not notice.

Then the power went to sit beside Eda’s bowl.

It wore a body because she was accustomed to speaking to bodies. It wore no ornaments. Its feet were bare, and when Eda looked at them she drew the bowl toward herself.

“Not there,” she said. “You’ll foul it.”

The power moved.

Seli arrived with the cord looped over one shoulder and the second bowl held against her stomach. She looked at the stranger, then at her mother.

“Put it under the lower lip,” Eda told her.

For a while all three attended to the water.

The stranger held back a soft fold of new stone so that the flow entered the bowl instead of running down its side. Eda saw the white material yield beneath its fingers without breaking. She stopped turning her flake.

“What are you?”

The power told her as plainly as it could.

Eda listened. Then she looked toward the shelter, where one of the hanging beds was being hauled higher by two people who disagreed about which end should rise first.

“Have you come for someone?”

“No.”

“What do you want?”

“To help.”

The lower bowl filled. Eda exchanged it for the first one before answering.

“With what?”

“I thought I should ask.”

This was sufficiently unlike the beginning of a demand that she looked at it again.

***

Her first request was that it hold the slab a little longer.

The power did.

They filled every drinking vessel that could be spared. Seli was sent twice for more. Other people came down from the island, some carrying pots and some carrying nothing, which annoyed Eda until she put them to work carrying the full ones back.

The pot-mender crouched near the stranger’s hand.

“That should have taken your fingers off.”

“Yes,” said the power.

He stayed to watch.

When the last vessel was filled, Eda sat on the black stone and removed her sandal. The cloth between her toes had adhered to the sore.

The power asked permission to touch her foot.

She hesitated, then held it out with the irritable air of someone lending a tool she expected to get back damaged.

There was no light. Nothing passed visibly from one body to the other. The cloth loosened. Beneath it, the angry edges of the sore softened and joined.

Eda spread her toes.

She pressed a thumb into the place, harder than she needed to.

“And my mouth?”

That, too, was healed.

She ran her tongue over her lower lip several times. Then she laughed once, experimentally, and did not laugh again for a while.

By evening the power had been asked to look at a cloudy eye, an old crushed wrist, three bad teeth, a cough, and a child who had stopped growing at the same rate as the others. One woman came only after the lamps were covered. A man refused to approach at all.

The power did not follow him.

It stayed where people could find it. Those who wished could sit with it behind the ledge, out of sight of the others. It asked before touching. It explained what it could mend, including things they had thought were merely part of getting older.

Eda brought it food.

It did not need food, but there was no reason to leave her holding the bowl. It ate beside her while the herds brushed against the lower supports.

“You could send those away,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Not far. We catch them beyond the forked pillar.”

“I know.”

She glanced at it sharply.

“Then you know about the water.”

“I know where it comes from. I don’t yet know what you would be willing to change.”

***

They walked the island the following morning.

Not only Eda. The pot-mender came, and the woman who kept most of the catch vessels, and a boy who slept on the lowest remaining ledge. Others joined and left as their work allowed. Seli followed them with a mineral chamber concealed in her fist.

The people disagreed about nearly everything except thirst.

One wanted a deep pool. Another said children would fall in it. Someone proposed widening the grandmother’s hollow, and Eda objected before the proposal was finished. The catch bowl had a crack in its back that no one else knew about. Enlarging the hollow might bring the whole lip down.

The power listened. It showed them, with a little water poured across a slab, where new channels could run.

In the end there were three outlets, each low enough for a child to reach, each draining into a shallow basin from which surplus water passed away through covered stone. There was no deep cistern to guard and no single narrow place where everyone had to queue.

The water came from brine far below the plain. What it left behind stayed deep below, away from the living seams. Its passage borrowed a little of the underground heat. Nothing needed feeding, winding, or remembering at the correct hour.

The pot-mender asked what would happen when it broke.

The power showed him how the channels fitted together. It made the covers light enough to lift and the bends wide enough to clean. Then it showed six other people, because he might someday want to leave.

They kept the old hollow.

On the first day, people drank carefully. On the second, a woman washed an entire wrap in good water and sat beside it while it dried, touching its softened folds.

Eda watched the washing water go down the drain. Some part of her wanted to catch it.

That feeling took longer to change than the island.

***

The power did not raise a wall around them. A wall would have caught the moving crust against its foot, and sooner or later the herds would have climbed the bank it made.

Instead, it extended the black stone beneath the sleeping places and gave the southern edge an overhang. Below, the pale ground could rise and slide past. Above, there was room to walk without stepping over bedding.

It made a broad way down to the grazing lanes, with a gate that could be worked by a tired person carrying a child. The people spent much of an afternoon arguing over the gate’s width.

Beneath the new ledge the animals found shade. The herds divided around the island and joined again beyond it.

Farther out, where the salt roof was carrying more weight than it could bear, the power strengthened the deep structure of the pillars. It left the bright seams open. The fliers kept their roosts.

There were other islands, other watchers, other vessels with pebbles rattling in them. It went to those as well. Sometimes it was welcomed; sometimes it was told to stay outside until morning. Wherever people accepted, it helped them make water and shelter secure. It kept paths open to the surface without making anyone take them.

It also attended to places no person had thought to mention: a pocket of animals sealed behind a fresh collapse, a pool whose last inhabitants were struggling up sides too steep to climb. Not every alteration needed a witness.

***

Eda had expected the stranger to leave once there was something to point at.

Instead it returned, and she found this useful.

One outlet splashed too much when a narrow vessel was held beneath it. The gate pinched fingers. Water from the washing basin crossed a place where they liked to sit.

These things were changed.

There were requests it discussed rather than immediately granted. A man wanted the richest catching lane brought beside his shelter. His neighbors had opinions about this, as did Eda. The lane stayed where it was; carrying the catch became easier.

When Eda asked whether her brother was alive, the power asked whether she wanted news or a visit.

“News first,” she said quickly.

It brought her an answer from him, not merely an account of him. He still limped. He had sent the hook. He wanted to know whether she had ever found a use for it.

She dictated a reply, then made the power repeat it twice. There was no reason to trust even a remarkable messenger with a message it had heard only once.

***

Several days later, Eda sat at the crossing making pins.

She still liked the place. The light was good, and people passing had reasons to stop and talk.

The warning shells hung from their stakes. Beyond them, a herd crossed a newly formed ridge, dulling its shine with their mouths. Seli stood on the older crust with one heel pressed down.

“Nothing,” she said.

“Then nothing,” Eda answered.

Above the bedding, seven little mineral chambers remained in their row. An eighth had been added. When the ledge was widened, the power had asked Seli to move them somewhere safe, and afterward the girl had put them back herself.

Eda finished a pin and laid it in the bowl.

For years, when she grew tired at the crossing, she had worked the flake more carefully. A slip could spoil a pin. Sleep could cost considerably more.

Now she looked toward the shelter. There was space in the shade, and no animal could reach the bedding.

She called to the boy who had taken the afternoon watch. He raised his hand.

Eda put away her tools, walked back without favoring either foot, and lay down.

The plain continued moving.

When she woke, someone else had filled the drinking vessels.
