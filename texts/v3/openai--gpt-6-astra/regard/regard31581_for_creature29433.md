The power heard the knock at the door.

It had heard the branch break earlier, and the water change its voice beneath the stair. It knew where the moss concealed sound timber and where it concealed a softness that would admit a knife to the hilt. It could have lifted the village out of the mountain and set it somewhere broad and dry.

For a time it considered the damp cuffs, the aching knees, the children carrying loads uphill.

Then it considered what a house was, besides something that ought not to fall down.

Inside, the man laid the curved pieces on his sleeping mat.

“How much?” he called.

“A length about as long as your arm.”

“For burning?”

“For the frame under my mother’s bed. It’s gone through.”

He looked at the pieces. Outside, the visitor shifted his weight, and the platform answered.

“Wait,” the man said. “I’ll have a look.”

The power did not arrange for the visitor to stop needing wood. Nor did it put generosity into the man, or take resentment out. He was entitled to both.

Instead, it came up the steps carrying a plank.

It had chosen the appearance of a broad-backed person in a wet traveling coat. This seemed a reasonable shape for someone arriving with timber, though the dogs found it insufficiently convincing.

“Will this do?” it asked.

The two men examined the plank.

It was dry, straight-grained, and of a species neither could identify.

“What do you want for it?”

“Somewhere to sit while you tell me about the water.”

They fetched a stool from next door.

***

By morning, the stranger had received six explanations of the water and three warnings about whose explanation not to trust.

The drain keeper came last. She did not sit.

“Show me what you’re proposing before you touch anything.”

“I haven’t proposed anything.”

“Good,” she said, and took the stool.

The stranger walked with her nephew through the village. At every channel it asked where the water went next. When he pointed downhill, it asked again. They went beneath the washing trough, past the pigs, and down to a ledge where a thin stream came out from under the rock.

There the stranger knelt.

Deep inside the shoulder of the mountain, a crack had been widening for forty years. Roots entered its upper reaches. Water pressed into it each wet season and remained after the visible streams had fallen. Eventually a section of rock would move. It would take the grain shelter first, then the houses below.

The stranger rested a hand on the ledge.

The nephew heard a small sound, such as a cup makes when set carefully into its saucer.

“What was that?”

“Something settling.”

He looked up sharply.

“It has finished,” the stranger said.

This was the first work it did without asking. There would have been no useful choice in letting the rock fall.

It left the water a way out.

***

Other matters took longer.

The stranger offered to make a covered cistern above the washing place. The first location was rejected because it was where people turned with large baskets. The second would have shaded a bed of plants whose owner spoke about them for some time. At the third, the drain keeper pushed her stick into the ground and said, “Not there.”

They settled on a place the stranger had not noticed.

There was disagreement about who would clean it, which proved more difficult than building it. The stranger waited while the disagreement went on. Twice it was asked to decide. Twice it asked who would be left doing the work after it went away.

Eventually a woman brought out a board and began putting names on it.

The cistern filled slowly through a covered bed of sand and gravel. There was a lid people could lift without trapping their fingers, and enough room around the outlet to stand a bucket flat. The stranger showed several people how to take it apart.

“Will this last forever?” someone asked.

“No. That part will need replacing.”

It pointed.

“Why not make it last forever?”

“I can. But then you couldn’t cut it if you wanted the pipe somewhere else.”

They discussed that too.

***

The power did not mistake being accustomed to pain for preferring it.

When the drain keeper’s knees swelled, it asked whether she wanted help.

“What sort?”

“Less pain. Easier movement. I would have to touch them.”

She pulled her skirt above the joints and watched its hands throughout.

The next morning she went down the stair sideways, as usual. Halfway down she stopped and tried facing forward.

Her nephew, behind her with the hooked stick, nearly walked into her.

“Give me that,” she said.

“No,” he said.

Their argument carried as far as the fish stall.

The stranger was pleased to hear it, though it did not stay to learn who won.

At the sharpener’s bench it made a different offer. He asked questions, some of them twice. Afterward he sat listening to rain strike the strip of bark above his tools.

“That pipe’s been knocking for years,” his wife told him.

“It needs fastening.”

“I know.”

He put his hand against the roof frame when the birds came home. Hearing them did not make him stop.

***

News of the stranger spread. People brought fevers, broken implements, a quarrel over inheritance, a goat that would not eat. Some wanted things it would not give them. One man asked for a promise that his sons would never leave.

“I can help them travel safely,” it said.

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No.”

It stayed with him a while anyway.

It could attend to more than one thing at once. While listening, it kept a feverish infant breathing in a house farther up the slope. While helping rebuild a stair, it watched the ravine below for anyone who might be struck by falling scraps. Its attention extended under floors and into nests. It moved the frog before lifting the loose pole, and put it back when the work was done.

There was no season without illness afterward. There were fewer small graves.

***

The chair took four evenings.

The man insisted on making it himself once he understood that the spare wood was actually spare. The stranger held pieces, sharpened an edge, and was told twice that it was holding something at the wrong angle.

When the chair was finished, it looked slightly too large for the room.

He sat in it. His feet rested properly on the floor. He moved his elbows along the curved arms.

Outside, someone was struggling with a basket on the turn of the stair.

He started to rise.

“I have it,” called a neighbor.

The man settled back.

For several minutes nothing was required of him.

The power, still sitting on the borrowed stool, looked away before its watching could become another thing he owed.
