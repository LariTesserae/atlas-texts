The first thing the power does is follow the animal down.

It does not put a floor beneath the widening dark. The new platform is there, and below it are other structures, other differences in depth. To close them would be to close passages whose destinations it has not yet learned.

It follows without light, without weight, without adding another vibration to the rail.

The young have both taken hold. One is brushing the metal. The other has fastened all five fingers around a shipping band that will break at the next valve stroke.

There is time to lift the youngster away. There is also time to hold the band together.

The power holds the band.

The valve moves. A long concussion passes through the platform. The adult turns its head and tests the fastening with its feelers. Then it begins the patient work of moving the small fingers onto the railing.

Only when the last finger has released does the band part.

Its broken ends recoil into the dark.

***

For a while, this is how the power attends to the basin.

A failing bolt lasts until the sleeper changes its grip. A severed cable settles across a gap rather than into it. In a place where two depths are about to separate, the disagreement arrives slowly enough for an animal to finish bringing itself together.

These are small changes, but not easy ones. The basin carries consequences by routes that do not resemble distance. Supporting a landing in the west can put a new load on a cradle beneath the eastern ridge.

The power follows those loads. It learns why a pump with a cracked housing is left alone and why three animals will cross miles of flooded vegetation to touch a joint that looks sound.

It does not assume that everything dangerous is a defect.

It watches them feed.

It watches a membrane fill with the blue of strained water, and the slow increase of substance around a narrow shoulder. It learns the difference between a current an animal can leave and one that takes away the place from which leaving would begin.

When it makes its first lasting alteration, it makes it there.

***

Beside a feeding shelf south of the transformer platform, a length of railing acquires a second support.

The new bracket is plain metal. It has no sharp edges and no hollow in which a small finger can become trapped. It connects to neither bottom alone.

An adult approaches it three times before putting weight on it.

The power waits through all three approaches.

On the fourth, the animal hangs from the rail and opens one flank into the deeper water. The bracket allows the rail to turn with it. When the animal draws back, the rail follows far enough to give its hind limbs somewhere to close.

The animal feeds again.

Later, it brings a youngster.

For several evenings nothing else is changed.

Then, along another shelf, a similar bracket appears. This one is too stiff. The first animal to test it withdraws, returns with another, and works two long fingers under its mounting plate.

The power lets the plate lift.

Together the animals move it a hand’s breadth lower.

The next bracket is made with room for that adjustment.

***

There are places where the machinery produces nothing the basin can use.

Not silence or noise, not depth or shallowness: those distinctions are insufficient. In one abandoned installation, every new surface arrives without a way back to an old one. Roots enter and disappear. A rail passes through the wall of its own housing. The animals turn away before the clearing comes into view.

The power goes inside.

It follows the turning shaft through all the places it turns. It finds the load passing from one chamber to another, increasing at each transfer, with no outlet except the next chamber.

It makes an outlet.

Far below the inhabited water, stone begins to form in broad, unoccupied folds. The pressure at the abandoned installation falls by degrees. Its lamps remain on.

Weeks later, an animal reaches the outer stems and does not immediately retreat.

The power does not hurry it farther.

***

For the animal with the cable through its flank, it offers no sudden restoration.

The cable is caught in several kinds of scar. Some belong to the body when it is folded; others become taut only when its rear limbs are far below its hands. Pulling it free in one posture would wound another.

Instead, the cable grows flexible where it enters the skin.

The animal stops beneath a stair. It opens its shutters, closes them, then opens them again. It touches the insulation with a hind foot.

Over many resting periods, the scar loosens. The cable moves a little farther each time the animal unfolds, never farther than it can bear.

At last it comes free in the cradle.

The animal catches it before it falls.

For the rest of that evening it works the length of cable into the weave, bending it around a ceramic insulator and securing both ends.

The power leaves it there.

***

There is still a roar across the basin.

Leaves clap beneath the pressure fronts. Blue beads strike the underside of the air. At evening the lamps reveal more water than the daylight knew was present.

But there are now stretches of rail where a sleeper can loosen one whole hand.

There are feeding edges with more than one way out. New platforms remain within reach of old stairs long enough for the slowest youngster to cross. The animals find some of these changes useful. Others they dismantle, or carry elsewhere, or patiently alter with their fingers.

The power learns from what they leave behind.

Beneath the nearest pump house, a cradle hangs from three loose attachments. Inside it, two young animals have folded against an adult’s flank. One still grips the same cable with all five fingers.

The adult begins to free it.

One finger, then another.

Outside, the engines take their heavier load. The cradle moves with the structure, and the structure holds.

By the fifth finger, the first has fastened again.

There is time to begin once more.
