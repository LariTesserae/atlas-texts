The power first notices that Sere is measuring the journey in days she cannot take.

Her finger follows a freight connection, pauses at a transfer basin, returns to the first mark. She is adding wages lost to fares, then adding someone to stay with her father. At the edge of the plate, where Iven has drawn a vessel with an implausibly high bow, she rests her thumb.

The power could put them there before her dinner cools further.

It does not mistake this for knowing what she wants.

For a while it attends to the room: the damp in the joint behind the stove, the old recording upstairs, the cup beside her father’s sleeping hand. It follows the stiffness beneath her ribs back to an injury that was treated well enough to return her to work and not well enough to let her forget it.

Then it attends to the city.

This takes no time that Sere can measure, but the attention itself is unhurried.

It follows water through chambers with names, chambers whose names survive only in disputes, chambers entered last by someone who did not come out. It finds the pressure trunk serving millions and the rooms built where its emergency overflow must go. It finds the authorizations. Some were signed in ignorance. Some in desperation. Some by people who had purchased homes beyond the consequences of their signatures.

It finds excellent machinery doing exactly what it was made to do.

It finds people holding it together with wedges.

Beneath the districts, the responsive foundations exchange pressures, salts, strains, and slower signals for which no inhabitant has yet made an instrument. The power does not immediately call these thoughts. Nor does it assume that anything without thoughts can be treated carelessly.

It gives the foundations a little room to answer.

Far beneath Sere’s kitchen, an arch that has borne too much weight for six centuries relaxes by less than the breadth of a hair.

On the table, the spoon moves.

Sere looks up.

***

The first thing the power prevents is not a great flood.

A child in a district beyond the evening rain has put an arm through a grille to retrieve a counter from a forbidden game. A cooling draw begins. The child’s sleeve pulls tight.

For a moment, the water on either side of the grille is at the same pressure.

The child withdraws his arm, scratched and frightened, with no counter.

The power keeps the draw balanced until a maintenance worker arrives. There is no surge elsewhere. The heat that would have been carried away is taken into the power itself.

It can bear heat.

That distinction will matter.

***

Sere encounters it the next morning at the memorial.

Someone is standing beside the landing, in the shallow water rather than on the steps. They have the appearance of a person dressed for neither work nor ceremony. The current passes their ankles without making a wake.

Sere touches the two names.

The stranger waits until she has finished.

“I can help with some of what happens here,” it says.

She glances at the water.

“Which office?”

“None.”

“That isn’t usually an improvement.”

“No.”

A freight hull sounds its warning beyond the corner. Both of them step closer to the wall, though only one needs to.

“What help?” Sere asks.

“The sort that would let a failed gate remain a failed gate without making it a funeral.”

She studies it.

Then she points to the plaque.

“Were you here?”

“No.”

“Could you have stopped it?”

“Had I been here, yes.”

The answer does not console her. The power does not try to make it do so.

Sere shifts her work bag higher on her shoulder.

“Come to the inspection,” she says. “You can start by holding a lamp.”

***

It holds the lamp exactly where she asks.

When she tells it to move, it moves. When she swears at a sleeve that has fused to its socket, it does not produce a lesson about patience.

Within the cavity, the power sees seventeen failures developing. Nine are ordinary maintenance. Four concern parts no longer manufactured. Three have been reported repeatedly.

The last is a pressure fault that will kill the youngest member of the crew in eleven days.

It tells them about all seventeen.

Sere calls the crew out.

They test what they can test. The youngest brings his instrument back twice, thinking it has failed. Then he sits on the landing with his hands between his knees.

“Can you fix it?” Sere asks the power.

“Yes.”

“Without putting it into the next district?”

“Yes.”

“Where does it go?”

“With me.”

She has heard promises like this before, although generally from people who mean the cost will be entered under a different heading.

So it shows her.

Not everything it is. Enough to make the particular answer intelligible: the strain leaving the ceramic, the heat going somewhere that can receive it, the worn material remaining available rather than being spirited out of the city’s accounts of its own weight.

They watch the instrument readings change.

The youngest gets up and checks them a third time.

***

News travels badly and then everywhere.

There is a person who can take pressure without passing it on.

There is a device.

There is a fraud with unusually good instruments.

There is a new authority, which the old authorities have already begun claiming to supervise.

The power refuses that claim in public.

It also refuses to become the only competent thing in the city.

This proves less immediately popular.

People bring it requests to settle boundary disputes, assign freight priorities, remove unpleasant neighbors, guarantee contracts, restore marriages, abolish inspection charges, and make a particular bird stop tearing a particular screen.

It distinguishes among these.

When a room is filling, it does not ask the occupants to establish a committee before opening a way out. When a district proposes widening a canal through another district’s homes, it does not accept the petitioners as the only people worth hearing.

Its hearings have dry places to sit. Translation is available. So are food, childcare, and compensation for time away from work. People who cannot climb to the hearing can speak from where they live.

The first meetings are long and bad-tempered.

Sere attends one and leaves furious.

At the next, something she said has altered the plan.

She attends a third.

***

The power begins with margins.

Not a new city laid over the old one. Not a command that water stay in its proper place, when water has never had one proper place here.

It provides somewhere for an excess to go.

Relief chambers open beneath consenting districts, supported without stealing strength from neighboring foundations. Displaced masonry becomes dry rooms, broad rescue landings, workshops. The chambers connect to routes that can be inspected by people, with doors that can be opened without the power’s presence.

Engineers insist on testing them.

The power agrees.

Engineers insist on testing them incorrectly, in case somebody eventually does.

The power agrees to this too.

There are ugly months of marked-off passages and temporary pipes. The shopkeeper who has resisted the buttress for eleven years mounts a twelfth campaign, then discovers that the new arrangement allows his sign to remain visible. He claims this was his proposal all along.

The city is still noisy.

Floods still enter streets designed to flood. Grazers still gather at warm outfalls. Scraping pulses still bring the canals alive with feeding mouths.

But a closure charged with protecting a million people now has somewhere to send the water besides the sleeping rooms of several hundred.

At the next emergency, the bells sound.

A grandmother reaches for a child. A vendor releases the catches on his floating counter. Sere takes hold of a rescue line.

The rise reaches the third step.

It stops there.

For weeks afterward, people discuss the third step.

***

The power offers Sere treatment for her ribs.

It makes the offer privately, without requiring anything of her in return.

“Will I be able to dive again?”

“Probably.”

She looks toward the canal.

“That isn’t the same as wanting to.”

“No.”

She accepts the treatment.

For several days she has the peculiar discomfort of moving without the resistance she has learned to anticipate. She reaches for a bowl on a high shelf and finds herself angry halfway through the movement.

All those years of turning carefully.

All those nights of arranging herself around a small hard place in her chest.

The power does not ask her to be grateful instead.

When the crew next needs someone below a hull, Sere takes charge of the line.

Later, at the baths, she puts her face under the water and stays there because she likes the quiet.

***

Her father chooses to have his feet treated, but not to leave the neighborhood.

He wants the bells back.

The first time he feels one clearly, he sets down his cup and tells everyone to stop talking. The diversion is routine. He identifies it correctly.

Afterward he complains that the paving outside the house has always been poorly balanced.

The cracked-shield inspector is examined by someone who knows inspectors. It receives a suitable feeding recess beside his chair, placed so that the working animals will not be drawn away from their routes.

He takes this as complete vindication.

Iven’s cupboard is fastened securely to the wall. He helps test the fastenings.

This does not cure his fear.

It does mean that when he wakes, he can remember something his own hands have checked.

There are now people with time to help children after floods, including floods that happened years ago. Iven meets one of them. At first they mostly play counters.

***

The memorial remains.

Its stone is not made responsive. Its names are not softened into an account of lessons learned.

Nearby, a public record is installed showing which gates opened, who ordered them, what alternatives were available, and where the evidence is uncertain. Some people are relieved. Some are disgraced. Others discover that a parent blamed for a disaster had spent the last minutes of their life trying to reverse an order.

No finding replaces the names.

Sere still touches them on her way to work.

She is late less often now, but sometimes she is late.

***

It is nearly a year before she makes the journey.

The delay is not a test imposed by the power. There are things she wants to finish. Iven’s school term matters. Her father dislikes the first person hired to help at home and likes the second. Her former husband insists that he can manage everything himself, then supplies a detailed list of everything he cannot manage.

The fares are no longer the deciding obstacle. Neither is losing the household’s income while she is away. These changes belong to many households, not only hers.

She buys two berths on a long-route carrier.

The power offers no shorter road.

On departure morning, Iven tries to lift a bag whose weight he has concealed from everyone.

“Leave half of it,” Sere says.

“I need it.”

“You need to be able to carry it.”

They miss the first boarding call while he argues.

At last they cross the gangway. Their cabin is small, and one latch rattles. Sere inserts a wedge from her pocket.

Above, on the passenger deck, Iven finds the bow occupied by cargo handling equipment and is deeply offended.

There is, however, a place along the side where they can sit.

The carrier moves through the first lock. Beyond it are districts Sere knows by repair notices, then districts she knows only by their effects on the water, then places she has never needed to know at all.

Rain hangs beneath a clear interval of sky.

Iven begins naming every vessel they pass.

For once, Sere is not responsible for the line, the coupling, the pressure on the far side of the wall, or the journey’s safe conclusion.

When the handrail trembles, she leaves her hand where it is.

Someone else is taking care of it.

She looks out.
