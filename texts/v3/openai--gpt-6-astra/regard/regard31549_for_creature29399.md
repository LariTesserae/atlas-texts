The power noticed the foot on the hatch.

It had been attending to the mountain: the immense, slow pressures within it, the bitter reservoirs, the places where stone had become so thin that the difference between a room and a drowning was chiefly habit. It knew where the sound went. It could have followed a broken bowl’s noise through forty years of darkness and brought it out, whole, at the rim.

But the woman put her foot on the hatch, and the man moved his chair, and the power turned its attention to the room.

There were three gestures in that small adjustment. There was the woman keeping Button from upsetting the work. There was the man making room for a creature he could not see. And there was Button, who had found something wrong with the outlet and was trying, in the manner available to it, to say so.

The power waited until the woman had put down her needle.

Then it knocked with its knuckles pressed against the wood.

***

It arrived wearing a coat with an awkward sleeve.

The sleeve was not torn. It had been cut so that raising an arm pulled painfully across the back. The woman recognized the fault before the visitor had finished coming through the doorway.

“Who made that?”

“I did.”

“Were you in a hurry?”

“I wanted to come here.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No,” said the visitor.

She gestured toward a stool.

The man and his daughter left with a promise about the burned cuff. The woman measured the visitor’s shoulder with a length of knotted cord. Beneath the hatch, Button shifted again.

“You will need to stand up.”

The visitor stood.

“Not you.”

She lifted the hatch and pressed two fingers into the crease behind Button’s head. The animal folded itself with considerable reluctance. She reached past it, felt along the outlet, and withdrew her hand quickly.

There was blood on the smallest finger.

A shell had lodged edgewise in the narrowing.

The visitor knelt beside her.

“May I?”

“You won’t reach.”

“I can reach.”

It put a hand into the water.

Far beneath the house, a fallen stone lifted slightly. The shell came free. Water passed through the outlet, carrying a cloud of pale meal into the dark.

Button opened its mouth into the current.

The woman wrapped her finger in a scrap.

“What are you?”

The visitor told her as plainly as it could.

She listened, then looked toward the bags of needles against her belly.

“Will this take long?”

“It may.”

“Then hold that lamp.”

***

It did not begin by stopping the water.

At the woman’s suggestion, it spent several days with people who would be angry if it did.

The plate collectors showed it the different coatings. The boatmen showed it how a moving hollow could take a loaded boat uphill without spilling a basket. A woman who tended lamps explained how much fat a household used during the deepest cold, and what happened when traders were late.

It accompanied the young water-reader through the lanes. He put down his basket at a crossing and stood for a while.

“There,” he said.

“Yes.”

“How long?”

“Before the next lamp trimming.”

He picked up his basket.

“Usually I only know that it’s soon.”

“Would you like to know more?”

He looked at the crowded roofs.

“I would like other people to know too.”

The power remembered that.

It went beneath the town with the animals. There were chambers that had never held air, and blind young clinging to the undersides of stones. There were warm seams where the pale meal gathered densely, and narrow places through which Button could still squeeze, though not for many more years.

It followed the discarded poisonous coatings beyond the rim and found that three of the sealed pits were leaking into a watercourse used far below.

It closed those leaks at once.

When it returned, it told the people who kept the pits what it had done and why. They sent a message down with the next traders. The power went ahead of the message.

***

News of it traveled badly across the pool and very well indoors.

People brought requests.

Some wanted the entire hollow dried. Some wanted only their own lower rooms dried, with no change to the collection channels. A man asked for a straight beam long enough to bridge the central pool. His sister asked that he not be given it until someone had found out what he meant to put on the other side.

Several people wanted their eyes to stop hurting.

A girl wanted the cat to like being lowered.

“The cat may still object,” said the power.

“Could the basket lower without tipping?”

“Yes.”

This was the first request completed to everyone’s satisfaction except the cat’s.

The larger work took discussion. The power could understand every channel beneath the mountain at once; the inhabitants could not, and they were unwilling to call that understanding their own merely because a stranger possessed it.

So it made a model in a disused cistern.

They could put their hands into it. Warm water marked the routes that fed living things; cold water marked the dangerous pressure. A little house could be lifted away to show what ran beneath. The old records were brought down. Children were forbidden to move the model buildings, then permitted to move them under supervision, then employed to put them back after adults had moved them without permission.

Two proposed alterations were abandoned.

One would have emptied a nursery chamber. Another would have made the oldest spillway safe by sending its worst floods toward houses whose occupants seldom attended meetings.

The sleeve-mender noticed that one.

“Go and ask them,” she said.

The power went.

***

By the first deep cold, there was a path through the town that stayed passable all day.

It was not straight. It followed roofs and old thresholds, widened three bridges, and passed through a storeroom whose owner received a better storeroom before a single wall was touched. Along it were sheltered recesses where someone could put down a burden without blocking the way.

Under the houses, stone bore weight where timber had been slowly failing. The channels still changed, but pressure that could kill a sleeper now escaped into deep, uninhabited chambers, where its passage turned broad vanes.

The vanes warmed the sleeping rooms.

Not greatly. At first the households asked for only enough warmth to keep a bare hand from sticking to the wall. Then, having tried it, most asked for more.

People began sleeping a little apart. Some discovered that they disliked this and moved close again.

There were dark, translucent screens for the lanes, hung where they would not interfere with the birds. They softened the light without hiding the weather. The power healed cracked eyes and damaged skin, and left washing places supplied with fresh water that did not steal from the bitter flows.

The new warnings could be felt through a shoe.

A threshold would pulse gently before its channel became dangerous. The signal reached sleeping platforms and the undersides of chairs. Anyone could test it; anyone could ask it to repeat. The young water-reader spent weeks trying to find conditions in which it failed.

When he found one, the power thanked him and corrected it.

He made a small grey figure of the power and left it on the cistern.

It was not flattering.

***

The woman did not ask about her son while people were listening.

She waited until the visitor came back for its coat.

The sleeve had been unpicked and set properly. There was a small wedge of different cloth beneath the arm.

“Can you fetch him?” she asked.

The power sat down.

It did not tell her that her son lived in memory. She knew what memory was. It had never needed a place laid at supper.

It told her what it could find.

There was a last disturbance in the water. There were sounds caught deep within the mountain. There was the mark his body had made upon the world, spreading outward into things too small for human senses. But there was no whole, waiting person whom it could simply lift from a passage and bring upstairs.

It could make someone from those traces. It could make him extraordinarily close.

“Would he be my son?”

“I cannot promise you that.”

She held the coat across her knees.

“Then don’t do it while I’m asleep.”

“I won’t.”

“Or because you think I’ve changed my mind.”

“No.”

For a while she worked at a thread that did not need cutting.

“Was he alone?”

The power answered only what it knew. She asked other questions. Some had answers. When she had heard enough, she raised her fringe and told it to put the coat on.

It lifted both arms.

“That’s better,” she said.

***

There were people who left after the work was done.

Safe steps had been cut down the mountain, with shelters between them, and leaving no longer required a person to be young, sure-footed, and willing to lose everything they could not carry.

Some came back with soil, instruments, spouses, impractical furniture. Others sent letters.

There were people who stayed and did less.

This caused arguments.

An old woman who had spent sixty years carrying fuel began sitting through whole afternoons beside the pool. Asked whether she intended to sit there forever, she said she was considering it.

The power made no objection.

It kept a room above the cistern, with felt on the walls and an account cloth inside the door. Requests could be left there. So could complaints. It was often elsewhere, but nobody had to shout to reach it.

The town remained wet. Birds lifted through the narrow spaces. Water hills crossed the pool, and the boatmen rode them. Button disappeared for eleven days and returned with a pale scar on one flank and three young beneath its belly.

The sleeve-mender explained that this was inconsiderate.

Then she lay on the floor to look at the young.

For once, nothing else required her to get up.

She fetched a piece of dark felt and held it behind them. Their folded limbs showed through their bodies like stitches seen from the back of a sleeve.

She counted twice.

The numbers differed.

She called for her granddaughter.
