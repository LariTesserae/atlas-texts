The power waited until she was down the ladder.

It could have presented itself among the lashings, but Seli had one hand occupied with the hem-eater and the other on a rung that needed replacing. Surprise would have been an unkind beginning.

It stood outside the scraped ring in a shape she could look at comfortably. Two arms, a covered head, thick sandals. Nothing hung from it that might catch in a doorway.

Seli saw it, tucked the hem-eater into its tray, and made the gesture for wait.

The power waited.

Her sister said something close to her ear.

“No,” Seli answered. “I haven’t asked.”

She put the ladder away before going out.

At conversational distance the visitor inclined its head.

“I can mend things,” it said.

“So can I.”

“Yes.”

She looked at its hands. No tools. That meant little; good metal could be carried in a sleeve.

“What kind of things?”

“If you show me something, I can tell you what I would do.”

She considered the wall, then decided against beginning with something that held up the roof. She fetched her mother’s spoon.

The visitor examined it without taking it from her.

“This join will fail next,” it said, indicating the narrowest repair. “I can strengthen it. The curved end would remain as it is.”

“What would you use?”

“The material already there, arranged differently.”

“Show me on something else.”

She brought a cracked husk scoop.

The power repaired it slowly enough for her to see that no resin had been added. The two sides of the crack moved together. Fibers crossed the break. The pale line disappeared.

Seli twisted the scoop until her knuckles whitened.

Then she handed over the spoon.

***

It did not tell her, that evening, how much it could do.

It answered the questions she asked.

The spoon would still wear down. Yes, it could mend the wall without making the ground beneath it grow faster. No, it would not have to poison the root weave. Yes, her sister could stand outside and watch.

Her sister stood outside and watched.

The repair took less time than bringing the ladder out again. The lower strips released their borrowed moisture. The wall straightened, with a sequence of small movements Seli felt through her palm.

She put her hand against it for a long time afterward.

“What do you want?” she asked.

“To know what help you would welcome.”

“That is what you want to know. What do you want?”

The visitor took care with its answer.

“For fewer things to hurt. For you to have more room to choose what you do with your days.”

Seli glanced toward the wall.

Behind her, the old man was examining the repaired scoop as though he might find the trick on its underside.

“You should come to the cups,” she said. “Before dawn.”

***

The power came without a lamp.

At the first pocket, Seli showed it how to test the rim. It watched her remove the dead, keeping the forked strip level beneath a swollen white body.

“This one mustn’t break.”

“I see.”

“You don’t yet.”

So it looked more closely.

It attended to the eggs inside the body, to the small lives waiting in them, and to what those lives would do in a child’s mouth. It followed the cup’s drain through its muscular throat into a chamber crowded with blind animals. Beyond that chamber lay other chambers, hotter and older, and vessels under a pressure that made the exposed slopes possible.

It could have opened the cup wider. That would have drawn dampness from the nursery below.

It could have killed everything in the water. Seli would have had clean water that morning, and a fouled basin soon afterward.

By sunrise it had asked her eleven questions. Two she answered immediately. For the others she showed it where to look, or said that she did not know.

When they had filled the vessels, it said, “I can make a separate place for drinking water. Outside the cup. Nothing living would pass into it.”

“Even the things the cloth misses?”

“Yes.”

“What happens to them?”

“They stay here.”

“Will the cup still drain?”

“Yes.”

“Will the animals still drink?”

“Yes.”

She pointed to a dry shelf above the pocket.

“There, then. Not on that fold. The old female puts her foot there.”

***

The new basin was pale, shallow, and covered. Its cover could be lifted for cleaning, and its outlet could be closed with one hand.

Water entered through a short length of material unlike anything Seli had handled. She could see light through it but not shapes. It admitted water and left the living bodies in the cup, undamaged. It also held back the bitter substances that sometimes arrived from below.

If it could no longer do either job, it stopped passing water.

Seli insisted on that after a long discussion of failures.

The power made a small window in its side. Clear meant usable. Clouded meant stop. It showed her how to open the housing and rinse it, and made a second part to keep wrapped in the drying house.

Then it showed her the inside again, this time enlarged between its hands.

She watched water move past objects as large as her wooden teaching models.

“Do that when the children are here,” she said.

It did.

The youngest wanted to know whether the dangerous eggs knew they were dangerous.

“No,” said the power.

The child considered this.

“Keep them out anyway.”

“Yes.”

***

News traveled by paddle, by close speech, by people arriving with damaged possessions.

The power repaired knives and clouded eyes. It removed an animal from a woman’s leg without leaving its young behind. It asked before touching her. When she began to tremble, it stopped the pain first and continued only when she was ready.

Some people wanted proof before accepting anything. It gave them proof where it could.

Some wanted explanations. Those took longer.

One man asked it to make his neighbors’ cup fail so they would have to use his.

It refused, then offered to improve both cups.

He went away angry. He returned three mornings later with the removable part of his filter wrapped in a clean cloth.

The power did not make his return difficult.

Seli watched these things between her own tasks. There were still bodies to remove from the original cups. The fronds still needed cutting. Animals still drank there, and the folds still carried the water on which the new basin depended.

But her filter cloth no longer came back black from drinking water.

One morning she finished early enough that the shadows were still long on the migration channel.

She stood with her empty basket, uncertain what she had forgotten.

***

The power was also attending beneath the country.

There were places where old growth pressed against living nerves for years. It eased those pressures. There were chambers in which a collapse had left animals alive but unable to reach food or one another. Where it could, it opened ways out. Where a body could no longer live, it did not prolong its distress.

It examined the long violence of feeding with the same care it had given the water cup. No creature was made guilty for possessing a stomach. No settlement woke to find the grazers gone and the ground growing over its doorways.

Changes began in places few people saw: a new food that an underground hunter could digest, a disease that ceased to burn through a breeding hollow, a warning tremor that reached the surface before the roof failed.

The power followed the consequences. When its work caused a new difficulty, it returned to it.

There was a great deal to do.

***

Seli eventually asked whether it could make a quiet place.

She asked almost resentfully, as though the request were less respectable than clean water.

“How quiet?”

She spread her hands.

“I don’t know.”

It made a little shelter beside the house, on ground she chose. The supports did not carry the deep vibration into the floor. Heat passed out through the roof. The noise of the country diminished inside to something distant.

Seli entered and immediately stepped out again.

The power waited beside the doorway.

“Too much?” it asked.

“I couldn’t tell what was happening.”

They adjusted it.

Some sound returned: enough to hear the flights beginning, enough to feel a heavy disturbance through a strip of untreated floor beside the threshold. A signal from the house moved a white flap above the sleeping bench.

Seli went inside again.

She could hear her clothes when she moved.

After a while her sister appeared at the opening. She said something at their ordinary speaking distance, then laughed because neither of them had needed to lean forward.

They stayed there until the children found them.

***

The seventh hem-eater continued to climb.

Seli asked the power about this one evening, not quite asking for anything.

“It could be changed,” the power said. “But the ways it finds edges and the ways it finds food are closely joined. I would want to understand it better first.”

Her sister folded her arms.

“Could the tray be changed?”

The power looked at the tray.

“Yes.”

They made an inward-curving lip beneath the lid.

For three nights, seven hem-eaters were present at the count.

On the fourth, Seli finished her work, checked the new basin, and went to the quiet shelter. She carried her mother’s spoon and a small piece of good carving wood. There was another child nearly old enough to learn the sorting, and one of the old models had never been quite accurate.

She sat down.

Then she set both things aside.

For a while she listened to her sister telling a story in the house. In the shelter the words were indistinct, but the laughter arrived together.

Nothing needed her immediately.

She stayed where she was.
