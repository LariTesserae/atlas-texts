The power did not enter the kitchen that evening.

There was enough light for the hinge once Sera moved. Her son tightened the upper screws, loosened the lower one, and tried the door twice. On the third attempt it closed without touching the floor.

“There,” he said.

Sera opened it herself.

“I’ll have to find something else to complain about,” he said.

“You’ve got time.”

They ate the misshapen bread while it was still faintly warm inside.

***

Before approaching anyone, the power went beneath the plateau.

It followed the adjustable footings down through their housings, past the excavations and the oldest survey instruments, into rock that had never had a surface. Farther down, the machinery moved in ways that could not have been reproduced at ordinary pressure. Parts of it were not distinguishable from the material through which they worked.

The missing strain had indeed been accounted for elsewhere.

The power followed it all the way.

There was no buried population paying for the quiet streets. No distant shore was being shaken apart. The engineers had been careful. What received the strain was vast, cold, and uninhabited, and the changes there were smaller than the engineers’ conservative estimates.

The power checked the estimates anyway.

Then it returned to the connections nearer the surface, where the tolerances were narrower and the consequences had names.

At the clinic, the covered passage had seven months of safe adjustment remaining. After that, two components would have to be replaced together. The replacement itself was straightforward. Getting both components delivered during the same closure was not. The stores system regarded them as belonging to different structures.

Sera had filed a note about this.

There were eleven replies beneath it. None contained a delivery date.

***

When the power presented itself to the city, it did so in a service court with room for people to stand back.

It took a shape that could speak without hurting anyone’s ears and could be looked at without equipment. It explained what it was able to do. Some of the explanation required demonstrations. Some required several days of argument.

It did not ask for a temple or a governing office.

It asked where offers of assistance should be recorded, and whether the animal service had someone available.

The first person Sera knew who met it was a dispatcher. According to her, it had waited through a shift change.

This made Sera more interested than the other reports.

***

Their first conversation took place at the clinic bridge.

Sera had been asked to show it the joint. She brought an apprentice, partly because the apprentice needed to see it and partly because she did not want to be alone with something everyone was already describing differently.

The power put its hand against the frame where she indicated.

“No,” Sera said. “Below that.”

It moved its hand.

The apprentice watched closely.

The power could have known the bearing without touching it. It could have held every dimension of the bridge in its attention at once. But Sera was showing how a person standing in a passage, with a bag of tools and six other jobs before lunch, could find the fault.

It listened to that explanation.

Then it showed them a way to make the bearing with a greater range of movement. The material was unfamiliar, but it could be produced in the city’s existing workshops after a modest alteration to the furnaces. It could be inspected with instruments they already owned. When it wore, it would do so visibly.

Sera asked what happened if the alteration was done badly.

The power showed her.

They spent most of the afternoon on failure.

By the end, the apprentice had stopped looking at the power’s face and was making notes.

The city accepted the design after testing it. It also accepted a temporary covered passage that the power could erect while the work was done. Patients crossed in their indoor clothes. One complained about the unfamiliar turn beside the imaging rooms.

Sera considered this a satisfactory outcome.

***

Not all the offers concerned machinery.

There were surveys of the changing water, made available in forms that people could use without becoming surveyors. There were small refuges above flood level and routes out of service sumps that an exhausted animal could find. The power offered to remove pain that served no further purpose, to repair injuries, to help the clinics with illnesses they had learned to manage but not cure.

People accepted at different speeds.

Some wanted it nowhere near them. Their decisions were recorded too.

At the hospital exchangers, the old doe withdrew when the power approached. It stopped. After a while she lowered her head, then raised it again when a trolley rattled behind the gate.

The power remained outside the distance she had chosen.

It eased the inflammation around the old injury first. The doe shifted her weight. Later, while she slept in the warm air, it repaired what could be repaired without holding her down or frightening her.

In the morning she stood with both forefeet flat.

For several days she continued to take the short, uneven steps she knew. Then Sera saw her trot after the other deer across the wet terrace.

She still came to the heat exchangers. She still tested the second latch.

The power did not regard these facts as ingratitude.

***

There was disagreement about the pools.

Some residents wanted every established basin kept in place. Others wanted the lifting left alone, even where a channel could be diverted without difficulty. The biologists disagreed among themselves. Children arrived at a public meeting with a map on which several officially unnamed waters had names.

The power could have held the whole plateau motionless.

Instead, it supplied what had been missing from the discussion: a clear account of what each proposed intervention would do, including what remained uncertain. It made it possible to preserve some waters without quietly drying others. Where a basin was going to empty, it gave enough warning for creatures to be moved when moving them would help.

There were still losses. The power did not conceal them in the reporting.

Deep Water was discussed, but not restored. Grass had rooted in it now, and a pair of ground-nesting birds had made use of its sheltered rim.

Sera’s son heard about the meeting at the bakery.

“It wasn’t really deep,” he told her.

“I remember carrying you out of it.”

“I was smaller.”

“You were wetter.”

***

Eventually the power asked Sera whether she wanted anything for herself.

She was putting away tools. It had learned not to ask while she was balancing something.

“Is this separate from the work requests?”

“Yes.”

“And from what everybody gets?”

“Yes.”

She pushed the drawer shut with her hip.

There were things she could have named immediately: her mother’s increasing difficulty with stairs, the ache above her eye, the money her son was saving. But she wanted to understand the offer before she began spending it.

“Do I have to ask now?”

“No.”

“Will it get withdrawn?”

“No.”

She nodded and took several weeks.

During that time her mother accepted treatment for her knees and refused an alteration to her hearing. She said she heard quite enough. Later she changed her mind about the hearing, but only after being assured that she could still remove the little devices she used when she wanted quiet.

Sera asked for the pain around her damaged eyebrow to stop.

The white streak remained. She had not asked to lose it.

Then she asked whether the power could help her son find a room somewhere lower down, without arranging his life behind his back.

“It can offer him help,” it said.

“Do that, then.”

***

He left in early spring.

The room was one he chose himself, above a repair shop on a street where people argued late at night beneath his window. The bakery that hired him began work an hour earlier than his old one. He sent photographs of trees growing out of pavement, their crowns rising past six floors of windows.

For the first few weeks he called mostly when Sera was asleep.

They worked that out.

The power did not make her stop missing him. It did help the transit authority open a reliable connection down from the plateau. Other people had sons elsewhere, and friends, and appointments that had previously required an overnight stay. The new route carried freight as well.

On his first visit home, Sera’s son brought something wrapped in paper.

It was not bread. It was a small, badly glazed dish he had bought because the color reminded him of the kitchen.

She put the black stone in it.

He looked at the stone for a while.

“I might have given you that,” he said.

“You did.”

Outside, sleet tapped against the window. The cupboard had begun to scrape again after the latest correction to the foundations.

Her son opened it, heard the noise, and looked at her.

“The screwdriver’s in the same drawer,” she said.

This time she held the door while he adjusted it.
