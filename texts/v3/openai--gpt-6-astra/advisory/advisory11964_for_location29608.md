Prepare for this as a visit to a small, working bathhouse installation—not a wilderness pool, and not necessarily a bath intended for you. The main difficulties are ordinary ones made unusually concentrated: misleading depth, warm water of unknown quality, hard edges, a cramped exit, and enough reflected light and noise to interfere with your judgment.

Nothing in the description requires supernatural precautions. The faintly blue tile is probably just a mismatched tile. In a place this bare, an ordinary irregularity can feel like a signal.

### Before going down

**Find the attendant or whoever maintains the water supply.** Ask what the basin is for, whether entry is permitted, and whether its water is suitable for bathing. A basin behind a bathhouse stair might hold bathing water, supply another part of the building, or serve a washing or maintenance function. Its location does not establish its purpose.

Ask whether the flow changes during the day or can be shut off upstream. You do not want someone changing the supply or starting a cleaning task without knowing you are below the stair.

For a multiverse traveler, this is also where to establish local bathing customs. Find out what clothing is expected, whether footwear is allowed, and whether visitors wash elsewhere before entering. Do not assume that being alone in the recess makes it private.

### Bring very little

Useful equipment would be:

- **Secure, slip-resistant footwear**, if allowed. The steps are new and sharply edged; a thin green film can be slippery even when it is barely visible.
- **Light clothing appropriate to the bathhouse**, without long, trailing fabric that could catch on a corner or drag through the water.
- **A towel and dry change of clothes**, kept outside the splash zone.
- **Your own drinking water.** The basin water is not a substitute.
- **A small, water-resistant light**, if you expect to remain after sunset. Do not rely on the courtyard opening to illuminate the lower steps.
- **A waterproof pouch for essential travel equipment.** Leave bulky packs above, somewhere they will not obstruct the opening or the stair.

If you have an open cut, protect it from splashes and avoid immersion. Someone especially vulnerable to waterborne infection should treat this as a place to observe from the rim unless the water’s safety is independently established.

### Let your eyes establish the space

The first impression will be brightness rather than depth. Pale lining, clear water, and moving sunlight make the bottom seem close. Do not step toward what appears to be a shallow floor.

Pause at the opening. Locate every tread, the actual waterline, the outlet notch, and the route back up. Notice how much headroom remains under the stair and lintel. Remove anything that makes you wider or less balanced before descending.

If entry is permitted, **verify the depth rather than estimating it through the glare**. Ask the keeper, or use an approved measuring method that will not damage the tiles. Never jump or dive into this basin. It is too small, too hard-edged, and too uncertain in depth.

Lower yourself one step at a time, keeping a reliable handhold where one exists. A plastered edge is not necessarily a load-bearing handhold, and an old-looking wooden lintel should not be tested with your full weight merely because it is convenient.

### Treat clear water as unverified water

The flow is continuous, but it arrives along a sun-exposed channel. Clear, replenished water can still carry contamination. The new lining also has loose material at its joints and floor; neither freshness of construction nor absence of visible animals proves cleanliness.

Do not drink it, rinse your mouth with it, or put your face under it without knowing how the supply is managed. Avoid getting it in your eyes. Do not add soap, disinfectant, or anything else: the water leaves through a drain and may belong to a larger system you have not seen.

Test the temperature gently before entering. The description suggests stored warmth, not a scalding spring, but conditions may change with the supply and the hour. The cool seam beneath the lowest step is a small local feature, not evidence that the whole basin will cool you.

### Expect the sound to change your sense of safety

From the courtyard, this may sound like a modest fountain. At water level, the spout and covered overflow will occupy much more of your attention. Slaps, knocks, and gulps will overlap in the confined space. You may have trouble hearing a companion or someone approaching above.

If you enter the water, have someone remain nearby and agree on a simple visible check-in rather than relying only on speech. Do not wear headphones.

The sound itself is not necessarily a warning. The crooked inlet produces collapsing pockets of air, and the overflow changes character as its flow changes. A shift from continuous wash to distinct drops may simply mean less incoming water. Still, an unexpected change is a reason to pause and look at the level and outlet—not to reach into the pipe or drain.

Keep hands, feet, cords, and loose clothing away from the covered outlet. There is no need to assume powerful suction, but there is also no benefit in investigating a hidden opening with your fingers.

### Leave the small objects alone

The wooden plug, bowl, and fiber brush are maintenance tools, not an invitation to experiment. Plugging the inlet changes the basin’s operation; do it only with the keeper’s permission. Leave the upper step clear, since it is part of your exit.

Likewise, do not scrape the green film, pull the algae threads, or touch the liverwort to see how it attaches. These are the first inhabitants of a newly made surface. A finger or an idle brushstroke could remove most of what is growing there.

The loose pale grit is best left where it is. Avoid grinding it into the glaze with your soles. Do not pry at the blue tile or interpret its difference as proof of a concealed mechanism. If this universe has hidden mechanisms, you will need better evidence than an off-color tile.

### Choose your hour

**Late afternoon is probably the best time for a first, observational visit.** As the stair’s shadow advances, the deepest glare diminishes and the workmanship becomes easier to read. Arrive early enough to learn the steps while daylight is still good, and remember that shade will not immediately remove the stored heat.

Midday offers the strongest visual experience, but also the harshest reflection and greatest warmth. If you are prone to dizziness, sensory overload, or discomfort in enclosed places, stay at the opening first. There is no obligation to descend just because the space is small.

The basin may feel more enclosing than its dimensions suggest. Above you are a stair, a lintel, and a limited rectangle of sky; beside you, bright walls; around you, amplified water. Once seated low down, much of the courtyard may disappear. Know how to stand and leave before settling in.

### What the visit is likely to reward

This is a place for patient attention rather than exploration. From a stable, dry position, you can watch bubbles gather beneath a tile lip, see grit shift by tiny increments, and hear the overflow change from a sheet to a cord to drops. The human workmanship remains conspicuous: chisel marks, imperfect glaze, a crooked spout, a tool-skipped patch of plaster already supporting life.

You do not need to immerse yourself to experience it fully. **The safest and perhaps most revealing first visit is to sit where permitted near the upper step, keep the exit open, and let your eyes and ears adjust.** What initially looks like an empty square of white water will gradually resolve into a very small place with many things happening.
