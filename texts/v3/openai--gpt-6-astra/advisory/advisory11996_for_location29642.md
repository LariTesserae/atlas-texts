Prepare for this city as you would for a long stay aboard an enormous, crowded vessel whose decks happen to be streets—and whose plumbing can change the shape of your room.

The first mistake would be to think of it as a flooded city. Flooding is not an interruption here. Water is part of the transport system, the food supply, the climate control, the architecture, and the timetable. Your task is not to remain dry at all costs. It is to learn which kinds of wetness are ordinary, and which mean you should already be somewhere else.

## Before you arrive

**Confirm that an ordinary human can live here, not merely breathe here.** The light and atmosphere look familiar, but that does not establish whether the food is digestible, the microbial films are harmless, or the mineral-rich water is safe on broken skin.

Arrange a human-compatible medical contact and bring an initial supply of drinking water, familiar food, prescription medicines, and oral rehydration salts. If your travel service offers biological compatibility screening, use it. Local people eating shellfish from a canal is evidence about their physiology and food-handling practices, not yours.

Book someone to meet you at the actual arrival point. Not “near the market”: the market may occupy several levels, with a navigable waterway where you expected the entrance.

For a first visit, choose a short stay in one district. The city is too large to experience by trying to cross it. Its scale is closer to a country than a conventional urban destination, and movement between districts may mean crossing hydraulic systems as well as neighborhoods.

## Where to stay

Spend money on a well-managed room rather than a picturesque one.

Look for:

- An established lodging house accustomed to visitors.
- A room above the locally recognized emergency flood level.
- Two usable evacuation routes, preferably leading to different structures.
- Reliable ventilation and somewhere to dry clothing.
- Protected drinking water and human-compatible sanitation.
- Staff who understand pressure warnings and can explain them to you.
- Distance from major freight gates, warm outfalls, and large ventilation mouths.

Ask explicitly: **“Where do guests go if the lower shutters close, and how do I reach it without power or a guide?”**

A dry room below an old street may be perfectly normal housing. That does not make it a good first room for you. Its safety depends on pumps, seals, and routes you cannot yet interpret.

Nor is the highest available room automatically best. Weather machinery dominates some high structures, and roofs may contain working cooling surfaces rather than public terraces. You want a designated refuge, not merely elevation.

## What to wear and carry

Dress for continuous heat, abrasion, and intermittent immersion.

Wear lightweight, quick-drying clothes that cover enough skin to reduce sun exposure and minor scrapes. Bring several changes of socks and enough footwear rotation to let one pair dry. In this humidity, “I’ll dry it overnight” may be wishful thinking.

Your shoes should have secure closures, toe protection, and soles suited to wet stone, slime, and metal. Loose sandals are poor everyday equipment. Tall waterproof boots may be useful for particular work, but are not a universal solution: they become hot, and water can still enter from above.

Your daily kit should include:

- A small waterproof bag with independently sealed inner pouches.
- Drinking water and a reliable snack.
- A headlamp, with a backup light.
- Simple wound-care supplies.
- Sun protection.
- A locally usable communication device.
- Your lodging address, emergency contact, and essential medical information in the local script.
- A current route map or navigation service.
- Hearing protection that reduces damaging noise without isolating you from warnings.

Carry less than you normally would. You may need to climb a wet stair, board a moving boat, or pass through a crowded upper landing quickly. Avoid dangling straps, trailing cords, and anything easily caught on a rail or gate.

If you expect to travel by small boat, obtain a properly fitted flotation aid appropriate to local conditions. Flotation helps in open water; it does not make sluices, culverts, or intake channels survivable.

## Learn the warnings before the sights

Your first guided outing should be a safety lesson.

Ask your guide to show you:

- The distinction between routine pressure bells and emergency signals.
- Flood marks and any markings for safe pedestrian routes.
- Public refuges and rescue embarkation points.
- Machinery exclusion zones.
- The signs used for impending structural work.
- What a normal lock-induced rise looks like.
- What residents do when they feel an unfamiliar vibration.

Do not settle for memorizing a bell sequence. Find out what action each warning requires **in that particular district**. In a place this extensive, assuming every neighborhood uses identical procedures would be unwise.

The bells travel through your shoes because vision and hearing are already crowded. Allow yourself to notice the ground. Noise-canceling headphones and loud music would remove useful information.

The animals offer clues, but they are not interchangeable alarms. Grazers leaving a canal may indicate an approaching change in water conditions. Birds gathering at a sluice may be anticipating an excellent meal in water that would kill you. Learn what their behavior means before acting on it.

Above all, do not imitate the children testing currents against grilles. They have local knowledge you lack, and suction can turn a moment of curiosity into entrapment.

## Treat shallow water as concealed machinery

An ankle-deep paved shoal looks forgiving. Beneath the gray-green surface there may still be a stair edge, a damaged cover, a channel lip, or an opening into a much deeper system.

Use established walking routes. Do not wade across an unfamiliar space simply because you can see people standing on its far side. They may be on another level.

Keep away from:

- Grilles and intake mouths.
- Narrow passages where water accelerates.
- Gate recesses and the spaces around movable barriers.
- Culvert entrances, even when apparently inactive.
- The gap between a hull and a wall.
- Water immediately downstream of industrial outfalls.
- Freshly opened or actively repairing masonry.

Do not enter a canal to retrieve a dropped bag. Do not tie yourself to street furniture as a flood precaution. Do not assume a handrail is outside the machinery merely because people hold it.

When a walkway or bridge begins moving, follow its designated procedure. Trying to jump onto apparently stationary ground can put you directly into a closing gap.

## Navigation requires time as well as place

A map here is a description of arrangements that may no longer be current.

A route can fail because a street has flooded, a bridge has lifted, a freight movement has priority, or a wall has spent the week becoming thicker. Large works may be announced in advance, but their timing depends on conditions far beyond your sight.

Give yourself generous travel margins. Confirm the return route before an excursion, especially in the evening. Ask whether the route will still be usable after the next scheduled water-level change.

Learn addresses as local people give them: district, level, access stair, landing, and entrance—not merely a building name.

There is no useful instinct of “heading toward the shore.” There may be no shore within any practical traveling distance. Your emergency destinations are specific refuges, transport nodes, and protected routes.

If your way home becomes uncertain, wait somewhere staffed and safely above the water. Paying for another room is better than improvising a shortcut through a lower service street.

## During a sudden flood

The crucial preparation is to decide where you will go **before** water starts moving fast.

At the first recognized warning:

1. Leave low rooms and waterside recesses.
2. Use the designated evacuation route while it is still dry.
3. Avoid shutters, rising barriers, and other mechanisms that are about to close.
4. Abandon belongings that delay you.
5. Follow local emergency personnel rather than trying to interpret the flow yourself.

Do not descend into a pumped lower street because it appears dry. Do not cross a standing ridge of water. Do not assume that the flood is over when the level begins to fall; the new drainage route may be producing the strongest current of the event.

If you are caught in moving water, keeping your airway clear and reaching an accessible rescue point matter more than saving equipment. Avoid trying to stand in fast flow, where a trapped foot can pull you under. Local rescue instruction should be part of your arrival briefing, not something you hope to infer in the moment.

The speed with which the market reopens afterward is not proof that nothing serious happened. Residents can be resilient and the danger can still have been real.

## Food, water, and your body

Eat at established places that understand your dietary compatibility. Begin with thoroughly cooked food and protected drinking water. Avoid raw shellfish, untreated canal water, unfamiliar microbial preparations, and anything recommended chiefly because it is “fresh from the warm outfall.”

A food system based on infrastructure-grown microbes need not be dirty or primitive. It may be highly controlled. What matters is knowing which products and preparation methods are safe for you.

Wash with suitable water before eating. Clean small cuts promptly and protect them from canal exposure. Inspect your feet daily. A minor abrasion that stays warm and wet can become a trip-ending problem.

Heat deserves as much respect as water. Humidity limits evaporative cooling, and the foundations continue releasing heat after sunset. Schedule demanding outings early, rest during the worst afternoon conditions, and drink regularly. Confusion, faintness, or unusual weakness are reasons to stop and seek help, not push through.

Expect sensory fatigue. The city will reach you through your soles, ribs, skin, and ears all day. Even a comfortable room may vibrate. Plan quiet breaks and protect your sleep.

## How to behave around a city that repairs itself

Treat the masonry as working infrastructure, not inert scenery.

Do not pick at a healing surface, collect its clear fluid, wedge open a scheduled door, or attach equipment to an emerging brace. Temporary ribs may carry laundry and still be supporting an arch.

Likewise, apparent empty spaces may be someone’s access route, ventilation opening, nesting site, or maintenance corridor. Ask before putting down a bag or stopping to photograph something. Keep clear of crews waiting for a wall to open; their inactivity may be a precise stage of the job.

Residents hanging dinner over a canal or leaning against a shifting column are not necessarily careless. They are reading familiar conditions. You are not yet entitled to their ease.

## What visiting will feel like

The surprise will probably be how quickly the impossible becomes domestic.

You will stop staring at the submerged windows. You will learn which landing sells breakfast, which passage has tolerable air, and where to pause while a freight hull passes. A warm handrail will become a familiar landmark. Then a pressure change will remind you that the handrail belongs to a system extending beyond the horizon.

There may be nowhere nearby to escape into woods, dry ground, or silence. Arrange rest deliberately rather than expecting to find it by walking away from the busy streets.

For your first evening, do very little. Eat near your lodging. Watch a routine change in water level from a place your host has identified as safe. Listen to the pauses people leave for the bells.

The best preparation is to become attentive without becoming frightened. This is someone’s ordinary neighborhood, full of dinners, repairs, arguments, and sleepy birds. It is also a place where ordinary safety depends on knowledge you have not acquired yet. Hire that knowledge, learn it patiently, and leave yourself time to enjoy the city once you can recognize what it is doing.
