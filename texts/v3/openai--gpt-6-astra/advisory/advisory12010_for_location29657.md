Prepare for this hollow as an **active geothermal chamber at extreme altitude, with a living floor and no reliably empty space**. The animals are not necessarily your greatest danger. The air, the heat, and the difficulty of leaving may kill an ordinary human before anything decides whether they are edible.

Your safest visit would be a short observation from the entrance, supported by instruments and someone waiting outside. Going farther in should be an expedition decision, not a moment of curiosity.

## Before you climb

Establish what “below the summit” means for your body. Summits beneath you and storms passing below suggest substantial altitude, but they do not establish atmospheric pressure or oxygen content. Measure both. In another universe, a familiar-looking sky is not a guarantee of breathable air.

Acclimatize if the atmosphere permits it. Bring supplemental oxygen for the ascent if needed, but understand that **oxygen supplementation is not protection against volcanic gases**. An oxygen cannula leaves you breathing the surrounding atmosphere.

Plan for two incompatible environments within a few steps:

- Outside: severe cold, wind, exposure, intense daylight, and a potentially fatal drop.
- Inside: radiant and conducted heat, possible oxygen deficiency, unknown gases, and exceptionally crowded terrain.

You need a retreat station on a surveyed, stable part of the exterior ridge, not simply “just outside.” Air discharged from the hollow may travel unpredictably in the wind. Keep spare breathing supplies, water, medical equipment, and communications there. A companion should remain at that station, with an explicit instruction **not to attempt an unprotected rescue**.

If your multiverse travel method provides a return gate, test its position and operation before approaching the hollow. Do not make your only escape depend on reaching the chamber’s interior, seeing a small control through a fogged visor, or standing on a particular patch of floor.

## Treat the air as unknown, not merely unpleasant

The sulfur smell is a warning, not a measurement. Depending on the mountain’s chemistry, the atmosphere could contain hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, or other hazardous substances. Smell cannot tell you when conditions are safe, and some dangerous exposures undermine your ability to notice them.

Use remote sampling before entry. Measure at several heights and locations, including near the threshold. Vent heat and cold incoming wind can produce complicated circulation; do not assume that standing tall or staying near the opening guarantees clean air.

For a human entry, the conservative choice is a **positive-pressure, full-face supplied-air system or self-contained breathing apparatus**, selected for the actual conditions and backed by an escape supply. A dust mask, damp cloth, or ordinary filter respirator is not enough for an unknown or oxygen-deficient atmosphere.

Breathing protection also does not make extreme heat survivable. You can overheat inside a perfectly functioning mask.

The description’s most important bodily warning is that a few paces inward, breathing becomes difficult. Do not reproduce that observation with your own lungs.

## Dress for heat without creating a portable oven

Bring a protective system chosen after measuring temperatures, rather than assuming a heavy expedition suit will handle everything.

At minimum, you want:

- A helmet, eye protection, and fully enclosed skin.
- Heat-resistant boots with soles suitable for smooth, irregular rock.
- Sealed cuffs and closures that do not leave inviting gaps.
- Gloves that preserve enough dexterity to operate your exit equipment.
- Protection against hot surfaces and whatever chemical hazards testing identifies.
- A way to monitor heat strain, with a strict exposure limit.

If active cooling is necessary, its failure should trigger immediate withdrawal. All of this equipment adds weight and makes a high-altitude approach harder, so test the complete load before the climb.

Remove loose straps, dangling cords, exposed hook-and-loop fasteners, and unnecessary mesh. The chamber contains hooks, limbs, skins, and narrow spaces occupied by animals. Anything trailing from you is an attachment opportunity.

Keep food, drinking water, and waste contained. In this dry ecosystem, a leaking bottle or a sweat-soaked fabric edge may be a much more significant disturbance than you expect.

## Reconnoiter without stepping inside

Start with a camera, atmospheric sampler, and thermal instrument extended from a secure position. Map the entrance lip, accessible stone, ceiling clearance, and any possible retreat route.

A thermal camera can help, but it cannot certify a safe surface: the visible surface may be an animal’s back, not the rock underneath it. Likewise, a laser rangefinder may measure a hanging body rather than the ceiling.

Avoid dropping objects to test depth. Avoid smoke tests, flames, and casual rock tapping. This habitat communicates through pressure and vibration. Your dropped carabiner might mean nothing, or it might provoke a wave of movement directly across your route.

A small flying drone is not automatically gentle reconnaissance. Rotor wash could scatter animals and shells, disturb the hot airflow, and bring the drone into contact with nearly invisible suspended limbs. A carefully positioned fixed camera may tell you more while changing less.

Observe several heat changes if you can do so safely. A quiet ten minutes does not establish a quiet hour. The pulses are irregular.

## Decide whether there is actually somewhere to stand

The apparent pebbles are animals. The wall is occupied. The ceiling is occupied. A convenient handhold may contain a clutch of eggs or a compressed predator.

**If you cannot identify a safe route that does not depend on crushing, brushing aside, or trusting the animals, do not enter on foot.**

That is both an ecological precaution and a practical one. A floor made of overlapping bodies may shift under your weight. Killing grazers may attract scavengers. Grabbing a rock rib may dislodge whatever is folded around its far side.

Do not assume that the chamber’s small inhabitants cannot hurt you. Their feeding structures, defensive chemistry, and ability to penetrate equipment are unknown. Equally, do not assume they will attack a human. The observed predation tells you how they feed on one another, not how they respond to you.

If entry proves feasible, move deliberately and keep your exit continuously available. Do not kneel, sit, or brace against a wall. Avoid stepping over seams you could not cross quickly on the return journey. Do not let fascination carry you farther in than your breathing reserve and thermal margin allow.

A safety line is useful only if its anchor and routing are safe. Dragging one through the chamber could snag animals, cross hot fissures, or become entangled in the overhead cords.

## Watch the inhabitants—but do not use them as your only alarm

The animals detect changes before a human hand can feel them. That makes their movement valuable information:

- Grazers streaming sideways.
- Traffic thickening along particular ribs.
- Hanging bodies descending.
- Pale spans drawing taut.
- Multiple species crowding into the same band of space.

For a visitor, these should be **withdrawal cues**, not invitations to stay for the spectacular part.

A strong pulse can rearrange the very surfaces you planned to use. It may also change heat exposure and airflow faster than you can comfortably manage. Leave while the route remains recognizable.

A low crack felt through your boots is another reason to retreat. The fresh bare patch beneath it is not newly available standing room. It is evidence of a recent geological change beneath a ceiling that just shed material.

## Leave the stone structures alone

The three low buildings are compelling precisely because they are deliberate. They are also inhabited, partly unstable, and poorly understood.

Do not lift a capstone, insert your fingers through an opening, brush away shells, or trace the five grooves. Record them with photographs from several angles and use a noncontact scale if possible.

Their current use as refuges does not tell you their original purpose. The ash does not prove that humans once warmed themselves there. The grooves do not establish a warning, a count, or a ritual.

And the buildings are not shelters for you. One has already collapsed; another is shifting under biological pressure. Whatever safety their builders intended may have depended on bodies, tolerances, or conditions unlike yours.

## Plan for a very short visit

Set turnaround criteria before entry: gas readings, temperature, breathing reserve, equipment faults, animal movement, visibility, and your own physical condition. Leave at the first trigger. Do not negotiate with the threshold because you have almost obtained a photograph.

Headache, nausea, dizziness, clumsiness, or unusual breathlessness could reflect altitude, heat, gas exposure, or several at once. You do not need to diagnose the cause inside the hollow.

On departure, inspect equipment for attached animals, eggs, skins, and mineral material under controlled conditions. Keep exposed gear isolated from your home environment until it can be assessed. Do not automatically kill or chemically wash off anything living; arrange containment that protects both you and the organism.

The emotional preparation matters too. You will probably feel an urge to find an empty patch, make a little room, or rest a hand somewhere solid. Here, those ordinary gestures are interventions in someone else’s habitat.

The hollow is not waiting to receive a visitor. It is already full. A successful visit may consist of standing securely at its edge, watching the floor turn once, and leaving without requiring anything inside to make way for you.
