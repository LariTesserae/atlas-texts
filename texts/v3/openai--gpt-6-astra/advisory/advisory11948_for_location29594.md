Prepare for this place as a **hot, lightless wilderness in which neither the ground nor the living cover can be trusted to stay where it is**. Its lushness is misleading. You could die of thirst surrounded by water-filled plants, or be injured in a landscape that closes over the accident before anyone can find you.

The central difficulty is not necessarily predation. It is that almost every usable space is already occupied, and periodically all those occupants have to move.

## Before you enter

Your first visit should be short, supported, and fully supplied. This is not a place to learn local survival by running out of provisions.

If your means of multiversal travel allows it, arrange a return point outside the dense forest, preferably on terrain that has been assessed for stability. A fresh bare ridge is easy to see and may be comparatively open, but it is not necessarily safe: new rock is evidence of recent change, not a promise that the change is finished.

Have an extraction method you can activate while injured, in darkness, without speaking. Test whether it works beneath the canopy and across ridges. If it requires reaching one particular doorway, clearing, or coordinate, keep your excursions conservative. A path you used an hour ago may become a crack, a crowded animal corridor, or a fallen tangle.

Before exposing yourself, establish:

- Whether the atmosphere is breathable, including in low hollows and beside fissures.
- Whether temperature and humidity permit sustained human exertion.
- Whether local organisms, sap, dust, or secretions damage human skin and lungs.
- Whether your navigation and communication equipment actually functions here.

The description gives good reason to suspect heat stress and unstable footing. It does **not** establish that the air is poisonous or that the wildlife can infect you. Those are questions to test, not assumptions to settle by taking a deep breath.

## Dress to keep the forest off you

Wear a smooth, close-fitting outer layer with as few exposed loops, straps, and pockets as possible. Loose webbing will catch roots; dangling equipment will touch animals you have not seen.

You want:

- Tough, well-fitting boots with puncture-resistant soles and enough insulation from the warm rock.
- Ankle protection and sealed trouser cuffs.
- Gloves that preserve dexterity.
- Eye protection, a light helmet, and a face screen or suitable respiratory protection.
- Clothing that excludes small organisms without making you overheat.

That last requirement is the hard one. A heavy sealed suit may protect your skin while creating a lethal heat burden. If testing shows that full isolation is necessary, bring active cooling and treat its operating time as a strict limit on the visit.

Avoid sandals, bare forearms, exposed jewelry, and sitting directly on logs or stone. Here, bare skin is not merely vulnerable skin. It is a source of salt, warmth, and moisture in a place where those resources attract attention.

Carry a compact medical kit suited to falls, cuts, eye exposure, and heat illness. Bring clean water specifically for wound and eye irrigation. You do not want to be deciding whether an inhabited leaf cup is “probably clean enough.”

## Bring your water with you

The forest contains water, but very little of it is unclaimed.

Those few mouthfuls beneath a root may be the center of a feeding territory. A leaf cup may contain larvae, digestive secretions, or compounds harmless to local animals and harmful to you. Clear fluid from a cut stem is not evidence of potability.

Carry all the drinking water needed for the planned visit, plus a realistic delay reserve, distributed between multiple durable containers. Make drinking possible without repeatedly exposing the container mouth to insects and dirty gloves.

Do not plan around finding a stream. There may be none to find.

For a longer expedition, water collection needs prior testing. Filters and disinfectants address some hazards, not every dissolved toxin or unfamiliar chemical. Rain collected on a clean, freestanding surface might be preferable to water drained through the living canopy, but getting such a surface above the leaves could be difficult and dangerous.

Also bring your food. Do not assume the root nodules are edible because the stone-carrying creatures eat them. Their physiology, preparation methods, and knowledge may differ profoundly from yours.

## Carry your own light—and do not let it become your whole world

You need a dependable, dimmable primary light and independent backups. A diffuse light is useful for nearby roots; a more directed beam helps examine the next section of route. Avoid relying on a single bright headlamp that makes everything outside its narrow cone disappear.

Test the wildlife’s response to illumination before a long penetration. Light might attract insects, repel animals, or alter behavior in ways that increase your exposure. There is no basis for assuming a particular color is invisible or reassuring to local species.

The disturbed insects are not a lighting system you control. Their flashes may reveal a body without revealing which way it is moving, how large it is, or what supports it. A sudden constellation overhead is a reason to pause and assess—not to stare upward while continuing to walk.

Thermal equipment could help, but warm stone and dense foliage may limit its value. Nothing that sees heat should be mistaken for something that sees safely through the forest.

## Move by measured stages

The terrain demands deliberate foot placement. Leaf litter may conceal a narrow fracture; a root may be carrying tension across a widening gap; a surface that looks like soil may be a loose skin of decomposing material.

Use a short, sturdy probe to examine footing, without driving it blindly into inhabited holes. Keep your pack narrow and your center of gravity manageable. You need to turn around without wrestling the forest.

Do not travel alone. A small, disciplined party is preferable to a large group that cannot remain together through constrictions.

Agree beforehand on simple visual or tactile signals for:

- Stop.
- Retreat.
- Ground movement.
- Animal movement.
- Injury.
- Immediate extraction.

Ordinary shouting may be useless in the soundscape described. Bring hearing protection that reduces sustained noise while preserving as much awareness as possible, and use tested communications rather than trying to overpower the forest.

Treat apparent sound direction with suspicion. A call beside your leg may seem distant; a distant chorus may feel physically close. Do not investigate a noise merely because you believe you can place it.

Keep an independent record of your route. Satellite positioning, magnetic compasses, radio beacons, and inertial navigation each have assumptions that may fail in another world. Test them. Use removable markers where appropriate, but do not depend on a marked branch remaining upright.

## Have a ground-movement drill

The animals provide warning, but they are not guides.

A climber may have a secure refuge you cannot reach. A burrower may emerge precisely where you are standing. The broad-backed grazers may know an escape route, but getting into their lane could kill you.

When you feel pressure through your feet, hear stones chattering, or see coordinated changes in animal behavior:

1. Stop advancing.
2. Alert your companions.
3. Move to the nearest **previously assessed** refuge, if the approach remains clear.
4. Avoid fissure margins, taut roots, constricted animal routes, and visibly damaged trees.
5. Be prepared to extract if no defensible refuge remains.

There is no universal instruction here to “climb” or “get onto a ridge.” Either could be wrong at a particular site. Identify possible refuges during the approach, before you need them.

Once the movement subsides, inspect everything again. The fact that feeding resumes does not mean a fracture is safe to cross. Local animals may tolerate risks your body cannot.

## Give large animals more room than seems necessary

In this forest, you may meet the consequences of a large animal before you meet the animal itself: trunks separating, branches loading, smaller creatures going silent.

Back away early. Do not wait to identify the whole body.

Avoid putting yourself between a moving browser and the vegetation it is approaching, and never count on a trunk being an immovable barrier. Some of these animals routinely press trunks apart.

Keep food, waste, and scented supplies sealed. Check cuffs, seams, footwear, and pack contact points frequently. Do not interpret every small organism as aggressive, but do not allow one to remain against your skin while you decide what it is doing.

## Treat the stone-carriers as people you do not yet understand

Stone use alone does not settle their intelligence or social organization. It is nevertheless ample reason for restraint.

Do not take their tools, open their food stores, occupy their sleeping forks, or assume an unattended feeding place is abandoned. A stone that looks ordinary to you may be carefully selected and difficult to replace.

Observe from a distance. If contact occurs, keep your hands visible, leave them an escape route, and withdraw if your presence changes their behavior toward alarm. Do not offer human food or water as an improvised gesture of friendship.

Most importantly, do not follow them into sleeping trees because they seem to know what they are doing. They do—for bodies and abilities that are not yours.

## Do not plan to sleep on the ground

A conventional ground tent is a poor default. The substrate conducts heat, small animals are everywhere, and the tent floor does nothing to make a fracture stable.

A hammock is not automatically better. The canopy is a transport network under heavy loads, and branches are already near failure.

For an overnight stay, you need a surveyed site and a shelter system whose support, ventilation, exclusion barriers, and emergency exit have been designed for it. If you cannot establish those, return elsewhere to sleep. An insulated mat and insect net are useful equipment, not a complete solution.

## Prepare for the strain of never quite understanding what is near you

The noise, warmth, darkness, and constant incidental movement will exhaust your attention. You may spend an hour bracing for a large animal and then make the more consequential mistake of stepping into a hand-wide crack.

Schedule short observation stops in places you have checked. Drink before thirst becomes distracting. Use a companion check for overheating, confusion, damaged equipment, and unnoticed hitchhikers. Do not interpret determination as remaining capacity.

There is extraordinary beauty here, but it arrives in incomplete glimpses. You may never get the satisfying view that makes the landscape feel comprehensible.

**Go in self-sufficient, move slowly, and leave while you still have generous reserves.** The forest is not necessarily hostile to you. It is simply very crowded, very active, and unlikely to make any allowance for the space a human needs to hesitate.
