## Prepare for an industrial ocean, not a submerged ruin

The most important thing to understand is that this place is working.

The floor is not an archaeological surface. The ladders are not invitations. The lights do not necessarily mark a route suitable for visitors. A quiet sector may be about to receive a large volume of water, and a screen with fish hovering beside it may be the most dangerous object you will ever approach.

There is nothing in the description that requires a supernatural explanation. An ordinary human’s vulnerabilities are quite sufficient here: pressure, cold, exhaustion, disorientation, machinery, and distances that are difficult to comprehend from inside the water.

Your preparation should therefore begin with a change of category: **you are visiting a flooded industrial facility large enough to produce its own seascape.**

## Choose how you will visit before choosing what to carry

For a first visit, the best option is a certified, pressure-rated observation submersible operated by someone who knows the basin, with a support vessel and clearance from its operators. Ideally, scout the intended route with a remotely operated vehicle first.

This is not excessive caution. It lets you experience the place without making every minute of observation part of a breathing-gas calculation.

A submersible is not immune to the basin. It must still stay outside intake exclusion zones, avoid entanglement, maintain contact with support, and have a credible recovery plan. But it gives an ordinary visitor a much better margin against cold, pressure, and a current that can change while they are looking at something.

If you intend to swim outside:

- Be appropriately trained and medically fit for the actual dive, not merely comfortable in water.
- Use an authorized team experienced with deep diving and industrial water infrastructure.
- Have a site-specific dive plan, surface support, rescue capability, and suitable breathing-gas redundancy.
- Do not make this your first deep dive, first drysuit dive, or first experience of limited visibility.

At forty metres, pressure is roughly five times surface atmospheric pressure in Earthlike conditions. Breathing-gas supplies are consumed much faster than near the surface, air diving can involve significant nitrogen narcosis, and allowable exposure without decompression stops is short. The pale floor and daylight do not make this shallow water.

If your multiverse transport can choose an arrival point, choose a staffed, dry reception area. Do not arrive on the bottom and then discover which local assumptions your equipment has failed to accommodate.

## Obtain the operating picture

The most useful thing you can bring may be permission accompanied by an accurate maintenance schedule.

Ask the basin operators for:

- Current sector maps, with depths and usable coordinates.
- Intake, outlet, gate, overflow, and moving-machinery exclusion zones.
- Expected flows and the ways those flows change during normal operation.
- Planned pumping changes, gate movements, maintenance, and compartment draining or flooding.
- Vessel routes and active lifting operations.
- Approved entry, recovery, and emergency access points.
- Local communications procedures and stop-work authority.
- Recent water-quality and visibility information.

For any authorized work near intakes or moving equipment, safety must rest on formal isolation and verification—not somebody saying the pumps “should be off.” Isolation can involve gates, remote controls, automatic restarts, and flows driven by differences in water level as well as by motors.

As a visitor, you should not need to go close enough for isolation to become your personal protection. Stay on an approved route well outside those hazards.

**Fish are not flow meters.** Their ability to hold position beside a screen says little about your ability to do so, especially while wearing bulky equipment. A current you can resist at one distance may become inescapable nearer an opening.

## Dress for prolonged cold and very little useful shelter

The light will make the basin look more hospitable than it is.

Choose thermal protection for the measured water temperature, expected exposure, and a plausible delay in recovery. For a diver, that may mean a drysuit with appropriate insulation, hood, and gloves, all familiar from previous use. Carry proper thermal protection on the surface as well: wind on a wet deck can finish what the water began.

Avoid loose straps, trailing accessories, and unsecured clips. This environment contains cable, brackets, screen mechanisms, and projections that may disappear into blue-grey visibility until you are beside them.

A professional team should select and configure equipment for the site, including suitable redundant gas, lights, instruments, communications, and accessible cutting tools. Do not add a tether casually. A correctly managed professional umbilical is part of a complete diving system; an improvised rope can become another entanglement hazard.

For a submersible passenger, bring warm layers, any necessary medication, and a realistic account of your tolerance for confinement. Visit the cabin before departure. Find out what recovery delays would mean for temperature, sanitation, and comfort.

## Navigate by a system, not by the landscape

This place is especially capable of making competent people feel geographically certain when they are not.

The joints are straight. The masts repeat. The floor is almost level. Sector numbers may be the only meaningful distinction between places separated by a long swim. A cloud can remove the farthest part of your apparent map without moving anything at all.

Do not plan to “follow the floor until the wall.” The description makes clear that the wall may be days away.

Use an operator-approved navigation system appropriate to your mode of travel: surveyed waypoints, acoustic positioning where available, site-checked instruments, and a route with explicit turnaround limits. Large steel structures can complicate compass use. Ordinary satellite positioning will not give a diver a reliable underwater fix.

Know your recovery point independently of the next visible mast.

The ladders deserve particular caution. They are intended for drained compartments. They may lead toward the surface without leading to a usable platform, exit, or safe place to wait. Likewise, an underwater lamp tells you that a route has operational significance, not that a person can safely follow it indefinitely.

## Treat changes in the water as information

The basin’s warning signs may be small before they become overwhelming:

- Suspended particles change direction together.
- A loose cable begins moving differently.
- Silt rises from previously clear joints.
- A vibration changes pitch or rhythm.
- Visibility starts to close around a structure.

These are reasons to follow the team’s abort or withdrawal procedure, not to investigate the source.

Sound will be a poor intuitive guide. A pump may feel nearby through your jaw while remaining far away. A tool striking a mast may sound disproportionately large. Do not pursue a noise or use apparent loudness to estimate a safe distance from machinery.

Plan the first visit for calm weather and daylight. The basin is broad enough to develop substantial waves, and those waves can disturb the bottom, particularly around structures. Forty metres of water is not a guarantee of shelter from a long storm.

Keep clear of suspended cages, working divers, lifting wires, and vessels. Their crews should know you are present; your ability to see them is not a substitute for coordination.

## Do not explore beneath the floor

Drainage galleries, inspection passages, trenches, and gaps beneath panels may be fascinating. They are not extensions of an open-water visit.

They can combine overhead entrapment, silt, pressure differences, restricted exits, and machinery with no safe refuge. Entering them belongs to a separate, specialist operation.

The same restraint applies to apparently abandoned objects. A cable lying on the concrete may still be connected. A panel may conceal moving water. A fastener may look like litter while sitting inside an active maintenance area.

Bring a camera, not a collecting bag. The small patches of life are concentrated precisely where a hand or fin is most likely to land.

## Prepare for the emotional scale

The unsettling part may not be danger. It may be how little happens.

You could spend much of a visit looking at seams, grey concrete, and particles moving sideways. Then a school of fish will arrive, and for a minute the whole basin will seem inhabited. When it leaves, the emptiness may feel larger than before.

There is also a particular unease in seeing human construction continue beyond human sight. You recognize every individual feature—bolt socket, handrail, enamel number—but cannot assemble them into a place with edges. The long ladders offer the shape of escape without necessarily offering escape itself.

Expect some combination of awe, boredom, confinement, and exposure. You can feel trapped inside a diving mask while looking across an apparently limitless plain.

Keep the first excursion short and deliberately modest. One approved mast, a seam with eelgrass, a view across the open floor, and a safe return would be a complete visit. You do not need to reach a pump trench to understand the basin.

Before departure, establish what you will do if you feel panic, become separated, lose visibility, or simply want to stop. Practice those responses in safer conditions. In the water, discomfort must become a clear signal to your team—not an impulsive dash toward the bright surface.

The right preparation lets you notice what is extraordinary here: the first shrimp beneath a washer, the way sunlight runs over a newly cast corner, the shells beginning to turn a construction into a habitat.

Go with enough support that you can watch those things without forgetting that, around you, the water is being moved on purpose.
