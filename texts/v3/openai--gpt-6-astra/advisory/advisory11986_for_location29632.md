Prepare for a long crossing of something that is neither solid ground nor a floating island, but has properties of both. It can carry a forest without difficulty and still become unsafe beneath a person who stands in the wrong place for an hour.

The most important adjustment is this: **do not confuse slowness with stability.** Almost everything here happens slowly enough to escape your first impression—water moving toward a load, a predator closing an opportunity, heat accumulating inside your clothes, a route becoming difficult to retrace.

You should arrive with a way home, food you recognize, and no urgent need to reach the interior.

## Before committing to the ascent

If you enter from the lowlands, treat the first several days as reconnaissance rather than travel toward a destination.

Measure altitude and air pressure. The warmth and dense vegetation may disguise how high you have climbed; being above clouds does not establish a particular elevation, but neither does comfortable air establish that there is enough oxygen. Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, or poor judgment should not be dismissed as ordinary hiking discomfort.

For a first visit to an unfamiliar world, check atmospheric compatibility before breathing freely. After that, continue checking air in enclosed root chambers. An atmosphere that is safe on an open path may be less safe in a deep, poorly ventilated hollow full of respiration and decomposition. Carry an oxygen monitor as well as whatever atmospheric screening equipment your crossing normally requires. A particulate mask does not correct low oxygen.

Plan the return while you are still somewhere easy to leave. The ascent is gradual enough that turning around may mean another week of walking, not an afternoon’s descent.

If you have a portal, beacon, or other extraction device, find out whether it needs a stable location, exposed sky, or a particular kind of ground. None is guaranteed here. Keep its essential component on your person rather than at camp. A beacon with a dead battery in a shelter you cannot relocate is just another object being incorporated into the forest.

## Dress for being warm and wet for days

This place does not merely receive rain. It produces dampness around you.

A heavy waterproof shell may keep falling water out while trapping enough sweat to soak you from within. Favor light, quick-drying layers, ventilation, and a loose rain covering when conditions allow. Carry a genuinely sealed reserve of dry sleeping clothes.

Foot care will matter more than heroic endurance:

- Wear footwear with good wet traction and enough room for swollen feet.
- Carry several changes of socks and inspect your feet daily.
- Dry your feet during protected rests, even if the boots remain wet.
- Treat small abrasions early and cover them against repeated contamination.
- Bring the supplies to manage blisters, skin maceration, and your usual medical needs.

Use sleeves, trousers, and insect netting. The insects are quiet, not absent; many will approach by walking along vegetation rather than flying into view. Inspect cuffs, pack straps, and bedding. Do not assume organisms riding a grazer’s back would treat your skin, clothes, or equipment harmlessly.

Protect electronics, medicine, food, and insulation in separate waterproof compartments. One failed seal should not ruin everything.

Bring reliable artificial light with spare power. The slight midday brightening will not be enough for every task, and there is no clean sunset to tell you that you have left camp-making too late. Keep time deliberately.

## Learn to read load, not just footing

A surface that holds your first step has answered only one question.

It has not told you what it will do under sustained weight, whether there is a cavity beneath it, or where the nearby water will go once it deforms.

A blunt probing pole is useful for detecting holes and finding firmer interwoven growth. It cannot certify a thick raft as safe. Watch the surroundings as well as your feet:

- Is a pool edge creeping toward you?
- Is the waterline climbing a particular root?
- Are separate patches of standing water beginning to join?
- Has a formerly level surface acquired a slight tilt?
- Are fibers tightening, separating, or beginning to sound?

At uncertain crossings, move one person at a time. Do not assemble the whole party at the narrowest point to discuss whether it is safe.

**Rest on ground you have watched under load, not merely ground you have crossed.** Before settling for a meal or the night, observe what happens after packs are set down. Spread loads where practical, but do not mistake a load-spreading mat for a bridge over an unknown void.

The broad-backed grazers are useful evidence, not proof. Their bodies, feet, and behavior belong to this terrain. Yours do not. In particular, do not stop beside one at a pool margin: the animal may be changing the slope of your footing without either of you appearing to move.

When a low wooden note passes through the ground, pause and check your immediate surroundings. The sound may be a routine adjustment far away, as it often seems to be for the animals. It is not necessarily a collapse warning. But changes in water level, local sagging, or fresh fiber failure deserve a prompt move toward previously observed footing.

Do not run blindly in response to a sound you cannot locate.

## Carry your calories; distrust the apparent abundance

There is food everywhere in the ecological sense. That does not mean there is food for you.

Leaves, fruit-like smells, clear water, and grazing animals establish nothing about human edibility. Even familiar-looking plant chemistry could be troublesome. On an initial expedition, carry all your food.

Water requires similar discipline. Warm sheets spread across roots are collecting whatever those roots and their inhabitants release. Clear drops are not necessarily cleaner than green ones. Green drops should certainly not be treated as a refreshing novelty.

Bring a treatment system suited to biological contamination and, where your equipment permits, unknown dissolved substances. Ordinary filtering and boiling do not make every unfamiliar liquid safe; boiling does not remove many dissolved toxins. If you cannot establish that local water is usable, your carried supply must determine the length of the visit.

Collect water without wading into pools or lingering on a yielding margin. A vessel on a line may help, provided you can use it from sound footing.

Bring a contained cooking system rather than planning on a wood fire. Fuel may be saturated, and the material beneath and around you is also the structure supporting your camp. Keep heat away from living surfaces and do not assume that a damp-looking organic mat cannot conceal smoldering material.

## Navigate by records, not atmosphere

You will remember a great chamber, three leaning columns, and a waterfall. Later you may discover several places that fit that memory.

Carry redundant navigation tools and test them near your entry point. Satellite navigation may be unavailable beneath the canopy—or nonexistent in this world. A compass is useful only after you establish that it behaves consistently.

Keep a route log with distances, turns, elevations where measurable, and photographs in both directions. Record junctions at close range. “The broad opening beyond the pale leaves” is not a durable instruction.

Sound is particularly treacherous. A waterfall may be a valuable landmark without being an accurate bearing. Calls may appear displaced, and knocks can travel through connected wood. Establish a lost-party procedure before entering. Do not let everyone scatter in pursuit of the same misleading voice.

Use removable markers if needed. Do not cut blazes into roots that may be structural or inhabited.

The old paths are worth investigating, but they are not maintained trails. Dry footing beneath foliage does not promise a passable route ahead. A passage made for another body, or another surface level, may lead you into an awkward space with no human-sized exit.

Choose a turnaround time before curiosity starts negotiating.

## Treat the shelters as possible homes

Those rounded doorways are the most promising places for a dry night, and the places most likely to make you overconfident.

Approach them as potentially occupied structures. The absence of visible inhabitants does not establish abandonment. Look for recent use, wait, announce your presence without pounding on the walls, and avoid blocking entrances. If you meet anyone capable of communicating ownership or custom, ask.

Before using a shelter, check:

- Whether the air is safe and moving.
- Whether the sleeping rim remains dry.
- Where condensation drains.
- Whether water could enter through a lower doorway.
- Whether there is a second usable exit.
- What is already living in the depressions and holes.

Do not evict the small animals merely because their resting places resemble furniture. Their presence also does not prove that the air or structure is safe for humans.

A lightweight freestanding sleeping enclosure gives you some independence. A hammock is not automatically better: it concentrates force at its anchors, and two apparent trunks may be parts of the same loaded structure. Never anchor life-safety equipment to an unassessed root simply because it is large.

Avoid sleeping in low hollows, under fresh breaks, or beside newly sagging water-filled gardens.

## Give wildlife space before it asks for it

The grazers may appear contemplative. They are still heavy animals on deformable ground. Stay clear of their routes into water and leave them room to retreat.

Do not touch the vegetation growing on their backs, their passengers, the suspended young of branch-dwelling animals, or swimmers beneath leaves. What looks like a convenient handhold may be occupied.

The predators make the dry crossings especially important. A narrow rib between pools is attractive to you for precisely the reason it is attractive to them: travelers converge there.

Inspect bottlenecks before entering. Keep the party close enough for mutual observation without concentrating everyone on one doubtful patch. Avoid camping beside a crossing, a water approach, or a route heavily used by grazers. A few minutes without visible movement is not a meaningful clearance check for an animal whose hunt unfolds over days.

Ask local guides about deterrents if guides exist. Do not assume noise, eye contact, or weapons that work on familiar wildlife will produce familiar reactions here.

## Keep well back from the rim

The brighter air and open view will be powerfully attractive after days beneath leaves.

Do not let that relief pull you onto springy margins. The edge is not necessarily a cliff with a clear lip. It may be a gradual transition into less-supported growth, with cloud hiding the consequences.

Observe from farther back than seems necessary. Do not descend the root curtain without specialized equipment, a known route, and genuinely assessed anchors. Some of those roots end in air.

## Prepare for the place’s effect on your judgment

The upland will probably feel peaceful before it feels dangerous.

There is little sharp noise, no hard sunset, and no obvious boundary between shelter, forest, and ground. You can become tired without feeling that you have accomplished a day’s travel. You can stop noticing damp skin, diminishing light, and a pool drawing nearer to your bedding.

Keep a human schedule: meals, water checks, foot care, navigation checks, sleep. Make a deliberate habit of asking one another what has changed since you stopped.

For a first visit, choose a modest objective: reach one great interior chamber, observe a suspended garden from safe footing, spend a carefully selected dry night, and return. The distant swells do not need to be crossed to understand something of the place.

The right attitude is neither conquest nor fear. It is the courtesy of a guest combined with the caution of someone walking across a very old bridge whose structure is still growing.

And when you stop to admire the view, keep one eye on the water.
