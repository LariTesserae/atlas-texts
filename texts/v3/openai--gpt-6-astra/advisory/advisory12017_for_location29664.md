Prepare for this place as an expedition to a high-altitude lake, a submerged forest, and an unstable mountain—then add one rule that matters more than all three:

**Do not assume the water will hold you merely because it holds itself.**

The unsupported margins are beautiful, but nothing in their appearance establishes what happens when a person presses against them. The boundary might resist you, admit you slowly, or let you pass into open air while retaining almost all the water behind you. A depression that lasts for hours is evidence of an unfamiliar process, not evidence of strength.

You can have a very good visit without finding out.

## Arrange a way to arrive—and leave—on solid ground

Your arrival point should be a surveyed rock platform well inside the supported basin, not a patch of apparent meadow and not one of the knife-edged summits.

From above, the waxy leaves disguise the water. They may look continuous enough to walk on. Treat them as vegetation floating over depth, regardless of how firm an individual leaf feels beneath a hand. Even if a leaf could bear your weight, its stem and the neighboring leaves might not.

The exposed summits offer a different trap: they are real ground, but not necessarily usable ground. A rough volcanic blade is a poor place to unload equipment, sleep, or retrieve an injured companion. Fresh stone can be brittle; an apparently substantial ledge may be part of a detached plate.

Before coming, arrange:

- A confirmed landing or transfer site with enough space for the whole party.
- A return route that does not depend on swimming to the nearest visible peak.
- Communications tested from the basin itself.
- A sheltered place to rest, warm up, and administer oxygen.
- A rescue plan that accounts for altitude, immersion, and the absence of ordinary shores.

If your multiverse transit system can return you directly, verify its operating limits beforehand. Does it work underwater? Through rock or dense foliage? Can it retrieve an unconscious person? Does it require you to stand in exactly the place where you arrived?

An escape mechanism with unknown conditions is another environmental mystery, not a rescue plan.

## Acclimatize before entering the water

“Above the weather” does not tell you the atmospheric pressure. Measure it.

Unless local measurements establish otherwise, assume the exposed surface may combine thin air, strong ultraviolet light, wind, and intense sun reflected from water. The warmth of the lake does not make the altitude harmless. Indeed, warm immersion and exertion can make it easier to overlook fatigue until leaving the water becomes difficult.

Spend time at the arrival station before undertaking anything strenuous. A headache, unusual nausea, poor coordination, or breathlessness at rest is a reason to stop and seek assessment—not something to dismiss because the landscape feels peaceful.

Bring drinking water rather than relying on the lake. Clear geothermal water may contain dissolved substances that ordinary filters do not remove. Its abundant vegetation establishes that local organisms can live in it, not that humans can drink it.

Use protective clothing and sun protection compatible with the local ecology. Your most memorable sunburn here could occur while you are floating comfortably in warm water, looking down.

## Make the first visit an observation trip

For an ordinary traveler without substantial diving experience, the best underwater vehicle is a **professionally operated, enclosed observation craft**, rated for the actual pressures, temperatures, and water chemistry encountered here.

It should remain in broad channels with room to turn. This is not a place to push a vehicle through foliage simply because the leaves appear willing to move aside.

A low-impact surface excursion can also be worthwhile, provided it follows mapped, supported water well away from the outer margin. Use a stable craft with suitable flotation and a guide who knows where there is rock beneath the basin. From open lanes, a submerged camera may show much of the upper forest without anyone entering it.

Do not make learning to dive part of the itinerary. Learn elsewhere, then return.

The forest makes this lake an overhead environment even where there is no stone roof. Overlapping crowns, long ribbons, and stems reaching up from deep crevices can obstruct a direct ascent. Water is not “open” merely because sunlight passes through what is above you.

## If you dive, plan for altitude and entanglement

A qualified team should establish the diving procedures after measuring the site. Ordinary sea-level assumptions are not sufficient. Surface pressure affects dive planning, and further ascent after a dive—including travel to a higher arrival station—must be accounted for. An instantaneous journey home does not necessarily make a pressure change physiologically harmless.

Use an appropriate altitude-diving plan, redundant breathing capability, reliable depth and pressure instruments, and thermal protection selected from actual measurements. Emergency oxygen and access to treatment for diving injuries need to be arranged before the first descent.

Do not breathe the gas collected beneath leaves. It might contain oxygen, but its composition, purity, and suitability are unknown.

For the first dives:

- Stay in mapped channels with a verified route to the surface.
- Keep equipment streamlined so it does not catch on stems.
- Carry accessible cutting tools for emergency self-rescue, not for clearing a path.
- Maintain close buddy contact.
- Use conservative turnaround limits.
- Avoid penetrating beneath dense crowns without the appropriate overhead-environment training and equipment.

A line is not automatically an improvement. In this forest, a loose tether could wrap around several plants before you notice resistance. Where lines are needed, their routing and management should be deliberate, with anchors on verified stable rock—not on trees, loose slabs, or the yielding water boundary.

Good buoyancy control is especially important. The bottom is neither a safe floor nor an empty space. It contains sharp plate edges, fragile growth, and fine sediment that may turn a clear passage opaque with one misplaced kick.

## Measure warmth instead of trusting your skin

The gradual temperature changes are one of the lake’s more serious ordinary hazards.

You may pass from comfortable water into water that adds heat to your body without crossing any obvious thermal boundary. A person swimming in protective equipment can become overheated while still thinking of the lake as pleasantly warm. Conversely, an inactive observer may lose heat during a long immersion at temperatures that initially felt comfortable.

Carry temperature instruments and establish exposure limits. A bath-temperature impression at the surface is not a survey.

Avoid fissures and enclosed spaces where geothermal fluids or gases may accumulate. Little bubbling does not establish little activity. Nor should you anchor beside a vent merely because the surrounding plants look healthy.

The occasional clicks are also worth respecting. Young, fractured stone is still changing. Do not park beneath overhanging slabs or use a fresh crack as a convenient handhold.

The eastern ledge would make an excellent place to observe succession from a distance. It would make a poor place to stand while discussing geological stability.

## Keep well back from the outer rim

Obtain a map that distinguishes **the visible edge of the water** from **the last supporting stone**. They are not the same shoreline.

Choose a conservative exclusion zone around the unsupported water, and enlarge it wherever the underlying structure is uncertain. No universal safe distance can be inferred from the description alone.

Do not:

- Swim out to press your face against the boundary.
- Put a hand through to feel the air.
- Push equipment into an old depression.
- Assume a craft can float safely over the overhang.
- Drop objects through it to see what happens.

Anything released may become a hazard in the country below. Even water leaving the rim is an event with a long visible aftermath.

If the margin is to be studied, begin with remote observation under controlled conditions. Watch its shape, the growth crossing it, and the way natural disturbances persist. There is already plenty happening without adding the weight of a visitor.

Also, remember that a conventional surface rescue may be impossible there. A person in trouble near an ordinary shore can be pulled toward land. Here, “toward the edge” may mean toward a fall.

## Expect navigation to feel strangely difficult

The water is clear, but the view is obstructed. Those are different things.

You will see individual leaves beautifully and still fail to see the channel beyond them. Pale trunks may resemble each other. A hillside glimpsed through a gap may appear close enough to reach, although it lies across a deep passage. The lingering motion after a fish has passed may draw your attention away from your route.

Use surveyed navigation aids and instruments known to work locally. Do not rely solely on a magnetic compass near volcanic formations without checking it.

Carry lights even for midday visits. The basin’s faint green illumination is not equivalent to useful working light. Colors fade with depth; equipment labels and signals should remain distinguishable by shape and contrast.

Sound is unlikely to help much. A hollow note or a click may seem nearby without offering a useful direction. Establish visual or tactile communication procedures rather than expecting to locate a companion by noise.

Take particular care when looking through the outer margin at the clouds below. Your eyes will be presented with open sky beneath water that your body experiences as continuous support. That could be profoundly disorienting. Make your first view from a secure craft, not while free-swimming beside the drop.

## Bring as little of your own world as possible

Clean and decontaminate equipment before arrival. Boots, seals, hoses, and sampling containers can carry organisms between waters.

Avoid touching the leaves. Their hairs and gas beads may be important to how they function. The slow turning where crowns meet is a reason to move more carefully, not an invitation to force a passage and expect the forest to accommodate you.

Do not feed the fish, collect the translucent snails, or move a small animal into better light. Scarcity makes casual disturbance more consequential. A snail that remains on one stem for weeks may occupy a very particular habitat.

Keep fins, propellers, and thrusters away from sediment and root beds. Leave no markers attached to living growth. If you need a record of a site, photograph it and record its position.

## Prepare to spend time doing very little

Leave room in the schedule for a long, stationary observation.

At first, the forest may seem almost disappointingly still. Then you will begin to notice that a leaf is turning, a gas bead has advanced one crown higher, or a root is disappearing beneath the newest slab. Rain may cross the surface without appreciably disturbing the place where you are watching.

The temptation will be to supply movement yourself—to swim farther, touch something, chase the fish, or investigate the rim. Plan enough comfortable observation time that you do not need to manufacture an event.

A good first visit would be modest: acclimatize at a secure station, travel along a surveyed channel, watch the upper forest from a protected position, and return with ample reserve.

You would miss some spectacular things. That is acceptable. This is a landscape in which a single bubble may take most of a day to reach the sky. It rewards a visitor who does not insist on reaching everything at once.
