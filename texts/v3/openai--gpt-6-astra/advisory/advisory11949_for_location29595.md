Prepare for this place as you would for a freezing industrial floodplain in which the machinery is alive, the floor is not reliably solid, and the emergency signals are changes in the behavior of animals.

The most dangerous mistake would be to think of it as a coast you can walk across. It is a densely occupied living surface that happens, in places and at certain times, to resemble land.

An ordinary human should visit briefly, bring an independent refuge, and plan never to need the ground.

## Decide how you will leave before deciding where to arrive

Your exit must not depend on reaching a particular exposed stone at low tide.

Water arrives ahead of the tide. Basins acquire new slabs overnight. Apparently solid surfaces move, fold, or admit predators without breaking. A return point placed on “dry land” could become submerged, crowded with animals, or incorporated into something you do not understand.

If your means of multiverse travel permits it, arrange:

- A return mechanism you carry, rather than one left at the arrival site.
- An independent backup that does not share its power supply or activation method.
- A scheduled extraction if you fail to check in.
- A way to return from inside your shelter, without opening a hatch or stepping outside.

Do not assume a portal, beacon, or other travel device is immune to electrical discharge merely because it works between universes. Establish its limitations beforehand.

Visit during the longest available period of gray daylight, outside the deepest freeze. Winter does not make this landscape walkable. It concentrates large animals beneath a deceptive, shifting crust and reduces your margin for every mistake.

For a first visit, stay close to the point of arrival and return well before your supplies require it. Crossing the plain is an expedition, not an afternoon walk. “Most of a day” is how long the distance takes under favorable conditions; it says nothing about whether the route remains available.

## Bring a room, not just clothing

The preferred refuge is a sealed, insulated observation vehicle capable of remaining clear of both water and ground. If your travel technology cannot provide that, consider observing from a protected threshold instead of entering.

Flight is not automatically safe: there is wet icing weather, poor contrast, and uncertain electrical behavior. Any airborne platform needs appropriate redundancy, weather capability, and a known way to remain safe during a discharge. An ordinary drone or lightly equipped aircraft is not an adequate substitute.

A useful refuge would have:

- Independent heating and breathable air.
- A small entry compartment for shedding contaminated outer equipment.
- Windows or external cameras that can be cleared of ice.
- Protected electrical systems and physically separated backups.
- Interior controls operable with gloves.
- Supplies sufficient to wait through a prolonged failure of visibility or extraction.

Electrical protection requires particular care. The described storms are not simply lightning coming down from clouds. They propagate through a continuous wet landscape, across bodies and between exposed surfaces. Rubber boots, a plastic groundsheet, or an ordinary tent would not establish a safe refuge. Saltwater films can bridge nominally insulating surfaces.

A purpose-built protective enclosure would need engineering for this specific mode of exposure. Do not discover its limits while sitting in it.

If all you have is expedition clothing, a backpack, and a tent, this is probably a location to decline.

## Dress for persistent wet cold

The cold here is intimate. It gets onto the face, into fasteners, between layers, and into everything you briefly put down. Warm animal outflow creates local liquid water, not a habitable climate.

Wear a waterproof expedition suit with insulating layers beneath it, a protected face, and enough hand dexterity to operate your exit equipment. Carry dry replacement gloves and inner layers in genuinely sealed storage. Boots need insulation and traction on both glazed stone and greasy biological films; neither property implies electrical safety.

Keep essential controls large and mechanically simple. Expect tiny switches to become difficult to use, exposed hinges to ice up, and touchscreens to become unreliable under wet gloves.

Treat the iron taste in the air as a reason to protect your breathing, not as evidence of any particular harmless mineral. The mist may carry salts, biological material, or other substances. For an initial visit, bring your own breathable air until the atmosphere has been assessed. A filter cannot correct every possible atmospheric problem.

Bring all drinking water and food. Nothing described here is a plausible emergency ration for an unadapted human. Boiling would not remove salts or necessarily neutralize unfamiliar chemical hazards.

Do not warm your hands in an animal’s outflow.

## Learn what the noise is telling you

You will probably find the sound more exhausting than expected.

It comes through your feet and bones as well as your ears. The irregular pumping encourages the brain to search for a stable rhythm, then repeatedly breaks that rhythm. Distance is hard to judge. A deep sound may be transmitted through stone from far away; a thin scraping may come from a film of life immediately beside you.

Use hearing protection, preferably with adjustable environmental monitoring. Keep a visual or tactile alert system so that protecting your hearing does not leave you unaware of changes outside.

Before moving anywhere, observe an ordinary stretch of activity long enough to recognize its baseline.

The crucial warning is **the pumping stopping**.

Large animals cease in sequence. Smaller ones flatten. Swimmers turn edge-on. That collective change precedes the blue discharge. Treat it as an immediate shelter signal, not an opportunity to get a better photograph.

The interval is unspecified and may be very short. Your plan cannot involve being several minutes from protection and running back when things become quiet.

Do not assume the danger is over because pumping resumes. Confirm that your equipment remains functional, inspect for damage, and reconsider whether to continue at all.

As for the enormous answering knocks beneath the plain: their cause is unknown. You do not need to identify it before choosing to leave. A new disturbance that makes the resident animals pause is sufficient reason to end a human visit.

## Regard every surface as unverified

Three different hazards can look like ground here:

1. Actual stone, which may be slick, newly risen, or crossed by something beneath it.
2. An animal’s armored back.
3. A predator presenting the appearance of a basin.

Ice adds another unreliable layer. It may be warmed from below, lifted by early water, or supported by moving animals rather than by anything stable.

Do not step onto a broad flat patch merely because it is empty and convenient. Do not step onto a crowded patch because other animals are using it. Small grazers cross the folding predators safely until enough weight accumulates.

Equally, do not conclude that joining a procession protects you from the hunters beneath the rock. The description suggests that crowding matters to those hunters, but it does not establish that a human can imitate the relevant pattern. Their sensory world may distinguish you immediately.

There is no demonstrated safe walking rule here.

If a brief ground excursion is unavoidable, it should be treated as a controlled exposure with immediate retrieval—not as hiking. Stay together, avoid trailing lines in channels, and do not anchor yourself to an animal, an ice shelf, or a convenient-looking slab. Even an ordinary rescue tether can snag or create an unwanted connection to wet terrain; the entire retrieval arrangement needs to be designed for the environment.

A knee-high bulge moving through intact stone is not an obstacle to step over. It is a reason to get off the surface.

## Give the inhabitants much more room than they appear to need

The large pumping animals are not docks, heaters, or shelter walls.

Their openings draw material inward. Their plates can close violently. Their bodies shudder, discharge warm jets, and participate in the electrical behavior of the channels. A person kneeling beside one could be injured without ever being treated as prey.

Stay away from:

- Intake and breathing openings.
- Overlapping plate edges.
- Dense outlet communities.
- Channels packed tightly enough that retreat would require crossing bodies.
- Egg masses and newly exposed impressions.

Avoid touching the smaller creatures as well. Their apparently manageable size says nothing about their chemistry, electrical properties, or response to being trapped against human skin.

Do not assume you are immune to predation because you are biologically unfamiliar. A predator that closes when enough weight gathers may not need to recognize you as food.

## Navigate with instruments, but do not trust only one kind

The horizon is deceptive. Islands may be herds, pale patches may be moving animals, and ringing sounds can remain after their sources depart.

Those lingering tones might be useful local landmarks, but first establish whether they stay fixed and how long they persist. A sound marking where an animal used to stand is not necessarily marking solid ground.

Bring independent means of recording position, elapsed time, and your route. Keep a protected record inside the refuge. Do not rely solely on a compass in a landscape with conspicuous electrical phenomena and mineral deposits, or on a satellite system whose infrastructure may not exist here.

Record water levels directly. Familiar tide predictions would be inadequate even if the astronomical cycle were known: local water movement demonstrably precedes the visible sea.

Prefer short, retraceable observations to ambitious progress toward a distant landmark. In this landscape, “I can still see the way back” is weak reassurance.

## Take the impressions seriously without deciding what they mean

The stone sometimes contains injuries that have not yet occurred. That does not prove a single, dependable system of prediction. It does mean you should arrive with a rule for handling such discoveries.

If you find an impression resembling you or your equipment:

- Do not lie in it, fit a limb into it, or test the correspondence.
- Document it remotely if doing so does not delay departure.
- Check your condition and equipment.
- Return to safety.

A crack depicted in a helmet would justify inspecting and replacing that helmet. It would not justify assuming the helmet must inevitably crack, or deliberately putting it under stress to “get the event over with.”

Do not turn an anomaly into an experiment involving your body.

Also avoid carrying away fresh stone or egg-bearing material. The relationships among electrical shocks, hatching, sediment, and newly formed rock are poorly understood. A souvenir could be a living colony, a reproductive structure, or simply an object whose behavior changes outside its original basin.

## Expect your judgment to deteriorate before you feel defeated

A human visitor is likely to become tired, cold, overstimulated, and uncertain of what is moving. You may start misclassifying animals as terrain, or seek relief in an apparently quiet, empty patch—the very sort of place that deserves suspicion.

Use conservative turnaround criteria agreed upon in advance:

- Moisture reaches insulating inner layers.
- Hand function declines.
- Visibility no longer supports confident retrieval.
- A critical instrument fails.
- Water begins changing unexpectedly along the return route.
- The animals show a coordinated behavior you have not previously observed.
- Anyone in the party starts insisting on “just a few more minutes” after a warning.

A companion watching your condition is more valuable than another piece of sampling equipment.

The best first visit would be deliberately modest: remain in your protected refuge, watch the green traffic emerge and vanish, listen to the pumping through instruments, and leave with a better understanding of how little of the apparent ground is available to you.

This place does not need to be hostile to kill a human. Almost everything living there has ways to survive cold brine, electrical shocks, crushing crowds, and an unstable relationship between rock and flesh. You do not. Bring a small, dependable human environment with you—and preserve your ability to take it away.
