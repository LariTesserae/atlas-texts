Prepare for this city as you would for a winter harbor built inside a living cave. You will be cold, often damp, and unable to see very far. The ground will not always be where your instincts say it is. Much of the apparent architecture will be alive, and much of the actual architecture will be moving—usually too slowly to notice, but not always too slowly to matter.

Your most important equipment will be a local guide. Your second most important preparation will be learning not to switch on a bright light when you become frightened.

## Arrange your arrival before you leave

Do not arrive unannounced on a roof, in an apparently empty plaza, or beside the central basin. A flat, pale surface may be ice over deep water. A black opening may be a flooded service gallery. What looks like paving may be a gathering of animals with their breathing folds exposed.

Arrange to arrive inside a confirmed, occupied room, with someone expecting you. Ideally, that room should connect to the city’s sheltered pedestrian passages without requiring an exposed crossing.

Your host or guide should establish:

- Whether the air is breathable for a human, including in enclosed lower levels.
- Whether the local atmospheric pressure is tolerable.
- Where humans can obtain safe drinking water and food.
- Which nearby routes are currently maintained.
- How residents communicate an evacuation or a major water release.
- How you will leave if your equipment fails or you become injured.

Do not assume that a city full of breathing creatures is safe for your lungs. Its ecology runs on unfamiliar compounds brought up from newly exposed rock. Human-compatible air is something to verify, not something the presence of animals proves.

Keep your return point in a dry, accessible location. If your travel system requires precise coordinates or an anchored device, account for the fact that buildings and streets adjust relative to the mountain. Do not fasten anything into the mineral teeth, window fields, or other structural machinery.

## Dress for cold water, not merely cold air

The cold here has several ways to reach you: wind through a bridge, saline water inside a boot, a wet hand against stone, an hour standing still while an occupied staircase clears.

Wear a layered system that stays useful when damp:

- Moisture-managing underlayers.
- Insulation that tolerates damp conditions.
- A windproof, waterproof outer shell.
- Waterproof, insulated boots with a sole compound suited to cold, smooth surfaces.
- Gloves that allow you to identify textures without exposing your fingers.
- A hood and face covering that protect your cheeks, lips, and teeth.

Bring spare socks, glove liners, and a dry insulating layer in a waterproof bag. Avoid long scarves, loose straps, and dangling cords: there are joints, seams, animals, and narrow passages everywhere.

Do not automatically put mountaineering spikes on your boots. They may help on some ice and fail badly on polished mineral. They may also damage the city’s tactile markings or injure animals hidden underfoot. Ask what residents use.

You will want a balance between insulation and sensory contact. The neighborhood notes are felt through the feet, and wall textures carry information. Boots so thick that they erase vibration, or gloves too bulky to distinguish a doorway marker from a drainage seam, may make you warmer but less able to travel safely.

Have local equipment adapted for you if possible.

## Learn to move without making daylight

Bring a small, hooded, dimmable amber lamp. Have its wavelength and brightness checked locally before using it outside.

A white lamp does not merely reveal this city. It changes what the city is doing.

Tiny canal animals rise toward it. Larger feeders follow. The listening creatures descend from above. Your attempt to see the next ten steps may reorganize a food web around your ankles.

Cover incidental lights as well: watch faces, device screens, camera flashes, charging indicators. Disable automatic flashes and bright startup displays. If you need a powerful lamp for a genuine emergency, keep it secured against accidental activation and understand that using it may create an additional hazard.

Passive low-light or thermal equipment could help, but neither should become your sole means of navigation. The temperature differences are small, walls contain warm water, and animals gather precisely where warmth emerges. A thermal image may tell you that a surface is occupied without telling you whether it is safe to touch. Active imaging systems also need local approval; “invisible to humans” does not mean invisible to the wildlife.

Spend your first day learning a short route with a guide:

1. Your room to the nearest shelter.
2. Your room to drinking water and food.
3. Your room to the departure point.

Learn the route with your hands and feet, not just your eyes. Make a tactile or audio record if that helps.

Do not touch every convenient-looking wall. Some walls are animals. Follow surfaces your guide identifies as handrails or navigation strips.

## Take sound seriously

The city will probably feel loud before you recognize it as loud. The calls enter the chest; echoes return from below; whole buildings may seem to breathe or hum. An ordinary human could find this nauseating, disorienting, or exhausting even without any direct danger.

Bring hearing protection, but do not plan to wear strong noise-canceling headphones while walking. They would remove part of the city’s street-sign system.

A sensible arrangement is adjustable protection for unusually loud areas and stronger protection for sleeping in a secure room. Ear protection may reduce what reaches your ears without stopping the vibration you feel in your ribs.

Ask your guide to teach you the difference between:

- A neighborhood’s identifying note.
- A familiar animal chorus.
- Routine movement of water through a building.
- A sound that residents respond to by stopping, moving uphill, or leaving.

Do not expect to learn the distinction by intuition. A distant fracture may sound as if it happened beneath you. A creature close by may sound architectural.

Avoid whistles, ultrasonic pest devices, loud playback, and casual tests of the echo. This is a place where sound is both public information and an animal’s means of perceiving its surroundings.

If you become separated, use the locally agreed signal rather than inventing one.

## Assume “empty” is a conclusion, not an appearance

You should arrive prepared to be patient with animals.

The brush-faced creatures are not necessarily threatening, but their abundance creates practical hazards. They can obscure steps, occupy rails, conceal drains, and move just enough to change your footing. Wait for a path rather than forcing one.

Do not push a boot into a mass of bodies to see what is underneath. Do not peel resting creatures off warm seams. Do not step over a frost-bound shell unless you know where the whole animal lies.

Your own warmth may interest them. An exposed heater, a warm battery case, or a leaking hot-water bottle could become an attractive resting place. Keep heat sources enclosed and do not leave warm equipment on the floor. Use proper beds and benches rather than sleeping beside a conveniently warm wall.

Carry food in sealed containers. There is no reason to assume local animals will recognize it as food, but there is also no benefit in finding out in your bedroom.

Avoid feeding, petting, or collecting anything. These creatures are part of the drainage, ventilation, and parasite-control arrangements, whether intentionally managed or merely accommodated. Moving them can have consequences beyond the animal you can see.

## Treat every body of water as a concealed depth

The water is not drinking water simply because it has not frozen. Some of it is highly saline. Some comes from chemically active rock. Conventional filtration may remove suspended material without removing dissolved salts or making unfamiliar chemistry safe.

Bring a verified supply for your initial stay, then use a source confirmed suitable for humans. Carry familiar emergency food as well. A productive ecosystem does not guarantee edible calories for you.

Avoid immersion altogether. A slip into a shallow channel could mean soaked insulation and rapid cooling; a slip through reservoir ice could put you beneath an opaque roof among large animals.

In particular:

- Do not judge ice by its apparent thickness.
- Do not walk onto a reservoir because animals occupy it.
- Do not lean over the water to examine something under the ice.
- Do not enter pressure chambers, flooded foundations, or service galleries without specialist supervision.

Stagnant-looking water may still be connected to an active hydraulic system.

Use ropes and flotation equipment only where local conditions call for them. A loose tether in a crowded passage can become an entanglement hazard. For routes beside open water, arrange an actual rescue plan, not just a piece of equipment you hope will help.

## Choose lodging for stability and dryness

For a first visit, stay in a well-maintained inhabited building with a dry sleeping room, reliable ventilation, and a familiar route to higher pedestrian levels.

Ask questions that would sound peculiar in most cities:

- Is this floor still intended to remain a floor?
- When were the stairs and doorframes last adjusted?
- Does water enter this level during a basin rise?
- Is the room near a major calling colony?
- Can you reach the exit without crossing reservoir ice?
- Where can wet clothing be dried without attracting animals or obstructing ventilation?

Do not block a pulsing air intake because its rhythm bothers you. Do not patch a seep, wedge an adjustable frame, or interfere with a window field. The distinction between a defect and a working component may not be obvious.

Cold-weather condensation will be a persistent problem. Dry your clothes deliberately. Inspect your feet every day. Rinse salt contamination from equipment with suitable fresh water when available, and keep connectors, batteries, and medical supplies protected.

## Have a plan for the basin before visiting it

The central basin is not a scenic lake. It is an outlet for processes beneath the city.

Visit it only with someone who understands the adjoining district. Stay on established observation routes. Never step beyond the railing, and do not assume that its presence is a promise that the ground beyond is safe.

If the railing begins to withdraw, drains open, or residents start moving away, leave with them. Do not wait to see how far the water rises.

The rise is small in height but distributed through a large, interconnected structure. Water entering a low plaza can conceal its edges, change its footing, and freeze across an exit. You do not want to be discovering the local escape route at that moment.

Afterward, keep away from the new stone and fresh ice until residents declare the route usable. The first animals gathering there are following warmth, not certifying human load capacity.

## Give yourself time to become accustomed to it

For your first few days, keep outings short. Return before you are cold or exhausted. Plan a warm, dry place where you can recover without having to navigate another bridge.

You may have difficulty sleeping. There may be no useful visual day, and the sounds may never cease. Bring a reliable clock and maintain a regular schedule. If you need bright light to regulate your sleep, arrange to use it inside a screened room where it cannot spill into passages or water.

Most importantly, do not mistake the city’s patience for safety.

A corridor takes forty years to close, but you can fall down its wet stairs in a second. The mountain rises slowly, but the water released by that process still reaches a human body at the speed of water.

The right visitor is not especially fearless. They are dry, well-rested, lightly equipped, and willing to wait. They learn where to place a hand. They let a stairway finish moving—not because the stone is shifting, but because the animals have not yet uncovered the next tread.
