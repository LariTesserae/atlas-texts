Prepare for this place as an expedition onto a cold inland sea, not as a visit to ruins. The ruins happen to occupy almost everything beneath you, but they offer none of the usual comforts of a built environment: no reliable shelter, no firm ground you can readily reach, no promise that a road leads somewhere you can stand.

The most important fact is not how deep the water is. It is that **you may be unable to get out of it even where it is shallow**.

## Arrange somewhere dry to return to

Your arrival point should be aboard a stable vessel or on a verified, self-supporting platform. Do not accept “at the surface” as a sufficiently precise destination. You could arrive among floating sedges, beneath a leaf mat, or in open water days from anything resembling shore.

If your means of multiverse travel permits a return passage, establish its conditions before departure:

- Can it reopen on a moving boat?
- Does it require a solid surface?
- Will it function underwater or beneath ice?
- Can someone else activate it if you are unconscious?
- Does it remain available if your original arrival point becomes submerged or obstructed?

A fixed return point just above today’s waterline is a poor refuge in a landscape where distant weather can move water through the basins for hours.

Bring a vessel with an enclosed, heated cabin, sleeping space, dependable communications within your expedition, and enough supplies to make returning early a choice rather than a crisis. A shallow-draft craft is useful, but its propulsion must tolerate vegetation. Exposed propellers and cooling-water intakes can become liabilities in dense weed. Carry a practical means of clearing fouling without putting a person overboard.

Do not count on anchoring to the ancient work. What looks like a substantial masonry foundation may be a thin facing over softened fill. Choose and test mooring arrangements with the expectation that the bottom is layered, fragile, and locally hollow.

## Assume you must supply your own living conditions

The description gives you water and plants, not a habitable environment for humans.

Before opening your arrival enclosure, verify atmospheric pressure, oxygen, temperature, and any obvious airborne hazards. Once those are established, the more ordinary expedition problems will probably dominate.

Bring all your food. There are no visible fish or shellfish, and unidentified aquatic plants are not an emergency pantry.

Bring drinking water or a treatment system validated for the water you actually find. Brown, peat-stained water is not necessarily poisonous, but neither boiling nor a simple backpacking filter establishes that unfamiliar water is chemically safe. Its apparent stillness and absence of animals tell you very little about its suitability for consumption.

Carry medical supplies, spare dry clothing in separate waterproof packages, and a reliable method of warming an immersed person. Everyone working on deck should wear suitable flotation; clothing and immersion protection should match the measured water temperature, not how mild the air feels.

Cold water is especially unforgiving in a place where rescue may involve cutting through vegetation before anyone can reach you.

## See it from above before going below

The best first visitor is a patient surveyor.

Begin with surface observations, depth soundings, and small exploratory movements near your base. Use sonar where practical. An aerial camera may reveal the faint geometry of channels and embankments much more clearly than your eyes can from deck level. Do not assume familiar satellite navigation exists here; bring a way to establish local coordinates and maintain a route independently.

Build your map from measured position, depth, and observed obstacles. Do not navigate solely by the shape of a sedge field. Some fields are rooted to walls; others can lift, settle, or move. Two apparently identical dark passages may lead into very different basins.

Also, resist the urge to treat former thoroughfares as safe waterways. Their continuity is archaeological, not navigational. A channel can be blocked by a fallen lintel, a submerged timber, a mat of hornwort, or a sill too shallow for your hull.

Travel slowly enough that an unseen obstruction is an inconvenience rather than a wreck.

## For an ordinary human, the first underwater visit should be remote

A small remotely operated vehicle with cameras, sonar, depth sensing, and recoverable tether management would be far more useful than a mask and fins. Even that vehicle should begin in open water: loose tether can snag on roots, timber joints, and displaced stone.

If you want to descend in person without becoming a specialist diver, use a professionally operated submersible designed for the measured conditions. It still needs to avoid vegetation and unstable structures; a pressure hull does not make entanglement harmless.

**Do not make your first dive here an exploratory swim under the plants.**

The suspended vegetation creates an overhead environment. A pale patch above you may not be an exit. It may be thin leaves, interwoven roots, or ice with snow on top. You cannot count on ascending directly to air.

The hazards reinforce one another:

- Cold reduces dexterity.
- Darkness limits recognition.
- Root hairs and stems catch equipment.
- A fin stroke can raise sediment and erase visibility.
- The bottom may not support your weight.
- A displaced slab can release more fill than its size suggests.
- Returning by sight may become impossible after your own passage.

A recreational diving qualification alone is not preparation for that combination. Any human diving should be planned by divers trained for the particular cold, low-visibility, overhead conditions involved, with appropriate redundancy, navigation procedures, surface support, and a rescue plan that does not depend on seeing the casualty.

For everyone else, a camera lowered into the water is not a lesser encounter. Here, it may show you more.

## Do not use the ruins as equipment

Do not stand on a submerged wall, pull yourself through a passage by a timber, or secure a line around an exposed coping stone without an engineering reason to trust it.

The whole landscape is made of accumulated work rather than bedrock. A firm-looking surface might be paving over softened brick over another layer of paving. A pole can penetrate an archaeological sequence without finding anything suitable to bear a person’s weight.

The preserved wooden joints and baskets deserve particular restraint. Their survival in cold water does not mean they will survive lifting, drying, or being handled. Leave objects submerged and in place. Photograph them with scale and position information rather than trying to bring back a sample.

Your visit may be the largest physical disturbance a particular courtyard has experienced in years. Keep boat wakes small, avoid dredging with propeller wash, and do not clear vegetation merely to improve the view.

## Choose a season that leaves exits open

Unless winter conditions are the purpose of the expedition, avoid ice season.

Ice turns open water into a roof, complicates launching and recovery, and can immobilize a vessel among vegetation. Its sounds may also travel far through the water without telling you where the danger lies.

In any season, monitor water level and current over time. A calm local sky does not guarantee stable conditions. The description specifically suggests slow disturbances arriving from elsewhere. A passage that was easy on the outward journey may have a different clearance or current on your return.

Keep generous margins for daylight, fuel, heating, and travel time. The terrain does not offer convenient places to stop when a schedule becomes unrealistic.

## Prepare for how little will happen

This is not a place that rewards an itinerary of landmarks.

You may travel for hours along what proves to be a rise no taller than you are. Your strongest light may reveal only suspended particles and a small patch of stone. There may be no skyline, no animal movement, and no clear distinction between having reached a district and still approaching it.

That can produce an odd kind of fatigue. You will keep looking for a center because places made by people usually teach us to look for one. Here, scale and water have removed the clues.

Set modest tasks: map one basin, record one repaired terrace face, watch the movement of one floating field against a fixed stone. Keep regular meals, warm breaks, and contact with your companions. Do not let the apparent quiet persuade you to extend an excursion beyond its planned limits.

And leave room for the smallest details. The most intelligible thing you encounter may be a thumbprint in a brick, not the shape of a district. The successive repairs may tell you more than any grand view could: people lifting another course into place, reusing whatever fragments remained, making an edge hold a little longer.

You do not need to decide why they stayed, or what finally happened to them. The water does not provide that answer.

A successful visit would leave you warm, dry, and able to return, with a few careful observations and almost no physical trace. You would have seen a place whose enormous scale was achieved by repeated manageable acts—and whose quiet does not make it safe.
