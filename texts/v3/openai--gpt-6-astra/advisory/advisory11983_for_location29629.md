Prepare for this place as you would for a warm, fragile cave whose walls happen to be alive—not as a forest, and not as a garden that someone has necessarily planted for you.

The chief mistake would be to confuse its apparent gentleness with hospitality. There are no thorns in the description, no predators announcing themselves, no violent movement. But neither is there bare ground, a dependable water supply, a breeze, or anything that can confidently be called a road. Almost everything that looks useful belongs to a system you do not understand.

Your first visit should be short, lightly equipped, and explicitly planned as a reconnaissance.

## Before going down

Leave someone outside with your intended route and a firm return time. Go with a companion if possible. The entrance is broad enough to feel inviting, but a hollow extending for miles is a substantial underground expedition, regardless of how brightly it is lit.

Establish a rendezvous immediately outside the split roots. Photograph the entrance from both directions. Once inside, turn around frequently: an opening recognizable from above may look like just another pale seam when seen from below.

Bring a clock that does not depend on observing the sky. The even light, constant warmth, and lack of visible change will make elapsed time difficult to judge. Set alarms for drinking, turning back, and reporting in. Do not let the absence of sunset become permission to keep walking.

For a first trip, take:

- All your drinking water, with a generous reserve.
- Familiar food that can be eaten without cooking.
- A headlamp and a backup light, despite the luminous ceiling.
- A mapping device or notebook, compass, and ordinary watch. Test instruments at the entrance rather than assuming they work normally here.
- A communication device, tested through the entrance and at successive depths.
- A compact first-aid kit, clean wound coverings, and enough clean water to rinse an exposed eye or patch of skin.
- A lightweight, waterproof sitting sheet.
- Sealed bags for every scrap of rubbish and, if necessary, human waste.

If you have access to atmospheric monitoring equipment and know how to use it, bring it. A vast, poorly ventilated biological interior is not guaranteed to have breathable air merely because it contains green tissue and a few animals. A particulate mask may help with airborne material; it does not correct oxygen deficiency or make unknown gases safe.

If the air cannot be assessed, keep the first visit close to the entrance. Headache, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, nausea, or confusion are reasons to leave immediately—not sensations to investigate farther underground.

## Dress for heat and constant contact

Expect to brush against living surfaces even when trying not to.

Wear lightweight long sleeves and trousers, preferably with smooth fabric that will not snag on the fine leaves. Take thin gloves for unavoidable hand contact. Footwear should have secure traction without spikes or aggressively cutting tread: the floor is springy, sometimes waxy, and potentially wet, but it is also living tissue.

Avoid trailing straps, exposed hooks, large rigid packs, and anything that makes you wider than you need to be. The narrow passages will repeatedly bring both shoulders into contact with foliage. A compact pack is worth more here than elaborate expedition hardware.

Use a brimmed hat and moderate glare protection. The ceiling is an extended light source, and even the shadows are reflective. You may experience eye strain without ever looking toward anything that resembles a sun. Do not stare through the thinnest plates in an attempt to find the surface.

Do not overdress simply because you are underground. There is no cool rock to lean against, and the floor itself supplies warmth. In still air, sweat may provide less relief than you expect. Walk slowly from the outset rather than waiting to feel overheated.

## Learn to walk without assuming the floor is ground

The main physical hazard is not necessarily that the landscape will move. It is that you will misread what is supporting you.

A rounded ridge may be a thin lobe suspended above a basin. A leafy mat may cover a seam. A column may support considerable weight without tolerating sideways force. Something that has held its own position for years has not thereby demonstrated that it can hold yours.

Favor broad, continuous surfaces. Test doubtful footing gradually, keeping your weight over a secure position. Avoid jumping between folds, climbing the tall fins, or stepping across openings whose depth you cannot see.

Do not drive stakes, screws, or climbing protection into the tissue. Do not assume a root is a suitable rope anchor. If a section requires technical climbing, it is beyond the appropriate scope of an initial visit unless you have a support system independent of the organism.

Trekking poles may puncture surfaces or slip on wax. If used at all, they should have broad, soft tips and serve as balance aids, not probes driven into the floor.

The empty watercourses may look like convenient paths. Be cautious about using them. They are channels, even if nobody has seen them carry water. Their perpetual readiness is not proof that they cannot function.

## Carry water; do not adopt the pools

The clear, shallow cups will be especially tempting in the heat. Treat them as parts of the organism, not as springs.

Their water may contain dissolved compounds that ordinary filtration would not remove. The converging ribs and central pores suggest some sort of exchange or drainage, but do not establish what the water is for.

Do not drink it, bathe in it, or put tired feet into it. Keep soap, sunscreen residue, insect repellent, and food out of the basins. Avoid touching the suspended droplets just to see whether they will fall.

Likewise, eat nothing found here. Sweet smells do not establish edibility, and a surface that resembles a leaf need not have familiar chemistry.

## Navigate by records, not impressions

This country offers long views without straightforward routes. Seeing a bright court through several ranks of columns does not mean you can walk directly to it. You may repeatedly approach the same basin from different sides while believing you have entered a new one.

At each significant junction:

1. Record the time.
2. Photograph the onward route.
3. Photograph the way back.
4. Note a distinctive feature that is visible at walking height.

Count junctions as well as estimating distance. Recheck your route before committing to a descent.

Avoid cutting marks into tissue or breaking leaves to blaze a trail. If temporary markers are necessary, use conspicuous freestanding ones that you can retrieve. Nothing should be glued, tied tightly, or pinned to a growing surface.

Do not rely on shouting to maintain contact. The dense divisions may hide your companion only a few steps away, and you do not yet know how sound travels through them.

## Treat the enclosures as unclaimed spaces, not supplied accommodation

They may be the most psychologically persuasive things in the hollow.

A doorway at human height and a shelf at sitting height invite an immediate conclusion: *this is somewhere to rest*. That conclusion may be right. It may also be an accident of scale, or evidence of a relationship whose participants are absent.

Approach visibly and announce yourself in an ordinary voice. The absence of tool marks does not prove the absence of builders, inhabitants, or ownership. Nor do unworn thresholds prove abandonment.

Before entering, look for animals, fluids, narrow rear openings, and any change in air quality. Stay near the entrance initially. If you sit, use your sheet to limit contact between your clothing and the surface.

Do not make a fire. Do not burn incense, smoke, or cook against a wall. Do not assume the rear chamber is a bedroom.

For the first visit, return outside to sleep. The enclosure may relieve glare without offering any relief from heat, and an attractive recess is a poor place to discover that your communications no longer work.

## Resist the urge to complete anything

You will encounter droplets poised to fall, fronds apparently waiting to spread, roots almost touching the floor, and a leaf with no space to open.

Do not help them.

Do not pull a sheath away, clear a space, straighten a fold, or tip water into the channel prepared beneath it. What appears stalled may be a stable condition rather than a malfunction. Even if it is arrested development, you have no reason to believe that intervention would be harmless.

There is also no need to conclude that time itself has stopped. Your breath, footsteps, and the trembling droplets indicate that at least some ordinary processes continue. Measure what happens rather than turning the place’s appearance into a rule.

## Prepare for the quiet effect on you

The hollow is likely to be tiring in a way that does not feel immediately like effort.

Your eyes receive abundant light but few useful shadows. Your skin receives warmth from every direction. Smells change across invisible boundaries. Your ears receive almost nothing unless you move. Meanwhile, every ridge and folded leaf suggests an event about to happen.

You may find yourself waiting.

Give that experience room, but give it a limit. Sit for ten minutes rather than “until something changes.” Drink when the alarm sounds. Leave at the agreed time even if you feel you have nearly understood the place.

Afterward, clean your footwear and equipment before entering another ecosystem, and wash exposed skin. Report any delayed rash, breathing difficulty, or unusual illness with an accurate account of the exposure.

The right disposition is patient, observant, and restrained. This is not visibly a hostile country. It is, however, a country in which every available space already seems accounted for. Visit as someone passing through—not as the missing element that will finally make it unfold.
