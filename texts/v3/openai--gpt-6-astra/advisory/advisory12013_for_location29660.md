Prepare for a high-altitude industrial city whose streets are also the exposed workings of a watershed. You will spend much of your visit walking over, beside, and occasionally through the systems that keep its inhabitants warm and fed.

The mistake would be to pack for either a mountain expedition or a tropical wetland. This is neither. You need modest protection against both, and enough patience to learn how a very old, very inhabited place works.

## Before you arrive

Confirm three things through a traveler service familiar with ordinary humans:

- **The atmospheric pressure and oxygen level.** The description suggests altitude that a newcomer will feel, but does not establish how high it is or whether the atmosphere exactly matches yours.
- **Which foods and treated water supplies are compatible with human physiology.** Familiar shapes—fish, shellfish, birds—are not proof of familiar biochemistry.
- **The local quarantine rules.** A plantless city need not be susceptible to terrestrial plants, but you should not test that by bringing seeds, muddy boots, live cultures, or an uninspected apple.

Arrive with clean footwear and luggage, a supply of familiar medication, and a small reserve of approved, shelf-stable food. If biological compatibility has not been established, reserve provisions for your whole stay rather than assuming that cooking will make local food safe.

Find out how addresses work. Here, “across the street” on a flat map may mean across a deep cutting, and a building’s roof entrance may be several inhabited levels above its canal entrance. You want maps that show **terraces, stairs, lifts, bridges, and public tunnel routes**, not just street names.

Book lodging before arrival. Aim for a ventilated room in an established visitor district, near reliable vertical transport. Ask specifically about:

- Pump and freight noise at night.
- Nesting colonies outside the windows.
- Drying facilities.
- Whether reaching the room requires long staircases.
- The route home after lifts or public passages close.

An inhabited room with a balcony is not necessarily a room with a balcony you are permitted to use.

## Pack for wet surfaces, not deep water

Your most important equipment is footwear.

Bring closed, supportive walking shoes or boots with excellent traction on wet stone and metal. Waterproofing helps, but avoid heavy insulated boots unless the season requires them: warm masonry, humid air, and climbing will make those miserable. Bring several pairs of socks and a second pair of shoes so that one set can dry.

A useful compact kit would include:

- Quick-drying trousers and light layers.
- A breathable rain shell.
- A light insulating layer for the exposed upper plateau.
- Sun protection for bright, high-altitude weather.
- A small, washable daypack with waterproof inner bags.
- A refillable bottle for verified drinking water.
- A headlamp for poorly lit public stairs—not for exploring tunnels.
- Blister dressings, wound-cleaning supplies, and any personal medication.
- Earplugs for sleeping.
- A mask suitable for dusty or animal-heavy public areas, if recommended locally.

A particulate mask is not protection against hazardous gases. Do not treat one as permission to enter a poorly ventilated passage.

Keep your luggage manageable on stairs. Large wheeled cases are excellent on an arrival concourse and punishing on sagging stone treads.

Do not bring waders unless your work requires them. An ordinary visitor should not be entering cultivation channels, settling ponds, or flooded passages at all.

## Give yourself time to adjust to the height

The warmth is misleading. You may step out into mild air, see bright windows and laundry rather than snow, and assume you can walk as you would at sea level. Then the first staircase will interrupt that assumption.

Keep the first day short. Eat, drink normally, rest, and learn the district immediately around your lodging. Avoid making your first outing a long descent into the city: going down can feel easy enough to conceal how difficult the return will be.

Plan outings around the route back. A lift closure is a minor inconvenience until it leaves you hundreds of steps below your bed.

Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, or dizziness after arrival deserve attention rather than another coffee and another terrace. Worsening symptoms warrant medical advice; breathlessness at rest, confusion, or difficulty walking require urgent help. Do not assume that descending a few city levels is an adequate treatment—the whole settlement may still be high.

## Learn to distinguish water from water

There is water everywhere, but its appearance tells you very little about its use.

One channel may carry cooled spring water. Another may carry mineral-processing discharge, animal-house runoff, or water already used by several districts above you. A clear, shallow basin may be less suitable for contact than an opaque municipal washing trough.

On your first day, ask someone local to show you the markings for:

- Drinking water.
- Public washing water.
- Bathing facilities.
- Industrial and cultivation channels.
- Routes subject to sudden flow changes.

Use designated facilities rather than reasoning from temperature or clarity. Do not drink spring water merely because it emerges from rock, and do not assume boiling removes dissolved minerals or industrial contaminants.

Keep open wounds covered. If you fall into a channel, clean yourself and your clothing promptly, and seek local advice if you swallowed water or sustained a cut.

Most importantly, **do not step into a runnel simply because it looks ankle-deep**. Films can make the bottom extremely slippery; pale sediment can hide a broken edge, intake, or deeper opening. The stone beside it may be just as slick.

## Treat the city as maintained, not abandoned

Much of this place will look like somewhere you could explore.

There will be old arches, half-hidden stairs, bricked-up openings, mineral-encrusted pipes, and courts that appear untouched for decades. Some will still be carrying essential utilities. Others will be unused precisely because reaching them is dangerous or inconvenient.

Age is not permission.

Never turn a valve, cross a barrier, climb a pipe, or enter a service passage without authorization. A tiny adjustment in a mixing basin may matter to a cultivation hall far below. A dry-looking overflow route may become wet during an ordinary operational change.

The sulfurous smell near some drains is a reason to pay attention, not a reliable measure of danger. Potentially hazardous gases cannot be judged safely by smell. Stay out of low, enclosed, poorly ventilated spaces unless they are approved public routes.

Slow machinery also deserves more respect than it initially commands. A mineral wagon moving at walking speed is still an enormous moving mass. Do not squeeze around it, step over its cable, or assume its operator can stop for you. Copy the locals when they stand back and wait.

A guided visit to a working cultivation gallery will teach you more than an afternoon trespassing in obsolete ones.

## Animals are part of the street plan

Do not approach this as a city with unusually abundant wildlife. Approach it as a city where humans share circulation space with other established occupants.

The large animals on spillways may look placid. Leave them alone. Do not step over them, touch them, or get between an adult and a smaller animal. If one blocks a gate, use another route. The presence of a purpose-made notice hook tells you that waiting or detouring is the normal solution.

Avoid feeding anything. Feeding teaches animals to investigate travelers, bags, and hands, and may interfere with an ecology the city already manages carefully.

Keep food enclosed. Do not rest your lunch on a canal coping. Avoid lingering below occupied ledges, and learn whether a local bird’s warning posture means “keep walking” or “take the other stair.”

The shell-covered walls are living surfaces, not merely decorative stone. Do not scrape them for souvenirs.

If bitten or scratched, wash the wound and obtain medical advice appropriate to the local species. Familiar animal-care assumptions may not transfer between worlds.

## Expect food to be unfamiliar in a particular way

The likely surprise is not scarcity. It is the absence of the ingredients you normally use to balance a meal.

You may find abundant broth, eggs, animal fats, fermented microbial preparations, and things resembling fish or shellfish. What may be missing is a piece of fruit, a leafy garnish, bread made from familiar grain, or the sharp smell of cut herbs.

That does not mean the local diet is inadequate. It means you should not assume it meets *your* nutritional needs.

For a short stay, eat at establishments accustomed to human visitors and keep your approved backup food. For a longer one, verify sources of essential vitamins, fiber, and other nutrients rather than relying on the resemblance of local dishes to familiar food.

If you have allergies or dietary restrictions, obtain ingredient explanations in advance. “Made from the gallery harvest” is not a sufficiently precise answer for someone with a medical restriction.

Wash your hands before eating, especially after using rails near rookeries. In this city, an attractive canal-side snack spot and an animal waste pathway may be the same place.

## Prepare for continuous sensation

The sound may be more tiring than you expect.

Nothing has to bang or shriek to prevent rest. Pumps transmit through the bedrock. Water occupies every gap in conversation. Fans and rookeries provide a background that never quite falls silent.

Choose lodging for its actual noise conditions, not its apparent distance from a factory. A quiet-looking wall may carry vibration from machinery several buildings away.

There is also the humidity: damp cuffs, a warm strap against your back, glasses fogging when you enter a gallery. Keep electronics protected and air your shoes each evening.

And there is the absence of green.

At first, you may not notice. Then you will see an empty courtyard and realize there is no tree to stand under, no weed in a neglected corner, no leaf smell after rain. Shade exists, but it is built. Neglect produces crusts, animals, and redirected water rather than vegetation.

If you know that greenery matters to your wellbeing, bring images, music, or another familiar comfort—not a live plant. Plan occasional time on the open upper plateau if you find the lower districts overwhelming.

## A good first visit

Spend your first morning learning one terrace. Locate drinking water, medical help, transport, and a reliable place to eat. Ask your host which nearby stairs are safest when wet.

Take a guided descent later, using public lifts or railways where available. Visit a mixing basin, a cultivation gallery, and a market in that order if possible. Once you understand how warmth and food move through the city, its layout will feel less bewildering.

Leave room in the schedule for delays. A wagon passes slowly. A stair is being cleaned. A gate has a sleeping animal in front of it. Fog has swallowed the view you came to see.

The best preparation is to stop thinking of these as interruptions to the visit. They are much of what visiting this place will be like.

You are entering a city whose inhabitants have spent centuries learning where to stand, what to leave alone, and when to wait. Bring good shoes, take the return journey seriously, and let them teach you.
