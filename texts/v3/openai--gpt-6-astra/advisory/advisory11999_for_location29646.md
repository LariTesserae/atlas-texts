Prepare for a city where the infrastructure is more trustworthy than the landscape.

The place does not sound hostile. It sounds carefully maintained by people who have learned that a street, a shadow, and a patch of shade may each require different rules. An ordinary human could probably visit comfortably—with local guidance—but should not assume that familiar appearances imply familiar physics.

Your most useful equipment will be good footwear, an independent way to contact your host, and the willingness to stop when everyone else stops.

## Before arriving

Arrange accommodation in an occupied district, preferably in a building accustomed to visitors from elsewhere. You want a host who can answer mundane questions without mistaking them for philosophical ones:

- Is the air safe for a human to breathe continuously?
- Which water is potable, and where does drinking water come from?
- What food is compatible with human digestion?
- When does the ground system adjust, and how are changes announced?
- Which routes remain open during an adjustment?
- What is the local procedure if a visitor becomes separated, disoriented, or stranded beyond transit service?

Do not assume the visible pools or condensation channels supply drinking water. Water can be perfectly suitable for a garden without being suitable for you. Likewise, the lush vegetation says little about whether the atmosphere has the oxygen concentration and pressure you need. Have those checked through your normal multiverse travel service rather than testing them with your lungs.

Get a current map, but also get a **route**: arrival station, correct exit, maintained walkway, building entrance. A map of this city may remain accurate in its relationships while becoming inaccurate in its distances.

For a first visit, avoid accommodation beside an active expansion boundary. The view would be interesting; your first night is not the time to find out what “there have been mistakes” means.

## What to wear and carry

Dress for sustained warmth, long walks, and occasional brief wetting—not for a bright tropical holiday.

Bring:

- **Closed shoes with sturdy, cut-resistant soles and good grip.** The unweathered stone has sharp edges, while garden thresholds may be wet.
- **Lightweight long trousers.** An ankle brushing a plate edge could be more consequential than a fall onto ordinary gravel.
- **Breathable layers and a light rain shell.** You are more likely to want ventilation than insulation, but local indoor conditions are unknown.
- **Clear protective glasses.** These would be useful when the fine flower strands are drifting. A brimmed hat may help keep them out of your face.
- **A small, shaded flashlight.** Use it to inspect footing, steps, and signs, not to flood courtyards with light.
- **Drinking water and a modest supply of verified food.**
- **Your medications, blister care, and supplies for cleaning and covering cuts.**
- **A communicator that works on the local network, plus a backup power source.** Do not assume a familiar-looking charging rail has a compatible output.
- **An offline copy of your host’s address and emergency instructions in the local script.**

A walking aid may be useful if you need one, but do not use a stick to probe the dark seams. Discovering their depth is not necessary to establish that you should stay out of them.

Keep your pack reasonably light. The city’s sparse buildings conceal substantial walking distances, and warmth without strong sunlight can make exertion easy to underestimate.

## Learn the boundary language first

The most important orientation lesson is not the transit map. It is the meaning of the markings on the ground.

Have someone show you an active work boundary, an ordinary crossing, a utility access zone, and a place where an adjustment is expected. Do not infer that every white line means the same thing.

In particular:

- Stay behind temporary fences.
- Do not step onto newly obtained stone, even if it looks solid.
- Do not cross an expansion seam simply because it is currently narrow.
- Do not enter the slits beneath lifted plates.
- Respect rails around apparently empty space, including the demolished canopy’s shadow.

That last point matters. A barrier need not surround an object you can recognize to be doing useful work.

The fresh stone is described as warm and flexible. There is no reason to assume it would support your weight consistently, or that its joining to neighboring ground is complete. A strip that becomes ordinary pavement tomorrow is still a worksite today.

Nor should you treat gradual movement as safe movement. A seam opening slowly enough to watch can still separate you from your return route.

## During a ground adjustment

Ask to observe your first adjustment from a locally approved place, accompanied by someone who knows the district.

The towers are infrastructure, not obviously observation decks or portals for public use. Their open rooms show things that do not reliably correspond to what lies behind them. Do not enter one merely because it appears to offer access to reeds, sunlight, or a better view.

Learn the practical cues: channels becoming quiet, transit pausing, vegetation leaning, the tower rooms clouding. None should be your sole warning system, but together they tell you that the city has entered a different operating state.

When an adjustment begins:

1. Follow posted instructions and local staff.
2. Pause your journey if transit and pedestrians pause theirs.
3. Stay on an approved, established surface, away from edges and work zones.
4. Avoid boarding, climbing stairs, or crossing unfamiliar ground until you know whether those activities remain safe.

If you are already aboard a stopped transit car, remain aboard unless instructed otherwise. Its pause is likely a precaution, not a breakdown inviting passengers to improvise.

Do not race an adjustment to reach a landmark. Here, “only another hundred meters” may be the least reliable part of your plan.

## Keep ordinary human needs on an independent schedule

The dim bronze sky will not give you dependable cues for direction or bedtime.

Bring a clock and maintain a deliberate sleep schedule. For a short stay, preserving something close to your home cycle may be easier than interpreting the slight change called dusk. An eye mask and a controllable bedside light would be sensible additions.

Eat and drink by routine rather than waiting for strong hunger or thirst. Walking through warm, quiet avenues can feel effortless until you discover that the next occupied court is much farther away than expected.

Before a longer walk, identify:

- Your next water source.
- A staffed or occupied stopping place.
- The return service and its operating hours.
- Somewhere you may wait through an adjustment.

Working lamps and clear drains do **not** mean someone is nearby to help. This city maintains places before it fills them.

For navigation, favor station names, district identifiers, and marked intersections over skyline silhouettes. A distant green rise might be a roofed reservoir, and a tower opening is not a dependable window onto the surrounding terrain.

## Be cautious about what your senses confirm

The temporal effects described are subtle enough to invite small, ordinary mistakes.

A footstep heard ahead of you does not necessarily locate a person. A reflection may not describe the sky at that moment. Leaves apparently resting on a pool may already have been removed.

You need not become suspicious of everything. Just avoid making safety-critical decisions from a single sound, reflection, or glimpse. At crossings, use the traffic controls. Around stairs and pools, verify the actual edge and surface before committing your weight.

Stay out of the public pools unless swimming is explicitly permitted. They are deep, their surfaces are visually deceptive, and the passing horizontal sheet is maintenance equipment whose interaction with a human body is unspecified.

The unusual trees call for a similar restraint. People sit beneath them, so their shade is not necessarily dangerous. But if six benches can appear to be seven, do not use that shade as your first rendezvous point with someone unfamiliar with the effect. Meet at a named station entrance instead.

An independent clock, map, or camera may be helpful. None has been established as immune to the phenomena, so do not elevate a device’s reading above local safety practice.

## Treat the gardens as working systems

The greenery is not simply decoration softened by unusual weather. It participates in water collection, distribution, and reproduction.

Do not:

- Pull leaves aside by force to reach a hidden doorway.
- Hang belongings from condensation channels.
- Interfere with swollen water joints or transparent conduits.
- Remove strands between flowers.
- Cut, dig up, or collect the pale roots emerging from below.
- Pour leftover drinks into planting beds.

Ask before touching even an apparently convenient railing if it seems integrated with the watering apparatus.

Carry plain drinking water for rinsing your eyes if airborne strands cause irritation, and seek help for persistent symptoms. Do not assume the strands are toxic, but do not assume unfamiliar reproductive material is harmless either.

Leave the small channel-grazing animals alone. Their habitat is also part of the building’s water system. Watch the solitary birds without feeding or attempting to attract them.

The appropriate attitude is closer to visiting a greenhouse attached to a public utility than wandering through a conventional park.

## Prepare for the quiet

The strongest discomfort may not be physical danger. It may be the city’s scale relative to its visible population.

You will pass functioning intersections with no traffic, station approaches designed for crowds, and long stretches where every sound seems to belong to one identifiable person. That can feel peaceful at first and unsettling later.

Do not interpret the empty intervals as abandonment. Do not assume that a service door beyond the last residence is unimportant, or that an unoccupied avenue is available for camping. People are maintaining these places for reasons you may not yet understand.

Start with short outings. Learn one district well enough that you recognize its water cycle, its transit rhythm, and the sound of an ordinary evening. Use a guide for your first trip toward the outer ground.

There is no need to make the frontier your principal destination. A courtyard during condensation offers an extraordinary experience from somewhere people have already made habitable: leaves turning before water appears, beads climbing a facade, the small ticking of a building distributing moisture.

**Visit as a guest of the maintained city, not as an explorer of apparently empty land.** Let the crews decide when new stone has become a street. Your task is to arrive prepared enough that you can spend your attention on the place itself, rather than on getting safely back from its newest edge.
