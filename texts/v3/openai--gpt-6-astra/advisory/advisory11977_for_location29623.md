Prepare for this as a visit to **an active construction site whose machinery, geology, and ecology may be the same thing**.

The shelf is not necessarily hostile. It supports delicate animals, repeatable routines, and something resembling a visitor’s shelter. But none of that establishes that it can support an ordinary human safely. Its inhabitants know how to live on ground that is still becoming ground. You do not.

Your first priority is not exploring the edge or understanding the frames. It is making sure you can leave after the distance to your exit has changed.

## Before you arrive

Arrange a short reconnaissance visit, preferably during the brightest part of the day. Do not make your first visit an overnight stay. Dusk brings different animal activity, poorer visibility, and stronger wind; it may also conceal changes in the terrain until you step into them.

Your return method should not depend solely on fixed coordinates, a measured number of paces, or an object left somewhere on the shelf. All three could become unreliable as the ground expands.

Ideally, have:

- A return device carried on your body.
- An independent means of retrieval operated from outside the location.
- A companion, with a firm rule against separating.
- A scheduled check-in and an overdue-response plan.
- Enough reserve power and supplies to outlast a delayed departure.

Verify that your transport can retrieve you from the shelf while it is moving or changing shape. If it requires a stationary, level surface, this is not a suitable place to discover that limitation.

Establish breathable air, tolerable pressure, and acceptable temperature before stepping out. Warm stone and abundant local animals are not evidence of human-compatible air. The mountain view also does not tell you the altitude or pressure: too much else here is already departing from ordinary mountain physics.

If you cannot verify the environment remotely, arrive within a protective enclosure rather than trusting a mask and a quick breath.

## What to wear and carry

Dress for **warm, wet footing and exposed, windy air at the same time**.

Wear waterproof boots with heat-resistant soles and good grip on slick mineral surfaces. They should protect your ankles without being so cumbersome that you cannot make a quick, controlled step away from an opening seam. Check sole temperature periodically; warmth that feels pleasant at first can become a problem during prolonged contact.

Use lightweight layers beneath a windproof, rainproof shell. Avoid loose straps, dangling cords, and broad fabric that can catch the evening wind. An umbrella would be particularly unhelpful here.

Bring:

- Gloves for incidental contact with hot or unfamiliar surfaces.
- Clear eye protection against mist, flying grit, and drops whose final descent may be faster than it looks.
- A close-fitting hood or fine insect veil.
- Sealed drinking water and food.
- A compact first-aid kit and emergency insulation.
- A thermometer, atmospheric monitor, and any instruments needed to check conditions for your particular means of travel.
- A small, adjustable lamp.
- A camera and spoken or written log.
- A closed-cell sitting pad, rather than a blanket laid directly on wet, warm stone.

Carry everything in a closed pack. Do not scatter equipment around a “base camp.” Even without animals investigating it, equipment may become separated by growth, wetted from beneath, or stranded beside a newly formed depression.

A harness is useful only if it connects to something genuinely trustworthy. The frames have no visible foundations. The white post is a control mechanism. The shelter’s hooks are not certified anchors. Do not turn an unexplained structure into safety equipment because it looks convenient.

## Your first hour: watch before walking

Arrive on an established, broad part of the shelf, well away from the perimeter, pools, active seams, and frames. If no such landing area can be identified, do not land.

Remain near your exit through several of the deep reports from below. The apparent twenty-to-thirty-minute interval is useful for observation, not a timetable you should trust with your life.

Watch what happens in sequence:

1. Do the droplets or animals react before you feel the vibration?
2. Where does the ground move?
3. Does the surface become hotter?
4. Do familiar distances change?
5. How do the frames respond?
6. Does your return system remain functional afterward?

The sensation in your jaw may be your first warning. When it comes, stop walking. On a broad, established plate, lower your center of gravity and wait. Do not hurry toward a doorway or leap across a seam while the terrain is changing.

Avoid kneeling with bare skin against the stone. Avoid bracing a hand over a crack. A seam is not simply a gap: it is an emergence route for animals and new terrain.

There may be no moment when the shelf is entirely still. You are looking for a manageable pattern, not for a guarantee.

## Treat the edge as much farther away than it looks

Stay several body lengths back from the translucent lip, and increase that margin in wind or poor visibility. There is no reason to assume that a newly grown projection has the strength of the older ground behind it.

The fresh northern lobe is especially tempting because it briefly reveals its interior. Observe that from a distance. Transparency is not an invitation to stand on it.

Do not follow grazers onto new rock. Their weight, heat tolerance, and relationship to the surface are unlike yours.

The disappearance of falling objects below the shelf is another reason not to rely on ordinary visual judgment. Whether they vanish into cloud, optical distortion, or something stranger, you would lose sight of a falling person quickly. A companion on the shelf might have no practical rescue option.

Nothing at the edge is worth a test fall, including a test performed with essential equipment.

## Water is not a safe reference

The tilted pool and slowing rain mean that at least some familiar assumptions about water do not hold here. That does not prove gravity itself is changing everywhere, but it is enough that you should not trust a pool to tell you what is level.

Do not drink the water, wash a wound in it, or assume boiling would make it safe. Its chemistry is unknown, and it is heavily inhabited.

Stay out of the pools altogether. Knee-deep water is not necessarily safe water. The transparent hunters are numerous, difficult to count, and capable of propelling water and themselves into the air. There is no evidence that they hunt humans; there is also no need to place your fingers or ankles over their feeding surface to find out how they respond.

Allow space around descending columns of winged animals. Their collapse toward the stone is a feeding behavior, and near a pool it may trigger a strike.

During rain, resist the urge to touch the hovering drops with your face or tongue. Wear your eye protection and let the phenomenon remain beautiful at a slight distance.

## The shelter is promising, not proven

The shelter is the best candidate for a place to pause. Inspect it without committing your whole party inside.

Check the roof, floor temperature, air, and doorway clearance. Keep the exit unobstructed and your return device with you. The bench may be useful after inspection; the hooks should remain unused until you understand them.

The coat is a perceptual inconsistency. Leave it alone. You do not need to decide whether it is an image, an object present under particular conditions, or something else. Its practical significance is that **what you can see through the doorway does not reliably describe what you will encounter after crossing it**.

Enter deliberately, one person at a time, while the other observes. Compare what each of you sees.

The dry square deserves attention because it is also an area where growth has stopped. It might be a deliberately stabilized surface. It might have another function entirely. Dryness alone does not establish that it is a bed, landing pad, or safe storage compartment.

Likewise, do not assume the slow pulse beneath the shelter is harmless simply because it is slow.

## Leave the white handle alone

Do not pull it out of curiosity, to level a pool, or to make the shelf feel more secure.

It affects the whole shelf. The cloud compresses, the water levels, and every animal becomes silent. That is a major intervention, not a door handle with a disappointing mechanism.

Its worn surface suggests prior use. It does not tell you:

- Who used it.
- How they prepared.
- What increased “heaviness” would do to a human body.
- Whether the shelf can be damaged by repeated use.
- Whether the effect is protective, corrective, or part of some larger operation.

The nest also matters. Using the handle would disturb animals directly, and possibly put your hand among them.

If reliable instructions become available, learn them before visiting. Without those instructions, the handle should not be part of your planned safety system. In an emergency, departure is preferable to experimenting with a control that changes conditions everywhere at once.

## Do not interfere with the builders

Give the frames a generous berth. Do not pass beneath them, reach into the gaps under their uprights, or place an instrument in the rising mineral stream.

Their adjustments may be slow and minute while the process they govern is powerful. You cannot infer a safe clearance from a mechanism whose moving parts you cannot follow.

Leave the stalked gravel attached. Do not scrape away the cream-colored film or collect a mineral bud. These are fresh biological or geological interfaces, not loose souvenirs.

The pebble triplets are particularly important. Once fused, they contain eggs and coincide with arrested growth. Step around them carefully. Do not rearrange them to make a stable campsite. The apparent effect may depend on something you have not noticed, and you would be destroying a nursery in the attempt.

Treat the seven dark tiles and the ground beneath the post as clues to stability, not materials to move.

## Plan to leave before evening

Set your departure time well before the stronger wind normally arrives. Leave earlier if:

- The reports become stronger or cease to follow the pattern you observed.
- A seam opens near your refuge or return point.
- The ground becomes uncomfortably hot through your boots.
- Visibility deteriorates.
- The frames make a substantial change.
- Animals fall abruptly silent without an understood cause.
- Your equipment behaves inconsistently.
- You experience headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual weakness, or confusion.

You do not need to identify the cause of a warning sign before leaving.

After departure, inspect and clean your boots, pack, and clothing in a controlled area. Check for small animals, eggs, mineral grains, and adhering film. Do not carry the shelf’s living surface into another world unnoticed.

The right attitude here is patient rather than heroic. Sit on established ground, keep your belongings close, and watch the rain finish falling. Notice where the animals go after a tremor. Let the new stone cool without putting your name or footprint on it.

You are visiting a place that has not yet settled what shape it will be. Prepare to accept that it may not make room for you for very long.
