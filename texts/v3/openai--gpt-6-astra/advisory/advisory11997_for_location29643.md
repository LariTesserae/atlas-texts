Prepare for a difficult mountain ascent that ends in a very short visit to something smaller, louder, and more alive than you will be ready for. You are not going into the hollow. An ordinary human cannot fit, and putting even a hand inside would mean placing it among currents and newly forming blades of stone.

The visit is to its threshold: a place where you may have to lie sideways above an immense drop while the mountain makes itself a few inches from your face.

### Learn the approach from people who work there

Find the mountaineers who know the hollow, preferably someone who has collected its flakes. Ask about the route, the season, where they secure themselves, and what makes them abandon a visit.

Useful questions are quite specific:

- Has the opening recently narrowed?
- Does the approach rock change, or only the stone inside?
- Where can a person rest out of the wind and noise?
- How do workers recognize a convulsion that requires withdrawal?
- Are visitors welcome, and who maintains the bone gate?

Do not assume the eleven notches count visits, harvests, deaths, or years. Their meaning is something to ask about, not something to supply.

The gate is evidence of patient local knowledge. Whoever made it found a way to influence a process that otherwise builds and dissolves stone in a blink. Arriving with stronger materials does not mean arriving with a better understanding.

### Prepare for the mountain before preparing for the hollow

Acclimatize to the altitude. Bring windproof insulation, gloves that preserve dexterity, eye protection, reliable climbing equipment appropriate to the route, and enough food and warm drink for delays. Arrange a turnaround time and a descent plan that does not depend on being able to hear.

The description gives you no shelter at the opening. Sort equipment and put on protective layers lower down, somewhere you can stand and use both hands. At the hollow, an adjustment as simple as retrieving a glove could become awkward.

Go with a companion, but do not crowd the entrance. Ideally, one person approaches while another remains at a secure station, able to assist without sharing the same precarious position.

Have your protection established on rock that experienced local climbers regard as dependable, away from the actively changing hollow. Pale, clean facets are not proof of sound anchorage. Do not drive anything into the entrance, the ceiling, the gate sockets, or the little working ledge.

Secure loose equipment so it cannot fall down the slope, but keep straps and cords out of the split. A tether is useful outside; inside, it could become something the currents seize.

### Take the sound seriously

Bring properly fitted earplugs and compatible over-ear protection if available. The local wool wrapping may help with cold and some airborne sound, but it plainly does not make the place quiet.

Even good hearing protection will not eliminate vibration transmitted through the rock. Do not repeat the jaw-against-stone experiment. There is no useful reason to give the lowest impacts a direct route into your head.

Agree on visual signals before approaching: **withdraw**, **need assistance**, and **all well**. Speech may be useless, and a partner should not have to distinguish a call for help from the roar.

Make the first exposure brief. Do not choose a long observation period merely because you have climbed a long way. Ear pain, ringing, dizziness, nausea, altered hearing, or difficulty orienting yourself are reasons to retreat, not sensations to push through. There is no established safe exposure time for this place.

The danger is not only hearing damage. A moment of vertigo matters considerably when both legs are braced against a steep slope.

### Keep yourself outside the water

The stable water face is not a promise that the interior is gentle.

A loose object can wait motionless and then strike a wall. Stone can appear where there was water. Several currents can oppose one another within the width of a thumb. Even the apparently stabilized region behind the gate is described as suitable for slow mineral growth, not safe human contact.

Do not:

- dip a finger in to test the cold;
- insert a probe, camera, collection bottle, or light;
- drink or sample the water;
- try to make it drain;
- introduce dye, salt, air, or any other tracer.

Observation does not require these interventions. The hollow is unusually generous with visibility. Use that advantage.

A camera or close-focusing optic should remain outside, without resting on the gate or pressing against the water. Protective eyewear is sensible near forming mineral edges, but it is not a reason to lean closer.

### Leave the gate and working tools alone

Do not touch the teeth, tighten the hair lashings, straighten the crosspiece, or try to stop its clatter. Its movement may be part of how it functions; at minimum, you have no evidence that fixing it in place would be an improvement.

One removed tooth was followed by months of closure. Treat every component as consequential.

The wooden wedge and frayed cord are not abandoned curiosities. Leave them where they are. Do not deepen the notches or add your own.

If you hope to bring back a flake, arrange that with the people who harvest here. Let an experienced worker decide whether anything can be taken and how much. Pack any legitimately obtained piece in a rigid, puncture-resistant container suitable for an extremely sharp edge. A folded hide is a practiced local method, not necessarily a safe souvenir wrapper in an unfamiliar traveler’s pack.

### Treat the life as exceptionally vulnerable

There is very little habitat here, and nearly all of it is in use.

The green strands occupy a protected crease. The brown cups depend on passing sunlight. The animal survives by adhering to the floor and using seams on a schedule that reverses what a human might expect: it comes out during the loud intervals.

Do not interpret its emergence as an all-clear.

Avoid shading the hollow for the whole observation period. Keep dirty gloves, sunscreen, fibers, food crumbs, and equipment out of the water. Take no organism, molt, rooted mineral, or “empty” patch of substrate. Something that looks like a stain may be the oldest living presence you can see.

### Plan what you want to notice

If weather and local advice permit, sunlight will make the visit more legible. But do not sacrifice a safe descent to catch the noon display.

Choose a few things in advance: the three gate shadows, a forming flake, the protected crease, the difference between a current and the brief optical distortion left by vanishing stone. You may not have the comfort or concentration to discover everything while you are there.

Expect the scale to be disorienting. Your ears will report a gorge or a glacier; your eyes will report a cavity the size of an oven. Neither impression needs correcting. The loudness belongs to the work being done, not to the size of the room.

The best preparation is to make peace with a modest visit: arrive safely, look without reaching, and leave before fascination turns into endurance. You do not need to catch the largest convulsion or carry away a piece to have witnessed the mountain arriving.
