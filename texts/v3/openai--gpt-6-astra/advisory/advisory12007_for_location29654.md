Prepare for this as a visit to a fragile inhabited place, not an expedition into a ruin. You have seen almost no movement, but you have not established that the town is empty. Its messages may be things you would mistake for grit. Its inhabitants may be sheltering, absent for a season, or simply too still to distinguish from their surroundings.

The first requirement is a way to arrive without making an entrance.

## Arrange your scale and your return before you go

An ordinary human cannot physically enter this hollow. Do not lift the step, widen the seam, or drill through the stone. The stair’s weight, the cracks supplying air, and the slow delivery of water are all part of the town’s environment. Exposing it might be as destructive as removing the roof and changing the weather over a human city.

You need either a genuinely nonintrusive means of visiting remotely or a transit method that reduces you to a suitable scale. If shrinking is involved, establish that it also handles mass, breathing, heat loss, and the behavior of your equipment. Being small enough to enter a doorway is not sufficient if you still weigh as much as an adult.

Choose your size by the doors and ladders, not by the overall cavity. You should be able to use the streets without brushing roofs or squeezing between walls. Leave room for clothing and a small pack.

Your return method must work:

- Without reaching the daylight crack.
- Without a spoken command.
- While you are wet, disoriented, or unable to move.
- Without expanding you inside the stone.

Set a timed retrieval as a backup. For a first visit, plan a brief reconnaissance, not an overnight stay.

## Dress for cold that never gives you a break

The temperature may not be violently low. The danger is that nothing offers relief from it. Walls, paving, air, and water all draw you toward the same chill. You are not likely to find a sunny ledge, a warm kitchen, or enough dry material to make a fire.

Wear a close-fitting, insulated, waterproof suit with covered hands and feet. It should have no trailing straps, absorbent cuffs, or loose fibers. At the town’s scale, a thread shed from your sleeve could foul a channel or become a troublesome piece of debris.

Your footwear needs soft, nonmarking soles that grip wet mineral surfaces without biting into them. Spikes would damage paving; strong adhesives could pull it apart.

If your transit system does not automatically preserve ordinary human physiology at reduced scale, you will need a self-contained life-support envelope. Do not assume that air reaching the hollow through mortar makes it sufficient for an additional breathing visitor. Bring your own reserve, and avoid venting warm, damp exhaust into houses.

Carry food and drinking water in sealed containers. The central water is infrastructure and habitat, not a convenient supply.

## Treat water as a structure you must not touch casually

The most unfamiliar danger will probably be surface tension.

At human scale, you step through a trickle. Here, a swollen surface may behave more like a clinging membrane. Depending on your final size, contact with a droplet could pin a limb, cover your face, or drag you against a wall. A thin film might be enough to make a passage impassable.

Do not assume swimming ability will help.

Bring a water-shedding outer layer and a compact retrieval system that can lift you clear without relying on a local bridge or roof for support. Test both before arrival. A rescue line is useful only if it cannot itself sweep through buildings or catch across drains.

Avoid:

- The impact sites beneath falling drops.
- Alleys passing under arches of water.
- Narrow lanes bordered by raised menisci.
- Bridges whose undersides are already touching the current.
- Pale mineral margins that conceal the actual edge of a basin.

Never introduce soap, oil, solvent, or an untested waterproofing residue. Anything that changes surface tension could alter water levels and routes throughout the town.

The drains may look improvised. They are not invitations to improve them. A grain you move could be a blockage—or the thing preventing an overflow.

## Bring quiet light and independent communication

Bring a dimmable, diffuse lamp with minimal heat output. Begin at its lowest useful setting. Shell lintels and wet roofs may provide enough reflected light to navigate once your eyes adjust.

A powerful beam could flatten the very details you need to see: transparent water boundaries, fine cracks, cup marks, and nearly invisible animals. It could also disturb residents accustomed to a narrow blade of grey daylight.

Sound is a separate problem. The cavity is already full of impacts and reverberation. You may struggle to locate a noise, understand speech, or notice something approaching.

Use adjustable hearing protection rather than simply blocking everything. If traveling with a companion, establish visual and tactile signals beforehand. Carry communication equipment that does not depend on speaking into the open air.

Do not shout to announce yourself. You could be both painfully loud nearby and unintelligible a few streets away.

## Assume the paving can carry a conversation

The polished recesses, cup marks, arranged grains, and fitted chips deserve the caution you would give writing.

Do not put a hand into a recess merely because it is at a comfortable height. Do not straighten a row of grains. Do not borrow a pale chip to mark your route. A thing can be both physically insignificant and socially indispensable.

Record your route privately. Leave no cairns, chalk, tape, or breadcrumbs.

If you encounter an inhabitant, stop at a respectful distance and make yourself easy to observe. Let them initiate proximity or show how communication works. Keep gestures slow. Do not assume touch is welcome simply because the town appears to use tactile signs.

Bring patience rather than a prepared speech. You may spend most of your visit learning whether someone has acknowledged you.

## Travel as though every repair is still working

Use broad, supported routes where possible. Cross narrow bridges one person at a time, without bouncing or leaning over their sides. Do not pull on an old arch or use a translucent lashing as a handhold.

A collapsed roof may now protect a drain. Sediment may be sealing a route that must remain sealed. A shutter’s original fastening hole is not necessarily a convenient anchor point.

Map the water before venturing downward. Note the sequence of drops, the positions of overflow notches, and where each visible route disappears. The town is buffered against outside weather, but buffered does not mean immune. A change overhead may arrive late enough to surprise you.

If water levels or the established rhythm begin changing, leave while your route is still clear.

Give the translucent creature beneath the bridge a wide berth. It may be harmless, but its feeding current and shelter are not yours to disturb. The empty hollows and white casing do not tell you enough to identify it—or to conclude that it is the last of its kind.

## Prepare yourself for very little to happen

This may be a difficult place to visit if you need a discovery, an encounter, or a task to justify being there.

You will be cold. Conversation will be hard. Much of what you see will be obscured by masonry. You may wait a long time to watch one limb fold toward a mouth. You may never learn who built the houses.

Resist filling that uncertainty with action. Do not open doors to settle whether anyone remains. Do not clean a window to see better. Do not repair the deeply cupped roof beneath the loudest drop on the strength of a first inspection.

And do not choose the little dry patch beneath it as your campsite.

Someone kept that paving dry despite everything pressing water toward it. It may be a refuge, a working place, or something whose purpose you cannot recognize. Until you know, keep yourself and your equipment off it.

A successful visit could leave you with unanswered questions and no evidence of your passage. In this town, that would be a considerable accomplishment.
