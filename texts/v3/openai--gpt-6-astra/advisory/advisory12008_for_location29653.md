Prepare for a place where **water determines the usable shape of the village**, and where that shape can change while you are standing in it.

This is not an abandoned ruin disguised by vegetation. It is a densely occupied, closely maintained settlement whose roads, drainage, buildings, trees, and animals overlap. Much of what initially looks accidental—the empty strip between houses, a root left across a path, a bench that can be quickly unfastened—is probably deliberate. Your first safety measure is to arrive willing to be shown how things work.

## Before entering the village

Arrange an introduction and a place to sleep if you can. A settlement this small may have neither an inn nor spare covered ground. Turning up with the expectation of pitching a tent could leave you choosing between a drainage course, someone’s work platform, and a structurally unsuitable patch of forest floor.

Ask your contact three practical questions:

- **Which route should I use, and which bridge carries people?**
- **Where should I wait if the water rises?**
- **What can I bring that will be useful rather than burdensome?**

Plan to arrive in daylight, preferably with enough time to learn the route to your lodging before the midday market fills the square. The village may be only a few shouted conversations across, but its routes go above and below one another. Seeing your destination through the leaves does not mean there is a direct path to it.

If your means of multiverse travel permits precise arrival, use an agreed, unobstructed landing place rather than materializing on a roof, bridge, or seemingly empty gap. Do not assume a platform can support you and a large cargo load. Keep your return equipment waterproof and accessible, with a rendezvous point that will remain reachable in bad weather.

Nothing in the description establishes unusual gravity, toxic air, or supernatural hazards. Standard destination checks are still appropriate, but most of your preparation should address ordinary, consequential problems: slipping, getting chilled, contaminated water, obstructed routes, and misunderstanding local infrastructure.

## Dress for continuous wetness, not just rain

The canopy keeps delivering water after the weather overhead has cleared. You will brush wet screens, lean against wet rails, and stand under leaves that release their load when the wind changes. A forecast saying “no rain” will not make this a dry outing.

Wear:

- **Secure, well-fitting footwear with good wet-surface traction.** Expect rounded stone, green film, muddy approaches, and wet wood. No sole makes algae-coated surfaces safe. Avoid smooth soles and loose sandals.
- **Quick-drying trousers and a long-sleeved layer.** Keep hems out of puddles and away from projecting pegs.
- **A waterproof shell with an adjustable hood.** You need to retain peripheral vision and hear people approaching.
- **A warm layer sealed away until needed.** Cool mist, inactivity, and soaked clothing can chill you even without severe cold.
- **A cap or brim that keeps drips off your face without blocking your view overhead.**

A close-fitting rain jacket will usually be easier to manage than a broad poncho. Loose fabric can catch on rootlets and lashings or intrude into other people’s space. An umbrella is more likely to obstruct the stairs than solve the problem.

Carry spare socks and one genuinely dry sleeping outfit in separate waterproof bags. Protect the dry outfit even if everything else becomes damp. Ask where clothing may be hung; an apparently convenient line or pipe may be part of the water system.

Do not count on drying your boots overnight.

## Keep your luggage narrow

Bring a compact pack you can turn sideways in without knocking a hanging pot or forcing a porter toward the ravine. Secure straps, buckles, and dangling equipment. Keep both hands available on stairs and bridges.

Useful contents include:

- A waterproof headlamp and spare power.
- Protected drinking water and a treatment method suitable for the destination.
- A small first-aid kit, including wound dressings and blister care.
- Personal medication in waterproof packaging.
- Soap or hand cleanser.
- A light emergency insulating layer.
- A little ready-to-eat food.
- A sealed bag for carrying out waste.
- Offline directions, essential local phrases, and your host’s name.

A walking pole may help on the approach, but stow it where it becomes an obstacle. Do not plant its tip between floor poles or against a moss-covered edge whose structure you cannot see.

Leave bulky expedition furniture and unnecessary hard cases elsewhere. Here, every item needs somewhere to stand, somewhere to stay dry, and someone to step around it.

## Learn the paths before you explore

Have someone walk you between your lodging, the square, the drinking-water source, the latrine, and the designated storm refuge. Do the walk yourself; do not settle for someone pointing through a screen of leaves.

Pay particular attention to the empty spaces.

**A dry runoff groove is not spare ground.** Do not rest in it, store your pack in it, or treat it as a shortcut. The houses have been kept off those grooves for a reason that may become visible within minutes.

Similarly:

- Use the designated pedestrian crossing.
- Do not follow children onto the animal crossing.
- Do not step onto the aqueduct to pass a crowd.
- Do not pull on a vine or root merely because it resembles a handhold.
- Do not assume moss conceals decay—or that living roots guarantee safety. Ask which structures are in service.

The root bridge may be more dependable than a newer-looking timber walkway. Its reliability comes from its condition and local care, not from whether it resembles infrastructure you recognize.

Move slowly over polished rock and wet steps. Use established handrails without giving unfamiliar sections your full weight. Avoid stopping at bridge entrances, stair junctions, or the narrow strip someone needs to carry a load through. To admire the view or take a photograph, first find a place where stopping is acceptable.

At night, use a modest light directed at your footing. Bright beams can attract insects, disturb animals, and shine straight through someone’s bamboo screen.

## Treat a change in wind as information

The gust described here does more than move leaves. It dumps stored canopy water, shifts branches, tightens structures, and precedes a rapid rise in the stream.

Learn the local sequence before you need it:

1. Where does water appear first?
2. Which steps become unusable?
3. Which route remains open to your lodging?
4. Where should a visitor stand while residents clear drains and move stalls?
5. What signs mean an ordinary rise has become an evacuation?

When wind comes down the fold and people begin lifting goods, put away your camera and get yourself and your bag out of the working route. Do not wait for someone to interrupt a familiar task to manage you.

Offer help, but accept a small, specific assignment. Holding a basket where you are told may be genuinely useful. Opening a drain, pulling out a leaf blockage, or unfastening a bench without instruction may redirect water or release something someone else is supporting.

Do not enter moving water to retrieve dropped belongings. Avoid the ravine edge during heavy runoff, and do not shelter beneath an arbitrary overhang or tree merely because it seems dry.

If a branch breaks or you hear a heavy sliding sound, stop and listen. The villagers’ brief attention to the possibility of a second sound is worth learning from. Their return to work is not proof that nothing dangerous can happen; it shows that they are distinguishing familiar noise from warning signs.

## Be careful about water without treating the village as unclean

There is abundant water here, but its uses are differentiated. Roof runoff, washing troughs, household storage, and drinking supplies are not necessarily interchangeable.

Ask explicitly: **“Which water is for drinking, and does it need treatment?”**

Until you know, use reliably treated water. A filter alone may not address every microbial hazard; use an appropriate combined method or boil water when that is the established safe option. Keep bottle mouths out of trough water and do not dip your personal container into a communal cistern without permission.

Wash your hands before eating and after using the latrine or handling animals. Keep small cuts clean and covered. Persistent damp makes minor skin damage harder to manage, so inspect and dry your feet each day.

Eat food prepared and handled in ways you can trust. Fish carried up from the valley may be excellent, but thorough cooking matters more than its attractive leaf wrapping. Do not sample unfamiliar shoots, roots, or fruit simply because they resemble foods from another world.

For a multiverse traveler, biological compatibility is a separate preliminary check: verify that local foods are digestible for you and that ordinary local microbes are not an exceptional hazard. Familiar-looking beans are not sufficient evidence.

## Accept that the animals have established routes too

Chickens on stairs are a traffic problem, not an invitation to kick or chase them. Give dogs room. Do not feed rats, pigs, birds, or tethered goats, and keep your food closed away.

The goats’ distance from bridge lashings is plainly intentional. Do not untether one because it looks uncomfortable or move its foliage closer to a crossing.

Look before putting your hand behind a shutter or setting equipment into the open end of a bamboo pipe. A space that appears unused may contain a nest. Avoid brushing beams thick with moths or disturbing nesting birds for a clearer photograph.

Ask locally about biting insects, ticks, leeches, and any medically significant animals; the description does not establish which are present. Bring suitable repellent and consider a sleeping net if recommended. A closed, screened sleeping space is valuable both for comfort and for protecting your equipment from curious small animals.

## Understand how little private space there is

Voices arrive from unexpected directions because homes and workspaces are layered. Assume that people above and below can hear you, even when foliage prevents you from seeing them.

Ask before photographing residents, entering covered platforms, or leaning against a house. A roof beside your elbow may cover someone’s room. A convenient shelf may be their working bench.

At market:

- Keep purchases and payment accessible so you do not unpack in the passage.
- Repeat prices patiently over the stream.
- Step aside to finish conversations.
- Let loaded carriers pass.
- Do not block access to drains or movable stalls.

Bring trade goods or gifts only after asking what is wanted. Compact repair supplies might be welcome, but the village already has sophisticated knowledge of its materials. Do not assume imported fasteners or sealants are improvements. A rigid attachment can be wrong for a growing trunk; a waterproof repair can trap moisture where a structure needs to drain.

Learn at least the local words for “excuse me,” “safe,” “stop,” “water,” “help,” and “may I?” Learn how residents call for urgent attention. Shouting across the ravine may be normal conversation, so your own alarm needs to be recognizable.

## Protect the living parts of the settlement

The roots, trees, and moss are not just scenery. Some bear loads, some retain water, and some occupy places where residents have chosen to tolerate them.

Do not cut a bothersome rootlet, scrape a rail clean, collect moss, or take fibers from a binding. What a bird removes in a strand is not permission for a visitor to loosen a handful.

Clean footwear and equipment before arrival so you do not introduce seeds, soil organisms, or pests. Clean them again before traveling onward. Do not release off-world animals, discard food into the ravine, or use soaps in the stream without knowing the approved washing arrangements.

Firewood has been carried uphill under its own little roof. Fuel and dry space cost effort here. Ask how bathing, cooking, charging equipment, and drying clothing are handled rather than assuming those services are freely available.

## Prepare for the sensation of being there

You may find the village more tiring than its size suggests.

You will make constant small adjustments: ducking, stepping sideways, choosing footholds, listening for someone behind you, keeping your sleeve out of a trough. The sound of the stream will mask some voices and amplify your need to concentrate. At night, frogs, water pipes, roof drops, livestock, and footsteps may make it difficult to identify unfamiliar noises.

If you use earplugs to sleep, first establish whether you need to hear a warning call or your host knocking. Keep your shoes, light, shell, and essential bag together where you can reach them in darkness.

Expect your first impression of structural precariousness to be only partly reliable. Expect, too, that familiarity can make residents comfortable with risks you should not copy. You do not yet know which rail is trustworthy or how quickly the lowest step disappears.

The best preparation is to reserve time for being taught. Spend your first clear hour learning routes rather than pursuing the distant view. Watch where people put their feet, where they leave gaps, and what they move when the wind changes.

You can be comfortable here without ever being completely dry. What will make the visit work is having a protected place to sleep, a small manageable load, and enough local knowledge not to occupy the space the water—or someone carrying a basket—needs next.
