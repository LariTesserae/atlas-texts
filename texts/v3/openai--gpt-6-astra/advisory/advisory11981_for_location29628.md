Prepare for this city as you would for a cold, exposed upland crossed with an immense archaeological site—not as you would for a city that can supply you with food, directions, or a room.

Its main danger is that almost everything looks manageable. The water is shallow. The buildings are low. The next ridge seems close. A room beneath a vault looks like shelter. None of those impressions is necessarily wrong, but they can persuade you to keep making small commitments until you are wet, tired, and a long way from a route you recognize.

**Bring what you need to leave without finding anything you need there.**

## Before you arrive

For a multiverse crossing, establish the ordinary prerequisites first: breathable air, tolerable pressure, compatible gravity, and whether local water and organisms pose unfamiliar risks. The description suggests a place physically congenial to humans, but familiar-looking limestone and brown birds are not proof of biological compatibility.

Once those checks are satisfied, the useful preparations are quite ordinary:

- Cool-weather hiking fitness, especially balance and repeated short climbs.
- Experience navigating terrain without prominent landmarks.
- Enough weather knowledge to recognize deteriorating visibility and approaching storms.
- A fixed return time and an exit method that does not depend on reaching an unknown district.

If your crossing equipment permits it, place a return beacon at the arrival point. Test it from behind stone and from a lower terrace before trusting it. Do not assume that satellite navigation, a compass, or communications will function as they do at home.

For a first visit, plan a daylight out-and-back excursion rather than a traverse. The description gives no useful upper bound on the city’s extent. “Across the city” is not yet a meaningful itinerary.

## Dress for water, wind, and stopping

You are likely to be more comfortable walking than resting. Movement generates heat; waiting out rain in a mineral-cold room does not.

Wear a wicking base layer, an insulating layer that remains useful when damp, and a waterproof, windproof shell. Waterproof trousers are worth the weight. Pack a warm hat and gloves even if the arrival weather looks pleasant.

Footwear deserves particular attention. Choose well-fitting boots with soles suited to wet rock. Waterproofing helps against shallow runoff, but no boot remains dry after water comes over its collar. Gaiters reduce incidental entry; they do not make deep wading sensible.

Bring:

- Spare socks and a dry insulating layer in waterproof bags.
- A small insulated sitting pad.
- Trekking poles for balance and probing uncertain footing.
- A headlamp, with spare power.
- Blister care and a compact first-aid kit.
- An emergency bivy or comparable windproof shelter.

The sitting pad may be one of the most appreciated objects you carry. A sunlit wall can feel welcoming while the stone beneath you steadily draws away heat.

Do not plan on finding firewood. There is little vegetation, much of it is wet, and the small living patches are better left intact.

## Carry drinking water despite being surrounded by it

The water is visually clear because it runs over pale stone. That tells you little about whether it is safe to drink.

It passes through silt, old rooms, marsh pockets, and channels you cannot inspect. In another universe, familiar filtration or disinfection methods may not address every biological or chemical hazard.

For a short visit, carry all your drinking water. For a longer expedition, bring treatment equipment appropriate to whatever environmental testing has established—not merely a filter and confidence.

Carry all your food as well. This is not a landscape in which an ordinary visitor should expect to forage successfully. Pack something easy to eat without a long stop; cold hands and continuous light rain can make elaborate lunches surprisingly unappealing.

## Navigate by recording, not recognition

From within the streets, the city gives you too many landmarks. From above, it gives you too few.

A doorway facing a doorway, a flooded court, a stair climbing an outer wall: these features will repeat. The route that felt obvious going uphill may become a row of indistinguishable openings when seen from above.

Record your outward journey. At important junctions:

- Take a photograph looking back.
- Note changes in terrace level.
- Record the relationship between your route and natural ridges or depressions.
- Mark the location on a locally functioning mapping device, or make a sketch.

Use removable markers only where necessary, and collect them on departure. Do not scratch arrows into stone or build cairns from the walls.

An elevated viewpoint is useful, but reach it by sound ground or an intact, assessable route. A roof is not an observation platform simply because it has survived.

**Turn around according to time and conditions, not according to how close the next district looks.** Repeated ascents, wet footing, and route-checking will make travel slower than the distances suggest.

## Learn to distinguish streets from waterways

The city’s original circulation and its present drainage no longer fully agree. A stair is not necessarily a walking route now. An alley may be a stream channel. A square may be a temporary collecting basin.

Move slowly on submerged steps. A thin coating of algae can be more consequential than several inches of water. Avoid jumping between wet stones, and do not trust a loose-looking block as a handhold.

Clear pools still need caution. Reflections can conceal depth changes, missing paving, and openings. Probe uncertain footing rather than stepping into it to find out.

Most importantly, look uphill as well as overhead. Rain in a distant district may eventually reach the passage you are using.

The end of a shower is not the end of rising water. After rain:

- Watch whether a pool edge is advancing.
- Check whether an easy crossing is gaining speed or depth.
- Stay out of narrow descending passages with no convenient side exit.
- Avoid low chambers whose only doorway could become a water entry point.
- Allow extra time for the return route.

There is no need to imagine every gutter becoming a torrent. The subtler problem is enough: a route that was merely wet on the outward journey may be slippery, obscured, or unpleasantly cold to cross on the way back.

## Choose shelter by where water will go

A surviving vault may offer excellent short-term protection, but shelter from rain and safety from runoff are different things.

Before settling beneath one, inspect what you reasonably can. Avoid fresh-looking falls, displaced masonry, visibly sagging stonework, or loose material overhead. Long survival is reassuring, not a structural certificate.

Prefer a shelter that has:

- An uncomplicated exit to higher open ground.
- A floor above nearby channels.
- No large catchment draining toward its entrance.
- No need to pass deeper underground to stay dry.

Keep drainage grooves clear. Do not set a pack across one, plug a draft with stones from a channel, or improve your resting place by rearranging a threshold. The floor described in the city works because water can pass through its intended route.

For an overnight stay, bring independent shelter and seek a well-drained site away from unstable walls and exposed high points. A dry depression is a poor campsite if its shape suggests that it sometimes holds water.

## Prepare for the silence and the scale

This place may be emotionally more demanding than its weather.

It contains the repeated evidence of people—shared walls, stairs, carefully graded floors—without the ordinary signs that someone is nearby. You may repeatedly expect to hear a conversation beyond a partition. Instead, you will hear your own clothing, your steps, and water moving somewhere beneath you.

Do not mistake sparse animal sightings for proof that nothing lives there. Equally, do not feel obliged to turn every sound into a threat. Stay observant without manufacturing an inhabitant for every dark opening.

Travel with a companion if possible, and stay within reliable voice contact. Walls and terrace changes can separate people quickly even when the map distance is small.

Schedule rests deliberately. In a landscape without shops, squares in use, or clear destinations, it is easy to postpone stopping indefinitely. Eat, check your feet, check your warmth, and compare the remaining daylight with your actual return time.

## How to visit it well

Leave the masonry, drains, marshes, and small accumulated patches of soil undisturbed. Do not clear a blocked channel to see whether it still works. Water redirected at one terrace may affect several below.

Give yourself permission to see very little of the whole.

A single neighborhood would reward a long visit: watching which wall dries first, finding where water enters a court and where it leaves, noticing how a natural seam becomes a foundation. The city’s extent is not something you need to overcome.

The best preparation is to arrive warm, self-sufficient, and willing to turn back. Then you can afford to stand still long enough to understand what the place is doing.
