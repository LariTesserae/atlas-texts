Prepare for a place where the ground looks forgiving and the air is not.

The first danger is likely to be a mistake of scale: seeing an easy afternoon walk where there is actually a gulf, or mistaking a smooth, grassy slope for safe footing because nothing about it looks alpine. The second is treating wind as weather you can endure with a good coat. Here, wind is part of the terrain. It has channels, obstacles, sudden drops, and places you should not enter.

An ordinary human can probably visit comfortably with local help. Traveling independently would require much more than ordinary hiking experience.

## Arrange your arrival before choosing your equipment

Your most valuable preparation is a local host who knows exactly where you will arrive.

Ask for an arrival point inside an established shelter, or on a broad, verified patch of stone well away from rims, vents, and vegetation-covered holes. Do not select a destination merely because it looks flat from above. A green surface may be a canopy over a hollow; a pale one may end at a lip invisible against another mountain.

If your means of crossing universes permits emergency return, establish how you will use it while crouching, with both hands occupied, in noise too loud for speech. A device that must be unpacked, laid flat, or operated by voice is poorly suited to this country. Secure it to your body and practice using it without releasing it.

For a first visit, stay on one inhabited mountain back. Crossing to another should be an excursion planned with residents, not an incidental part of finding somewhere to sleep.

## Pack close, quiet, and securely

Think of everything you carry as something the air will eventually try to take.

Wear close-fitting layers, flexible trousers, and footwear with excellent grip on both smooth stone and wet organic surfaces. Test the footwear before traveling: a sole that performs well on rough mountain trails may be less reassuring on polished rock beneath a film of moisture.

Useful equipment includes:

- **A low-profile pack with a secure hip belt.** Avoid external bundles and dangling straps.
- **A properly fitted climbing helmet.** The risks include falls, branches, and debris stripped from traveling plant mats.
- **Wraparound eye protection**, preferably with a retention strap, for airborne soil, pollen, insects, and leaf fragments.
- **Gloves that preserve dexterity.** You will need to feel holds and operate fastenings, not just keep your hands warm.
- **A fitted waterproof shell and a warm layer.** The general warmth does not prevent chilling when you are wet, exhausted, or immobilized.
- **Several sealed water containers**, rather than one vessel whose loss ends the trip.
- **Compact food that can be eaten without spreading out packaging.**
- **A headlamp, first-aid kit, and any essential medication**, all accessible without emptying the pack.

Secure glasses and small tools with short, purpose-made retainers. Do not cover yourself in long loops that can catch on hooked roots. Heavy items belong inside the pack, not swinging from a wrist or neck.

An umbrella is worse than useless. A poncho or large loose cape is liable to become something you must fight. A conventional tent should not be your primary shelter plan.

If your route requires a harness and fall protection, get training before arrival and have the system chosen by someone who understands the route. Carrying a rope is not the same as having a safe crossing.

## Learn how to stop before learning how to walk

Spend your first hours near shelter, watching the vegetation with someone who can interpret it.

You need to learn what the different wind sounds mean locally, where gusts tend to strike from above, and which surfaces remain comparatively protected. A distant flattening of grass may give useful warning. A change in a vent’s note may matter. But neither should become a rule you trust after one demonstration.

The moving disturbance you can see is not necessarily the only one approaching.

Agree on a few simple visual and touch signals before setting out:

- Stop.
- Get low.
- Return to shelter.
- Do not follow.
- I need help.

Use large, unmistakable gestures. Test any radios in the actual wind; a working transmitter does not guarantee intelligible speech.

Your basic movement pattern should be conservative: look ahead, identify the next secure place, move to it, reassess. Keep enough distance that one person losing balance does not knock down another, but remain close enough to communicate.

**Do not chase dropped equipment.** The local habit of catching something with a foot is a practiced movement, not a reason for a visitor to lunge on polished stone. Let an object go if recovering it threatens your balance.

When a gust approaches, lower your center of gravity and move into a known protected position if it is safely reachable. Do not sprint toward the nearest greenery. It may be lifting, concealing a hole, or rooted beside an edge.

Residents may know root loops that reliably hold a person. You will not know them by appearance. A plant that survives the wind is not automatically a safe human anchor.

## Treat the landscape as deceptive, not hostile

There are few sharp features to tell your eyes where one surface ends and another begins. Haze, cloud, and similar-colored stone will flatten distances further.

Use established routes and learn the drilled-cup markers. Carry a local route sketch or recorded track where your equipment supports it, but do not assume satellite navigation exists here. More importantly, do not let a map persuade you that a gap is crossable.

Never take a shortcut toward a shelter you can see across a depression without confirming what lies between you and it. An apparently shallow dip may open into air.

The old river channels are tempting walking routes, but they also lead to spillways. Watch for the point where a comfortable corridor becomes a funnel toward an edge.

Likewise, the lush gardens are not simply soft places to rest. Their vegetation may conceal openings, deep pools, unstable humus, or an entire hollow beneath a living roof. Enter unfamiliar ones with a guide.

Plan journeys by **safe route and retreat options**, not straight-line distance. A short separation may require a long detour—or may have no safe crossing at all.

## Sleep where people have already solved the problem

Book space in a maintained, inhabited shelter.

A hole in the rock is not necessarily a refuge. It may be part of the mountain’s ventilation system, quiet now and strongly breathing later. A creeper-roofed chamber may be pleasant for a brief supervised visit without being dependable overnight accommodation.

Ask your host:

- Whether the shelter is affected by vent reversals.
- Where to go if the main entrance becomes unsafe.
- How the roof and its living covering are maintained.
- Where drinking water is kept.
- Which nearby surfaces are safe after dark.

If you bring a stove or heater, ask before using it. A covered smoke hole is not proof that an unfamiliar combustion appliance can operate safely inside. Airflow that changes direction makes improvisation especially unwise.

Keep your boots, lamp, and packed essentials within reach at night. Do not use the roof or doorway ledge as overflow storage.

## Bring water; do not merely bring a filter

This country is damp in ways that do not reliably fill a bottle.

Carry enough water for the planned outing and a substantial delay, with the amount based on exertion and local advice. Refill whenever your host identifies a dependable supply. A pool that exists this evening may be empty tomorrow.

Treat surface water as untreated water. Its amber color could be largely leaf tannins, but color does not establish safety. Filtration and disinfection address many biological hazards; they do not make unknown plant chemicals or mineral contamination disappear.

Do not drink directly from a breathing hole, tap plants for moisture, or eat unfamiliar leaves because local animals use them. Ask which water sources and foods residents actually consume.

The shelter’s slowly filling bowls also imply a matter of manners: water may take hours to collect. Ask before drinking it, washing with it, or pouring out what looks like an insignificant remainder.

## Prepare your body for noise and uncertainty

Do not assume that warmth means low altitude or adequate oxygen. Establish atmospheric pressure and breathable-air conditions before arrival if your travel technology allows it. “High country” and clouds below the cliffs are not enough to judge human physiology across universes.

The continuous roar may be more exhausting than the walking. It can disrupt sleep, make conversation laborious, and leave you irritable or slow to decide. Bring hearing protection, but choose and use it carefully: outside, you still need access to agreed signals and whatever wind cues your guide relies on. Stronger protection may be most useful in shelter.

Schedule a short first outing and a generous rest period afterward. Balance, grip strength, and judgment all deteriorate with fatigue.

If you are strongly affected by exposure, tell your guide before encountering a crossing. These cliffs may be particularly unsettling because their depth becomes apparent suddenly. There is no shame in arranging a visit that never approaches one.

## Be a guest, not another loose object

Keep doorways clear. Put things into niches, baskets, or secured bags rather than on convenient surfaces. Ask before touching ropes, moving path markers, or using trees and roots as supports.

Do not judge a crossing rope by whether it looks frayed. The residents’ frequent replacement of apparently sound ropes is information about their experience, not wastefulness.

Expect conversation to happen close together or indoors. Let people teach you when to wait without speaking. In this landscape, someone pausing mid-task to watch a distant rim may be doing something more urgent than finishing your sentence.

The best visit would probably be modest: several nights with a household, short walks over familiar stone, a dawn spent watching water gather on leaves, and an afternoon listening until the roar begins to have separate parts.

You do not need to cross the great gaps to understand the place. You need to stay long enough to notice how carefully people inhabit the few feet around them—and to learn that a moment of perfect stillness is something to enjoy, not something to trust.
