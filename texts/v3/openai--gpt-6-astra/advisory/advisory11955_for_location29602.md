Prepare for this garden as you would for a high, exposed conservatory built over a working engine room. It may feel sheltered, intimate, even gentle. None of those qualities means that the ground is ordinary ground.

The essential habit is to distinguish **what looks established from what is designed to bear your weight**.

### Before you arrive

Arrange your visit with whoever maintains the column. Ask for three things: a marked visitor route, the irrigation and service-arm schedule, and an explanation of how you leave if the normal entrance becomes unavailable.

The yellow maintenance door is worth locating immediately, but do not assume it is an exit, unlocked, or safe for visitors. Find out where it leads. Likewise, determine whether reaching the garden involves a lift, stairs, or a transfer from another world, and whether your return method works in cloud, spray, and interference from the machinery.

Ask about actual atmospheric pressure. Being above clouds does not by itself establish an altitude: these could be low clouds, and the column’s visible height is not a measurement. Nevertheless, a warm garden can conceal the discomfort of cold, thin air outside it. If conditions are unsuitable for an unassisted human, arrange the appropriate support before arrival.

A first visit should be in daylight, in settled weather, with an attendant available. You will learn more when you are not simultaneously trying to discover how the storm cloth works.

### What to wear and carry

Wear closed shoes with good wet-surface traction. They should remain secure on your feet in shallow water and have soles that will not damage soft deposits. Avoid smooth soles, heels, and aggressive metal traction devices.

Dress in layers:

- A light, moisture-managing inner layer for the warm, humid interior.
- An insulating layer for waiting near the entrance or descending through the column.
- A windproof, water-resistant outer layer for the exposed edge and sudden weather.

Keep scarves, straps, and loose sleeves secured. The garden has moving machinery, valve handles, tendrils, and overhead tracks; a trailing strap can become part of several systems without your consent.

Bring drinking water rather than relying on the pools. Clear water here is process water carrying dissolved minerals and nutrients. Its clarity says nothing about whether it is potable.

A small waterproof bag, protected communications device, compact light, sunscreen, and secured sunglasses are useful. Pack lightly. A broad backpack makes it harder to turn without brushing leaves or stepping off the walkway.

Clean your footwear and equipment before entering. This is a young, closely managed ecosystem, not an appropriate place to introduce seeds, soil organisms, insects, or anything accidentally carried between worlds.

### Learn the floor before you admire the plants

At the entrance, pause long enough to identify the approved walking surfaces. Ask what the numbered depth marks mean and how high the water normally rises during a feed.

Do not infer load-bearing strength from appearance. A pale shelf may be a mature mineral deposit, a thin crust over mesh, or an actively forming edge. A mossy rise may still be a cage. A warm “boulder” is equipment with a convincing silhouette.

Stay on the designated route even where roots and mineral growth blur its boundaries. Do not test a surface with your foot, walking stick, or finger. You could puncture something that is growing into strength rather than already possessing it.

The few places that look least like a garden—bare grating, inspection seams, exposed ceramic—may be the places that explain it best.

### Respect the machinery without treating it as scenery

Locate the service arm’s operating zone and ask how visitors are expected to yield to it. Its deliberate movements and blunt shears do not establish that it recognizes people. Do not stand beneath it to watch pollination, reach toward its tools, or assume that a pause means it is waiting for you.

Leave valves, gauges, root tangles, drain combs, and lifted trough lids alone. A vine wrapped around a pipe might be a maintenance problem, an accepted adaptation, or both. It is not yours to untangle.

Avoid resting against vents, the capped shaft, or warm hollow structures. Heat delivered through water and metal can be much more concentrated than the surrounding air suggests.

Notice the normal background rhythm: pump vibration, occasional relay clicks, water moving in threads. If that rhythm changes sharply—especially with an alarm, sudden steam, unexpectedly rapid flooding, or a loss of footing—follow the attendant’s procedure. Do not investigate the machinery yourself.

### When cloud comes over the wall

Expect visibility to disappear unevenly. You may still see your feet while losing the door and the far side of the garden.

Stop before you lose your orientation. Use the established route back to a designated waiting place; do not cut through a bed because the garden is only forty paces wide. Small places can become confusing when their landmarks vanish.

Keep away from the perimeter during gusts. The cold rail and the sharply bounded warm interior mean that a few steps can change your exposure considerably. Do not lean out to see the column, climb for a photograph, or reach through drainage slots. Secure anything you could drop.

Ask in advance what happens when the storm cloth deploys. A roof moving into place is a machinery event, not merely a change in weather.

### Give the unexplained pool some room

The pool that moves against the pump cycle deserves attention, but not experimentation. So do the tiny stairways forming along its mineral edge.

Their regularity does not tell you what makes them. It certainly does not tell you whether they are harmless, inhabited, or intended for anything recognizable.

Observe from a permitted surface. Photograph without touching, if allowed. Do not add dye, take a sample, interrupt the water, or scrape away a step to see whether it returns. Ask the attendant what has been observed and whether the pool has special restrictions.

For a multiverse traveler, restraint is useful fieldcraft: **you do not need to resolve a mystery during your first visit**.

### Leave time simply to stand there

Once you understand the boundaries, this is a place to move slowly. Let your eyes adjust from the distant country below to a single bead caught on mesh. Listen for a leaf emptying itself. Watch the evening water reach roots that have been waiting just above its previous level.

There is no need to arrive equipped for conquest or prolonged wilderness survival. You need reliable access, suitable clothing, respect for a wet industrial surface, and the patience not to mistake newness for incompleteness.

The garden’s particular wonder is that you can watch land being made. Visit lightly enough that it does not have to make repairs after you.
