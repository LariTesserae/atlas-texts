Prepare for this plateau as a place that may be perfectly hospitable to your presence and indifferent to your departure.

The animals do not look trapped. They look accommodated. That is the important distinction. An ordinary traveler should worry less about being attacked than about discovering a place so exactly suited to them that leaving no longer seems like the next thing to do.

## Establish your return before you arrive

Do not make the outer edge your route home. The description gives no reason to believe its descent behaves like a cliff, and several reasons to believe it does not. Ropes, wings, and climbing experience are poor answers to a drop that returns your gaze to the plateau.

Use an arrival method that can retrieve you from an interior basin, preferably without requiring you to reach a particular marker. Arrange for someone outside the location to end the visit at a fixed time.

The best retrieval system would not require you to confirm that you are in trouble. You may remain lucid, comfortable, and entirely satisfied with where you are.

If possible, make retrieval automatic unless your outside support explicitly authorizes an extension. Do not give your future self, standing among those animals, sole authority to decide whether another hour is harmless.

For a first visit, plan a brief excursion within sight of your arrival point. There is no obvious benefit to crossing several miles of this country before learning whether you can reliably cross ten yards and come back.

## Dress for an exposed high plateau

Some of the hazards may still be ordinary.

Bring:

- Supplemental oxygen with its own supply, not merely a filter.
- Warm layers and a windproof outer shell.
- UV-protective glasses, sunscreen, and a brimmed hat.
- Well-fitting boots with good grip and enough flexibility to place a foot precisely.
- All your drinking water, food, and necessary medication.
- A compact emergency shelter that does not require stakes.

The air is thin, and the clear distances will make exertion difficult to judge. A ridge may look close enough for a casual walk while lying miles away. Walk slowly from the beginning. Do not wait for breathlessness to decide you are at altitude.

Do not infer reduced gravity from the apparent lightness of things. Check your balance and the effort of lifting your equipment somewhere safe.

Nothing described suggests a usable water source. The pool-shaped depressions are not promising reservoirs; they are features whose resemblance to reservoirs may mislead you. Do not plan on digging, collecting condensation, or obtaining moisture from plants.

Bring food you can eat without cooking. There is little fuel, and neither the vegetation nor the shelter materials should be treated as available supplies.

## Carry equipment that can disagree with your impressions

The plateau makes extraordinary conditions look settled and natural. Your own judgment may become unusually tolerant of them.

Carry two independent clocks, a recording device, and a short written itinerary. Keep the clocks running before arrival. If communications are possible, compare them with a clock outside.

You are not merely checking whether time “stops.” It plainly allows at least some sort of walking and observation. You are checking whether your processes continue to behave consistently:

- Does your watch advance?
- Does a spoken recording play back normally?
- Can you complete a sentence you began a moment earlier?
- Can you deliberately change direction?
- Do you still remember an unfinished task as something you intend to finish?

Before entering, write down a few concrete commitments:

> I will return after the first survey.  
> I have people to report to.  
> I have work that remains unfinished.

Read them at scheduled intervals. If they remain comprehensible but begin to feel irrelevant, leave.

A pulse monitor may help distinguish ordinary altitude problems from other changes, but do not trust a reassuring number more than an outside observer. The animals have living eyes and do not breathe. The plateau may preserve appearances that normally reassure a human.

## Learn the difference between a path and an exit

The corridors make room for you. That does not establish that they lead where you intend.

At each stage of the first walk, turn around and retrace a few steps. Test reversibility while the test is small. If you pass beneath a tail and cannot account for the space you crossed, do not proceed merely because another foothold presents itself.

Keep your hands free and your pack narrow. Avoid dangling straps. The animals are densely fitted together, and the paths may admit a person more readily than a person carrying an awkward load.

Do not squeeze, climb over a back, or push a limb aside. If the visible passage seems inadequate, wait and look. If it remains inadequate, turn back. The promise that there is somewhere to put a foot is not permission to use a living body as a step.

Stay well away from the edge trails. “None has a turning place” is enough information to exclude them from an initial visit. Do not investigate whether that means insufficient room, altered geometry, or something more consequential.

## Treat the animals as aware

Do not touch an eye to test awareness. Do not pinch skin, offer food, lift a shell, or try to provoke movement.

Their stillness is not evidence of unconsciousness. Their unfamiliar anatomy is not evidence of injury. A body that seems impossible to you may be entirely comfortable here.

Speak quietly if you wish. Announce your presence as you would when entering an occupied room. Do not expect a response, and do not escalate your efforts to obtain one.

Avoid getting down among their feet. The smallest occupants live in precisely the spaces a human might overlook. Examine the ground before setting down a bag.

If you discover that several animals are looking at you, there is no need to panic. Being seen is not itself a threat. More concerning would be your certainty that one particular space among them is yours.

## Do not make yourself at home on the first visit

The shelter is likely to be more dangerous to your judgment than the strange animals.

A repaired bowl, a needle, and a worn threshold offer a familiar explanation: someone lived here; therefore a person can live here. But the objects do not tell you whether their owner departed, remains nearby, or ceased to need a distinction between residence and arrival.

Leave the mat unoccupied. Leave the bowl empty. Do not repair the walls, add a cairn, or take a souvenir.

This is partly respect for an inhabited place. It is also restraint about entering an arrangement you do not understand.

If you need to rest, use your own seat or groundsheet near your return point, with a companion attending you. Avoid sleeping during the first expedition.

## Know what should end the visit

Ordinary warning signs—headache, nausea, confusion, unusual fatigue—are sufficient reason to return. Do not attribute them to the plateau’s mystery when altitude illness could explain them.

Other warning signs would be subtler:

- You repeatedly postpone a simple movement without feeling reluctant.
- Your equipment seems unnecessary, although nothing has supplied its function.
- The return journey becomes hard to imagine but not frightening.
- You stop wanting water or warmth despite still needing them.
- A resting position feels less like a position than an answer.
- You remember your life elsewhere accurately, but no longer feel that anything there awaits you.

Agree beforehand that a companion may end the visit without debate. If traveling in a pair, both of you should still answer to someone outside; two people can find the same silence persuasive.

The right preparation is not aggression or suspicion toward everything you see. This place deserves careful attention. Its animals may possess a kind of ease unavailable elsewhere.

But bring your own air, your own water, and a return that has already been arranged. Keep the first visit short.

There was a place for your arrival. Before entering, make sure there is also someone expecting it to end.
