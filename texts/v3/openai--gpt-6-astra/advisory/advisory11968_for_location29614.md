Prepare for this hollow as you would for a wet alpine ledge built over active machinery: lightly, carefully, and with a way out that does not depend on the ground remaining where you found it.

Its small size is deceptive. You can cross it in a minute, but that minute takes you past an unguarded drop, newly formed rock, unfamiliar organisms, and a device that intermittently lifts things into the sky. There is almost no distance available for getting away from a change in conditions.

The most important preparation is to resist treating it as a picturesque rest stop.

## Arrange your departure before you arrive

The seven steps are not a usable approach. They end in empty air. Do not assume a trail is hidden just below the cloud, or that the staircase will connect to one by evening.

Use a method of arrival that also permits departure from inside the hollow. If your multiverse travel equipment requires a fixed return point, stable coordinates, a level surface, or an uninterrupted countdown, find out how it handles moving terrain before committing yourself. The description suggests that the hollow is changing position or shape as well as growing new stone. The distant scar might be related to that process, but it is not enough evidence to tell you how.

Ideally, your return system should:

- Travel on your body rather than remain on the ground.
- Work without support from local structures.
- Have a backup power source protected from heat and water.
- Be operable one-handed, with wet fingers.
- Return you somewhere known to be safe, not merely “back along the approach.”

Give someone outside the location your expected return time and a missed-contact plan. If assistance would require another person to enter by the same uncertain route, that is not a robust rescue arrangement.

For a first visit, plan a short reconnaissance, not an overnight stay.

## Dress for hot stone, constant wetness, and sharp footing

The blue summits may make you think of cold mountain air. Dress for the actual basin: strong sun, radiant heat from the rock, little wind, and humidity that prevents sweat from doing its job.

Wear lightweight, quick-drying clothing with enough coverage to protect against ultraviolet light and incidental contact with vegetation. Bring a brimmed hat and UV-protective glasses. Do not rely on exposed skin to keep you cool; when sweat will not evaporate, bare skin can simply collect more heat.

Your footwear matters more than your walking speed. Use boots or sturdy approach shoes with:

- Good wet-rock traction.
- A sole resistant to sharp gravel.
- Toe protection.
- Enough support to tolerate a stone shifting underfoot.

The mineral paste is not ordinary soil, and the gravel is not worn smooth. A slip could put your palm onto hot rock studded with little cutting edges. Carry close-fitting protective gloves for brief contact, but avoid routinely using your hands to test surfaces.

Keep the pack small and snug. Secure loose straps. Put essential equipment in waterproof pouches. Anything you set down can get soaked, displaced, or—near the ring—lifted.

Bring drinking water from elsewhere, a modest supply of electrolytes, a basic first-aid kit, and something clean for irrigating cuts. Do not make this an expedition in which you arrive already tired or dehydrated.

The altitude is unspecified. If you are coming from a low-elevation world or region, allow for that uncertainty as well. Heat stress and altitude illness can both begin with symptoms easy to dismiss as excitement or exertion.

## Do not drink the channel

Its clarity tells you very little.

The water passes through actively forming mineral, supports bubbles that behave unusually, and eventually produces suspended droplets at face height. Even if there are no harmful organisms, dissolved substances may make it unsuitable for humans. An ordinary filter would not necessarily remove those.

Bring all the water you expect to drink, plus a reserve. Avoid tasting mineral deposits or eating seeds, leaves, or flowers. The scaled animal’s ability to lick the wall says nothing about your own tolerance.

Basic atmosphere and environmental screening are sensible before arrival. If conditions cannot be checked remotely, stay close to your exit while checking them. Ordinary instruments may identify familiar hazards; they cannot certify that unfamiliar physics or biology is harmless.

If water begins collecting in the air around your face, avoid walking through it unnecessarily. Eye protection is useful here. A simple mask is not a guarantee against whatever those droplets contain.

## Give the ring more room than its diameter

Treat the ring as an active lifting device, not as a novelty patch of low gravity.

“Taking the weight away” does not establish what happens to a human body. Objects rise, water domes, and some seeds disappear upward. You do not know the acceleration, the effective height, or whether a person would settle back neatly inside the circle.

Do not stand in it, reach across it, jump over it, or test it with something attached to you.

The exact boundary is especially concerning. The grass bends sharply where it enters. That does not prove a human limb would be injured, but it is ample reason not to put different parts of your body on opposite sides.

Keep your whole body, pack, straps, and equipment clear. Leave an additional margin for a stumble. Remember that mineral growth obscures parts of the rim: the visible metal may not show you the entire boundary.

There is also an overhead hazard. Material lifted earlier could return after you arrive. Do not lean over the circle to inspect it between activations.

The slow bands in the metal are not a countdown unless repeated observation establishes that they are. One quiet interval does not make the next few seconds safe.

## Use the shelter cautiously

The shelter may be the most hospitable feature in the hollow. Shade that removes the punishing sunlight while allowing cool rain through would be genuinely valuable.

But it is not a dry refuge, an enclosed shelter, or necessarily independent of the ring.

The fine lines connecting its legs to the ring, and their drying during activation, suggest a relationship. They do not tell you whether that relationship is electrical, thermal, structural, or something without a familiar equivalent.

Do not scrape those lines, touch them to find out whether they are warm, bridge them with metal equipment, or fasten ropes to the shelter’s legs. The roof’s ability to block sunlight does not imply that it can support a person or an anchor.

If the shelter can be reached without crossing the ring or disturbing the lines, use it as a place for brief, alert rest. Keep your water and exit device with you. Do not interpret the seed husk in its floor as evidence that someone has tested it for human use.

It is also too small to accommodate a large party safely. A companion is valuable, but crowding two people and bulky packs into the only comfortable shade could create its own problem.

## Walk as though the floor has not passed inspection

The ground is explicitly unfinished.

Fresh rock may be soft. Sharp fragments may be loosely held. The paste closing behind a footprint is not restoring the displaced stones to a stable arrangement. A surface that looks undisturbed may have been stepped on minutes earlier.

Choose broad, apparently continuous areas of rock where possible, and test each step before transferring your full weight. Stay away from the eastern seam and the far lip. Do not assume old-looking structures are embedded in old, reliable foundations.

Conventional climbing equipment is useful only if you can establish a trustworthy anchor. Newly forming stone, shelter legs, and vegetation are not trustworthy by appearance alone. A rope secured to something doubtful can turn a small slip into a larger failure.

Do not approach the edge for a better view when the cloud opens. There is no lookout rail, the footing is wet, and the view arrives at exactly the sort of moment that makes people forget their feet.

## Let the afternoon phenomena end the visit

For a first reconnaissance, arrive early enough to observe the hollow before its heat has accumulated. That is a conservative choice, not proof that mornings are safe; the ring operates irregularly.

The slowing current, rising water threads, and suspended droplets should be your cue to leave while departure is still straightforward. Do not wait for the low knock simply to experience it.

The knock accompanies falling water, a channel surge, exposed new stone, and movement at the stairs. Whether this is a gentle growth pulse or part of a larger displacement is unknown. Either way, it is a poor time to be balancing beside an edge.

If it happens before you can depart, stay away from the lip and seam, lower your center of gravity on the best footing available, and reassess your route afterward. Do not automatically retrace your footprints. The floor has changed.

## Keep your curiosity gentle

Clean your footwear and equipment before visiting so you do not carry foreign soil or seeds into those few crowded patches of life. Inspect and clean them again before entering another ecosystem.

Do not step on the translucent film to see its vanes rise. Do not pull a furred grass blade into the ring. Do not collect a seed merely because it falls into your lap. Avoid touching or feeding the scaled animal, and give its oddly delayed shadow the same respectful space you give the animal itself.

A camera with a wrist strap is better than a specimen jar. Observe from established footing rather than kneeling among roots or craning over the water.

Above all, expect the place to feel quieter and kinder than your precautions imply. There is shade, clear water, delicate growth, and enough stillness to hear individual droplets. That intimacy is likely to lower your guard.

Enjoy it without asking it to be stable. Leave while you are still comfortable, while your equipment is still behaving normally, and before you need the hollow to provide anything it has not explicitly promised.
