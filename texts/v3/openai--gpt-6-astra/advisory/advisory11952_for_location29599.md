The first thing to prepare for is that **you cannot visit this town at ordinary human size**. You could visit the marsh root, but that would be rather like visiting a city by placing your face against its sky. Moving the sedge, opening the bark, or breathing into the seam could change the conditions throughout the chamber.

A proper visit requires a reliable way to become small enough to enter as a guest, with your equipment and a means of returning. If your multiverse travel method cannot preserve a functioning human body at that scale, do not improvise. Observe from outside without disturbing the entrance.

Assuming that problem is solved, prepare for a cold, crowded settlement whose architecture is still growing—and whose residents are much better equipped to live there than you are.

## Arrange a welcome before an entrance

The entrance seam is also a drain, a possible flood inlet, and a boundary between freezing air and the root’s retained warmth. It is not somewhere to stand while sorting out your luggage.

Arrange a local escort and an arrival signal in advance. Let the inhabitants choose when you enter, where you wait, and what route you take. Ask for someone who understands bridges and water movement, not merely someone willing to show you the houses.

A human visitor will have several disadvantages:

- Only two feet, neither naturally suited to gripping living bark.
- No antennae to examine a doorway before the face reaches it.
- Poor unaided vision in near-total darkness.
- Little ability to distinguish a loose fiber from an animal by touch.
- Instincts about water, balance, and falling developed at a much larger scale.

Do not mistake the citizens’ lack of clothing or their thorn tools for a lack of technical sophistication. They build structures that can yield without breaking, manage drainage through living tissue, and recognize changes in their surroundings that you may not detect at all. Their expertise is what makes a visit possible.

Your first useful request is not “Show me the pool.” It is “Show me where a visitor can stand safely.”

## Bring a small, self-contained refuge

If possible, bring a compact shelter capable of keeping you warm and dry without using local fuel, water, or building material. Have its size, weight, heat output, and placement approved before arrival.

It should have:

- A broad, soft base that spreads its load.
- No stakes, sharp feet, or permanent anchors.
- An entrance you can close against small animals.
- Contained food, drinking water, and waste storage.
- A way to signal your escort from inside.
- Enough independent support to wait out a delayed departure.

It must also be movable. A safe patch of floor may not remain flat, dry, or open.

Do not wedge the shelter into a convenient crease. That crease may be closing, opening, draining, or already occupied. Nor should you assume an apparently empty terrace is available. The town’s empty spaces are part of its engineering.

At this scale, staying dry and warm may require more than miniature winter clothing. Make sure your travel system accounts for the changed balance of heat loss, breathing, and body function. Whatever keeps you alive should release as little heat and moisture into the chamber as possible. A heater powerful enough to make you comfortable could alter nearby condensation or frost.

There should be no flame.

## Dress for wet contact and narrow passages

Favor close-fitting clothing with a water-resistant outer surface, covered fastenings, and nothing dangling. Loose loops can catch on fibers, buildings, or animals. Exposed fuzzy insulation is likely to collect moisture and living passengers.

Bring gripping gloves and footwear suited to damp, yielding surfaces. Avoid spikes: a spike that helps you climb may wound the floor or cut a structural fiber. Test your equipment on a surface selected by your guide.

A fitted rescue harness is worth having. Let local bridge tenders decide where, if anywhere, a safety line can be attached. Do not clip yourself to the nearest upright strand. It may be a house support, a tender rootlet, or something alive that is about to move.

Protect your eyes and mouth from accidental contact with fibers and droplets. However charming this place sounds from outside, you will spend much of your visit at face level with wet biological surfaces.

Bring your own food and water. The town’s drinking places are maintained infrastructure, but their water is not thereby suitable for human consumption. Local stores may be equally unsuitable, and winter supplies should not become a visitor’s experiment.

## Treat open water as a serious trap

The pool is the feature most likely to exploit your ordinary instincts.

You will see a gap that looks jumpable. The inhabitants, who have broad gripping feet and lifelong experience here, do not jump it. Follow their example.

At a sufficiently small scale, the surface of water can become a difficult boundary to break through or escape. Being able to swim at human size does not establish that you can swim here. A wet garment, a droplet around the face, or adhesion to the pool’s edge could be more important than water depth.

Before approaching any crossing, learn:

- Where you may put your hands and feet.
- Which fibers are rescue lines.
- What signal means “withdraw.”
- Who performs a rescue, and how you should cooperate.
- Where to wait when a bridge is being moved.

Never rearrange the loose fibers over the pool for a clearer view. They are deliberately placed.

Keep back from growing ceiling drops. The falling drop is an ordinary event for the town, but “ordinary” does not mean harmless to someone standing in the wrong place. When the crowd withdraws into seams, withdraw with it. Do not remain exposed to discover what everyone is reacting to.

## Learn the dark rather than trying to abolish it

Bring a very dim, adjustable light with a shielded beam, and ask before using it. Start by illuminating only your own footing.

A strong lamp could disturb animals, expose people moving beneath predators, or introduce heat where the local balance is delicate. Even a harmless beam may interfere with work. The citizens do not need the chamber made visible in order to understand it.

Practice moving slowly without relying entirely on vision before the visit. Once there, learn a few local tactile distinctions under supervision: a secure handhold, a bridge edge, an occupied surface, a warning vibration.

Do not tap walls or stamp to test their strength. Vibrations travel through this settlement. Ceiling predators orient to disturbances in fibers; residents also use the floor to learn what is happening.

Ask how to get someone’s attention politely. A human voice may not be useful at your visiting scale, and an antenna reaching toward you may be an attempt to communicate rather than an unwelcome intrusion. Arrange translation if available, but establish a few simple signals regardless:

**Stop. Danger. Follow. Water rising. I need help.**

You will probably feel more secure with a guide immediately ahead and another person behind you during your first crossing.

## Expect contact with animals

You are unlikely to get through the town without touching something you did not recognize as an animal.

Prepare for that emotionally as well as physically. A railing moving under your hand is exactly the sort of surprise that makes a human jerk backward. Here, that reaction could put you in the pool or pull apart someone’s roof.

Practice stopping when startled. Keep your weight supported. Let the guide identify what is touching you.

Do not bring general insect repellent, pesticide, or a chemical barrier that sheds into the surroundings. This is an inhabited ecosystem, and some of the creatures you would instinctively exclude maintain its drains. Any substance intended for your protection needs to remain contained and be checked for local effects.

Likewise, arrive with clean equipment and leave without passengers. Your food crumbs, skin microbes, carried spores, and stowaways may have consequences in either world. Inspection and containment are better than indiscriminate spraying.

Give the armored grazer its route. Wait when a bridge is unhooked for it. Keep to the roofed streets beneath known hunting stations. Curiosity is not a reason to lift a roof flake: eggs, animals, and someone’s carefully maintained shelter may be behind it.

## Plan departure around water and growth

The flood marks are a record of people having to abandon their footing.

Ask about conditions in the marsh outside, not just inside the chamber. A calm pool does not guarantee a safe entrance later. Rising marsh water reverses the drain, and the route you used to enter may become the route water uses to arrive.

Have an agreed evacuation plan with a local guide and, if your travel allows it, an extraction method that does not depend on reaching the entrance seam. Do not assume “go to the highest terrace” is a sufficient plan; access, shelter, and the root’s movement all matter.

For a first visit, stay briefly. Leave a generous margin before changing weather or expected high water. An overnight stay adds unfamiliar risks: cooling, condensation, a shifting floor, and waking to find a passage narrower than it was.

Keep every essential item packed or immediately reachable. You should be able to move your entire presence without asking the town to dismantle anything around you.

## Be a light guest

Do not collect souvenirs. A polished thorn may have served through several chambers. A discarded-looking flake may be stored roofing. Fresh pith is not an inert sample; cutting it changes a living wall.

Ask before helping, too. Tightening a slipping bridge connection could defeat the feature that keeps it from snapping. Straightening a bent rootlet could undo days of work.

Useful gifts would be small, clean, durable, and chosen by the residents—not assumed improvements. Offer to carry something only after learning how it is carried and where it may be set down.

Finally, prepare for the possibility that the most memorable part will be waiting quietly: while a creature passes, while a drop lengthens, while workers judge whether a new opening is ready for another turn of the thorn.

When the brief green light arrives, enjoy it from where you have been told it is safe to stand. Do not move the sedge to make it last.

A successful visit leaves the town with no need to repair your curiosity.
