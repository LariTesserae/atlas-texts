Prepare as if you were visiting a very small, very old city in which your body is a weather event.

The inhabitants have made a workable home out of heat, vibration, scarce light, and dry mineral dust. What would endanger you is not necessarily what endangers them. Your first obligation is to bring your own conditions with you without spilling them into theirs.

## Before you approach

Arrange entry from somewhere other than the doorstep itself.

Do not stand on the stone while searching for the entrance. Do not tap it to announce yourself, blow dust from the mortar, or put your eye against the slit. A footfall is already a citywide event. Your breath could be considerably worse.

If your means of travel involves shrinking, establish **where the change in scale happens, whether your mass changes with it, and what air it displaces**. Being small enough to enter is not sufficient if you still weigh as much as an adult human. Nor should arrival involve a pressure pulse in a hollow whose architecture responds automatically to changes in airflow.

Ideally, a local guide will meet you at an agreed, sheltered point. If contact is possible beforehand, send a simple account of your needs: you require water internally, you exhale moisture, you are vulnerable to heat and continuous sound, and you will bring equipment to contain those problems.

Do not assume a population this small maintains facilities for strangers. An occupied house may have no spare room that can accommodate your biology.

## Bring your water. Keep it completely contained.

The most important distinction is between a dry visitor and a dehydrated one.

You cannot safely prepare by drinking less, suppressing sweat, or trying to acclimatize yourself to life without water. You remain human. You need a sealed environmental system that lets you remain human without exposing the gardens to your moisture.

Ordinary clothes, gloves, and a mask would not be enough. You release water through your breath and skin even while sitting still.

Your equipment should provide:

- A sealed breathing system that captures exhaled moisture rather than venting it.
- Internal drinking water, accessible without opening your helmet.
- Contained sweat, condensation, and bodily waste.
- Cooling that does not discharge water or a concentrated stream of heat into the city.
- A dry, clean outer surface, checked before entry.
- A leak warning you can see or feel.

Keep water in more than one layer of containment. A punctured drinking pouch should be an inconvenience inside your suit, not an irrigation event outside it.

The description establishes what a wet touch does, but not how much atmospheric humidity the plants tolerate. Until a host tells you otherwise, treat that uncertainty conservatively. Do not remove your helmet in a “quiet” room merely because it looks sheltered. Sheltered rooms may have gardens growing through their walls.

Any maintenance that exposes the wet interior of your equipment belongs outside the hollow, at a designated safe place.

## Protect yourself from the sound before entering it

Do not wait to discover whether you can tolerate the roar.

This is not just a loud street. The entrance excites the hollow, and the buildings carry and modify the sound. Some of it reaches you through the air; some reaches you through the surfaces supporting your body.

Bring hearing protection fitted to your altered scale, ideally combining in-ear protection with an isolating helmet. Active cancellation may help with part of the continuous noise, but it should not be your only defense. Damping in your soles and seat will matter too.

Even good equipment may leave you feeling the city in your ribs and teeth. Plan a short first visit. Headache, dizziness, nausea, difficulty concentrating, or trouble reading a familiar signal are reasons to leave—not challenges to overcome through determination.

Have someone monitor you. A traveler overwhelmed by vibration may become less capable of recognizing that they are overwhelmed.

Do not expect conversation to work. Learn the local signs for at least:

**Stop. Shelter. Follow me. Water leak. Injured. Do not touch. Exit.**

Carry a small flag and learn its conventions before crossing a street. Here, a flag is not quaint ceremonial dress. It is how someone above you learns that you exist.

A writing surface will help, but ask before using the clay-message channels. They may be the city’s only reliable means of reaching an occupied house.

## Prepare for heat without using the gardens as shelter

The warm stone, hot air, and broad reflected light make heat strain a serious possibility. Your sealed equipment makes cooling more complicated.

Know its endurance under local conditions. Set your return time from that endurance, with a generous reserve—not from how long you hope to stay.

Use a visor that reduces glare while preserving enough contrast to read hand signs and distinguish pale stairs from pale walls. Bring eye protection even if you expect your helmet to do most of the work; the dust is fine, mobile, and everywhere.

Do not adjust a mica plate to make your resting place more comfortable. Those plates are hand-tuned infrastructure. Moving one could alter light and heat for an entire terrace. Likewise, do not hang clothing over a vent, prop open a shutter, or wedge a door that closes during a gust.

The city contains many mechanisms whose purpose will not be obvious until the air changes.

## Travel light, close to the walls, and with a guide

The central hollow will be tempting. It offers a view, a clear route, and the apparent freedom of open ground.

It is also exposed.

The buildings provide interruptions to the wind, barriers to the sound, and places to take shelter when pressure rises. The central dust slope offers none of those things. Cross it only if a local recommends doing so, and then by the route they choose.

Wear a close-fitting suit with no loose straps. Secure your tools. A fluttering length of fabric could snag a creeper, catch a shutter, or pull you off balance. Do not use a balcony rail as a safety anchor without permission: braided root may be quite adequate for its intended users and entirely unsuitable for you.

Old neighborhoods require particular care. A doorway can lead into a facade rather than a room. A stair may end against living wood. An abandoned district may be structurally maintained by plants whose load-bearing role you cannot recognize.

“Unoccupied” does not mean “available to explore.”

When the roar changes pitch, stop and watch your guide. When screens close and vents open, get out of their way. Do not attempt to help the counterweights descend or hold a shutter against the wind. The city has been managing these events much longer than you have been present.

## Treat the gardens as homes, not specimens

Keep your hands off the leaves.

Their slow bending may make them seem tough, and their self-sealing wounds may make damage seem inconsequential. Neither is an invitation. A garden may represent generations of cultivation, shelter a household, reinforce old construction, or perform some function you have not yet understood.

Bring no soil, seeds, insects, or loose organic debris on your equipment. Remove traces of cleaning fluids and disinfectants as well; dry does not necessarily mean chemically harmless.

Do not taste the amber bladders. Their resemblance to fruit says nothing about whether a human can digest them. Carry your own sealed food and eat without producing crumbs.

Leave the ivory animal beneath its mica plate alone. Do not shade it for a better photograph, lure it out, or collect powder from its flowering stems. In a city with so little visible animal life, one creature’s habits may matter more than you can see.

The best souvenir is a drawing.

## Expect privacy, and a slower visit than you imagined

A dozen inhabitants can occupy a great deal of city without being available to you.

Do not search houses because nobody answered. Someone may be resting inside insulated walls, working behind a roof garden, or choosing not to receive a stranger. Arrange one host and allow that host to make introductions.

Bring patience rather than entertainment. A small, settled population may welcome outside news, but it may also have work that cannot wait while you explain the multiverse.

Ask before offering materials. Metal tools might be useful; they might also be too heavy, too conductive, or unnecessary. Water is not a gift here. Neither is an unsolicited attempt to quiet the entrance. Altering that airflow could change far more than the noise.

A better first offering is a precise question: *What would be useful for me to bring next time?*

## Make the first visit brief

Have a retreat route that does not depend on hearing instructions. Agree on visual emergency signals and a meeting point. Carry enough breathing and cooling reserve to wait out a pressure event without opening your suit.

For a moisture leak, your plan should be immediate containment and guided withdrawal. Do not improvise a cleanup by wiping affected plants, blowing on them, or applying heat.

For your own distress, leave early. There is no virtue in collapsing somewhere that cannot safely tend a wet, overheated human body.

Finally, prepare for the peculiar loneliness of the place. It is bright, but there is no sky. It is lush, but you cannot safely touch the growth. It is inhabited, but voices cannot cross the street. Your equipment may keep you alive while also making you feel like a visitor behind glass.

Let the visit be small: a sheltered route, one garden seen with permission, a few exchanges on clay, perhaps a period sitting beside someone at work.

If a weight passes across the doorstep and everyone looks up, look up with them. Stay still until your host moves again. Then make room for the careful work to continue.
