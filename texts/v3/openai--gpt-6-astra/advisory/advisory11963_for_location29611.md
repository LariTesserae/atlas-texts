Prepare for this place as you would for a polar sea expedition conducted inside a running engine. The apparent shallows are misleading. The apparent stillness is misleading. The light is perhaps the most misleading thing of all: it will make lethal water look accessible, and immense distances look like gaps you might cross.

For an ordinary human, this should be a **vehicle-based visit, not a swimming or diving trip**. Your most important equipment is a place you can retreat into that is warm, quiet enough to think, and capable of leaving without help from the stations.

## Bring your own interior

Do not count the industrial stations as accommodation. They contain dry air, but that does not establish that the air is breathable, that an entrance works as you expect, or that anyone permits visitors. Their bunks and cups suggest people, not hospitality.

The best base would be a self-righting ocean vessel with a heated, acoustically isolated cabin and a small submersible rated for the depths you actually intend to visit. A single craft capable of both surface travel and shallow submergence could also serve, provided it has genuinely independent emergency systems.

Prioritize:

- **Thermal insulation and redundant heating.** Nearly freezing water removes heat relentlessly. A hull that remains watertight can still become an uninhabitable refrigerator.
- **Independent breathing air and carbon-dioxide removal.** Carry reserves for a substantial delay, not merely the planned excursion.
- **Acoustic and vibration isolation.** Insulate the occupied compartment from both the surrounding water and the vehicle’s own structural members.
- **Positive buoyancy and a reliable emergency ascent system.** Do not make survival depend on a running motor.
- **Two independent ways to locate and recover the craft.**
- **A toilet, drinking water, food, and somewhere to sleep.** These are expedition necessities in a province where an uneventful crossing can consume the day.

Bring a return mechanism whose operation does not depend on local electricity, a readable station display, or a particular weather window. If your method of multiverse travel requires a fixed arrival point, establish that point aboard a stable platform or within a verified safe compartment. “Just above the highest ridge” is not an adequate specification. The ridge is underwater, the surface moves, and there is nowhere nearby to stand.

Before departure, decide what happens if your primary exit fails. If the answer is “find someone at a station,” you do not yet have a backup plan.

## Treat the sound as an exposure, not an atmosphere

This is the distinctive danger of the province.

You are not merely going somewhere loud. You are entering a landscape that conducts, reinforces, and redistributes mechanical vibration. Distance from a turbine will not reliably tell you how severe the exposure is. Neither will a quiet-looking valley.

**Earplugs and earmuffs alone are not sufficient protection.** Underwater vibration can reach the body through routes other than the ear canal, while a station’s floors, bunks, and walls can couple machinery vibration directly into you. Ordinary noise-canceling headphones are not a solution to structural resonance.

Send instruments ahead of your body. Use a remotely operated scout to measure sound pressure, frequency, and vibration along the intended route and inside any compartment you may occupy. Have the results assessed against human exposure limits; do not invent a safe duration by seeing how long you can tolerate it.

The description suggests abrupt spatial changes. A route that was tolerable a short distance away may cross a severe acoustic maximum. Survey at a useful scale, and keep recording during travel.

Plan for:

- Hearing protection appropriate to the measured environment.
- Vibration-isolated seating and sleeping arrangements.
- Clearly visible exposure alarms.
- Short initial excursions with generous recovery time.
- Immediate retreat for dizziness, nausea, ear pain, altered hearing, visual disturbance, or difficulty concentrating.

Do not wait for pain. Impairment may first appear as poor judgment, confused navigation, or an inability to perform a familiar task.

Most importantly, test whether your refuge is actually quiet enough **while it is in this environment**. A craft that performs well in an ordinary ocean may acquire an intolerable resonance here. Being inside is not necessarily being protected; the stations demonstrate that.

## Stay shallow, but respect what lies beside the shallows

The summit ridges offer sunlight and proximity to the surface. They do not offer a seabed you can rely on beneath the entire trip.

A modest sideways movement may take you from a few fathoms of water to kilometers of it. Clear water removes familiar depth cues, and a pale wall fading into blue can be much farther away than it appears.

Carry depth instruments, downward-looking ranging equipment, inertial navigation, and a mapped route. Do not navigate principally by the apparent size of rocks or the visibility of the bottom. There may be no local satellite system, and acoustic navigation may be compromised by the industrial noise and reflections.

Keep the first visit close to a surveyed summit, with your surface support directly available. There is no need to descend into the valleys to understand their scale. Looking across one from a safe observation point will be enough.

Avoid using a tether as a substitute for navigation. The plateau contains cables, gantries, sharp fractures, and intake structures: excellent places to snag one. Any necessary tether needs deliberate routing, monitoring, and an emergency-release plan.

### Do not casually enter the water

If external work is unavoidable, it calls for a qualified cold-water diving team, a suitable drysuit system, redundant breathing equipment, support personnel, and an established recovery and medical plan. It is not something to improvise because the hatch is only a few meters below the waves.

Cold shock, loss of hand function, and acoustic disorientation can turn a short swim into a failed return. A ladder is not a rescue plan for someone who can no longer grip it.

Never breath-hold dive out of a pressurized compartment, and never treat a station hatch as an ordinary door. Confirm compartment pressure and the entry procedure before operating anything.

## Give the machinery a wide margin

The open water’s slow drift says little about the water near an intake or inside a fault. This entire power system depends on locally concentrated flow.

Stay clear of intake bells, discharge outlets, bored passages, and gaps beneath machinery. Do not approach a fissure to feel whether it is drawing water. Use remote measurements.

Similarly:

- Do not attach your vessel to an unidentified cable.
- Do not shelter in a repair cradle without confirming its status.
- Do not touch exposed electrical equipment.
- Do not operate a valve, governor, or switch simply because its label is understandable.
- Do not shut down a station to obtain quiet.

You do not know what the power supports elsewhere. You also do not know how stopping one machine would redistribute pressure or flow through the connected passages.

The lit floodlights are particularly important to resist. They make the repair cradle look like a prepared visitor destination. It may instead be an active work envelope awaiting a machine, an automated maintenance sequence, or a worker who is not visible.

Approach it as operating infrastructure.

## Inspect any station remotely before entering

A station could be useful as a waypoint or emergency shelter, but only after verification.

Check for external damage, internal flooding, pressure, oxygen, carbon dioxide, toxic gases, temperature, fire hazards, and the actual operation of its locks. Seek authorization or evidence of a local access procedure. Intact paint and steady lamps are not a safety inspection.

Bring your own portable atmospheric monitor and retain a breathing reserve during initial entry. Keep the route back to your craft available.

Inside, expect ordinary tasks to become difficult. A trembling floor complicates handling tools. Continuous rattling consumes attention. You may find yourself repeatedly checking a gauge because you cannot remember whether you actually read it.

Use written checklists. Secure everything you put down. Keep loose straps, hair, and clothing away from equipment.

Do not plan to sleep there until you have established that the berth provides tolerable noise and vibration exposure. Exhaustion can make an unsafe compartment feel acceptable without making it safe.

## Assume speech will fail

Set up communication before arrival:

- Waterproof writing slates.
- Large, readable text displays.
- Prearranged hand and light signals.
- Visual alarms in every occupied space.
- A simple signal meaning **stop the visit and return now**.

Practice these while wearing your actual gloves and protective equipment. Fine handwriting and tiny touchscreen controls are poor choices for cold, tired hands.

Headsets may help within a properly isolated cabin, but they should not be your only channel. Underwater acoustic communications need testing here rather than assumption.

Travel with another person, and use explicit check-ins. Silence cannot tell you whether your companion is calm, overwhelmed, or unable to communicate.

## Leave the ecology alone

The absence of plants is not the absence of a food system. Much of the living structure is transparent, sparse, or distributed across enormous volumes.

Keep thruster wash away from ledges, bacterial films, and suspended feeding curtains. Avoid prolonged floodlighting of animals. Do not chase a white swimmer into open water; its slow apparent progress is no measure of your ability to follow and return.

Do not assume that local organisms are edible or harmless to touch. Bring all your food, contain your waste, and clean equipment before and after the visit to limit biological transfer between worlds.

Watch animals from a distance, but do not use them as human safety indicators. A fish holding position in a standing wave is adapted to conditions you are not.

## Prepare for the lack of events

This place may exhaust you without ever becoming dramatic.

There are few ordinary markers of change: no shore approaching, no vegetation shifting with altitude, no evening shutdown. The machinery supplies no reassuring rhythm of work and rest. Your nervous system may keep waiting for the roar to culminate in something.

Set a schedule independent of the landscape. Eat at planned times. Check instruments at planned times. End the excursion at the agreed time even if nothing has happened.

Bring a genuinely quiet recording or familiar music for use in your protected refuge—not to overpower the province, but to recover a sense of ordinary sound once you are isolated from it. Allow time afterward for rest and for assessment if hearing, balance, or concentration has changed.

For a first visit, I would choose one shallow ridge, one remotely inspected station, and a short observation period from inside the craft. I would leave the deep valleys unvisited.

That is not missing the place. Its essential experience is already there: bright stone beneath a moving ceiling, life arranged along invisible forces, and the unsettling discovery that an ocean can be almost motionless while an entire mountain range never stops working.
