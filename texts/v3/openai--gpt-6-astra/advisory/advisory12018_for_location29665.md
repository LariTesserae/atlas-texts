Prepare for a long stay, even if you intend a short visit.

The greatest mistake would be to mistake this place for an empty, primitive city with unusually good visibility. It is an inhabited landscape whose residents have accumulated an enormous amount of practical knowledge, much of it too ordinary to them to be written down. Its apparent simplicity is deceptive. You can understand how a door is made by touching it; you cannot necessarily understand when it is safe to go through.

Your first essential piece of equipment is a local host. Your second is enough time not to hurry.

## Before arriving

Arrange to arrive in an inhabited court, preferably in daylight, with someone expecting you. Do not choose an abandoned precinct because it offers a convenient open landing site. Open ground is not necessarily unused ground, and a street without buildings is not necessarily outside the city.

If you have a way of returning to your own universe, establish exactly what it depends on. Does it require a particular location, an agreed time, a functioning beacon, or a stable spatial relationship? Every one of those conditions may be troublesome here.

A device that has worked everywhere else is not yet a tested exit from this place. If possible, make your first visit a brief, accompanied arrival-and-return trial before committing to a longer stay. Do not leave your only return equipment in a neighboring district merely because that district is currently visible.

Give someone outside the destination a plan for a delayed return. A missed appointment here may mean that you are safely weaving in a guest room a month’s walk from where you expected to be.

### Treat the height as a serious medical constraint

The early boiling strongly suggests low atmospheric pressure. The description does not give enough information to calculate the elevation or the amount of oxygen available, and atmospheric composition may differ from what you expect. Measure rather than assume.

An ordinary human should arrive with:

- Confirmation that the air is breathable.
- A means of assessing pressure and oxygen availability.
- A gradual acclimatization plan, if the route permits one.
- Supplemental oxygen and an evacuation option if arriving directly at extreme altitude.
- Medical advice appropriate to their health and planned duration.

The flatness is dangerous psychologically. You will not feel that you have climbed a mountain, and there will be no summit to explain why carrying a bundle across a court leaves you breathless.

For the first few days, do very little. Let someone else carry your luggage. Headache, nausea, unusual exhaustion, and poor sleep are not proof of altitude illness, but they deserve attention. Confusion, loss of coordination, or breathlessness at rest require urgent help—not a determined walk toward lower country.

Here, lower country may be several seasons away. **Do not make “we can always descend” your emergency plan.**

## Dress for persistent cold, not a dramatic expedition

This is a place where you may become cold while waiting, listening, or doing careful work. Clothing should be comfortable enough to live in continuously.

Bring a moisture-managing base layer, warm insulation, a windproof outer layer, a hat, neck and face coverings, and gloves that allow delicate handling. Carry spare dry socks and glove liners. Footwear needs insulation and reliable traction on frosted stone, but it should also be suitable for days of walking.

The near-freezing temperatures are not mild when wind is continuous and moisture keeps appearing in seams, sleeves, and door fibers. Avoid working hard enough to soak your inner layers in sweat.

Bring a genuinely warm sleeping system and insulation beneath it. A guest room may be hospitable without being heated to your comfort. Ask which bedding and drying arrangements are customary.

Protect your eyes and skin. Thin air, pale paving, and frost may produce substantial ultraviolet exposure even beneath cloud. Good sunglasses, sunscreen, and lip protection belong in an everyday pocket, not at the bottom of an expedition bag.

## Bring food that does not depend on an ordinary boiling point

A pot visibly boiling is not necessarily hot enough to cook your dinner promptly—or safely.

For your initial stay, bring familiar, energy-dense food that is ready to eat or requires little preparation. A pressure cooker could be useful if its specifications cover the local conditions and your host approves its use. Otherwise, expect cooking to take more fuel and time than you are used to.

Local roots may be perfectly nourishing after a preparation sequence you have not noticed: slicing, soaking, fermenting, repeated heating, or simply choosing the correct variety. Do not infer edibility from resemblance. Ask to learn the whole process.

Likewise, intensely clear water is not necessarily safe water. The absence of animals does not establish the absence of microbes, plant toxins, or dissolved minerals. Bring water treatment appropriate to whatever testing is possible. Filtration alone will not address every hazard, and low-temperature boiling may require a different disinfection procedure.

Keep a reserve of safe water where you are staying. That reduces the chance that thirst will tempt you to ignore a street whose two sides are at different hours.

## Learn the warnings before learning the sights

Your first useful lesson should be a walk around the host district with someone who is willing to explain what everyone else already knows.

Ask them to show you:

- A passage you may use.
- A passage you must not use.
- The different household warning signs.
- Where you may safely fetch water.
- Which gardens and ground surfaces support a person.
- What to do when a familiar route changes.
- Who to consult when you hear a sound without an apparent source.

Carry a notebook with drawings. White cord, three pebbles, and an upturned rake may all be practical signs, but do not assume they mean the same thing. Learn what each means locally and who is authorized to remove it.

Adopt the stated rules without demanding an explanation first:

- Do not plant beside a shadow that outlasts its wall.
- Do not carry water across a street whose sides occupy different hours.
- If a stair gains steps on the descent, leave something on the last step.
- Do not force a suspended paving stone down.
- Do not step over a warning cord merely because the passage looks normal.

For the stair custom, ask your host for a small supply of suitable local leaves or other accepted offerings. Do not import seeds or shed foreign plant material around the city.

There is no advantage in finding loopholes. A sealed bottle is still something to ask about before carrying water across divided hours. Nor should you improvise a substitute for a custom simply because its purpose seems symbolic.

These practices may be closer to rules about live electrical wires than to superstition.

## Keep your party and your necessities together

The city’s scale will repeatedly fool you.

A crack visible in a distant stone does not mean the stone is nearby. A colonnade that suddenly looks reachable may genuinely be reachable—but not for long. A road that shortens is not necessarily safe to cross quickly.

Use local travel times rather than visual distance estimates. A map can record the ordinary arrangement of districts, but it should not be treated as a promise. Record which routes are normally used, where shelter exists, and who has recently traveled them.

Carry an overnight kit even on an outing expected to last a few hours:

- Warm layers and a compact shelter or emergency bivouac.
- Safe water and ready-to-eat food.
- Essential medication.
- Light and spare power.
- Local identification and your host’s details.
- A notebook and basic repair supplies.

A light source is for finding your footing, not proof that it is safe to continue.

If traveling with others, agree that no one crosses a temporary junction independently. Do not put all the medication, food, or return equipment in one person’s pack. You do not want half the group stranded with the bedding while the other half has the water.

During a conjunction, follow local movements and work at their pace. The residents’ slowness is information. If hurrying makes uncertain distance less certain, urgency is not merely an emotional problem; it may worsen the route itself.

Ask whether an exchange can be made without crossing. If you do cross, take what you would need to remain on the other side.

## Regard the gardens as deep terrain

The vegetation is not a carpet over ordinary ground.

Some beds fill ancient ravines. A surface dense enough to support a cup has not thereby demonstrated that it can support you. Streams disappear beneath it. Moisture, voids, and moving roots may be concealed underneath.

Stay on maintained paths. Do not jump across a bed to save a few steps, probe it with a sharp pole, or pitch a shelter on what looks like a convenient cushion.

Learn when gardeners cut paths and how they handle cuttings. Material that continues rearranging itself under weighted screens should not be stuffed casually into a bag or piled against your sleeping place.

Bring a well-fitting particulate mask in case pollen irritates your airways, but do not assume a mask makes an unfamiliar pollen drift harmless. Observe before entering. Your shadow may trigger release from plants you have not even touched.

Clean your boots and equipment before arrival. Avoid bringing live plants, animals, untreated soil, or unnecessary organic packing material. A world described as having no animals deserves exceptional care about imported insects, eggs, and contamination. Do not introduce a pollinator as an act of improvement.

## Become a tolerable guest before becoming an explorer

Bring compact, useful gifts, but ask what is welcome. Thread, needles, simple repair materials, or familiar hand tools may be more useful than a device no one can maintain. Even then, a seemingly superior tool may be poorly suited to local stone, fiber, or working customs.

Do not mistake slow work for ignorance.

These people maintain structures whose thresholds have long repair histories. They know that placing a slab successfully may involve more than lifting it into the correct position. An impatient visitor offering to finish everyone’s paving could be dangerous.

Useful skills to learn beforehand include hand sewing, mending, basic weaving, patient food preparation, and careful work with simple tools. Once there, begin with supervised tasks. Sweeping an approved street or sorting material may teach you more than an ambitious independent expedition.

Learn enough language to distinguish:

- “Not today.”
- “Not here.”
- “Wait for someone.”
- “Leave that where it is.”
- “This route no longer goes where you think.”

Pay fairly for instruction and hospitality. Guest rooms are a system of resilience, not an invitation to impose indefinitely.

## Leave the old precincts for later

Buried roofs, frost-filled street depressions, and finished walls inside quarries are compelling. They are also poor places to learn elementary rules.

Ordinary structural dangers remain: hidden rooms, unstable slabs, thin roofs, frost-fractured stone. Unusual geography does not replace gravity everywhere or make a fall less injurious.

Do not open a buried chamber alone. Quarry workers knock and evaluate the return of the sound; you do not yet know what “promptly” means to them, what a satisfactory response sounds like, or what they do when it fails to arrive.

If you hear an unexplained impact, tell someone who knows the buildings. Do not dismiss it because nothing has fallen, and do not assume you have been given a precise prediction you can safely investigate by yourself.

Ask permission before climbing a roof for the evening view. A low building can still have a dangerous roof, and a slab supported by broad pillars is not automatically intended to carry visitors.

## Prepare for the quiet and the waiting

You may find the silence harder than the cold.

There are no birds, insects, or distant herds. Wind moves against your clothes without supplying the familiar sounds of exposed country. Beyond the court, visual clarity may make immense distances feel intimate while offering little evidence of anyone nearby.

Bring something modest that anchors your days: a journal, a craft, books, familiar food, a routine. Keep track of sleep, meals, and medication without assuming every local time cue will always agree. If a medicine requires precise intervals, discuss that problem before traveling.

Expect homesickness to attach itself to small absences. You may miss leaves rustling overhead, rain arriving all at once, or a road whose length remains the same.

Do not turn that discomfort into unnecessary movement.

The best preparation is to become comfortable with a day in which the sensible achievement is drying your sleeves, helping repair a screen, and learning why the western passage is closed.

This place is not presented as relentlessly hostile. It has hearths, gardens, trade, guest rooms, and people who have made an ordinary life within its conditions. But their safety rests on attention and continuity.

Arrive ready to be taught. Carry enough to wait. When someone ties a white cord across a passage that looks perfectly harmless, let the cord be sufficient.
