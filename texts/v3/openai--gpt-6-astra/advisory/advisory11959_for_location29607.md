Prepare for this city as you would for a high mountain journey, a visit to someone else’s home, and a place where the ordinary agreement between motion and matter may not hold.

The mountain preparations are straightforward. The other two deserve most of your attention.

## Establish that you can visit, not merely arrive

The city does not sound like a living place temporarily immobilized. Its stillness is not an interruption. The hanging water, the unfinished wall, and the child’s hand in the animal’s fur are all in their proper condition.

Do not plan around finding a cause, ending a spell, or waiting for things to resume.

Instead, establish what happens to **you** when you enter. An ordinary human needs ongoing processes: breathing, circulation, nerve signals, digestion, heat exchange. Whatever allows you to walk through this city must also allow those processes to continue.

Before committing to a visit, your travel system should have demonstrated that:

- Your body and carried equipment continue to function.
- You can move through the local air without harmful resistance.
- You can see and hear sufficiently to navigate.
- You can leave through the same boundary you entered.
- Your return does not depend on anything inside the city changing.

A return mechanism that waits for sunset, a gate opening, a local clock striking, or a resident performing an action is unsuitable. So is a rescue plan that assumes you will be able to send a distress call later.

Prefer an exit under your immediate control, with a second means of retrieval operated from outside. Make the first excursion short, close to the arrival point, and easy to reverse.

Most importantly, determine whether your presence changes nearby things. There is a considerable difference between being permitted to move through the city and bringing a moving region with you. If your arrival causes a drip to fall, a leaf to bend, or an inhabitant’s posture to change, withdraw until you understand the effect. That would not necessarily be a rescue. It could be damage.

## Bring what a human needs, even where it appears abundant

**Do not count the cistern as a water supply.**

The water is clear and apparently plentiful, but nothing in the description establishes that it can pour, separate into a mouthful, or participate in ordinary chemistry. The curved spring may be no more available to you than the curve of an arch.

Bring all your drinking water in containers that remain under the same conditions as your body. Bring food you can eat without cooking. Confirm beforehand that supplies still behave normally after being carried across the boundary.

The same caution applies to air. A city can look open and breathable without supporting gas exchange for an arriving organism. Have that verified rather than inferred. If it cannot be verified, an ordinary human should enter only with independent breathing support and a properly tested means of maintaining a habitable environment.

Pack for an unexpectedly long delay:

- Drinking water and ready-to-eat food.
- Warm layers, a windproof shell, hat, and gloves.
- A compact insulated sleeping pad and emergency shelter.
- Essential medication, a basic first-aid kit, and foot care.
- A reliable light and spare power.
- An independent elapsed-time clock.
- Navigation notes and redundant return equipment.

Do not assume batteries last forever because the city does not change. If your equipment works, it is presumably undergoing processes and consuming resources.

## Dress for sharp stone and cool, persistent shade

The terrain presents ordinary hazards even before its stranger properties are considered.

Wear supportive boots with good grip. The pale grit can roll beneath your soles; exposed rock edges appear unusually sharp. Gloves will help if you have to steady yourself against masonry. Avoid dangling straps that could catch on a projecting stone, a timber crossing, or a low roof edge.

The sun is not a promise of warmth. Cool air, damp walls, snow near the crown, and long periods spent observing rather than walking can chill a person quickly. Carry insulation even for a short visit. Eye protection and sun protection are sensible on exposed terraces.

Do not use the sun to estimate time. Schedule rests, drinks, and your departure by your own clock. With no changing light, thirst, fatigue, and elapsed hours may become surprisingly easy to misjudge.

Stay on broad public streets at first. Their drainage channels, load grooves, and carefully separated approaches indicate deliberate circulation routes. Follow those routes rather than improvising shortcuts across the open ground.

Treat fissures and bridges cautiously. A lintel being in place does not prove that it will respond normally to your added weight. Nor does the appearance of newly fractured rock establish that a collapse is imminent. You simply do not yet know how imposed loads behave here. Avoid finding out over a deep opening.

## Enter as a guest, not as the only person who matters

The inhabitants are not vacant. That should govern your conduct even though they cannot offer an ordinary greeting or objection.

Their awareness is uncertain. Their dignity is not.

Do not touch faces, alter clothing, move hands, inspect pockets, enter bedrooms, or take objects. Do not pose among them for comic photographs. Give the child and the grazer the same distance you would give a quiet, intimate moment in any inhabited city.

The folded cloak and half-full cup are not abandoned belongings. The grain at the counter is not an unattended provision cache. The mason’s tools are not available equipment.

Use a simple rule: **if an action would require permission in an ordinary town, the absence of an answer does not grant it here.**

A quiet greeting on arrival is reasonable. Repeated shouting, clapping, or startling attempts are not useful. You are not obliged to pretend communication is occurring, but neither should you assume an inability to respond means an inability to experience your presence.

Keep your early exploration to public stairs, streets, squares, and overlooks. If you document the place, concentrate first on its architecture and landscape rather than making close studies of people who cannot consent.

## Do not test the stillness with your hands

You will probably want to touch the bead at the spout.

Decide against that before you arrive.

It is an unusually compelling temptation: a small, apparently harmless experiment that seems capable of answering the whole mystery. But you do not know whether contact would meet resistance, displace the water, alter its state, or transfer some unfamiliar condition to you.

The same applies to suspended birds, loose threads, leaves, and the lifted foot of an animal. Do not throw objects at them or pass a stick through them.

Any necessary compatibility testing should happen before the visit, at a controlled boundary, using your own expendable materials—not the city’s inhabitants or possessions.

Leave no litter or markers. You cannot assume that rain, growth, weathering, or a helpful passerby will ever remove them. Even a small intrusion may have no natural way to disappear.

## Prepare for the emotional difficulty

The city may be beautiful in a way that becomes hard to bear.

At first, you may feel wonder. Later, you may feel urgency: someone ought to finish the threshold; the bird ought to reach a roof; the child ought to grow older. Those reactions are understandable, but they are not evidence that the city is suffering.

Conversely, its completeness may make your own needs feel crude. You will become hungry in a place where hunger does not increase. You will tire beside figures whose balance never fails. You may begin to feel that moving is an imposition.

Eat anyway. Rest anyway. Leave when planned.

Travel with a calm companion if your means of entry safely supports two people. Agree on the duration, permitted routes, and reasons to turn back before entering. Someone who treats every anomaly as a puzzle to defeat would be a poor choice.

Listen briefly to the continuous tone in the market, but do not spend your reserves waiting for its next syllable. There may be none to hear.

## A good first visit

Arrive near an outer public approach. Verify your return system and bodily comfort. Walk a short route on broad paving, avoiding crossings and occupied interiors. Find an overlook where you can see how the streets frame the meadows, rock, and trees.

Spend some time there without trying to make anything happen.

Then return while you still have ample supplies and attention.

The best preparation is to relinquish the expectation that a successful journey must produce an event. This city offers something else: a place whose open spaces are intentional, whose unfinished things are not failures, and whose inhabitants do not need to become available to you for your visit to have meaning.

Bring everything you need to continue. Do not require the city to continue with you.
