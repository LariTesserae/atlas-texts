Prepare for this place as you would for a visit to a working port built over a living cave system, in a country where distance is part of the public infrastructure.

The most dangerous mistake would be to see the open plain and think it offers freedom of movement. It offers visibility. Those are not the same thing.

You will be able to see buildings you cannot safely walk toward, vegetation that conceals enormous depths, and weather whose relation to your own location may not be obvious. Much of what looks empty is probably being kept empty deliberately.

## Arrange your arrival before you pack

Do not arrive independently at a convenient-looking doorway.

Those frames appear to handle heavy transport, and their operation produces physical effects well outside the aperture. A frame showing the plain from a slightly different angle is not necessarily idle, pedestrian-safe, or even presenting an unobstructed view.

Arrange:

- A receiving contact who understands human physiology.
- A specifically designated pedestrian arrival point.
- An escort from that point to your accommodation.
- A return route that does not depend on the same frame remaining available.
- Confirmation that your accommodation remains safely habitable during transfers and local ground formation.

Ask your host a very literal question: **“Which places here are continuously safe for a human body to occupy?”**

“Safe district” is too vague. You need to know whether a room’s air, pressure, geometry, access, and floor remain dependable throughout a normal operating cycle.

For a first visit, plan one escorted outing and a short stay. Do not make crossing the plain your introduction to the city.

## Treat the sound as an environmental exposure

The noise is not merely atmosphere. Some of the reports are strong enough to move clothing, and others travel through the ground or seem to occupy the skull. Expect fatigue, impaired communication, disorientation, and possibly injury.

Bring professionally fitted hearing protection appropriate to a severe industrial environment, ideally with two layers and a communication system compatible with both. Ordinary noise-canceling headphones are not sufficient protection.

But do not assume that protecting your ears protects the rest of you. Ear protection cannot make a substantial pressure wave harmless, and it may do little about vibration conducted through the ground.

Your accommodation should have a locally validated refuge from both noise and vibration. A room that feels quiet is not automatically such a refuge: the district without echoes still contains immense sound.

Before leaving shelter, agree on simple visual or tactile signals for:

- Stop.
- Follow me.
- Return immediately.
- I am injured.
- I cannot orient myself.

Keep those signals distinct from any local traffic signals. Arrange a buddy check rather than relying on shouted warnings.

Persistent ringing, muffled hearing, nausea, headache, dizziness, or difficulty thinking should end the outing. Do not wait until you can prove which part of the environment caused them.

The first thing this place is likely to take from you is not your footing. It is your ability to judge how tired you are.

## Dress for sharp ground, dim light, and uncertain air

The surface is young, folded mineral with cutting edges. Wear puncture-resistant, high-traction boots with ankle support—not soft walking shoes. Protect your lower legs, and use gloves when contact with structures or terrain is unavoidable.

Avoid loose straps, dangling equipment, and flowing clothing. There are sharp seams, crowded branches, nearly invisible silk, and transport events that disturb the air.

Useful equipment includes:

- Eye protection against grit and sudden airborne debris.
- A shielded, adjustable lamp with a low-light setting.
- Spare lighting and power.
- A compact medical kit with dressings suitable for cuts.
- Sealed drinking water and familiar food.
- A locally compatible navigation and emergency communication device.
- A physical identification card carrying your host’s details and relevant medical information.

Do not make your pack so heavy that stepping over a low ridge becomes awkward. You need balance more than expeditionary grandeur.

The warm air should not be assumed breathable simply because the vegetation is luxuriant. Have its suitability for humans checked before exposure. Cracks that inhale or exhale may move gases between very different environments; avoid placing your face over them. An ordinary filter mask does not address oxygen deficiency or every hazardous gas. If breathable conditions cannot be verified and supported, do not make an ordinary unsealed visit.

## Learn routes, not directions

Here, “I can see the hotel” is not useful navigation.

Buildings can borrow rooms. A stair can have its lowest step miles from the others. Doorways can display a changed orientation. Distance is explicitly maintained, which means it can presumably be disrupted.

Use current, authorized pedestrian routes. Do not substitute a straight line for a marked detour.

In particular:

- Do not cross a threshold merely to inspect it.
- Do not climb an isolated stair or enter an apparently abandoned architectural fragment.
- Do not step across an active rail, even where it vanishes into the surface.
- Do not follow maintenance machinery into newly opened ground.
- Do not assume yesterday’s route is valid today.

The low lights matter, but you need to learn their meaning. Some brighten for hunters, apparently for the hunters’ benefit. A light coming on is therefore not necessarily permission for you to proceed.

Ask your escort to explain district boundaries before crossing them. A change felt in the teeth, an altered acoustic quality, or footsteps heard before contact should be treated as a location cue—not something to investigate alone.

A conventional map may still be useful, but it should be secondary to local routing information. Breadcrumb navigation only helps if the relationship between the breadcrumbs remains stable.

## Keep away from the green edges

The fissures will be the most tempting part of the landscape. They contain almost all the visible abundance: moisture, luminous leaves, intricate animals, shelter from the apparent barrenness above.

They are also places where depth is hidden, the ground may be sharp or unstable, and predators can move unseen.

Stay back from rims by whatever distance local guidance requires. Do not sit on their finished pale borders unless those are explicitly designated seating areas. Do not reach beneath overhanging branches, pull aside leaves, or test the depth with your foot.

Nothing in the description establishes that the plants are poisonous or predatory. That is not a reason to handle them. Their folded throats, soft growths, and moisture exchanges belong to an unfamiliar ecology, and you have no way to distinguish a harmless response from a harmful one.

Bring your own water. Do not harvest condensation, sample fruit from the rotating balcony, or accept that something eaten by local animals is safe for you.

The water cycle here is extraordinarily intimate. A drop on a leaf may already have passed across several organisms. Treat exposed moisture as biologically active, not pristine.

Clean and inspect equipment before arrival and before departure. Check boot treads, cuffs, pack seams, and any exposed fastening for hitchhiking organisms. Take no living souvenirs.

## Do not confuse small animals with small consequences

A modest flock can sound like a collapsing cliff. A flash in one basin can propagate for miles. Your presence could conceivably set off an alarm much larger than the disturbance that caused it.

Move steadily, keep lighting subdued, and do not use flash photography without local permission. Never provoke an animal to see it illuminate or inflate its chest sacs.

If leaves close, animals grip their supports, a frame darkens, or grit starts forming patterns, stop sightseeing and follow the local transfer procedure. Learn that procedure before entering the open city.

During a spreading biological alarm, avoid moving toward the disturbance for a better view. The description specifically places hunting activity under that cover. Seek a designated refuge rather than hiding in vegetation.

Do not improvise Earth wildlife advice. “Stand your ground,” “make yourself large,” and “run for shelter” are species-dependent strategies, not universal laws. Get instructions for the actual large hunters before your first walk.

## Respect the vacancy

Do not camp between buildings. Do not move benches, containers, markers, or planters to make a more convenient arrangement. Do not place equipment in a depression because it looks sheltered.

A depression may be a receiving berth for an entire grove. A vacant plaza may be reserved for the next emergence of ground. A generous separation between objects may be what prevents their interiors from overlapping.

This is also a matter of courtesy. The city appears to accommodate geological growth, transport, vegetation, and animal movement without separating them into tidy human categories. You are entering that accommodation, not visiting a failed attempt to pave a wilderness.

Stay out of the way of maintenance crews and machines. When new route lights come on, accept the detour.

## Prepare for the emotional experience

You may find the place more exhausting than frightening.

The horizon is open, but the sky gives little reassurance. The permanent dimness offers few cues for sleep. The roar makes ordinary conversation laborious. The city’s great separations may feel lonely even when you are standing in its center.

Bring a reliable timekeeping routine. Schedule meals, rest, and check-ins rather than waiting to feel hungry, sleepy, or isolated. Stay with someone who can notice when you become slow, distracted, or unusually confident.

And leave yourself time to look from safety. The careful circulation of water, the plants leaning toward newly formed ground, and the nests occupying old spatial mistakes are not incidental decorations. They are how this place lives.

A good first visit would be modest: meet your escort, learn one route, watch one basin from a protected overlook, perhaps observe a scheduled arrival from an approved shelter, and return before fatigue dulls your judgment.

**Go as a guest of the city, not as a walker on an empty plain.** The emptiness is doing work.
