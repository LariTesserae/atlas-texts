Prepare for this valley as you would for a visit to a small, hospitable town at the bottom of a deep sea—not as though hospitality makes the sea survivable.

Its gentleness is real. So is the weight of water above it. The ropes are polished because people use them every day; that does not mean an ordinary human can safely follow them wearing what the residents wear.

The most important preparation is to arrange a way to be there that does not require the town to keep you alive.

## Settle the question of pressure before you arrive

Nearly a hundred fathoms is roughly 180 metres of water. Under ordinary physics, that adds about eighteen atmospheres of pressure to whatever exists above the sea. The sea’s altitude and unusual boundary make the exact conditions uncertain, but neither is a reason to assume the valley is at a comfortable pressure.

Send instruments ahead. Measure pressure, temperature, water chemistry, and the composition of the air in the chambers. If your means of travel opens a doorway between worlds, establish how it handles unequal pressure **before opening it into the town**.

For an ordinary human, the best arrangement would be a **one-atmosphere submersible with good manipulators**, supported by a sealed habitat or a dependable return route. You could see the valley, move alongside a guide, and stop to watch work without turning every minute into a breathing-gas calculation.

A pressure-resistant walking suit might allow closer contact, but it has disadvantages here: bulk, poor access through narrow passages, and the possibility of breaking something simply by bracing against it. Have the residents identify routes wide enough for you. Do not expect their domestic spaces to accommodate your life-support equipment.

If you intend to swim at ambient pressure, this becomes a professional deep-diving expedition, potentially a saturation operation—not a recreational dive with extra cylinders. It requires appropriate breathing mixtures, redundant equipment, thermal management, surface or habitat support, and a planned decompression system. At such depths, ordinary compressed air is not a safe breathing solution.

Do not assume that because the inhabitants raise their faces into air pockets, you can do the same. Their physiology may differ from yours. Even if it does not, the pockets are still at local water pressure.

**An air chamber is a room, not a return to the surface.**

## Bring your own air, warmth, and way home

The hollow-leaved plants tell you something important about this place: breathable gas is cultivated, harvested carefully, and probably accounted for.

Arrive with an independent supply. Include enough reserve to wait out a blocked passage, a geological disturbance, or a delay in retrieval. Do not build your emergency plan around borrowing from the gardens.

The leaves grown to freshen the chambers may make the air pleasant, but a pleasant smell does not establish safe oxygen or carbon-dioxide levels. Test before breathing. An oxygen-rich pocket that suits local residents could also be hazardous to a human at this pressure.

Likewise, “warm water” is not a complete thermal plan. Water cooler than your body can chill you over a long visit, even when it feels welcoming at first. Near the new rock, the problem may reverse. Bring insulation or active temperature control appropriate to measured conditions, and do not rest against the black glass or the newest mineral seams.

Your essentials should include:

- Redundant breathing or sealed-environment life support.
- Independent power and a protected reserve.
- Pressure, temperature, and environmental sensors.
- Quiet, adjustable lights with backups.
- Navigation that works without a view of the sky.
- A means of communicating with both your guide and your support team.
- An extraction method that works if you are unconscious.
- A pressure-appropriate medical and decompression plan.

Make the return system independent of a single local rope, a single doorway, or a patch of mountain expected to remain the same shape.

## Learn to move without standing

The valley’s ground is not really ground yet.

A footprint can exist without the surface being capable of supporting a person. A pale slope may be a crust over paste. A white hollow may be a suspension of particles deep enough to swallow your equipment and destroy visibility.

Your instinct on losing balance will be to put a foot down or grab the nearest firm-looking edge. Both reactions may be wrong.

Practise neutral buoyancy and controlled movement before visiting. If using a vehicle, choose one capable of fine, low-disturbance manoeuvring. Powerful thrusters could strip gardens, lift mineral clouds, or batter a house with their wash.

Keep your equipment close to your body or vehicle. Secure tools with short, managed attachments rather than loose lengths that can catch on roots, ropes, and trellises. Carry an accessible line cutter, but learn to distinguish your own snagged tether from something that holds a household together.

Do not install an anchor by driving it into the mountain. Ask where loads can safely be attached.

And do not mistake translucence for softness. The new blades and glassy crests have not had time to become forgiving. Protect hands, suit seals, hoses, and exposed cables.

## Hire a guide, and learn the knots yourself

A map will help, but a local person will know which passage has begun to pinch shut, which staircase has acquired a foundation, and which apparently solid wall is mostly leaves.

The street ropes are both roads and information. Learn their basic language before your first excursion:

- **Three long loops:** the upper gardens.
- **A hard round knot:** an air chamber nearby.
- **Loose tassels:** a moving passage that should not be trusted in darkness.

Learn these by touch, wearing your actual gloves. A symbol you can recognise only in a brightly lit briefing room is not yet useful.

Ask about right-of-way, passing places, and whether particular lines can bear the additional load of your equipment. Do not add your own knots or markers without permission. In a town navigated partly by fingertips, an improvised marker can amount to changing a road sign.

Stay with your guide until you understand how to recover from a lost line without kicking up the bottom.

## Treat the edge as a precipice

The sea’s boundary is not a window you can lean against.

It appears to resist a hand only lightly. Beyond it is air, a change in buoyancy, and potentially a very long fall. The pressure transition may also behave in ways that ordinary diving knowledge cannot predict.

Do not test it with your fingers.

Do not assume that a submersible will remain supported if part of it crosses through. Do not assume a safety line fixed to a nearby pale ridge will hold. A vehicle made to float in water may become an extremely heavy object the moment the water stops carrying it.

For a first visit, see the lower world from well inside the boundary, with a local guide. An approach to the skin should be a separate, instrumented expedition using equipment designed for both sides.

The residents keep their homes back. Take that as accumulated knowledge, not timidity.

## Let the town set the pace

You will probably find the quiet more demanding than the darkness.

Movement is slow. Conversation requires a place where mouths can meet air—or technology that translates underwater speech into something the residents can understand. A gesture missed in green water may matter more than a sentence spoken perfectly.

Learn a small working vocabulary of hand signs:

**Yes. No. Wait. Follow. Help. I cannot breathe. I am caught. I do not understand. May I touch this?**

Agree on emergency signals that remain clear with bulky gloves and poor visibility. Bring a waterproof writing surface as a backup. Avoid relying solely on electronics that your hosts cannot interpret.

Use the least light you need. The town’s lamps illuminate a hand and the next turn, not entire streets. A floodlight could disturb gardens, wildlife, and people who are accustomed to dimness. Ask before shining into homes or taking bright photographs.

Plan short initial outings. There is a particular exhaustion in having to think about every movement while your surroundings invite you to relax.

## Be a guest, not another burden on the gardens

Arrange accommodation in advance. Unless a chamber has been assessed and fitted for human use, sleep in your own sealed habitat. A communal air pocket with an elbow ledge is not necessarily somewhere you can safely lose consciousness.

Bring food you already tolerate and contain all waste. Do not assume that edible stems are edible to you, or that their raw preparation is safe for an unfamiliar visitor.

Clean your equipment before entry. This town depends on living materials: cultivated gas leaves, luminous cultures, waterwood, root mats, and the organisms growing over new stone. Imported microbes or chemical residues could do damage you would never see before leaving.

Ask what supplies would actually be welcome. Compact repair materials or particular tools might be useful, but only if they work underwater and suit local practice. Do not arrive with an unrequested biocide, fertiliser, or “better” lamp.

Above all, do not touch the seams of the gas leaves. You have already been shown how carefully their growers handle them.

## Know when to stop moving

Ask your guide what happens when a mountain seam opens. The description suggests that work pauses; imitate that pause. Do not decide on your own that the settling white dust is harmless. It may obscure your route, affect equipment, or signal that nearby structures are shifting.

If the garden leaves fold before a dark swimmer arrives, go under the eaves with everyone else. Secure your belongings. Do not pursue it for a closer look, illuminate it aggressively, or infer safety from the fact that no one has seen it feed.

Have an agreed procedure for a mineral whiteout: stop unnecessary movement, retain a known handline if safe, signal your guide, and avoid searching with your feet.

The most dangerous moment may be when you become comfortable enough to forget why the ropes are there.

## What to bring in yourself

Bring patience, and some willingness to be physically dependent without becoming helpless.

You may spend an afternoon watching someone test a slope and decide not to build yet. You may have to wait outside an air chamber because it is occupied. You may see a familiar route become impassable during a longer stay, not through disaster but because the mountain has continued its work.

Do not interpret every change as an emergency. Equally, do not let the residents’ calm persuade you that your body shares their tolerances.

A good first visit would be modest: arrive in independent life support, meet a guide on a stable outer terrace, learn a short rope route, watch the gardens being tended, and return before fatigue makes you clumsy.

Leave the boundary for another journey.

The valley rewards attention more than boldness. Prepare well enough that, when the builder places her palm on the unfinished mountain and settles back to wait, you can afford to wait with her.
