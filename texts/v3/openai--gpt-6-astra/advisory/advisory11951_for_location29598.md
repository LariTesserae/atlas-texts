Prepare for this as a visit to a crowded, very old settlement whose weather you can change by breathing.

The inhabitants are not waiting for someone to discover them. They have houses, work, cultivated plants, managed animals, and a water system fitted to the exact conditions of their hollow. Your first responsibility is not to disturb those conditions. Your second is to establish whether they want a visitor at all.

## Decide what kind of visit is possible

At ordinary human size, you cannot enter this town. You can look through its entrance, and perhaps eventually communicate across it, but putting a hand inside would be less like knocking than reaching a piece of heavy machinery into a street.

If your multiverse travel arrangements include a reliable way to change scale, arrange that **before approaching the wall**, not at the entrance. Do not experiment with it here. You need to know that it reduces your mass as well as your dimensions, that your equipment changes with you, and that you will not suddenly return to full size if you faint, become cold, or run out of power. An involuntary return inside the hollow would be a disaster for everyone.

Without that capability, prepare for a threshold visit. That is still a real visit. It may also be the only kind you are ever invited to make.

## Approach without bringing the lane down on them

Find a time when the lane is quiet. Avoid carts, crowds, maintenance work, and freezing rain. Look at the retaining wall from a distance first: loose stone, fresh cracks, or accumulated runoff are reasons to postpone, not things to fix yourself.

Wear clean, close-fitting clothes without dangling cords or shedding fleece. Leave large bags well away from the opening. Turn off vibration alerts.

Lower yourself slowly. Do not drop to your knees beside the wall or brace a boot against it. The cart tells you something important: what feels like an ordinary movement above ground can become an event throughout the town.

A clean insulating mat may make lying still easier, but place it on the lane, clear of the entrance and its leaves. Do not use it to create a snug little viewing enclosure. That opening may be part of the town’s ventilation.

Keep your mouth and nose away from it. In this cold, dry environment, a human breath brings heat, moisture, and moving air. Even if it does no immediate damage, it could coat surfaces with frost after you leave.

Do not blow dust away.

## Let them determine whether contact happens

Bring patience rather than an elaborate introduction.

Their movements are slow, and many ordinary human sounds may be too loud or too low-pitched to be useful. Calling into the hollow, tapping the stone, or knocking with a fingernail would be poor first choices. Their own impact work is cushioned; take that seriously.

Allow yourself to be seen without repeatedly blocking the light. If an inhabitant approaches, make one small, slow gesture and wait. If there is no response you can confidently understand, do not manufacture one.

The feather at the entrance is especially important to leave alone. You do not know whether it marks a boundary, modifies airflow, or serves some other purpose. Its ambiguity is not permission to move it.

Likewise, a closed door is a closed door. The worn threshold beside the carefully supported shoot might belong to a household, a public room, or somewhere with special significance. Nothing about its beauty entitles you to inspect it.

A first visit might consist entirely of being noticed, remaining still, and withdrawing. That can be a successful introduction.

## If you are invited inside at their scale

Dress for prolonged contact with cold stone, not simply for cold air.

You would want light, close-fitting insulation, flexible gloves, and soft, clean soles with good grip. Avoid loose loops, bulky packs, and stiff outerwear that brushes both sides of a passage. The streets continue through buildings; moving through town may mean passing very close to people’s domestic lives.

At reduced size, do not assume your usual tolerance for cold still applies. Depending on how your means of travel handles physiology, your body may lose heat much faster. Test those limits elsewhere. Carry your own compact shelter and supplies, and arrange an exit well before you become chilled or clumsy.

Do not bring an open flame. Do not assume a chemical warmer or powered heater is harmless merely because it is small. Heat here can thaw a water reserve, alter a planting bed, or change the moisture conditions around a wall.

Let a resident lead you. Ask where your feet may go.

The moss is not necessarily cushioning for visitors. It may be a garden, a sound-deadening surface, a wick, or part of the structure holding soil in place. A root may be a railing; another may be supporting a bank. The town has very little space that is merely incidental.

Step over water channels and wicks. Do not hold a door open by moving its pebble unless shown how. Do not steady yourself on a plant simply because it looks sturdy.

When a cart passes overhead, stop where your guide stops. Do not run for the entrance. Sudden movement through narrow passages may be more dangerous than waiting through the vibration.

## Be self-sufficient, but do not arrive as a supplier

Carry enough food and water for your visit. Do not rely on their hospitality to meet human needs, even after changing scale.

Do not bring casual gifts of crumbs, sugar, cotton wool, potting soil, or water. Each could introduce pests, microorganisms, fibers, or more moisture than their systems can accommodate. A breadcrumb might be useful food, an obstruction, or something that molds beside someone’s door.

Clean equipment matters more than expensive equipment. Remove soil, seeds, and insects before arrival; avoid fragrances and fresh chemical residues.

If exchange becomes possible, ask what is wanted. Their materials are selected and worked with considerable precision. “Stronger,” “softer,” or “more plentiful” does not necessarily mean better here.

Above all, resist the urge to improve the place. Do not widen the entrance, clear vegetation, straighten a gutter, thaw a spout, or repair the retaining wall without agreement. Their settlement is an accommodation among light, cold, water, roots, and overhead weight. You cannot identify a defect merely by noticing something inconvenient.

## Expect the visit to require stillness

There is little privacy in a town you can see almost in its entirety at once. Avoid treating that visibility as an invitation to watch every household. Do not probe with a camera or illuminate rooms with a flash. Ask before recording anyone.

You may also find the pace difficult. You will want to shift position, ask another question, help with a task, or move closer to see. Prepare to do less.

Their silence does not necessarily mean fear or solemnity. Their economical movements do not tell you whether they are welcoming, irritated, amused, or simply busy. Give them room to become people rather than an impression.

Before you leave, account for everything you brought. Take away every fiber and scrap you can retrieve without further disturbance. Withdraw slowly, without using the wall for leverage.

The right ambition is modest: that after your departure, the light falls where it fell before, the water follows its old paths, and no one has acquired an afternoon’s repair work merely because you wanted to visit.
