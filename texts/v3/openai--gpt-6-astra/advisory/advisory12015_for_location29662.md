Prepare as though you are visiting **a living machine whose designers did not necessarily include a place for you**.

The animals are not an infestation, and the machinery is not intruding on their habitat. The distinction appears not to exist here. A basin is both a component and a resting place; a shadow is both a consequence of construction and a complete ecological district. Even death has an assigned route.

Your greatest mistake would be to see the clean surfaces, steady lighting, and handrails and conclude that this is an unusually well-managed public space.

## Before you go

### Establish that the place can accommodate a human

Ordinary-looking daylight does not establish breathable air. Abundant animals establish only that the atmosphere supports *them*.

Send an unmanned probe if possible. Test atmospheric pressure, oxygen, carbon dioxide, airborne irritants, temperature, and humidity at the actual arrival point—not just somewhere on the plain. Conditions in an open upper walkway may differ substantially from those beside a warm reservoir or inside a vapor district. Verify gravity as well; the familiar scale of an animal or railing is not a reliable measurement.

The most consequential environmental problem may be quite mundane: **your body could have trouble losing heat**. The ground is warm, everything is wet, and the air appears still. Sweat is of little use when it cannot evaporate. A traveler walking for hours with a pack could become dangerously hot without ever encountering air that feels fiercely hot.

For a first reconnaissance, use an independently supported, cooled suit if environmental compatibility remains uncertain. Do not mistake an ordinary filter mask for protection against an unsuitable atmosphere.

Arrange a short visit with a fixed return time. Do not make your first trip an endurance walk toward a distant vapor bank.

### Seek an authorized route, not merely a walkable one

Maintenance markings suggest that something navigates and services this place. They do not establish that those users are human-sized, human-minded, or safe to accompany.

If contact with an operator is possible, ask:

- Which paths are intended for visitors?
- Which surfaces bear your weight?
- Are there cleaning, flushing, pressure-release, or replacement cycles?
- How are foreign objects and organisms handled?
- Is there a way to summon assistance or stop a local process?
- What can you bring in, and what must leave with you?

That fourth question matters especially. The machinery classifies things by size, demand, useful life, and recovery rate. You should not assume it will recognize “lost tourist” as a category.

There is no evidence that it would deliberately harm you. There is equally little evidence that its routines would make exceptions for you.

## What to bring

Your equipment should be quiet, secure, resistant to moisture, and difficult to shed.

**Footwear:** Enclosed boots with excellent wet-surface traction, toe protection, and soles that resist cuts. Smooth ceramic beneath a thin water film is a serious fall hazard. Avoid cleats or spikes that could damage the plates or the small animals occupying them.

**Clothing:** Close-fitting sleeves and trousers, with no trailing cords. In verified safe air, choose clothing that manages dampness and dries readily, rather than automatically wearing a heavy impermeable shell that traps body heat. Carry a dry insulating layer in a sealed bag for emergencies.

**Hand protection:** Well-fitting gloves with a reliable wet grip. Handrails are damp and vibrating, and their undersides may be occupied.

**Navigation:** An offline route recorder, an inertial backup or equivalent, a clock, and a clearly identifiable return beacon. Test whether your instruments function near the machinery. Do not assume a magnetic compass will be useful.

**Lighting:** A modest, adjustable task light for reading instruments and checking footing. You will not need to illuminate the plain. Avoid powerful lights directed into occupied recesses.

**Supplies:** All your own drinking water and food, packed in containers you can open without spilling crumbs or powder. Bring more than the planned trip requires, but keep the load light enough to limit exertion.

**Containment:** Sealed storage for rubbish, used medical supplies, dirty clothing, and human waste. This is not a place where “biodegradable” means harmless.

**Emergency equipment:** A first-aid kit, communication and locator devices, and whatever independent air or thermal protection the survey indicates. A recovery plan is more useful than a pack full of improvised survival tools. You should not be planning to live off this environment.

Bring a companion if possible. A fall, heat illness, or failed return device becomes much more serious when the nearest apparent shelter is a pressure housing.

## Moving through the plain

### Stay on confirmed access surfaces

The broad animals look like stepping-stones. They are not stepping-stones.

Neither are pipe covers, thin gratings, feeder cabinets, or the roofs of service housings necessarily safe footing. A shallow flooded basin might have occupied floors below it. Something that resembles solid ground may be a cover over an industrial void.

Move slowly enough to distinguish:

- a seam from a drain;
- an animal’s back from a dark surface;
- condensation from flowing water;
- a structural platform from a removable panel.

Do not step across drainage mouths merely because they are narrow. Their hydraulic behavior is unknown, and the surrounding relief may funnel a slipping foot toward them. Give recovery channels an especially wide berth.

Never enter an inspection shaft, reservoir access, or service duct without an authorized confined-space procedure. A view of depth marks and part numbers is not an invitation to descend.

### Treat shade as occupied habitat

Here, a shadow is not a temporary convenience. It can be the whole world of the animals living in it.

That makes several ordinary traveler behaviors intrusive: sitting under a bridge, pushing a pack into a pipe recess, reaching beneath a railing, or erecting a canopy across a feeding surface. Look carefully before you rest anything anywhere.

For breaks, use a verified visitor or maintenance platform. If none exists, that is a reason to shorten the visit—not to clear a patch of animals.

Even your own shadow may produce a response. Watch rather than immediately interpreting animals gathering near your boots as friendliness.

### Keep your hands to yourself

The broad animal beside the parapet may seem irresistibly touchable. It is warm, slow, and apparently untroubled. Its skin also supports an entire community you can barely see.

Do not pet, feed, lift, or reposition the animals. Soft-bodied does not mean harmless, and apparent tolerance does not establish compatibility with your skin oils, microbes, or weight.

The slender predators deserve distance, but they are not necessarily the chief hazard. Their mouths and hunting behavior appear specialized for local prey. It would be equally unjustified to assume that they hunt humans or that they cannot injure one. If an animal investigates you, stop crowding it and withdraw without cornering it.

Inspect equipment before putting it on. Adhesive-footed grazers could occupy a boot sole or pack seam without being obvious.

## Keep your biology separate from theirs

Do not drink the water.

Its clarity and precise level are properties of a managed fluid system, not evidence of potability. It may carry dissolved feedstocks, process chemicals, biological products, or organisms invisible to you. Familiar field filtration should not be assumed to make an unknown industrial nutrient fluid safe.

Do not taste the grazers, collect shed skins, or assume the mouth-fringe feeding patches are edible because animals thrive on them.

Just as importantly, avoid contributing anything:

- no food scraps;
- no soap or disinfectant in the channels;
- no urination into drains;
- no adhesive route markers on inhabited surfaces;
- no deliberate removal of animals or infrastructure.

Arrive with cleaned equipment, and arrange controlled inspection and cleaning after you return. Contain the resulting residues rather than washing them into another ecosystem.

The lack of dust and unplanned growth suggests extraordinary control, but it does not tell you how that control works. The system might harmlessly process an introduced microbe. It might react strongly. The responsible approach is not to test it.

## Protect your sense of time and position

The light does not move. The shadows do not lengthen. You cannot use the sun to find direction, judge the afternoon, or feel the day ending.

Set alarms for drinking, rest, turnaround, and departure. For an overnight stay, you would need a genuinely suitable sleeping area, darkness you can provide without altering an occupied habitat, and a deliberate sleep schedule. Do not sleep beneath machinery merely because its operation sounds constant.

The repeating plates and endless fullness will also make distance deceptive. You may feel that you have made little progress after walking a long way, or recognize a ridge pattern and mistakenly believe you have returned.

Record route junctions with images and coordinates. Distinguish levels as carefully as horizontal position: the bridge above your arrival point is not necessarily connected to it.

**Turn back on a time and energy budget, not when the scenery changes.** It may never change enough to satisfy you.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect intimacy without welcome or hostility.

There are breathing bodies everywhere, but few of the sounds a human associates with company. No calls across an open landscape, no obvious rush of activity at evening, no wind-driven shift in the light. The most constant presence may be the low vibration entering through your feet.

At first, the abundance may be comforting. Nothing seems hungry for lack of provision. Nothing lies neglected. Then the same completeness may become oppressive. There is no dry empty patch that plainly belongs to nobody, no abandoned corner where you can stop participating, no visible outside against which to orient yourself.

Prepare for disgust as well as wonder. A traveler who enjoys watching one translucent grazer may feel quite differently when a narrow passage reveals thousands of moist bodies on every surface. That response does not make the place dirty or the animals threatening. It does mean you should know your own tolerance before committing to a route beneath occupied decks.

Keep your normal voice low, secure rattling equipment, and avoid striking the railings. Touch and vibration matter to the inhabitants. Use an alarm if safety requires it, but do not shout merely to hear what the plain does with the sound.

Above all, resist assuming that a visit needs a great crossing or a dramatic discovery. You could spend an hour beside one basin and see feeding, shelter, predation, circulation, maintenance, and an entire small world on an animal’s back.

For an ordinary human, the best first visit is probably brief: arrive on an approved platform, confirm that your instruments agree with your comfort, walk a short marked circuit, observe without touching, and leave with everything you brought.

**Your goal is not to find somewhere empty. It is to learn how to be present without making an occupied place yield to you.**
