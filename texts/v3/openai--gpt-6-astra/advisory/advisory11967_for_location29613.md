Prepare for this as you would for an active geological disaster occurring underwater—but with the additional possibility that distance, weight, and the direction of an exit may change while you are using them.

An ordinary human should not enter this basin in a diving suit. The water is only one of the things that could kill you, and it may not be the most important.

The safest visit would be a remote observation from another peak, with a return route that never touches the summit. If you want to enter the water, you need a vehicle or protective system whose performance has already been demonstrated in this particular environment. Ordinary deep-sea equipment is not enough merely because it can withstand pressure.

## Understand what you are approaching

The basin is not a lake held in an unusual container. It appears to be a meeting place for several environments that do not fully agree about how matter should behave.

That distinction matters. In a normal lake, you can make useful assumptions: upward leads toward air, currents pass around solid objects, a nearby shore remains nearby. Here, each of those assumptions has visible exceptions.

The apparent depth does not tell you the pressure. Warm water does not imply tolerable chemistry. A pocket of stillness does not imply safety. A wall does not necessarily separate two neighboring spaces.

Most importantly, **the summit’s moments of apparent normality are not evidence that it has become safe**. They are part of its cycle.

## Choose a visit you can survive

There are three substantially different expeditions available here:

- **Look at the basin from a distance.** Potentially feasible for an ordinary traveler with good mountain equipment and a cautious guide.
- **Approach the dry side of the rim.** An extreme undertaking. The route is exposed to unstable rock, airborne debris, ambiguous gravity, and possibly movements of the water boundary.
- **Enter the basin.** Not an ordinary-human excursion. It requires protection against conditions that first have to be measured.

Do not let the basin’s small width collapse these distinctions. A village-sized footprint is not a village-sized hazard.

If your multiverse-travel equipment allows you to arrive at arbitrary coordinates, do not select the basin floor as an arrival point. Send a probe first. Confirm that the arrival volume is empty, that it stays empty throughout several disturbances, and that your departure mechanism works from there. A return device that depends on stable coordinates, ordinary elapsed time, or a clear path may fail precisely when you need it.

## Establish your base off this mountain

The summit is continually breaking open. That makes it a poor place to keep your shelter, spare equipment, companions, or return anchor.

Choose a distant, geologically separate observation site. Even there, watch for ejecta and anomalous movement during the basin’s surges before committing to camp. Do not put the camp directly beneath the basin merely because the overhanging water has not spilled.

Bring normal high-altitude protection:

- Insulation and weatherproof layers.
- Appropriate oxygen support if the altitude requires it.
- Eye protection against grit and stone fragments.
- Independent navigation and communication systems.
- A medical kit and a realistic evacuation plan.

The warm water will be misleading. You can be overheated or burned inside the basin and emerge into freezing wind, soaked and injured, above miles of empty air.

Leave an observer outside the immediate environment. Their job is not to attempt a heroic rescue. Their job is to recognize that something has gone wrong, operate whatever remote extraction system you have, and avoid becoming the next casualty.

## Watch a complete disturbance before approaching

Preferably, watch many.

Record the ceiling’s descent, the fractures’ whistles, the upward movement of debris, the changes in the hollow, and the rearrangement of the floor afterward. Use synchronized cameras from separated viewpoints, pressure and temperature sensors, accelerometers, and multiple independent clocks.

You are looking for practical answers:

- How far beyond the rim do the disturbances extend?
- Does the water boundary move?
- What happens to equipment during a reversal?
- Do objects and their signals return together?
- Does an apparent route remain traversable through the entire cycle?

The description suggests warning signs: the light bows downward, cracks begin to whistle, and the central pebble lifts. Treat these as reasons to retreat, not as a dependable countdown. You do not know whether every event gives the same warning, or whether the warning reaches you before the event does.

Repeated survival by a probe would be encouraging. It would not establish a safe schedule.

## If you enter, remain inside a rigid enclosure

A wetsuit protects against cold and minor abrasion. It does not protect against a concussion transmitted through water, a flying block of basalt, or a gravity reversal.

A flexible diving suit leaves your body exposed to nearly all the basin’s distinctive hazards. Sharp edges can cut hoses and seals. Fine rock flour can destroy mechanisms. Ordinary buoyancy control becomes unreliable if “up” changes or leads toward a boundary that is not a normal surface.

A plausible entry vehicle would need:

- A rigid, pressure-rated hull with protected seals.
- Impact shielding and internal restraints.
- Independent breathing gas, power, thermal control, and communications.
- Sensors for pressure, temperature, chemistry, acceleration, and hull strain.
- Redundant ways to withdraw that do not all depend on the same current, tether, or coordinate system.

Even that is a starting point, not a guarantee. No ordinary engineering specification covers a hollow that becomes farther away than the basin is wide.

Do not assume an anchor makes you secure. The anchor rock may split, lift, or become part of a changing geometry. A tether can turn into a cutting wire or pull your enclosure against a sharp corner. If a tether is used, it needs monitoring and an emergency release; it cannot be your only way home.

## Avoid the places that look most inviting

**The rim:** It is not a shoreline. The orbiting stones mark a region where small objects already fail to leave predictably. Stay away from that region rather than testing it with a boot.

**The still patch beneath the rocking slab:** It offers relief from abrasion, but the suspended grains suggest that its stillness is not simply shelter from a current. Entering it might stop you from moving—or expose you to a boundary you cannot cross back through. The slab itself is an overhead crush hazard.

**The three low walls:** They are evidence of something, not necessarily hospitality. The opening that accepts water from both sides without releasing it is especially unsuitable as shelter. The tightening stones and escaping powder show that the structure is still undergoing forceful change. Do not put a hand, line, camera boom, or body into that opening.

**The deepest hollow:** Do not approach when it appears shallow, either. Its harmless state and its impossible state occupy the same place. A retreat measured in a few meters could cease to be a few meters during your retreat.

**The silver interfaces:** Treat contact between incompatible streams as an explosive boundary. The milky fragments are attractive specimens, but collecting them would require entering a place where pressure events are being generated.

The surviving plants and animals do not establish human-safe routes. They may tolerate conditions you cannot, perceive changes you cannot detect, or simply be the few survivors of constant injury. The pale animal’s scars are more useful information than its apparent ease of movement.

## Protect your senses and your judgment

The sound will be exhausting before it becomes informative.

Ordinary hearing protection may reduce airborne noise on the approach, but it will not solve vibration transmitted through water, hull, and bone. Inside, expect communication difficulties, disorientation, nausea, and an inability to tell whether a heavy impact was external or structural.

Use visual and tactile alarms. Agree on simple commands before departure. Do not plan an operation that requires someone to shout a complicated instruction during a surge.

Expect your eyes to be unreliable, too. Some distortions are thermal; others persist after the heat has passed. A ledge that appears to move might be refracted, physically displaced, or no longer at its previous distance. You should not have to decide which in time to avoid it.

Keep the visit short. The continuous violence will encourage hurried choices, while the brief intervals of clarity will encourage you to stay “just a little longer.” Both impulses are dangerous.

## Set your departure conditions in advance

Leave on the first unexplained change in your equipment’s behavior—not after you have diagnosed it.

Abort for loss of an independent return system, unexpected pressure or acceleration, changing separation from fixed markers, persistent disagreement between instruments, or disturbance beyond the range previously observed. If the extraction route itself begins to behave strangely, the expedition is already overdue to leave.

Carry no loose equipment. Take no souvenirs on a first visit. Do not taste the water to identify currents. Keep any later samples sealed and isolated; material taken across a boundary may not remain inert under different conditions.

Above all, prepare to come home without entering.

From a distant ridge, you may still see clouds tear against an upright sea, an avalanche rise along the mountain’s flank, and daylight descend into the water like a vast moving ceiling. That is a real visit. You do not need to reach the trembling pebble to have encountered this place.

The basin gives the impression of something small enough to comprehend if you can just get closer. Your most important preparation is to recognize that impression—and refuse to let it choose your distance.
