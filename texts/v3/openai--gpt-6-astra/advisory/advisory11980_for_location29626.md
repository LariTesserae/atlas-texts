Prepare for this city as you would for a high-altitude expedition on a mountain whose geography changes while you are standing on it. Then make one further adjustment: shelter, distance, and elevation are not entirely dependable categories here.

You do not need to assume the city is hostile. You do need to stop assuming that something which accommodates a human body was made to keep that body safe.

## Before you arrive

An ordinary human’s first visit should be short, self-sufficient, and confined to one district during the brighter hours.

Choose an old district on a broad summit, with worn, continuous streets and several open spaces. Avoid the narrow ridge with the half-erased houses, the youngest architectural growths, and any arrival point that puts a doorway between you and your means of leaving.

If your method of travel permits it, arrange departure independently of the local terrain. A return mechanism that requires you to find exactly the same room, doorway, or patch of paving is a poor match for this place. Carry the means of departure on your person, test it promptly, and understand its limitations before exploring.

If you cannot leave without retracing your route, your first excursion should be little more than an inspection of the arrival site.

Bring a companion if possible. Thin air, cold, fatigue, and prolonged visual uncertainty all impair judgment. A second person can notice that you have begun treating an apparent balcony across a gulf as though it were the next paving stone.

Agree on simple rules beforehand:

- Either person can call for retreat.
- Nobody rounds a corner alone.
- No route is taken solely because it looks short.
- An unexplained change is a reason to stop, not to hurry through.

## Your body is the first limiting factor

The atmosphere is thin, but its composition is not specified. Do not assume that “air” means a breathable mixture at a survivable pressure.

Ideally, send environmental instruments ahead of you. Measure pressure, oxygen availability, temperature, and airborne particles. Arrive with a breathing system rated for the conditions; if those conditions are unknown, a dust mask or a small oxygen bottle is not enough preparation. Sufficiently low pressure would require more substantial protection.

If the air proves breathable, you will still need to account for altitude. Acclimatize where possible. Keep the initial workload low. Headache, nausea, clumsiness, unusual fatigue, and confused thinking are not things to push through here. They compromise exactly the abilities the terrain demands.

Dress for continuous dry cold and intermittent exposure:

- Insulating layers beneath a windproof outer shell.
- Gloves that preserve dexterity while preventing bare contact with stone.
- Boots with reliable friction and enough flexibility to feel changes underfoot.
- Eye protection against fine mineral dust.
- A helmet for low passages, falls, and shedding stone.
- A compact insulated pad so that resting does not mean surrendering body heat to the mountain.

Carry all your water and food, plus a reserve for a delayed exit. There is no demonstrated local water source. A hollow is not a cistern merely because it resembles one, and there is no reason to expect snow higher up.

The warm courtyards may reduce discomfort. They are not a substitute for insulation.

## Do not let the city teach you distance by falling

The currents that bring another district visually close are among the clearest dangers in the description. They exploit an ordinary, useful human habit: moving toward something that appears reachable.

Here, reachability must be established by a continuous physical route.

Before advancing, identify the ground between your feet and your destination. If you cannot account for all of it, you do not have a route. A nearby-looking threshold on the far side of open air is still kilometers away.

Carry a sturdy probing pole. Use it to check edges, shallow steps, and dusty surfaces while keeping your weight over ground you have already tested. Do not lean out to prove that something is near.

Instruments are useful, but they are not exempt from the environment. Optical ranging may be affected by the same currents that distort your view. Barometric altitude may not correspond usefully to whatever “height” is doing here. Use several observations together rather than allowing a reassuring number to overrule a visible gulf.

Photographs and route notes can help you recognize changes. Treat them as records of what was true at a particular time, not permanent maps.

Above all, distinguish an opening from a passage. In the eroding district, a perfectly recognizable front door opens onto the absence of the rest of its house.

## Learn the passage of height before traveling far

The dimmest hour is not simply nighttime. It is a recurring terrain event.

A low step can become a high one. A courtyard can pass upward through the houses around it. A neighborhood can lean while its stones remain in place. Ordinary mountaineering instincts will help with balance and exposure, but they cannot fully explain what your supports are doing.

For the first visit, plan to leave well before this begins. Do not schedule departure at the last plausible moment.

On later visits, observe a complete cycle from a comparatively stable, open location before attempting to move through one. Watch fixed features at several scales: the paving beside you, a threshold across the square, a roofline farther away. Notice when the animals emerge and when their fringes lift.

Their behavior is valuable warning, not a route guide. They are light, flat, six-legged, and equipped with hooked feet. A creature that crosses a wall to avoid the middle of a square has not demonstrated a human escape route.

The safest streets follow the grain of old wear. Prefer those routes, especially where they are broad and offer room to stop. Avoid narrow doorways, overhead lintels, steep transitions, and places where a change in relative height could trap a limb or put stone against your chest.

If a passage begins while you are moving, resist the urge to sprint toward apparent stability. Stop on the broadest continuous footing available, away from edges and constrictions, and reassess.

A rope is not a universal answer. The stone is porous, anchors may be unreliable, and a line connecting points affected differently by the moving height could become a hazard. Use technical fall protection only where its supports and behavior have actually been assessed.

## Inspect shelter as terrain, not as hospitality

The rooms will be tempting. They offer recognizable proportions, benches, sleeping hollows, and relief from the wind. They may look more suitable for resting than any campsite you could construct.

That resemblance deserves caution.

Architecture here is a mineral growth form. A recess does not have to possess a human purpose to resemble a bed. An arcade does not owe you a stable roof because it resembles a public walkway.

For a short rest, favor an old, shallow, broad opening with:

- An unobstructed view outside.
- A wide exit.
- No new cracks or active shedding.
- No conspicuous recent darkening along strained stone.
- Enough room to stand and turn without squeezing.

Avoid deep rooms and passages that barely admit you. The larger animal may reduce interiors as it passes. Whether it is literally feeding on space is uncertain; the practical consequence is not. A room can become less able to contain you.

If that animal appears nearby, withdraw early toward broad, open ground. Do not hide farther inside a house. Do not approach it, block its path, or assume that its slow gait means its effects are slow.

The retreat of the small creatures into cracks is a useful reason to look up from your feet and survey the ridge.

## Do not spend your first night indoors

During the dim hours, enclosed space becomes difficult to interpret. A room may seem to continue beyond its visible walls. Adjacent windows may show incompatible perspectives.

You do not need to decide whether these are optical effects, changes in connectivity, or something stranger before making a sensible decision: do not investigate them with your body.

Do not put an arm through an anomalous window. Do not follow a voice, a light, or your companion’s apparent outline through a space whose geometry no longer agrees with its enclosure. If the view changes, back away over the floor you have already traversed.

Bring dependable lamps, spare power, and a clock. Artificial light will help you see steps and cracks; it should not be assumed to restore the daytime behavior of a room.

If an overnight stay is unavoidable, a broad, old, partially sheltered open area is a better starting choice than a deep chamber—but it is still only a starting choice. Observe it, retain an exit, and keep watch in shifts. Do not pitch a tightly anchored shelter across several features that may change their relationship during the passage of height.

## Treat the dust as both a hazard and a warning

Fine mineral powder is a respiratory and eye hazard even before considering its unusual behavior. Avoid stirring it up. Keep food, water openings, breathing equipment, and mechanisms covered. Clean seals and moving parts before relying on them.

Dust moving away from a descending foot may help reveal local behavior, but it does not certify the surface beneath it.

The horizontal seams below the city are not ledges. They are suspended material marking old heights. Nothing in the description suggests that they can carry your weight.

Likewise, the dark film is not an emergency food source. It is unfamiliar life, and its association with strain makes it more useful as a warning sign than a resource. Do not scrape it off for curiosity’s sake. There is very little living cover here; a small disturbance may be substantial on its scale.

## Keep a margin for getting frightened

This place is likely to exhaust you before it feels conventionally dangerous.

There is almost no ordinary background sound. Wind presses rather than whistles. Knocks arrive through your teeth, and echoes take minutes to cross the city. Views cannot always be reconciled. The architecture offers familiar forms without familiar assurances.

Expect vertigo, misplaced urgency, and the uncomfortable feeling that an empty doorway is attending to you. You do not have to dismiss those experiences or adopt them as facts. Describe what changed in concrete terms to your companion: “That window no longer shows the same ridge,” rather than “The house wants us inside.”

Rest before you become desperate for shelter. Eat before you become shaky. Turn around while retracing your route still seems disappointingly easy.

## The square is a place to pause, not a guarantee

The broad summit square sounds like the best place to experience the city without constantly moving through it. There is warmth in the hollow, visibility across the districts, and a brief interval when nearby things remain still.

Visit it if you have a verified route. Sit on your insulating pad. Keep your pack and departure equipment beside you, not across the square. Watch the distant windows turn toward the light.

But the final shallow line matters. The square’s long history of stability does not prevent it from acquiring a step.

When that line appears, stand up and reassess the ground.

The right preparation is not to make yourself fearless. It is to arrive supplied, rested, and free to leave, so that the city’s beauty never has to persuade you that a risk is necessary.
