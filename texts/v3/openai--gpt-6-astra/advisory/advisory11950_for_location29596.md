Prepare for a working city whose chief public service is making reality dependable enough for people to go to work.

You do not need expedition armor to visit its inhabited shelves. People raise children here, carry groceries, take trams, and keep gardens. But their ordinary life rests on expertise you will not possess when you arrive. A harmless-looking patch of pavement may deserve more caution than a loud factory. An apartment block being lifted on jacks may be perfectly safe to sleep in; a beautiful, silent ridge may not be safe to look for a shortcut across.

Your most important equipment will be good boots, a current local map, and the willingness to believe a maintenance worker over your own instruments.

## Before you arrive

Arrange to arrive at an established passenger terminal during staffed hours, preferably early in the local day. The city’s light will not give you an intuitive sense of time, and reaching an unfamiliar lodging across five shelves after the trams have thinned out would be an unpleasant introduction.

Book your first nights in an established residential district with:

- A staffed lodging house or hotel.
- A tram stop close to the entrance that is *currently in use*.
- A maintained pedestrian route to food and drinking water.
- Recent foundation and heat inspections.
- No active acoustic exclusion zone immediately outside.

Ask for directions that include the shelf, building, entrance level, and a recognizable maintained landmark. “Ground floor” is not a sufficient address here. Neither is a photograph of a doorway: the doorway may still exist after it has stopped being an entrance.

If your multiverse transit equipment needs a stable surface, fixed coordinates, or a consistent gravitational reference, confirm compatibility before bringing it. Do not assume that a device capable of crossing universes will understand rails that rise up a stone face while remaining instrumentally level. Use an established arrival and departure facility rather than planting a private return anchor in fresh rock.

For a short visit, avoid scheduling an onward connection immediately after your departure from the city. Leave room for a bridge adjustment, a tram diversion, or a departure platform becoming temporarily inconvenient to reach.

## Dress for cold metal, sharp stone, and intermittent wet

The climate sounds more persistently chilling than lethally cold. You will spend time standing at exposed tram shelters, crossing long mineral intervals, and moving between cold streets and unexpectedly warm interiors.

Bring:

- **Broken-in boots with grippy soles and good resistance to cuts.** Fine porcelain-like fragments will be hard on thin soles, exposed ankles, and soft luggage.
- **Warm layers and a wind-resistant, waterproof outer shell.** Sideways rain makes a long umbrella less useful than a hood and well-fastened cuffs.
- **Gloves with enough dexterity to use railings and fasteners.** Bare hands on the city’s metalwork will become painful quickly.
- **Spare socks and a dry inner layer.** One wet stairwell may soak you more thoroughly than an hour outdoors.
- **A modest headlamp or torch, plus spare power.** Choose something useful for seeing steps without blinding tram drivers or maintenance crews.
- **Clear protective glasses, preferably with side protection.** Sealed goggles are sensible near dusty stonework.
- **A well-fitting particulate respirator** for mineral dust, particularly around cutting yards or dense moth gatherings.
- **A refillable water bottle, snacks, and ordinary personal medication.**
- **A small first-aid kit** with dressings suitable for sharp-edged cuts.

Choose a backpack over small-wheeled luggage. There are too many stairs, walkways, changes of level, and plausible pavement interruptions to trust little wheels.

Do not turn your outfit into a collection of dangling straps. Freight elevators, exposed machinery, cable bundles, and pipework are part of the streetscape. Tie back long hair and keep loose clothing secured near industrial work.

## Establish what “above the weather” means for your body

The view suggests substantial altitude, but it does not establish breathable pressure, oxygen content, or how this world’s atmosphere behaves. Check the actual conditions at your arrival terminal.

If the inhabited shelves are at human-significant altitude, spend the first day taking trams rather than proving you can walk everywhere. Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, or breathlessness at rest should not be dismissed as travel disorientation. Find out where the nearest clinic is and whether there is an established route to lower-pressure relief—or, more conventionally, to a lower elevation with higher air pressure.

Carry enough medication for delays. A city can manufacture extraordinary bearings without stocking your particular inhaler.

## Learn the city’s current version

Buy or obtain the map people are using *this week*. Ask someone to explain its maintenance markings, acoustic fences, temporary entrances, and footpath status.

A static map may tell you where a building belongs without telling you how to reach it today. A digital map may be no better if it confidently routes you through an old courtyard entrance or onto a path that no one has walked recently.

On your first morning, learn:

1. Your lodging’s usable exits.
2. The last tram home.
3. The local emergency signals.
4. Where potable water is available.
5. How temporary closures are marked.
6. Whether maintenance notices are posted by building, shelf, or transit line.
7. Which routes a pedestrian visitor should simply not use.

Take the tram for the long gaps. Half a mile is not far in itself, but repeated half-miles of bare, dim, exposed ground make a city much larger than its number of buildings suggests. A station visible across an interval may not be connected by a permitted straight-line route.

Do not walk along rails merely because you can see the next shelter. The northern avenue is the clearest warning the city offers: an instrument can report something correctly without making it walkable.

At first, let posted routes and practiced local behavior settle disagreements between your eyes, your map, and your balance.

## Treat maintenance as essential infrastructure

Survey marks are not decoration. Do not touch up faded paint, clean a needle, hang a bag from a reference bracket, or lean a bicycle against a delicate measuring installation.

The grazers licking blue marks does not make those marks unimportant. It means someone has another maintenance task.

Likewise:

- Do not cross under a building being adjusted unless the pedestrian route is explicitly open.
- Do not use freight elevators as improvised public lifts.
- Do not step over hoses or cables laid out by a work crew without asking.
- Do not interfere with bolts, collars, jacks, or apparently abandoned foundation fittings.
- Keep clear of the small yellow heating locomotives and their working areas.
- If a new crack or unexpected heat appears in your room, report it rather than investigating beneath the floor.

Learn what is routine before deciding what is an emergency. A measured fraction of movement during scheduled work may be ordinary. An unposted entrance suddenly binding shut is worth mentioning immediately.

The correct response to uncertainty is usually to ask the caretaker, conductor, surveyor, or municipal crew—not to test the surface yourself.

## Be particularly careful with the fresh stone

Do not collect souvenirs from cliffs, loose fragments, or storage yards.

The stone is not simply a building material with an unusual origin. Some of it still changes shape, some changes weight, and some affects the behavior of a nearby plumb line. Putting an attractive translucent chip in your pocket is an experiment involving your body, your luggage, and whatever universe you intend to return to.

If you want a stone object, buy finished work made from material locally certified as mature. Check your transit operator’s import rules as well.

Stay out of stone-storage racks. “Stored” does not necessarily mean “finished changing.”

Translucent pavement will be tempting. Looking through a crack at roots, foundations, and trapped tools may be one of the city’s most memorable experiences. Enjoy it from a maintained surface without widening the crack, probing it, or assuming visible depth tells you anything reliable about load-bearing strength.

### Moth dust deserves its own rule

The description explicitly says the dust continues to crystallize when wet. That makes ordinary casual responses—rubbing your eyes, licking a finger to wipe a lens, washing dust off your face without thinking—potentially consequential.

Before going out on your first evening, ask a local pharmacist or clinic for the established exposure procedure. If treatment supplies exist specifically for this dust, carry them.

In the meantime:

- Do not handle the moths.
- Avoid brushing against occupied lamp poles.
- Keep dusty gloves away from your face.
- Protect your eyes around disturbed moths or stone dust.
- Keep contaminated outer clothing away from your pillow.
- If dust enters an eye, do not rub it; obtain prompt local medical help and follow the established protocol.

Do not improvise chemistry in an injured eye. The interaction with water is precisely why you need local instructions rather than a guess.

## Drink from the water system, not the scenery

Water is abundant in certain places, but that does not make every wet place a drinking source.

Roof runoff feeds tanks; tanks feed laundries; laundries feed planted channels. The gardens are therefore part of a working water cycle, not evidence of a clean stream. Water beading on new stone may also contain things your body should not ingest.

Use designated potable taps or water supplied by your lodging. Carry enough for the long dry crossings.

Do not wash equipment in the planted channels, dam them for a photograph, or move an escape board out of your way. Those boards are how the small channel animals survive maintenance drainage.

The watered corridors will probably become your favorite places. They offer leaves, shelter, company, and a green smell after the mineral expanses. Remember that they may also be among the city’s most heavily used shared spaces. Leave room for residents carrying laundry, workers inspecting pipes, and creatures trying to get back under the ferns.

## Give the animals ordinary urban respect

The grazers are not necessarily tame because they sleep beneath infrastructure. Do not feed them, approach young animals tucked into ramps, or force a group into a narrow crossing. They are adapted to eating a mineral film; your trail food is not an obvious kindness.

Avoid lingering beside transformer backs or nesting ventilation openings. Birds erupting at a shift whistle could startle you badly on a narrow walkway. Ask about whistle times before choosing an exposed place to watch the factories.

The channel creatures should be treated as wildlife, not rescued preemptively from every drained basin. If you find stranded animals without an escape board, notify maintenance. They already have a method.

And do not take seeds, cuttings, feathers, or moths home without permission and appropriate biological clearance. The city’s plants and animals have intimate relationships with stone, heat, and water that your destination may not share.

## Take acoustic fences seriously without inventing an explanation

There are places where sound arrives before its apparent cause. The city has surveyed them, fenced them, and developed customs around them. That is enough reason to respect the boundary.

Do not enter to test whether a recording predicts your next sentence. Do not shout a friend’s name across a fence. Do not assume that hearing an impact tells you it has already happened—or that you know how long remains before it does.

People avoid speaking names there. Follow that practice whether its reason is physical, social, or something the description has not revealed.

If you hear an unexpected sound near an exclusion area, pause somewhere safe and consult signs or a local worker. Do not try to locate it by walking toward the apparent source. On damp evenings, give these places more margin, since the reported interval can lengthen.

A useful question at your lodging is: “How do I distinguish an emergency alarm from sound returning out of sequence?” Residents must have some practical accommodation for this, even if the answer is simply that certain routes are closed under certain conditions.

## Save the frontier for a guided day

Do not make the unfinished peaks your first excursion.

A path maintained by walking is infrastructure, even if it looks like nothing more than footprints. Its continued usefulness depends on a history of passage you cannot see. A familiar-looking shortcut may lack that history.

Go with a local guide who actually uses the route. A postal carrier may know it exceptionally well, but is also at work; arrange an excursion rather than attaching yourself to a delivery round.

Before leaving:

- Confirm that the route is currently used.
- Leave your itinerary and expected return with someone.
- Check local communication coverage.
- Carry extra water, food, warm clothing, and light.
- Agree on a turnaround point and on what conditions require returning.
- Ask whether retracing your steps is considered reliable on that particular route.

Do not venture onto a ridge merely because it appears broad from where you stand. Do not approach the soft-edged upper mountains to see where the detail stops. Birds avoid those places without testing them; you have less experience than the birds.

A patch of lichen is evidence the locals use in judging dependability, not a universal visitor’s safety certificate.

At a frontier house, ask before entering the garden, touching an adjustable tool, or listening at a ground pipe. These are homes and working observation sites. If you hear a train beneath a place with no tunnel, you have heard something interesting—not received an invitation to find it.

## Prepare for the city’s effect on your attention

You may find this place more tiring than a brighter, busier city.

Its distances are difficult to read. Its illumination gives you little reassurance about the hour. Sound may be delayed, displaced, or premature. Buildings have old entrances beneath newer ones. The landscape is changing, but usually too slowly for you to catch it in the act.

You will spend energy deciding what deserves concern.

Counter that with simple routines. Eat at regular times. Wear a watch. Get adequate sleep. Visit the same café or bakery twice. Learn one dependable route before adding five more. Take a day with no frontier excursion and no technical tour.

Choose a room with controllable lighting and ask about nearby shift whistles. Earplugs may help at night, provided you can still hear the building’s required alarms.

The emotional surprise may be how domestic it all becomes. A tram full of coats and baskets crosses ground that has not finished becoming ground. Someone complains about a heating connection. Someone has carefully placed a board so a small animal can climb out of a basin. The extraordinary is not absent; it has acquired schedules and trades.

Do not mistake that familiarity for invulnerability. But do allow it to reassure you. The city is not waiting for every visitor to solve its mountains. It has people who have spent their lives learning how to stay.

## A sensible first visit

For the first few days, keep your ambitions modest:

- **Arrival day:** reach your lodging, check local health and dust advice, find food and water, and learn the tram home.
- **First full day:** explore an established shelf, preferably with a resident or guide. Visit the watered corridors and observe a public maintenance operation from the designated area.
- **Next day:** travel between shelves in daylight, leaving ample time for diversions. If factory tours are available, use one rather than wandering into a yard.
- **Later:** consider an acoustic-site viewing point or a guided frontier walk, once you understand the relevant local rules.

Leave some time simply to sit behind a window near evening and watch the lit trams cross the bare intervals.

You have prepared properly when you can enjoy that view without needing to prove that the ground is ordinary—and without forgetting that, for the people carrying their shopping home across it, it is.
