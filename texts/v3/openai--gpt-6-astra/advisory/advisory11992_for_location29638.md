Prepare for this place as you would for an exposed industrial disaster at extreme altitude—not as you would for a mountain hike. There may be no eruption to announce danger, no storm front to watch, and no roar before a collapse. The mountain is being made under your feet, and the process has no reason to leave you a way back.

For an ordinary human, a visit should be brief, instrumented, and supported from somewhere that is not part of the mountain.

## Before you arrive

**Establish whether a human can survive the atmosphere at all.** The description suggests thin air, suspended abrasive particles, and hot mineral gases. It does not establish a breathable oxygen level, a tolerable pressure, or an Earthlike gas mixture. The metallic smell is not a useful safety test, and an absence of condensation does not mean the fumes are harmless.

Send instruments through first. Measure:

- Total pressure and oxygen partial pressure.
- Atmospheric composition, including gases emerging from cracks.
- Air temperature, surface temperature, and radiant heat.
- Particle concentration and how quickly exposed equipment becomes abraded or clogged.
- Gravity, if this has not already been established.
- Ground movement over time, rather than in a single reassuring snapshot.

A reconnaissance probe should observe several candidate arrival areas and their approaches. A flat slab that remains motionless for five minutes is not thereby stable. You want to know whether it belongs to a larger moving plate, whether fractures are propagating toward it, and whether something above it can fall.

If you cannot establish tolerable conditions or bring equipment that makes them tolerable, visit remotely. There is nothing about being a multiverse traveler that makes an ordinary human immune to decompression, poisoning, or a collapsing ledge.

## Make your exit independent of the terrain

This is the most important preparation.

Do not rely on walking back along your arrival route. Shelves close, passages open, and recognizable landmarks change shape. A route can disappear without becoming visibly more difficult first.

Ideally, your return system should travel with you and function without a fixed foundation. If your method requires a stationary gate, vehicle, or extraction platform, establish it on support independent of the moving rock, if your technology permits. Keep someone outside the site monitoring it.

Before departure, answer three questions:

1. Can you leave if the ground beneath you breaks?
2. Can you leave if grit makes visibility nearly zero?
3. Can you leave if you become confused, unconscious, or unable to operate your equipment?

A system that needs you to stand still on a particular slab and complete a long sequence is poorly suited to this destination.

Carry an independently powered emergency beacon and agree on automatic extraction criteria. Loss of communication should trigger a response, not an indefinite wait to see whether you call again.

If no terrain-independent exit is possible, the sensible human visit is a short observation from a verified arrival position—not an expedition toward the towers.

## Dress for hot rock, cold exposure, and cutting dust

The contradictory temperatures matter. Your feet may be overheating while your hands are becoming too cold to work a buckle. A warm terrace is not evidence of warm air.

Your protective system should include:

- **Atmosphere-independent breathing equipment**, with reserve capacity for a delayed exit. An ordinary dust mask or climbing oxygen setup is not an adequate substitute for protection against an unknown atmosphere.
- **Pressure protection if measurements require it.**
- **A sealed helmet or equivalent full-face protection**, with replaceable outer visor protection. Sharp airborne grit can turn a clear visor into a scratched, light-scattering screen.
- **Layered thermal protection** suitable for cold ambient conditions and short exposures to hot surfaces and radiant heat.
- **Cut-resistant gloves and outer clothing**, compatible with any pressure or chemical protection beneath them.
- **Insulated, heat-resistant footwear with puncture-resistant soles.** The “gravel” is freshly fractured crystal, not rounded trail aggregate.
- **Protected joints, seals, cables, and closures.** Fine mineral particles entering a mechanism may become a failure long before the rest of the equipment looks worn.

Do not use your hand to judge whether a crack is safe. Do not kneel on apparently cool stone without checking it. Avoid letting hoses or tethers drag across sharp edges or hot seams.

Bring your own water, food if needed, and power. Nothing described here provides a dependable human resource. There is no reason to expect drinkable water, edible growth, or material that can safely substitute for equipment.

## Bring light—and do not confuse it with knowledge

You need multiple independent lights, including a broad beam for nearby footing and a stronger directional beam for examining routes. Position them so that suspended dust does not make every illuminated view a wall of glare.

Thermal imaging would help identify hot seams, recent exposure, and gas currents. Rangefinding and local mapping would help you understand drops and overhangs. Neither can certify that a slab will remain attached.

A drone may be useful, but only one suited to the measured atmosphere; ordinary rotors may perform badly in thin air. A tethered probe can inspect a recess, but its cable may snag or be cut by moving rock. Treat reconnaissance tools as expendable. Do not follow a damaged instrument into danger to recover it.

Maps should be time-stamped. Here, a good map can become a record of terrain that no longer exists.

## Move as though every attractive feature has a hidden cost

The clean surfaces are not reassuring. They are clean because they are new or continually swept.

Avoid:

- Projecting shelves, especially fresh ones.
- Narrow fins and the courts between them.
- Fracture edges, even when the gap looks easy to step across.
- Undercut walls and places beneath precarious blocks.
- Gas outlets and sheltered hollows where the atmosphere has not been tested.
- Any route requiring a particular bridge of stone to remain intact.

The basin of turning plates is an observation target, not a crossing.

Where walking is justified, favor broad areas set well back from edges, with several unobstructed escape directions. Reassess continuously. A slowly changing skyline is ground motion, not an optical curiosity. A new whistle, a stopped whistle, or a vibration through the soles is a reason to check conditions and retreat early.

**Do not wait for a loud warning.** The description repeatedly makes clear that immense failures can be nearly silent. Your eyes, instruments, and contact with the ground matter more than your ears.

Ropes are not automatically protective here. A rope attached to a failing fin simply connects you to the failure. Conventional climbing anchors require rock whose integrity can actually be assessed. Likewise, tying travelers together can turn one local collapse into several casualties.

## Do not plan to sleep in the enclosures

The shelters are the most psychologically dangerous feature in the landscape.

They suggest that someone once understood this place well enough to rest here. That may be true—but the structures appear to have been carried upward from somewhere else. Their survival says little about present conditions or the needs of their former occupants.

One has already split. Another hangs over a void. The square one is not necessarily safe merely because it has not yet visibly failed.

Treat them as archaeological objects, not accommodation. Do not lean on their walls, move the wedges, or disturb the remaining row of stones. Photograph them from a safe position if you can. Their arrangement may be the only surviving evidence of an intention you do not understand.

Do not schedule an overnight stay on this saddle. Sleep removes exactly the vigilance the terrain demands.

## Leave the dark growth alone

The film beneath the shelters is the only visible life, and its apparent simplicity is not evidence that it is harmless.

Avoid touching, scraping, or walking over it. Do not assume that something resembling soot can be brushed off your suit. Sampling, if there is a genuine reason to do it, should use sealed remote tools and a containment plan that also protects the organism from you.

Its patches may represent years of growth. A careless glove could destroy more history than a dramatic rockfall reveals.

On return, isolate and clean equipment before bringing it into inhabited areas. The same precautions help contain unknown biological material and remove chemically active or abrasive mineral dust.

## Prepare for what the place will do to your judgment

The silence will be deceptive. Humans often associate quiet with an interval of safety. Here, quiet is simply how thin air and vast distances present ongoing destruction.

You may also feel a strong urge to keep moving toward the next opening: another ridge appears through the grit, a fallen fin reveals a basin, a fresh shelf offers a better view. Each change seems to invite one more step.

Set your visit limits before arrival. Use conservative turnaround times, breathing-supply reserves, and explicit triggers for leaving. Do not negotiate with those limits because the view has just become extraordinary.

Expect fatigue, isolation, and difficulty judging scale. There are no trees, birds, familiar paths, or reliable horizons to make distance intuitive. A slab the size of a house may look like a stepping stone until your instruments correct you.

The right ambition is to witness a little and return intact.

And when you see that perfectly clean shelf with one small stone resting near its edge, do not take the stone as proof that the shelf can hold you. It proves only that, for the moment you are watching, it holds a stone.
