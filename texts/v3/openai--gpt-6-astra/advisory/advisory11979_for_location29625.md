Prepare for a place where **a few centimeters of height matter more than a thick wall**, where an apparently empty field may be part of the town’s machinery, and where the greatest hazard you bring could be something alive in the seams of your luggage.

This does not look like an intrinsically hostile destination. People have made a durable life here. But their comfort depends on close attention to water, ground, and maintenance. A good visitor should arrive ready to learn those distinctions rather than equipped to ignore them.

## Before crossing: find out what you are allowed to bring

The most consequential feature is the absence of visible animal life other than people. There are plants and fungi, but no birds, fish, grazing animals, or insects disturbing the pools.

That observation does not establish whether microscopic animal life, parasites, or pathogens exist here. Nor does it explain the absence. Nevertheless, it warrants unusually careful biological precautions.

If there is any established contact with this world, use its quarantine arrangements. Ask specifically about food, clothing, footwear, waste, and return travel.

- Bring no pets, live bait, seeds, untreated timber, or soil.
- Clean and inspect boots, bags, tent seams, hook-and-loop fasteners, and any equipment previously used outdoors.
- Avoid packing fresh produce, loose grain, or other material that could carry viable organisms unless expressly approved.
- Use approved, sealed provisions for the initial visit.
- Arrange how you will contain or dispose of food scraps and human waste before you need to do either.

Do not assume that cleaning your equipment makes you biologically harmless. An ordinary person carries a whole microbial community. If contact between worlds is new, that is a problem for an actual biosecurity protocol, not something solved by wiping your boots.

**Do not “improve” this place by introducing fish, bees, earthworms, or livestock.** Its agriculture and waterworks function without the animals you might expect. An absence is not an invitation.

## Dress for dampness, not for a swamp expedition

Most walking will apparently be on firm roads, paving, and raised paths. You do not need cumbersome waders as your everyday footwear. You do need shoes that grip wet stone, protect against sharp volcanic fragments, and remain comfortable through long, damp days.

Pack:

- Well-fitting walking shoes or boots with good tread.
- Several pairs of quick-drying socks.
- Lightweight layers, including a warmer evening layer.
- A breathable rain shell.
- A brimmed hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen.
- A waterproof liner for your pack and separate protection for sleeping clothes.
- A small towel and basic blister supplies.

The mild air is potentially misleading. Sitting against sun-warmed stone may feel almost summery; standing in shade above saturated ground may steadily chill you. Dress so you can adjust between those conditions.

Make **one completely dry sleeping set** a priority. Clothing eventually dries beneath an eave, but the description gives a useful warning: fabric resting against a wall stays wet. Hang things with air around them. Keep bedding, papers, medicines, and electronics off floors and away from masonry.

The elevation is unspecified. Learn it before arrival if possible. If it is high enough to require acclimatization, spend the first days on easy walks within the basin rather than climbing to the quarry overlook. Strong light does not mean warm air, and mild weather does not prevent altitude illness.

## Learn which ground is meant to carry you

The town’s empty spaces are not necessarily vacant land.

A green strip between neighborhoods may receive overflow. A shallow depression may be a settling area. A low wall may be keeping cultivated soil out of a drain rather than keeping people out of a garden.

On arrival, ask someone to show you:

1. The routes used during wet weather.
2. The drinking-water collection points.
3. The drains and channels visitors must not touch.
4. The nearest safe higher ground.
5. The proper washing and sanitation arrangements.

Stay on established paths until you understand the terrain. Firm volcanic gravel can lie beside black, saturated soil with almost no obvious boundary. Clear water can conceal an awkward step, a slippery slab, or an opening into a drain.

Do not lift fitted stones out of curiosity. Do not turn a sluice to see what happens. Do not park a bag in a channel because it happens to be dry.

The waterworks are infrastructure at household scale: their controls are simple enough for a stranger to move and important enough for that movement to matter.

If you offer to help, begin under supervision. Carrying baskets or moving material where directed is more useful than attempting an independent drainage improvement.

## Treat clear water as untreated water

There is water everywhere, but that does not establish potability.

These channels connect gardens, soils, buildings, and settled neighborhoods. The absence of fish or insects says little about microbiological safety. A spring may also contain dissolved minerals that neither clarity nor boiling will reveal.

Bring an initial water supply and a treatment method appropriate to whatever testing or established guidance is available. In familiar conditions, filtration combined with suitable disinfection can address many biological hazards; it does not make arbitrary interworld water safe, and boiling does not remove dissolved chemical contaminants.

The best starting point is the town’s designated drinking source, not the nearest attractive seep.

Keep soap, detergents, food washings, and bodily waste out of the channels. The high groundwater makes casual burial of waste particularly unsuitable. Use the local sanitation system if it is appropriate for visitors; otherwise follow the containment plan agreed before arrival.

## Expect plant-based meals, but verify the food

The cultivated landscape suggests meals centered on grains or grain-like seeds, leafy vegetables, stalk crops, and tubers. You should not expect meat, fish, milk, eggs, or animal-derived travel supplies to be readily available.

That need not mean poor food. The gardens look intensively managed, and the public grinding places and raised storehouses suggest established systems of processing and storage. But familiar-looking plants are not necessarily edible to a visitor from another world.

For an initial stay:

- Carry enough approved food to avoid needing to experiment while hungry.
- Establish whether local staples are compatible with your biology.
- Learn how foods are prepared before tasting them.
- Do not assume a crop eaten cooked can safely be eaten raw.

For a longer stay, check nutritional adequacy rather than assuming the residents’ diet will meet all your needs. Bring necessary medicines and supplements; do not expect local substitutes to be equivalent.

## Choose lodging by its drainage

The best room may not be the grandest-looking one. Look for a maintained roof, broad eaves, a raised floor or sleeping platform, dry bedding, and an unobstructed courtyard drain.

Ask where water reaches during exceptional rain. The nearly constant waterline describes ordinary conditions, not necessarily the worst ones.

Camping should be a fallback, not the default. An inviting flat patch may be a water route, a cultivated margin, or ground that becomes wet from below. If you must camp, obtain permission and local advice on the site. Avoid blocking paths or pressing stakes through managed beds and concealed drains.

A small headlamp is useful after dark. Aim it down: the footing matters more than illuminating the whole neighborhood.

## Do not confuse young terrain with safe terrain—or active catastrophe

The sharp escarpments, fresh-looking fractures, shallow stream courses, and preserved lava surfaces suggest a geologically young landscape. They do not, by themselves, prove an imminent eruption.

Ask about earthquakes, rockfalls, unusual spring temperatures, gas, and the history of the basin’s outlet. Local knowledge is more useful than deciding from appearances that the mountains are either dead or dangerous.

Until you know more:

- Stay clear of cliff bases and quarry working faces.
- Avoid scrambling over wet lava folds and abrupt lips.
- Do not enter caves, deep excavations, or enclosed low places casually.
- Turn back from unfamiliar mountain routes when cloud lowers.

The quarry overlook is an excursion, not the place to begin orientation.

## Prepare for the distances and the silence

The settlement is sparse, not small. A building plainly visible across the basin may still require a long walk along raised roads and bridges. Carry water, a light, and directions when visiting another neighborhood. Do not improvise a straight-line return across wet ground at dusk.

Sound will take adjustment. With no birdsong, insect noise, or ordinary animal movement, a dropped utensil or a conversation can occupy an entire court.

Speak normally, but pay attention to how residents use their voices. Avoid loudspeakers, unnecessary alarms, and noisy equipment. Do not assume that because nobody is nearby, nobody can hear you.

The quiet may initially feel peaceful and later become unsettling. Familiar signs of a living landscape are missing. Wind and moving water may seem strangely exposed. Bring a quiet means of occupying yourself, and allow time to stop interpreting silence as evidence that something is wrong.

Most importantly, do not read the broad empty spaces as abandonment. Look at the fitted stones, the clean channels, the raised thresholds, and the exact placement of shelters. This is an inhabited landscape whose care is expressed more through gradients than monuments.

**Arrive clean, keep your bedding dry, stay on the raised paths, and ask before changing anything that carries water.** Once those habits become natural, visiting may be remarkably gentle: long walks between gardens, warmth held in dark stone, and evenings when you can hear a small sheet of water only after sitting down beside it.
