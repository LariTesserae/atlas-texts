Prepare to visit an inhabited climate, even if you never meet an inhabitant.

The empty rooms are not inactive rooms. The lanes are air ducts, the courts are heat exchangers, and the loose material on the ground is in transit. Your chief difficulty will be entering without becoming a blockage, a source of moisture, or a rigid object caught between surfaces that need to move.

For an ordinary human, the best first visit is from outside.

## Decide what “visiting” will mean

The description does not establish that a human body can enter. There is no reason to assume that the doorway will accommodate you, or that the place will adjust to your scale.

Bring a small, remotely operated inspection probe with a close-focus camera, temperature sensors, and a microphone. Ideally, use something that can observe from the existing opening without resting on the buildings or dragging a cable through the lanes. Even a fine cable could catch a mineral blade or brace a joint that normally slides.

Do not lift the tile for a better view.

The tile is not merely a lid. Its underside provides the cooler surface toward which the plants discharge heat. Lifting it could alter the thermal gradient, the air circulation, and the mechanical loading of the whole settlement at once. The roof might remain visibly intact while the conditions that sustain the city disappear.

If your means of multiverse travel includes reliable miniaturization, prepare for a second, much more demanding kind of visit. Do not assume that being smaller makes you harmless—or that it makes hot surfaces, airborne dust, and falling flakes harmless to you. Verify that your equipment and physiology remain functional at the intended scale.

## Observe several complete heat cycles

Do this before inserting anything farther than the entrance.

Record:

- The temperatures of the bedrock, tile, and incoming and outgoing air.
- Where dust moves, and when its movement reverses or stops.
- Which crowns open first.
- Which cracks widen during heating and close during cooling.
- The interval between the central knock and the next pulse.

The sound will tempt you to use it as a clock. Resist that temptation until you have watched enough cycles to know how variable they are.

The deep knock is a release of tension somewhere under the court. It is not necessarily an all-clear. Other parts of the city may still be expanding, and the settling afterward can dislodge material overhead.

Likewise, a quiet passage is not necessarily a safe one. It may be quiet because a root has obstructed it, because its joints are binding, or because it is between abrupt movements.

You want a route with **clearance throughout the cycle**, not merely one that is open when you arrive.

## Bring your own air—and keep it to yourself

There is no basis for assuming the hot cavity contains breathable air. The mineral plants take gases from it, but they are not foliage in any familiar physiological sense. Green does not mean oxygen-producing.

For bodily entry, use a sealed breathing system with contained exhaust rather than a mask that releases warm, damp breath into the settlement. Exposed skin, sweating, and an open drinking vessel all introduce water to a place described as entirely dry.

Water might do little, or it might alter dust cohesion, mineral surfaces, and old clay joins. You do not need to establish which by accident.

Your equipment should therefore include:

- Independent breathing gas and atmospheric monitoring.
- Thermal protection suited to measured conditions.
- Protected eyes and sealed joints against fine mineral dust.
- Contained drinking water and waste.
- A way to leave without relying on the city’s passages remaining unchanged.

Ordinary dust filters would not address oxygen deficiency or unknown gases. Nor will insulation alone keep you cool indefinitely. Your cooling system must put its waste heat somewhere; venting it into a court could disturb the very temperature difference supporting the plant beside you.

Keep the first entry short.

## Dress for a place that moves by fractions

Avoid loose fabric, exposed straps, dangling instruments, and protruding fasteners. The mineral crowns are layered structures, not soft greenery. Something that looks like a fern fringe may split, scrape, or catch equipment.

Use compact gear with a smooth exterior and compliant contact surfaces. Do not wedge a rigid case into a convenient gap. Do not brace yourself between floor and ceiling. Do not use a loose stone under a lintel as a handhold: its looseness may be its function.

The useful rule here is:

**Never secure yourself by preventing the city from moving.**

A seemingly solid support may also be hollowed by roots. A thick ledge may be deposited powder rather than masonry. Test your route by observation and sensing, not by putting your weight on it to see whether it holds.

If no load-bearing route can be identified without experimentation on the structures, remain outside.

## Treat empty space as infrastructure

The broad courts and long bare lanes are not invitations to camp.

A body in a return channel could redirect the current that carries minerals toward a trough. A bag placed beside a perforated wall could interrupt its ventilation. Footsteps through the central drift would mix material that the city has slowly sorted.

Do not brush away dust to reveal a pattern. The dust is part of the pattern.

Do not straighten a leaning partition, clear a root-choked passage, or feed a small plant with material from a larger drift. The system is uneven, but you do not yet know which irregularities are failures and which have become accommodations. An apparently helpful repair could lock two surfaces together and produce the next fracture.

Carry everything out. Leave no marker, adhesive, cord, or sensor behind unless you can establish that it will not obstruct movement or alter the local chemistry. Remote mapping is preferable to physical trail marks.

## Prepare for misleading senses

Inside, sound will be a poor guide to distance.

A sharp report may come from a nearby chamber or reach you through several connected passages. The low roof will return impacts. The large knock beneath the court may feel like something enormous approaching, although no approaching creature has been observed.

Use instruments to supplement hearing, and expect to be startled. A reflexive step backward or an upward jerk of the head could do more damage than the sound itself.

Heat will also be deceptive. A plant’s folded interior remains hotter than the surrounding space. A cool-looking shadow offers no guarantee of a safe surface. When the fans begin to open, give them room; do not reach between blades to inspect their copper undersides.

Bring low-heat lighting, used sparingly. Brighter light will improve your view, but it will not necessarily improve the plants’ conditions.

## Leave time to watch without understanding

You may feel that the builders have just stepped away. The thumb-shaped depressions, stone repairs, and carefully separated structures make absence unusually tangible.

But the sounds are not evidence that someone is answering you. Nor is the lack of bodies proof that you know what happened to the builders. Prepare to leave those questions unresolved.

A successful visit might consist of watching one pale fringe withdraw beneath the tile, hearing several cycles of ticking, and mapping a single current without disturbing it.

If you can safely reach the roofless enclosures, give the young three-bladed plant the same clearance you would give a much larger crown. Do not touch the red grains along its margins. Watch the split beneath it open with the warming floor.

That is probably the clearest lesson the city offers a visitor: what looks like damage may be an opening something lives by. Your preparation should let you notice the difference without forcing it to become obvious.
