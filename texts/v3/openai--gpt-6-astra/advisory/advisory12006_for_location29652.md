Prepare for this hollow as you would for a flooded industrial site whose walls, floor, and machinery are still operating—but have become parts of the same living thing.

The principal danger is not an attack. It is that the space you are occupying may cease to be available, slowly enough that you keep postponing the decision to leave.

## Before entering

**Find someone who knows its cycles, not merely its layout.** A map made yesterday might still show the central growth, the brick building, and the rails correctly while being dangerously wrong about the passages between them. A useful guide should know how to recognize the rising and falling phases, how long a person actually takes to reach the exit, and what happens when a cycle deviates from its usual pattern.

Do not equate “a few minutes across” with a short visit. You may spend hours placing your feet, negotiating low openings, and waiting for a route to become usable. Budget your water, daylight, and stamina for an afternoon of difficult movement rather than a stroll across a small garden.

If no experienced guide is available, make your first visit an observation session at the entrance. Stay on the outside of the constrictions and watch an entire cycle if conditions allow. Learn the sequence:

- Water rises in the crown.
- The floor becomes firmer and small passages widen.
- The brick building clicks.
- Water spills down the black channels and the overhead whistle begins.
- During the falling phase, roots relax into the paths.

**Plan to use the high-water, firmer-floor interval for departure—not as an invitation to go farther in.** A sound heard overhead is not, by itself, a reliable signal; whistles also occur at other times. You want the pattern of several signs.

Leave your itinerary with someone outside. Establish a return deadline, but also explain that an overdue visitor may be trapped by a closing route. Your contact should seek knowledgeable help rather than simply following you in.

## Dress for dampness, traction, and constriction

Wear close-fitting, lightweight clothing that covers your arms and legs. The air is warm, and a heavy waterproof outfit is likely to make you sweat without keeping you dry. Choose fabric that remains comfortable when wet.

Footwear deserves particular care. You need good traction on wet, polished roots, a secure fit, and enough flexibility to place a foot on an irregular surface. Test your soles near the entrance. A tread excellent on mud may be poor on smooth bark.

Useful equipment includes:

- A small, close-fitting pack without dangling straps.
- A headlamp and a protected backup light. Daylight overhead does not mean light in the passages.
- Thin, grippy gloves for brief contact with rough or wet surfaces.
- Drinking water brought from outside.
- Compact food in sealed packaging.
- Basic first-aid supplies and any personal medication.
- A whistle and a radio, tested before you depend on them.
- A watch and a waterproof notebook for recording cycle observations.
- A dry change of clothing left outside.

Keep pockets closed. Secure essential objects inside clothing or in zipped compartments. Anything dropped can be taken out of reach by the leaves, even while you are still deciding how to retrieve it.

Avoid long equipment lanyards and trailing ropes. The hollow offers innumerable places for a loop to catch, and some of those places are moving. Likewise, do not tether yourself to a living trunk or root.

A helmet is sensible where low machinery and branches threaten your head. It does not make a narrowing passage safe.

## Move as though every foothold is provisional

Use the old rail bed as your primary route. Its metal splints make it the most dependable corridor described, but “safest” does not mean permanently open or structurally sound.

Place your weight gradually. A root that looks substantial may depress beneath you and transmit pressure elsewhere. Keep your hands free enough to balance, but do not put fingers into seams, joints, or the narrow spaces around embedded machinery.

Walk in a small party, close enough to remain in visual contact. A quiet voice will not carry well. Agree on simple hand signals for stop, retreat, and help. Radios may work, but test them around the actual growth rather than assuming they will.

At every constriction, look back. Ask whether you could negotiate it while tired, wet, and carrying an injured companion. If you must remove your pack or turn your shoulders precisely to get through, you are already spending too much of your escape margin.

**Never squeeze through a gap that is actively narrowing.** The plants do not need to move quickly to exert more force than a human can resist.

Treat new softness underfoot, roots settling into a path, or reduced clearance as reasons to retreat immediately. Do not wait for the route to look closed.

## Do not mistake the surviving machinery for visitor controls

The pressure gauge is worth observing. It is not necessarily worth touching.

You do not know its calibration, whether it still reads accurately, or what a particular mark means for the hollow now. Record needle positions alongside visible events rather than deciding that a number must be “safe.”

Do not tap the glass, turn fittings, clear pipes, or attempt to free the flywheel. The copper pipe becoming a plant bore is a warning: there may no longer be a meaningful distinction between repairing an old machine and injuring a pressurized organ.

The same applies to the rack with replacement teeth and the elevated insulators. Their original purpose does not establish their current function. Something that appears abandoned may be load-bearing, pressure-bearing, or capable of moving.

The brick building is not a refuge merely because it resembles one. Its walls have expanded, its door has shifted, and its plumbing continues into the hollow. Do not shelter there without checking that you can still get out.

## Keep away from openings and discharge paths

The black channels on the central growth should be treated as active drains. Do not stand in them, cross them casually, or leave equipment in them. Their usual dryness is misleading; the stains record what happens briefly.

Stay clear of the discharge gate, including the area immediately downstream. Its opening is not an opportunity to explore beneath the floor. A current strong enough to run through that confined space could take your footing, and the gate closes again.

The central basin is also not drinking water, a bathing pool, or a place to lean over casually. The moving diaphragm means its depth and available clearance change. The overlapping leaf rim may hold water perfectly well without supporting a person.

On an initial visit, I would not climb the iron stair. Its displaced steps and fresh tissue pockets provide apparent footholds, but not demonstrated structural support. More importantly, you could spend the best departure interval watching the cycle from above and descend after the routes have begun to close.

If seeing the crown is essential, do it with a guide who knows both the ascent and the timing of the return.

## Treat contact as exposure, not hospitality

The upholstered buttresses look inviting, but they wick moisture into clothing. Rest only where you have generous clearance, a stable position, and an obvious route back. Do not sleep inside.

Keep bare skin away from the soft seams. The description establishes that plant surfaces merge there; it does not establish that they can absorb a person. You need not invent that danger to recognize that an adhesive, slowly compressing junction is a bad place for a hand, sleeve, or boot.

Do not taste the seeds or leaves. The cucumber, yeast, and warm-flour smell is not evidence of edibility. Bring your own water even if the basin looks clear.

Clean footwear and equipment before and after the visit. A multiverse traveler should avoid carrying unfamiliar organisms into the hollow just as carefully as carrying its pale root tips elsewhere.

## Prepare for the way it will affect your judgment

The warmth, food-like smell, rounded surfaces, and domestic knocking sounds may make the place feel oddly habitable. Almost everything moves too slowly to provoke alarm. That combination is dangerous: discomfort accumulates while nothing seems urgent.

Set your retreat rules before entering, and follow them even when the hollow still feels peaceful.

You may also find the lack of sightlines more tiring than expected. The rim is low and apparently close, yet reaching it is another matter. Do not abandon the rails for a seemingly direct climb toward daylight. The intervening vegetation may be a continuous mass rather than a thicket you can push through.

A brief touch against a broad trunk can reveal the impacts traveling through it. That may help you notice a change in rhythm, but it does not make you capable of interpreting the system. Learn its sounds without assuming they are messages.

**The right first visit is a modest one:** enter lightly equipped, follow the reinforced route, observe more than you touch, and leave with space still comfortably open around you.

The hollow has had decades to accommodate its own crowding. You should not expect it to make an exception for the shape of a visitor.
