Prepare for this as a visit to a place that has almost no capacity to receive you.

The hollow is not a miniature cavern with a hidden clearing farther in. Its space is already committed—to roots, leaves, clay, trapped bubbles, and the slow accommodation of one living thing by another. Even its new chambers arrive occupied. The first requirement is to abandon the expectation that somewhere inside there must be room for a visitor.

For an ordinary human, the best visit will probably take place at the entrance.

### Decide what “visiting” means before you enter the lake

You can approach the drowned alder, find the moss-covered slit, and spend time watching it without crossing the threshold. That would be a real visit, not a failed attempt at one.

If your means of multiverse travel can make you small enough to enter, establish exactly what else it changes. A smaller body is not automatically a body adapted to cold water, narrow gaps, or the forces acting at that scale. You will need life support, insulation, vision, and mobility that remain functional after the change. Do not discover inside the hollow that your breathing apparatus still depends on a usable distinction between up and down.

More importantly, shrinking does not guarantee that you will fit. The water louse can withdraw into spaces that appear too narrow for it. That is evidence about the water louse, not permission for you.

Do not plan to enlarge yourself again until you are well clear of the alder.

### Make the lake portion of the journey conventional

Whatever happens inside, the approach is a cold-water dive beneath submerged roots. Prepare for those ordinary hazards with ordinary seriousness:

- Use exposure protection appropriate to the lake’s temperature and the planned duration.
- Carry an independent breathing supply and a reserve.
- Have a competent companion or surface support, with an agreed return time.
- Know your route out of the root tangle before approaching the slit.
- Secure hoses, straps, and loose equipment so they cannot catch on branches or moss.

Do not undertake the dive without suitable training. A remarkable destination does not make drowning less ordinary.

The hollow is colder than the surrounding lake. Treat the entrance as a place where your safe exposure time may shorten, not as shelter from the cold.

There is no breathable air inside. The bubbles are not air pockets you can depend on, and some plainly do not behave like ordinary bubbles. Leave them intact.

### Bring less into contact with it

Clean your equipment before entering the lake, especially if it has recently been in another ecosystem—or another universe. Avoid residues, loose fibers, exposed lubricants, and anything likely to shed particles.

Your own heat may matter here. So may the water displaced by a hand, a fin stroke, or a probe. This is a place where a leaf’s movement is passed through packed vegetation until a thread at the entrance twitches. A visitor need not break anything to disturb everything.

Bring a small, controllable light rather than a floodlamp. Begin with the weakest illumination that lets you work safely, and keep exposures brief. Light appears to persist in particular parts of the hollow and to sustain nearby leaves after the lake has gone dark. You should not assume that switching off your lamp removes what you have introduced.

Avoid flash photography. The aim is to see the existing conditions, not replace them with yours.

### Observe the threshold before touching it

The algae hanging across the entrance is useful precisely because it has been left alone. Watch its moving and motionless ends. Note where the lake’s ordinary motion seems to stop.

Do not brush aside the moss to improve the view. Do not widen the slit. Do not test the fresh clay with a finger.

The sharp ridges and tender stone edges suggest surfaces still forming, not durable walls. You could erase something important with a touch too slight to register through a glove.

If you use an optical instrument, keep it outside initially. A thin camera probe is still an intrusion; its cable can drag, its tip can push water, and its light can alter the scene. Insert nothing unless you have a clear, noncontact route and an equally clear way to withdraw it.

The best first observation may be several minutes in which you do nothing at all.

### Do not navigate by falling things

Inside, particles settle toward different surfaces. A vertical face can hold a stable drift of silt; a root’s underside can behave like a floor.

Use named landmarks rather than “up,” “down,” or “under.” The torn leaf, the bark curl, the notch holding light, and the entrance lip are more useful than a gravity-based mental map.

Even those landmarks require care. A leaf that disappears behind one stone and emerges from another may not indicate a traversable route between them. Visible continuity is not proof of accessible space.

If you cross the threshold, keep the route back short and directly observable. Do not count on a tether solving every navigation problem: it could snag, damage growth, or take a different route through the vegetation than you did.

If you cannot retreat without pulling against the hollow, you have already gone too far.

### Treat the terraces as potentially occupied

The bark-faced ledges and fitted splinter deserve the courtesy you would give an unfamiliar dwelling.

Do not step on them, lift the bridge, move a grain of sand to see whether it returns, or leave a gift. There is no evidence that anything here wants an offering. In a place where even disappearance takes up space, leaving something behind is a substantial act.

You do not need to decide whether the terraces were built before deciding not to damage them.

### Give the water louse priority

It is the resident you can see. Do not lure it out, feed it, follow it into a gap, or block its route back beneath the bark.

If its legs blur, the amber line loses definition, or the vegetation draws inward around it, stop approaching. The account does not establish what is happening. It does establish that the surrounding plants make room.

Follow their example.

Do not put a probe behind the bark to watch a chamber form. Do not interpret a newly opened space as an invitation. The one described is already threaded with growth, and nothing follows the animal into it.

You should not be the first thing to try.

### Prepare for an uneventful visit

Bring a reliable time limit from outside the hollow and obey it. Do not use a fading patch of light as a clock, or wait underwater for a leaf to finish unfolding. Events here can take seasons, and sounds may arrive in separated pieces.

You may see only a stationary animal, tightly folded vegetation, and a little light caught along a root. That is enough.

The particular discipline this place asks of a traveler is to remain curious without becoming insistent. Leave before cold or fatigue makes you clumsy. Withdraw without taking a specimen. Let the moss stay where it is.

A successful visit is one after which the hollow still has just enough room for its next leaf.
