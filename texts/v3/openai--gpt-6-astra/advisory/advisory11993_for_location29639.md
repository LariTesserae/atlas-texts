Prepare for a high-altitude climb whose destination is not the summit, but a few minutes spent lying very still beside something you must not touch.

The hollow is small enough that your face could block its opening. That does not make it a small environment. Your first preparation should be to stop treating those two facts as contradictory.

## Before the ascent

Acclimatize to the mountain’s altitude. Arrive rested, hydrated, and with enough reserve strength to descend after prolonged stillness in an awkward position. Breathlessness will be more than uncomfortable here: it will make it difficult to distinguish an effect of the hollow from the ordinary effects of insufficient oxygen.

Go with a companion. One person observes; the other watches the observer, the ledge, and the retreat route. Agree in advance that the companion can end a viewing without having to persuade you that it is necessary. A person trying to hold the leaf and the country of the leaf in attention may not be a reliable judge of how long they have been looking.

Arrange your return point well outside the hollow and away from the newest crest. The summit is still growing, so an exact coordinate should not be your only means of finding your way back. Use redundant references to the wider landscape. If your travel method permits an emergency recall, test it somewhere uneventful before approaching the ledge.

Bring ordinary mountain necessities even if your visit is intended to be brief:

- Windproof insulation, sun protection, gloves, and eye protection.
- Water and food in securely closed containers.
- A first-aid kit with appropriate supplies for burns.
- A reliable means of communication and a turnaround time.
- Fall protection suited to the approach, used by someone competent to assess its placement.

The last point needs care. Fresh, sharp stone is not necessarily strong stone. Do not trust a projection merely because nothing has weathered it. A material that is still developing its thickness may have an excellent surface and very little beneath it.

If there is no defensible way to protect the viewing position without driving hardware into the hollow or its immediate surroundings, do not take that position.

## Dress for cold air and radiant heat

You may have a cold back and a hot face at the same time.

The mountain’s thin air and exposed ledge call for alpine clothing; the opening calls for protection against localized heat. Keep loose straps, hair, sleeves, and scarf ends contained. Avoid exposed jewelry and dangling metal equipment near the cavity.

A narrow part of the floor occasionally becomes magnetic. That does not establish that anything will be pulled violently from your hands, but it is enough reason not to suspend a compass, steel tool, or loose carabiner over the plants. Secure essential equipment away from the opening. Do not assume a compass reading taken there is trustworthy.

Use a clean, non-shedding pad beneath your torso only if the ledge’s temperature and stability permit it. It should protect you without bridging across delicate new formations or insulating an active patch of mineral for an extended period.

An infrared thermometer or thermal camera could help you assess the approach. Treat its readings as provisional. Unfamiliar ceramic surfaces can make remote temperature measurements misleading, and a leaf with cool margins may still be dangerously hot along its veins.

Your hand is not a temperature probe.

## Approach as a very large disturbance

There is no dust or litter in the hollow. Anything you introduce will be an addition to a place that has not previously had to accommodate such things.

Clean your equipment beforehand. Remove soil, seeds, lint, and loose fibers; keep boot tread out of the opening. Do not eat beside it. Do not use smoke, powder, liquid tracers, or scented sprays to investigate airflow.

Above all, do not water the plants.

They are not visibly enduring a drought. They are living by exchanges that do not require drinking. Water could alter temperature differences, mobilize salts, or damage hot mineral. Even if none of those effects proves severe, pouring it into the hollow would be an experiment, not an act of care.

Your breath is also warm, damp, chemically active air. You do not need to stop breathing, but keep your nose and mouth outside the opening. Avoid panting directly onto the leaves. Do not insert your face far enough to obstruct the little cavity’s exchange with the mountain air.

Begin at a distance. Look at the ledge, listen, and let yourself become familiar with the background before lowering anything over its edge. A secured mirror or camera may offer an initial view with less exposure than your head. Keep it outside the opening, and accept that its image may not capture the depth effects at the back.

There is no need to reach all the way in to have arrived.

## Make the first viewing brief

Choose ordinary daylight, preferably with enough afternoon remaining that you can leave without hurrying. The interior’s light does not prove that the exterior route will remain illuminated.

Settle into a position from which you can withdraw without pushing against the cavity’s rim. Keep your weight on assessed rock, not on a newly forming lip. Your companion should remain far enough back that helping you would not bring both of you to the edge.

The first look should last only long enough to establish what your body does there.

Notice whether you become dizzy, overheated, confused, or unusually absorbed. Then withdraw completely. Look at the ordinary mountain. Speak to your companion. Check the time.

Only consider a longer observation if this first withdrawal is straightforward.

The low electrical note, its occasional cessation, and the click beneath the black plant belong to the described activity of the place. They are not established danger signals. Nevertheless, if the sound changes at the same time as the rock beneath you warms, shifts, or vibrates differently, retreat rather than waiting to discover the connection.

Do not reach beneath the black foliage to find what clicks.

## Let the uncertain distances remain uncertain

The leaf at the back is the part most likely to defeat ordinary habits of inspection.

Usually, leaning closer makes a small thing easier to resolve. Here it may do the opposite. Withdrawing may reveal a landscape rather than restore a manageable object. Do not respond by leaning progressively farther over the ledge.

Use an external reference: your companion’s voice, a timer, the pressure of your supported torso against the pad. Before each look, state a simple intention—“I am observing from outside”—and retain it even if what you see begins to resemble somewhere one could travel.

Seeing country inside a leaf is not evidence that an ordinary human can safely enter it. Nor does it establish what crossing its boundary would mean for your size, body, or route home.

Do not put a finger, probe, or tethered object into that region to test it. A tether is reassuring only when distance and continuity behave as expected.

You may come away unable to explain which scale was the real one. That is an acceptable outcome. It is safer, and probably more faithful to the place, than forcing a conclusion while suspended over a cliff.

## Plan to leave without a specimen

Bring a notebook. A sketch of the three plants’ positions, a record of the electrical note, or an account of the new leaf’s warmth becoming a green edge may be more useful than a close-up photograph.

Do not remove the apparent fallen leaf. You cannot be sure it has finished falling, that it is detached, or that “one leaf” adequately describes what you would be taking.

Do not occupy an apparently empty shelf simply because it has no plant on it. The hollow repeatedly makes room before the thing that will need that room becomes visible. Bare space is not necessarily unused space.

If you want to witness growth, arrange repeat visits rather than one exhausting vigil. A season-long revolution cannot be hurried by attention. Mark observations in your records, not on the stone.

The best preparation is enough practical security that you can afford not to investigate everything. Arrive able to notice a warmer line between two leaves. Leave while it is still becoming an edge.

You are visiting a place where making room is part of growth. Give it yours.
