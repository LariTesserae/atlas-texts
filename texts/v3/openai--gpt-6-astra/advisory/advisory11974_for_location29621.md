Prepare for this as a visit to a cold, exposed country where the ground is also infrastructure, the buildings have physiological needs, and a safe route is something people keep alive.

The most important thing to bring is not a weapon or a climbing kit. It is an introduction to someone who knows the local maintenance schedules.

## Before you arrive

Arrange a reception at an inhabited, maintained transit point. Have a named host, a confirmed place to sleep, and an arrival time that leaves plenty of the local working day. You do not want your first task to be finding an address while the bridges are lowering and everyone is shutting their windows.

Tell your host explicitly that you are an ordinary water-dependent human. Do not assume that “accommodation for travelers” includes breathable air, liquid drinking water, or food your body can metabolize.

Ask for answers to these questions before committing:

- What are the air pressure, oxygen level, and ordinary atmospheric constituents?
- Does the air remain human-breathable during a venting event?
- How long is the interval between building closure and reopening?
- Can the guest room tolerate moisture from breathing, washing, and bodily waste?
- What arrangements exist for someone unable to use the living transit grafts?
- Who can help with a medical problem, and how long would evacuation take?

The plateau’s position above the clouds is reason to check pressure, not proof that the atmosphere is thin. Likewise, “warm gas” is not a chemical identification. A human should not discover either answer by becoming dizzy in the street.

If these questions cannot be answered, make the first visit short and use independently verified life support. A dust mask does not supply oxygen or protect against every gas.

## Bring your own small human habitat

The place is conspicuously dry, but that does not mean it will preserve your moisture. Cold wind and dry air can dehydrate you without making you feel sweaty.

Carry all the drinking water you expect to need, plus a reserve for a delayed departure. Keep it in several sealed, impact-resistant containers, protected from freezing. Include food you know you can eat. Neither the grazers nor the apparent vegetation establish that anything here is nutritionally useful to you.

Do not plan to collect water from below the plateau. Those clouds are scenery, not an accessible refill station.

Your clothing should be windproof, warm, and reasonably smooth on the outside. Copper-colored spores and hooked filaments will be much easier to remove from a close-woven shell than from exposed fleece, fur trim, or elaborate straps. Bring:

- Layered insulation, including protection for your face, ears, and hands.
- Close-fitting goggles.
- Respiratory protection appropriate to the measured atmosphere and airborne material.
- Sturdy boots with good grip and no unnecessarily sharp studs.
- Sealed bags for clothing and equipment exposed outdoors.
- A hooded lamp with spare power; prolonged twilight is not useful working light.
- Your usual medications, basic wound care, and supplies for managing cold exposure.

Do not add spikes, crampons, or a trekking pole with a metal point just because the country looks rugged. You are walking on tissue, sometimes over thin valves and transport channels. Ask what local footwear is designed to do.

Electrical compatibility deserves its own inquiry. The landscape conducts charge, the spores respond to fields, and the fliers exploit an electrical gradient. None of that proves your electronics will fail, but it does mean you should get local advice about exposed conductors, charging equipment, and safe storage. Do not improvise a grounding rod by driving one into the road.

## Choose lodging, not a campsite

An inviting hollow is not necessarily a campsite. A sheltered patch of cork-soft surface may be a place where gases collect, animals emerge, or the plateau changes its activity.

**Do not sleep on ground that has become warm.** Treat that as a firm rule even if you are cold and the warmth feels wonderful.

There is an important distinction between a recognized wall heat supply and an unexplained warm patch beneath your bed. Have someone show you the difference. Book a room with a sleeping surface intended for visitors, not merely a hospitable-looking recess.

Ask your host to demonstrate the room in its nighttime configuration. Where does your luggage go when the building lowers? Does the door remain accessible? Which balcony or bridge should no longer be used? Are there clearances around the bed that must stay empty?

Never wedge a shutter open for fresh air without permission. Its movements may be regulating pressure rather than inconveniencing you.

Similarly, do not assume the street’s waste stripe is a universal rubbish bin. A system that digests local husks and shed skins may not handle your packaging, medicines, batteries, or human waste safely. Arrange containment and disposal in advance. Keep water spills contained until you know what they do to the local tissue.

## Learn to walk before you explore

On the first day, go out with a guide. Learn a short circuit between your lodging, a public shelter, your arrival point, and somewhere you can obtain help.

Stay on the maintained pale roads, near their centers, following the red buds. The margins are active experimental growth, not a convenient place to step aside.

Pay attention to the grain underfoot. Here, texture can convey a change that an Earth traveler would expect to see on a warning sign. If a field’s grain begins turning toward you, do not continue across it to see what happens. Withdraw toward the known route without making a hurried cross-country detour.

Other useful habits:

- Give new seams and exposed glowing tissue a wide berth.
- Do not probe openings or peel away loose surface layers.
- Do not leave baggage on the pulsing central waste stripe.
- Give road gardeners room to work, and follow their directions immediately.
- Stop short of visibly changing bridges and let local traffic show you when crossing is appropriate.
- Avoid the plateau edge without a guide, however irresistible the lightning below looks.

A map will help, but it cannot replace current route knowledge. A line marked “road” may depend on someone having spent the afternoon tending a junction.

The great old trunk rings are especially likely to deceive your sense of scale. The view is open, landmarks are sparse, and a curve several miles across can look straight from where you stand. Carry an offline record of your route and agree on a turnaround time rather than walking until a distant feature seems close.

## Treat the spore release as weather requiring shelter

Learn the precursors: the grazers stop feeding, their knocking accelerates, and the ordinary pattern of activity changes. When residents begin closing shutters and drawing screens, go indoors. Do not stay outside for one more photograph.

The described discharge lasts about twenty minutes, but do not make your safety depend on that being universal. Carry enough supplies to wait longer, and let residents decide when the streets are usable again.

During a release, you face several hazards at once: airborne dust, reduced visibility, gas from the ground, fleeing animals, descending hunters, and falling hooked filaments. You do not need to know which is worst to understand that an arcade screen or a proper room is preferable to an open plaza.

If caught outside, use the nearest **known** shelter and protect your eyes and breathing. Do not crouch over a slit or enter an unfamiliar opening simply because it blocks the wind.

Afterward, inspect boots, cuffs, straps, and luggage before going into your sleeping space. Remove deposits by the locally recommended method. Do not assume wet washing is appropriate, and do not shake a contaminated coat out inside. Have fresh cuts covered before going outdoors; whatever the spores do locally, you do not want particulate matter embedded in a wound.

## Use the doorframes as a passenger, not an experimenter

The distance grafts are cultivated connections with maintenance requirements. Treat them more like a staffed transit system than an open doorway.

For your first crossing, use an attended frame and follow the porter’s instructions about pace, luggage, and spacing. Ask whether human passage has been demonstrated safely. Seeing a cart cross proves that carts can cross; it does not settle every question about an unfamiliar organism.

Do not test the stretching effect with a hand, tether, camera cable, or half-inserted pack. Ask before bringing long equipment through.

Never remove a bud yourself, attach something to a frame, or walk through an unattended branch connection. The sealed third doorway is not an invitation to adventurous tourism. The unidentified pollen and continuous cold wind are ample reasons to leave it sealed.

Maintain a departure contingency. A route that takes decades to cultivate may not be replaceable on your travel schedule.

## Expect to feel exposed, then oddly intimate

The spaciousness may initially be reassuring. You can see for miles. There are few obstacles, scattered animals, and plenty of room between houses.

But there is very little inert background. You will feel freight passing under your feet. Walls will adjust while you are trying to sleep. Low sounds will seem to come from everywhere. Your footprint will disappear behind you.

You may find this more tiring than a crowded city. Schedule short outings, warm rests, regular drinks, and a quiet first evening. Use hearing protection only where your host says it is safe; animal calls, shutters, and changes in the landscape’s noise may carry information you need.

The right attitude is neither terror nor reverence. People live here. They walk to work, move cargo, maintain roads, and presumably complain when things are inconvenient. Follow their practiced familiarity rather than assuming either that everything is dangerous or that anything ordinary-looking is harmless.

For a first visit, success would be modest: arrive with your needs understood, learn one safe route, watch a release from behind a screen, cross a tended graft with an attendant, and be indoors before the town settles for the night.

Leave with your water containers closed, your boots cleaned, and no living souvenirs. This is a place where a small piece of growth can have consequences far beyond its apparent size.
