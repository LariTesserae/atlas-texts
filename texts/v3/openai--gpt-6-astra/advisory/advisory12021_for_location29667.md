Prepare for a small, cold industrial town whose buildings are also a cultivated living system. You do not need to arrive dressed for a hostile jungle. You need to arrive as a considerate guest in a place where the pavement, plumbing, foundations, and garden may be different parts of the same organism.

The most important precaution is to stop assuming that an inert-looking surface is inert.

## Before you enter

Arrange an introduction if you can. A resident, workshop attendant, or maintenance worker could tell you more in ten minutes than you could safely learn by exploring for a day. Ask:

- Which paths and buildings are open to visitors?
- Where can you obtain drinking water and dispose of wastewater?
- Are there restrictions on fire, chemicals, footwear, or imported plants?
- What should you do if you notice a leak, a new shoot, or a damaged surface?
- Where is it safe to stay overnight?

These are practical questions, not ceremonial ones. The town clearly accommodates ordinary walking, carts, iron machinery, and generations of repair. It is not so fragile that a misplaced foot must injure it. But its tolerances have been established by people who know which surfaces are structural, which are growing, and which are meant to give way.

For a multiverse arrival, use an established visitor protocol if one exists. Otherwise, arrive on firm, open ground outside the town and approach along a used path. Do not choose an empty court as a landing zone merely because nothing is standing there: there may be chambers beneath it, and the open space may be needed for drainage or work.

## Dress for persistent, understated cold

The cold is likely to be more wearing than dramatic. It comes from the ground, gathers overnight, and survives the daylight. You might be comfortable walking and then become thoroughly chilled while standing beside a machine or sitting in a room.

Bring:

- Layered clothing, including a warm midlayer.
- A windproof, rain-resistant outer layer.
- Waterproof walking shoes with grippy, non-studded soles.
- Warm socks and a spare dry pair.
- Light gloves for cold railings and outdoor waiting.
- An insulating sitting pad if you expect to spend time outside.

Avoid spikes, hobnails, and unnecessarily aggressive tread unless residents recommend them. The polished rootwood may be slippery when wet, but puncturing it is not a good answer.

For an overnight visit, confirm that your lodging supplies a dry bed and sufficient insulation. A lamp shining through a warm-colored membrane does not establish that the room behind it is warm. Ask before putting a waterproof sheet or bedroll directly on a living floor; some surfaces may need to exchange moisture.

## Bring clean equipment, not a little ecosystem of your own

Biosecurity deserves particular care here. A foreign mold or plant pathogen could potentially affect not just a crop but parts of the built environment. The description does not establish that the town is unusually susceptible, but the possible consequences justify taking simple precautions.

Clean soil, seeds, and organic debris from footwear, bags, walking poles, and tools before arrival. Leave live plants, untreated wood, loose growing media, and unnecessary biological specimens behind. Keep food contained and follow local rules about scraps.

Do not improvise sterilization by pouring disinfectant onto your shoes once you are standing in the square. Clean your equipment beforehand, using an approved method where available, and avoid bringing residues that could harm living tissue.

The same restraint applies on departure. Bark flakes, cuttings, amber water, and pale fibers are not automatically appropriate souvenirs. Ask before collecting anything, and transport permitted samples securely.

## Treat water as managed infrastructure

Bring enough known-safe drinking water to cover your arrival and any delay in finding a supply. The town has substantial water storage, but storage is not the same as public access.

Do not drink from the square, the abandoned loading bay, drainage channels, or condensation on a cistern wall. Clear or pleasantly woody-smelling water is not necessarily potable. The shallow amber pools appear to be places where water is allowed to remain, not necessarily places where people draw it.

Likewise, ask where to wash and where to empty anything. A street seam may lead into root channels or breathing spaces rather than a conventional sewer. Do not pour out:

- Soap or detergent.
- Alcohol, solvents, or disinfectants.
- Oily water.
- Concentrated food waste.
- Unfamiliar medicines or chemical treatments.

Even clean water should go where residents direct it. The workers are shifting moisture to prevent structural splitting; adding water indiscriminately is not inherently helpful.

Use designated sanitation facilities. Do not assume that an all-vegetable town can safely absorb human waste wherever it is deposited.

## Learn the difference between wear and trouble

A number of things that would be alarming in a conventional building are evidently routine here:

- A wall thickening around a pipe.
- Paving seams opening slightly as the evening cools.
- A roof changing its angle.
- Water briefly running beneath the square.
- Older buildings settling into terraces.
- Bubbles rising from the shallow water over underground passages.

Do not try to stop these processes. In particular, do not wedge a moving joint, seal an opening, block a flap, or pack a gap with cloth to “keep the damp out.”

Conversely, do not assume that every sag or soft patch is safe because the town is alive. The northern buildings are being deliberately retired, and their former rooms and stairs may no longer support visitors. Explore them only with permission and preferably with a guide.

Stay on established paths over the outer bank. Keep out of root chambers, drains, and underground maintenance passages. Their description as breathing spaces does not mean they contain breathable air for humans; they are confined spaces with uncertain structure and atmosphere.

A good rule is: **observe changes, report damage, and leave adjustments to the people tending the town.**

## Do not “help” by pruning

Pack any knife or multitool securely, and use it only on your own belongings unless invited otherwise.

The worker turning a shoot toward a paving joint is showing you something important. Maintenance here is often a matter of directing growth rather than removing it. A shoot in your way may have an intended destination. A rough patch may be functional tissue. A peg may be part of an ongoing repair.

Do not pick roof leaves, scrape nap from a sheltered wall, cut a dangling fiber, or pull out a wooden fastening. Do not hammer in tent stakes or attach equipment with nails. Ask before using adhesive on any surface.

This does not require treating every threshold as sacred. People plainly live and work here. Follow their example for ordinary contact, but obtain permission before cutting, fastening, loading, or altering anything.

## Respect the workshops as workshops

Their quietness could be misleading. A softly brushing belt and a padded press can injure you just as effectively as louder machinery.

Before entering:

- Secure scarves, straps, loose sleeves, and long hair.
- Keep clear of belts, shafts, feed openings, and presses.
- Do not touch the living sockets around machine feet.
- Ask where you can stand without obstructing work.
- Use eye, hearing, or respiratory protection appropriate to the task if you are invited to participate.

Do not distract someone feeding the drawing machine, especially when its speed changes. The workers are coordinating with a shared power supply; a pause that looks idle may be part of operating the equipment safely.

Bring a charged light and a modest independent power bank for personal electronics. Do not assume the town has spare electrical capacity or a compatible outlet. Its small motor is already serving several essential loads, including water circulation. Charging a large traveler’s battery could be a substantial request.

Avoid open flames and hot equipment until you know local rules. Living wood is not uniformly fireproof, and this town also contains dry structural fibers, packing cloth, insulation stock, lubricants, and machinery.

## Health preparations should be ordinary but thoughtful

Nothing in the description establishes toxic air, aggressive vegetation, or a need for a sealed suit. Begin with your normal destination-compatibility checks rather than assuming danger from the town’s appearance.

Useful supplies include:

- Your usual medicines.
- A small first-aid kit with dressings and splinter tweezers.
- Any prescribed allergy treatment.
- A well-fitting particulate mask for dusty work areas, if needed.
- A reliable means of contacting your host or arranging departure.

Avoid handling exposed sap or unfamiliar growth with broken skin. Wash cuts with known-safe water and use normal wound care rather than local sap as an improvised remedy.

The fermented smell near drains may be a normal consequence of managed decay. It is not proof of poisonous gas, nor proof that a drain is safe to enter. If you feel unwell, move to an open area, notify someone, and seek appropriate help.

## Give yourself time to understand the place

You may initially find the town oddly empty. Its streets are wider than its traffic needs; work proceeds without much noise; many of its most consequential activities happen too slowly to command attention.

Do not mistake that for abandonment or lack of purpose.

Leave yourself time to stand somewhere unobtrusive and watch. You will begin to distinguish a drying yard from an empty court, a working cistern from an unusually broad pier, and a retired house from an occupied one. Ask before approaching someone at work, but do not assume you need an elaborate ritual to speak to them. This is also a town where people make standardized goods, repair bearings, and check the time on a clicking clock.

There is no evidence that the town is conscious. Calling it a single creature, claiming it can feel your footsteps, or speaking as though the residents live inside a pet would be an imposition unless they describe it that way themselves. Let them explain what “living” means in their daily practice.

For a first visit, I would choose daylight arrival, arrange a room rather than camp, bring my own initial water supply, and plan to leave enough daylight for the walk out. I would carry little, keep my boots clean, and ask for a short orientation before wandering.

The right preparation is less about armor than about attention. You are visiting a place where civic upkeep and cultivation have become the same trade. Walk normally where people walk, keep your waste contained, and resist the urge to correct anything you have not yet learned to recognize.
