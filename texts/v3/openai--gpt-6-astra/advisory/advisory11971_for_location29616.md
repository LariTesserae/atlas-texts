Prepare for this town as you would for a winter expedition, a stay aboard a very crowded boat, and a visit to someone’s home where every object has been repaired by someone you are about to meet.

The essential mistake would be to think that the town is protected from its water. It seems to survive by accommodating it—continually, precisely, and with very little waste. You will need to learn how not to interfere before you learn how to help.

## Arrange your arrival, and your departure

Do not arrive unannounced at dusk.

Toward evening, the water’s changes become quicker and less legible to an outsider. You want several hours in which to meet your host, reach your lodging, and learn what a safe route looks like before that happens. The town is small enough to encourage a dangerous confidence: you may be able to see your destination while having no usable path to it.

Hire a local guide, preferably someone your host knows. Ask them to meet you outside the hollow rather than sending directions. “Take the second bridge” is not a dependable instruction where bridges are withdrawn and a passage can become a drain.

Before entering, establish:

- Where you will sleep, and where you should go if that room becomes unusable.
- How your host signals **wait**, **come**, **leave**, and **help**.
- Where the household gets water fit for drinking.
- Which routes are appropriate for a visitor.
- How you will leave if the weather, your health, or the town’s water prevents your intended departure.

If your means of multiverse travel can return you directly, verify its operating conditions before relying on it. A departure point under a kitchen floor may be dry when you arrive and inaccessible later. Choose a locally approved rendezvous rather than assuming that the rim, a roof, or any other high place must be safe.

Carry enough supplies to tolerate a delayed departure. But do not bring so much that someone else must repeatedly rescue your baggage.

## Protect yourself from cold, glare, and immersion

This is a place where you can get wet without rain, without falling, and possibly without hearing the water approach.

Wear insulating layers under a waterproof, wind-resistant outer shell. Avoid cotton against the skin. Bring at least one complete change of clothes in a genuinely watertight inner bag, including socks and gloves. A spare pair of mittens should be accessible without unpacking everything.

Your footwear needs insulation, ankle support, and grip on wet, rounded stone. Do not assume metal spikes are the answer: they may damage timber, slip on paving, or catch where you need to move quickly. Ask before using them.

Loose scarves, trailing straps, and long coat hems are liabilities around moving water, narrow bridges, and submerged projections. Keep your load compact. You should be able to turn sideways through a doorway without rearranging the household.

A walking pole may help, but get local advice about its use. Do not casually probe holes, lever up channel covers, or brace yourself against repaired wood. A stone cover lifting from below is a warning to get away, not an invitation to push it back down.

Bring:

- Well-fitted, high-quality UV-blocking sunglasses with side protection.
- A spare pair, ideally packed separately.
- Clear protective eyewear for dimmer conditions.
- Sunscreen and lip protection.
- An opaque sleeping mask.

The light comes from below and sideways as well as above. A broad hat alone will not solve it. Do not wait for your eyes to hurt before putting protection on.

For crossings near the central pool, ask about flotation equipment suited to the local boats and passages. Cold-water flotation could save your life, but a bulky vest that catches in a narrow hatch creates another problem. Ordinary swimming ability should not count as your primary protection.

Also remember the altitude. The description does not tell you the atmospheric pressure, but this is a high mountain settlement. Confirm breathable conditions if your travel system allows it, acclimatize where possible, and take persistent headache, nausea, unusual weakness, or confusion seriously. Those symptoms are not necessarily just the glare or a poor night’s sleep.

## Bring drinking water, not merely a filter

**The town is full of water. That does not mean it has water you can drink.**

The arriving water is saline and carries deposits that are sometimes edible and sometimes important enough to isolate in sealed pits. A camping filter will not remove dissolved salts. Boiling will not desalinate it; it can concentrate what remains.

For a short visit, bring your own drinking water and familiar food. For a longer stay, arrange a verified supply in advance. If you intend to treat local water, you need equipment appropriate to its actual chemistry, not simply equipment that produces clear water. In an unfamiliar universe, even successful desalination is not a complete guarantee of compatibility.

Ask specifically about water for drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, and rinsing irritated eyes. Keep those supplies covered.

Do not sample the pale deposits as a gesture of friendliness. The children’s ability to distinguish them by touch represents training you do not have. Nor does the fact that local people eat a coating establish that your body can safely digest it.

You can explain this without treating their food as dirty: “I don’t know whether my body can use it” is both courteous and accurate.

Bring familiar medication, a compact first-aid kit, dressings that remain usable in damp conditions, and supplies for protecting cracked skin. Salt, cold, and constant handling will make small splits in your hands disproportionately troublesome. Plan an evacuation threshold with your guide if you have a condition that may need urgent treatment; carrying medicine is not the same as having access to medical care.

## Learn to navigate without dependable sound

Do not rely on shouting.

Do not rely on a crash to tell you something has fallen, on splashing to tell you someone is in trouble, or on silence to tell you a structure is stable. Here, a sound may be absent where its cause occurs and arrive somewhere else later.

Before your first independent outing, learn a few local cloth signals and the proper way to announce yourself at a door. Carry a signal cloth that your hosts choose or approve. Inventing your own conspicuous signal could accidentally imitate something urgent.

Travel in pairs at first, with an explicit rule: if you lose sight of each other, stop at the last agreed safe place rather than each searching for the other.

A whistle or electronic alarm might still have some use, but it should not be your sole emergency method. Test radios locally rather than assuming they work—or assuming that the strange behavior of sound must affect them.

The pool introduces a second difficulty: sight is not entirely straightforward either. A moving thickness of water makes the opposite house appear distant. Do not estimate a step, jump, or crossing by looking through a water hill. Follow fixed architectural references and your guide’s instructions.

You will probably find the missing sound more disorienting than you expect. People use ordinary noise to understand how near things are, how hard they struck, and whether someone else noticed. Slow down when those cues disappear.

## Treat every crossing as temporary

A route you used this morning is not a route you have earned the right to use this afternoon.

Watch what residents are doing with planks, barriers, baskets, and children. Those actions tell you more than the apparent depth of a stream. If someone withdraws a plank, do not replace it for your own convenience.

Avoid stepping on floating ice or the soft grey skins forming on the brine. Do not put your weight on a channel cover that has begun to move. Do not reach into a small draining hole to retrieve something.

Give the central pool a generous margin. Its upright panes of water fall in several directions, and there may be no useful sound before they do. The water’s familiarity to residents does not make it predictable to you.

Use the boats only with an experienced boatman. Their technique appears to depend on reading an approaching hollow, not simply paddling toward the other side. Do not stand up to improve your view, trail your hand in the water, or shift your weight without warning.

Secure valuable equipment inside your pack. If something does fall into a channel, pause before pursuing it. Locals may urgently chase a mitten because they understand both its value and the channel. You understand only one of those things.

## Be careful around the animals without making them enemies

The animals seem woven into domestic life. That is not the same as being pets.

Do not feed, catch, or move them without instruction. A large animal drifting beneath a hatch may be harmless in intent and still capable of pinning a leg. Do not imitate a resident gently pushing one aside until someone has shown you where and how to touch it.

Keep food and water containers closed. Respect woven jar covers; they are working equipment, not quaint decoration.

Do not scrape creatures from bridge undersides or brush the films from wet masonry. You would be disturbing both animals and their food, and perhaps damaging a surface whose behavior you do not understand.

Watch the birds, but do not use them as a complete alarm system. Their sudden departure is a reason to look up, stop, and consult someone—not proof that you know what comes next.

## Bring useful things, and ask before deploying them

The most welcome gifts may be light, durable repair materials: suitable needles, strong thread, patches, or corrosion-resistant fittings. But ask in advance. A material that performs beautifully in your world may behave badly in this water, and an unfamiliar adhesive may make a future repair impossible.

Timber is especially charged with value here. Never burn scraps, trim a beam, pull out a peg, or borrow a plank because it appears unused. The bindings on a broken beam may embody several generations of careful work.

Do not attach a safety line to a bridge or roof fixture without permission. An anchor is only useful if it can bear the load, and your attempt at personal safety must not pull someone’s house apart.

Offer labor modestly. Good first tasks might include holding a basket, carrying a light load along a specified route, or keeping your belongings out of a working passage. Do not improvise flood control. A barrier that keeps water from your room could send it into someone else’s.

Ask where waste goes. Neither the channels nor the sealed pits should be treated as general disposal sites.

## Expect the house to keep moving around you

Your room may be dim with felt, warm only near a lamp, and crossed by the presence of water underfoot. You may sleep on a shared raised platform. Ask about bedding, privacy, footwear, and drying clothes before assuming you will have a private room with a stove.

Bring a dry sleeping layer and whatever insulation your host recommends. Keep your night essentials together: shoes, glasses, outer clothing, medication, and signal cloth. You should be able to get up and move without searching.

Do not cover lamps with your own fabric or rearrange drying clothes around the flame. In a settlement with little spare timber, animal-fat lamps, and crowded interiors, heat is precious and fire would be ruinous. A low-heat electric light is useful if locally acceptable, but aim it down and keep it dim. The dark felt is relief from light, not a deficiency to correct.

Most of all, expect hospitality to coexist with work. Someone may interrupt a conversation to lift a basket or brace a shutter. Let the conversation go. Step aside, then ask whether your hands are wanted.

A successful visitor here would not be the person who remains perfectly dry or sees every strange animal. It would be the person who learns when to wait, does not mistake quiet for safety, and leaves the household with no additional repair to make.

Keep your gloves dry. Keep your departure possible. And when everyone around you stops what they are doing to watch a small change in the water, watch them first.
