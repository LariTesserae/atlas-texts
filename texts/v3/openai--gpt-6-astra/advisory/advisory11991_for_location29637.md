Prepare for this place as you would for a visit to someone’s crowded home, a working pottery, and a water-scarce neighborhood—all at once. It is not a ruin, even where its oldest layers show. Almost everything you might mistake for an interesting remnant is still carrying weight, guiding water, sheltering animals, or helping somebody get through the day.

Your greatest advantage will not be elaborate equipment. It will be having a resident who expects you.

## Before you arrive

Arrange an introduction, somewhere to sleep, and an agreed length of stay. A visitor here takes up more than a bed: you need drinking water, somewhere to put your belongings, room at a wash ledge, and space to pass other people on the stairs. An ordinary household may welcome you sincerely without having much spare capacity.

If your multiverse transit system allows you to choose an arrival point, arrive on the surrounding streets—not in the court itself. There is no obvious empty landing area below. What looks clear from above may be a light shaft, the approach to the water wheel, or the space workers need to pass hot pottery.

Do the usual cross-world health checks before entering an inhabited district. Nothing described demands exotic protective gear, but unfamiliar food, water, allergens, and infections remain possible. Conversely, do not bring seeds, insects, soil, or untreated biological waste from another world into this one. This court already has a remarkably intricate ecology; it does not need yours added accidentally.

Learn enough of the local language to say:

- “Where should I stand?”
- “Is this safe to touch?”
- “Which water is for drinking?”
- “Where may I empty this?”
- “Someone is hurt.”
- “How do I get back to the upper street?”

Learn the names people use for the entrances. A map that records only horizontal distance will be particularly unhelpful here.

## Pack for narrowness, warmth, and hard surfaces

Bring a small, close-fitting bag rather than a framed pack or rolling case. Secure every loose strap. You will turn sideways often, and a dangling bottle can catch a railing, strike a drying pot, or pull something off a shelf.

Wear lightweight, breathable clothes that you can wash sparingly. Keep hems short of the ground and sleeves under control near the kiln. Closed shoes with dependable grip are more useful than heavy boots: the stairs are worn, ceramic can be slippery, and the footing changes through repeated repairs. You want to feel where you are placing your feet without exposing your toes to shards.

Useful equipment includes:

- **Your own filled drinking-water container**, protected against knocks. Bring enough for your initial visit and return journey without relying on the reserve.
- **A small light**, preferably one that can be dimmed and aimed downward.
- **A compact first-aid kit**, including wound dressings, blister care, and your usual medications.
- **A well-fitting particulate mask** for dusty work or grain sweeping. It will not make smoke-filled rooms safe.
- **A washable cloth**, useful for sweat and general cleaning, kept separate from anything used on wounds.
- **Seal-able food and waste containers.**
- **A modest backup power supply**, if your route home depends on an electronic device.

Avoid carrying so much emergency equipment that you become a moving obstruction. Leave bulky reserves with a trusted contact above.

Do not assume a pot, pipe, or wall is cool because it is not near an open flame. Heat has had time to spread through this place.

## Treat water as an arrangement between people

The basin is not simply a convenient source of water. It is the visible center of a rationing system.

Ask before filling anything. Ask which vessels are communal, whether cups are dipped or water is poured, and where washing is permitted. Do not put your own cup, hands, filter hose, or purification equipment into the reserve without explicit permission.

Clear water is not necessarily safe for an unfamiliar traveler. Use a verified supply or treatment appropriate to the actual hazards; an ordinary filter does not remove every possible contaminant. But keep your precautions discreet. You can protect your health without staging a public demonstration that appears to condemn your host’s household.

Be equally careful with wastewater. A trickle beside a wall may be feeding plants, passing beneath a doorway, or entering a part of the water system you cannot see. Soap, disinfectant, toothpaste, and food grease should not go into it merely because it resembles a drain.

Find out where the toilet is early. This is a practical question, not an embarrassing one. There is no patch of unused earth around the corner.

If you are invited to pedal the wheel, let someone teach you. The cups are imperfect in familiar ways, and the people living above know the proper rhythm. Faster is not necessarily more helpful.

## Learn the circulation before exploring

On your first circuit, follow someone who lives there.

Notice where people wait to let loads pass, which stairs serve households rather than public routes, and where workers need uninterrupted space. A three-plank bridge may be the only convenient connection between two busy rooms. It is not a viewing platform.

Do not assume every railing can take your full weight or every patched surface is weak. Both judgments require local knowledge. Equally, do not test old work by pulling, tapping, or climbing on it.

Keep your hands off objects you have not been invited to handle. A jar may be hot, newly formed, full, or positioned to catch a particular drip. A cloth may be cooling a doorway. An apparently abandoned saucer may have been doing its job for years.

Make a simple mental route back to the upper street, with landmarks you can recognize after dark. If there is another usable exit, learn it. Ask your host what people do if there is a fire or a passage becomes blocked. In such close quarters, the important safety question is not just “Can I get out?” but “Can I get out without stopping everyone else?”

## Expect the heat to stay after sunset

The court will probably feel warmer than its amount of direct sunlight suggests. Bodies, cooking, and the kiln continually add heat; masonry holds it.

Arrive rested and hydrated. Avoid scheduling your heaviest carrying for the hottest period. Spend your first afternoon learning where the moving air is, rather than assuming the lowest or shadiest room is coolest.

If you become dizzy, nauseated, unusually weak, or develop a headache, stop and get somewhere cooler. Do not confuse endurance with politeness. A visitor collapsing in a narrow passage creates work for everyone.

For a longer stay, ask where you will sleep and how that room ventilates when its door is shut. Also ask what the kiln’s schedule is. Warmth and work noise may continue well beyond the hours you expect.

## Share the place without feeding its problems

Keep food covered. Shake out shoes and inspect bedding, but do not approach every small animal as a threat. Lizards, birds, cats, insects, and bats are part of the ordinary occupancy of the court.

Leave bats alone, especially around the flue. Do not put your fingers into wall cavities or attempt to rescue wildlife barehanded. Any bite or scratch from an unfamiliar animal warrants prompt medical assessment.

Avoid disturbing dry pigeon litter or dusty nesting material. Wash before eating, particularly after touching public rails or helping with workshop tasks.

Do not feed the rats because they look enterprising, or the pigeons because they look picturesque. Somebody is already losing grain to them. Ask before feeding the cats, too.

And do not pick the mint or the shaded leaves. Greenery here is not public decoration. A single leaf can be somebody’s investment.

## Be careful about being helpful

This is a place where maintenance follows intimate knowledge. An outsider can easily “fix” the feature that made something work.

Do not straighten a gutter, move a catching vessel, seal a damp seam, or clear nesting material without asking. Do not assume the fig roots should be cut out of the drain. The household has already made that decision more than once.

If you want to contribute, ask what is wanted. Paying fairly for lodging, food, guiding, and pottery is often more useful than arriving with a technological solution. A small replacement tool or a requested material may be welcome; an unsolicited device may require storage, fuel, repairs, and negotiations about who controls it.

Offer specific, supervised help: carrying a load, holding something during a repair, sweeping where instructed. Let the experienced person set the pace.

## Prepare for closeness, not just crowding

You will hear people through floors and walls. You may hear private arguments, illness, affection, and the small frustrations of work. Being able to hear something does not make it yours to investigate.

Ask before taking photographs or recording sound, especially when doorways and household routines are involved. Do not describe residents as if they are components of an ingenious ecosystem. The court’s interdependence is real, but so are the nuisances, losses, and unequal burdens.

Rain will probably draw you toward the open spaces. Stand back first. Residents need to reach catchment shutters and set out jars, while previously dry surfaces become slick. Enjoy the smell of wet brick from somewhere that is not in the way.

The same rule applies when the kiln opens: watch where the workers put their feet before choosing where to put yours.

## What will make the visit rewarding

Give yourself enough time to stop interpreting everything as clutter.

At first, you will see obstacles and hear a general racket. Later, you may recognize the wheel starting, a loaded person approaching a blind turn, birds reacting to a cat, or the sudden quiet when a familiar task ends. Those distinctions are part of how residents navigate without having to see everything.

Do not expect to acquire that fluency in a day. Borrow someone else’s knowledge, move deliberately, and accept correction without making it awkward.

The best preparation is to arrive with your immediate needs covered, your belongings contained, and your attention available. There is room for a visitor here—but it is room people make, not room that was empty.
