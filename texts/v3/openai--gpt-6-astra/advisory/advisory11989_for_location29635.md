Prepare for this town as you would for a visit to someone’s home, a cold-water dive, and a walk along an unguarded mountain edge—all at once. The difficulty is that, once you are there, it may feel too peaceful to deserve that much preparation.

The water is clear. The streets are recognizable. Someone’s basket is coming down from the kitchen.

None of those things means you can breathe.

## Arrange to arrive above the town

Your destination should be a **host’s raft or another confirmed surface landing**, not the square, a doorstep, or an air chamber. Six or seven fathoms is roughly eleven to thirteen metres of water: a serious place for an ordinary human to arrive unexpectedly, especially carrying luggage.

If your method of multiverse travel chooses destinations by old maps or the historical identity of a building, specify the vertical position carefully. “Inside the family home” has at least two meanings here.

Arrange a welcome before arriving. A raft that supports a reed shelter may not have room or buoyancy to spare for a heavily equipped stranger. Ask where you should step, where your equipment can be secured, and whether there is a separate place for entering the water. Do not assume the central floor opening is a visitor entrance; it may also be a working hatch, a delivery route, and the family’s connection to its ancestral rooms.

Keep your departure method accessible from the surface. A return device sealed in a bag beneath a collapsed stair is not an exit plan.

## Learn enough to decline the wrong kind of visit

You do not have to descend to have visited this place properly.

An excellent first day would consist of sitting on a raft, learning names, watching maintenance work, and seeing the town through the floor opening. From there you can learn how people share ladders, signal to partners, lower baskets, and keep wet things away from bedding.

For an underwater visit, get cold-water diving training before you travel. If you intend to enter buildings or use enclosed air spaces, ordinary open-water experience is not sufficient. Those are overhead environments: you may be unable to ascend directly, and a single disturbed patch of silt can erase the exit.

The locals’ skill will be easy to underestimate because their equipment looks simple. A stone weight and a sedge rope may be backed by a lifetime of knowing exactly which foothold is sound, how long a task takes, and when a partner’s movements have become uncertain.

Do not copy the visible part of that expertise and assume you have copied the whole of it.

Hire a local guide for the town’s geography and customs, but make sure someone accompanying you also understands **your breathing equipment and your limitations**. Familiarity with the streets is not the same as familiarity with scuba rescue.

## Bring warmth before bringing curiosities

The most important equipment after a reliable breathing system is adequate thermal protection.

The surface may feel pleasant while the streets below steadily take heat from you. A sunny afternoon and beautifully lit water do not make a warm dive. Use an exposure suit appropriate to the actual temperature and your training, with protection for your head, hands, and feet. Do not make this the first outing for an unfamiliar drysuit.

Bring:

- A properly fitted mask, with a spare available.
- A complete breathing and buoyancy system appropriate to the dive, maintained before arrival.
- Depth, time, and breathing-gas monitoring.
- A compact primary light and a backup.
- A low-profile cutting tool for accidental entanglement.
- Secure, streamlined storage: nothing dangling that can catch a root or a binding.
- Dry insulating clothes, a windproof outer layer, towels, and a warm drink for afterward.
- A surface first-aid and oxygen provision, with someone trained to use it.
- A realistic evacuation plan, including access to appropriate diving medical care—or an explicitly reliable equivalent in your travel arrangements.

Do not wear a local stone belt merely to look less foreign. Weighting has to suit your body, suit, equipment, and means of returning to the surface. Excess weight turns a moment’s confusion into a struggle.

Ask your host whether fins are welcome along the route. They help a visitor move efficiently, but an unpractised kick can tear vegetation, knock a jar, or destroy visibility in a room. The answer may be to remain in the broad outdoor lanes rather than try to adapt unfamiliar equipment inside fragile houses.

Keep dives short and conservative. Losing finger accuracy is a reason to end the dive, not an interesting stage to work through. You still need those fingers to operate equipment and climb out.

## Do not plan around the air rooms

The chambers beneath the roofs are the most inviting hazard in the town.

They offer something emotionally irresistible: a place under the lake where people can speak. You will want to put your head into that darkness and hear an ordinary sentence.

But a pocket that contains air does not necessarily contain safe breathing air. Oxygen can be depleted, carbon dioxide can accumulate, and lamps can worsen both problems. Neither smell nor the fact that someone used a chamber earlier establishes that it is safe now.

There is also a pressure issue. **Breathing air at depth changes the ascent rules.** A breath-hold swimmer who takes a breath in one of these chambers cannot safely treat the return as an ordinary freediving ascent. Expanding gas can seriously injure the lungs if held during ascent.

For a first visit, treat the chambers as places to be shown, not as part of your life-support plan. Carry an independent gas supply, use it according to your training, and do not improvise a mixture of freediving, pocket breathing, and scuba.

Learn what the wooden toggles mean, but do not let a reassuring rope substitute for a safe route.

## Expect the sound to disorient you

You are going to hear the mountain more than you expect.

The waterfall’s roar comes through stone and water, not from an obvious object you can turn toward. Inside a house, the sound changes. A wall may hum against your palm. A nearby knock may seem disproportionately important against that constant thunder.

Do not rely on calling out, following a noise, or hearing that your companion is in trouble.

Agree on visual and tactile signals before entering the water. At minimum, everyone should be able to communicate:

- Stop.
- Stay close.
- I am cold.
- I have a problem.
- We are returning.
- I need breathing assistance.

Keep your buddy close enough for the conditions and the route. Do not separate because you can see a doorway across the square; visibility can disappear locally when a sleeve or fin disturbs a room.

Use high-contrast equipment markings rather than red alone. At this depth, the town has already taken much of the red out of the light. Bring a light that restores detail, but do not shine it into someone’s face during a narrow passing manoeuvre.

## Treat every surface as inhabited

The town is not a ruin with plants on it. It is a living structure whose masonry, roots, repairs, food supply, and traffic routes overlap.

A green ledge may be a sponge colony. A root that seems conveniently placed for your hand may be supporting a wall. What looks like an abandoned passage may simply be closed while a repair grows strong.

Ask where you may put your feet and hands. Follow established routes and your guide’s instructions; pale worn stone can suggest regular use, but it does not prove a surface is safe. Do not pull on bindings, push through a crown of leaves, or cut a root to make a shortcut.

Control your buoyancy and movements before approaching the buildings. If you cannot stay clear of delicate growth without grabbing it, stay outside.

Clean your equipment before arriving, and do not release water, organisms, or sediment brought from another world. This community lives in unusually intimate contact with its ecosystem. An introduced snail, disease, or aggressive water plant could be a structural problem as well as an ecological one.

The alternating snail-harvest sections are managed grounds, not an invitation to collect a meal wherever you find one. Ask before harvesting, feeding wildlife, or taking samples. Leave the otter room to pass.

## Learn the boundary before admiring the view

Have your guide identify the steep passage and the lip of the saddle early.

The late sunlight will make it look like a destination: bright steps, a handrail, blue beyond. But the town’s shallow, legible world ends there. Do not follow a dropped object, a fish, or a sinking piece of equipment into that blue.

The handrail is a boundary, not necessarily a safety anchor. The nearby weed’s slow drift does not establish that conditions beyond the saddle are equally gentle. Nor does the distant roar tell you exactly where dangerous flow begins.

For a first visit, remain well inside the town’s known routes. Watch the light reach the last steps from somewhere you do not need to recover from a mistake.

## Arrive as a guest, not a rescuer of ruins

These people have not simply failed to move out of a flooded village. They have made a way of living vertically: shelter above, work and memory below.

Do not begin by proposing that they drain the streets, cut back the roots, or replace slow repairs with something more efficient. Several seasons spent growing a bridge may be a sensible investment in a material that renews itself.

Ask before bringing gifts. Compact, durable supplies that the community can maintain may be welcome; unfamiliar machinery without spare parts may merely become another responsibility. Keep gifts and electronics sealed until there is a dry, agreed place to open them.

Bring your own safe drinking water until you know the local treatment practices. Clear lake water is not necessarily potable. Avoid washing equipment, using soap, or disposing of waste where it can contaminate the water around the homes.

At the white stone, pause. Ask what a visitor should do.

Do not assume that touching it is compulsory, religious, or merely decorative. It may belong to a custom whose meaning is local, private, or no longer expressible in a single explanation. Whatever the answer, leave it where it is.

## Give yourself time afterward

The first descent should be brief: perhaps a threshold, a stretch of street, and the square from outside its enclosed spaces. There is no need to reach every landmark.

Then come up, dry off, warm up, and let the place become understandable.

Underwater, you may discover that familiar architecture is more unsettling than wilderness. Your body expects to stand in a doorway and breathe. It expects stairs to lead somewhere you can walk. Here, the roofs are overhead twice, a room’s ceiling can be its most dangerous part, and ordinary domestic life passes above you as shadows through bright water.

That is the preparation to make beyond equipment: **expect to be a beginner in a place where everyone else’s ordinary movements are highly skilled**.

If you visit well, you may accomplish very little underwater. You might see one new binding tied around a root, wait while a basket passes, and follow your guide back toward a square of shaking light.

That would be enough.
