Prepare for this hollow as you would for a winter cave whose walls are alive, whose paths are not quite settled, and whose most important processes you cannot see. Your aim should be a short, quiet visit with a generous margin for leaving—not an expedition to the deepest point.

The most dangerous assumption would be that its small size makes it manageable. Thirty paces is not reassuring when distance itself is still being learned.

## Before you descend

Choose a time when the root seam admits you comfortably **in your full equipment**. An entrance you can squeeze through while warm and composed may be impossible to climb through with stiff hands, wet clothing, or an injured companion. Do not wedge it open or cut roots to improve access. The seam is part of the living arrangement, and you do not know what else a damaged root supports.

Watch the entrance from above before committing. Look for changes in its width, vapor flow, and the firmness of the surrounding ground. The pale swelling is not necessarily a roof that can bear your weight uniformly. Approach lightly; do not assemble a camp, heavy equipment, or a crowd over it.

Go with one companion, with another person remaining outside. Set a conservative return time measured by the person on the surface. Agree beforehand that a missed check-in calls for a planned response, not an impulsive descent by the last person available.

The references to future tissue and water drawn in different winters are enough reason to distrust simple timing assumptions. They are not proof that your watch will fail. Bring watches anyway, but do not make any single clock or communication link your only safeguard.

## Dress for cold that will also make you wet

This is not dry cold. Water films, sugary liquid, condensation, and freezing outer layers will steadily compromise ordinary winter clothing.

Wear:

- A moisture-managing base layer and substantial insulation that retains usefulness when damp.
- A waterproof, abrasion-resistant outer layer with sealed cuffs and as few loose straps as possible.
- Insulated waterproof boots with good traction and relatively simple, inspectable soles.
- Warm liners beneath waterproof gloves, plus a spare dry pair sealed separately.
- Head and neck insulation, and a face covering you can replace when it becomes wet or iced.
- A helmet and two independent, broad-beam lights, with batteries protected from the cold.

Leave exposed fleece, tassels, mesh pockets, and dangling cords at home. They offer too many places for seeds and filaments to attach. Smooth surfaces will not make you immune to attachment, but they will make inspection easier.

Carry a complete dry change and emergency insulation at the surface. Bring your own drinking water and ready-to-eat food. **The cucumber smell is not evidence that the pools are drinkable.** Dissolved sugar explains some of their behavior, not their biological compatibility with you.

If multiverse travel has given you access to a genuinely reliable sealed environmental suit, this is a sensible place to use it—provided you can still climb, see your feet, and fit through the exit. Do not trade mobility for impressive equipment.

## Check the air, but respect the limits of the check

A crowded, poorly ventilated hollow full of respiring organisms deserves atmospheric testing before entry and during the visit. A conventional oxygen and carbon-dioxide monitor would be useful. It would not certify that unfamiliar vapors, spores, or fine biological material are harmless.

A particulate respirator may reduce inhaled material; it cannot replace missing oxygen. Do not enter an unsafe atmosphere on the strength of a filter mask.

Keep your exertion low. Sweating inside your insulation will become a problem quickly, and fatigue will make the climb out harder. If you become unusually breathless, confused, clumsy, or sleepy, leave immediately rather than deciding whether the cause is cold, air, or something stranger.

## Bring light, not heat

Let your eyes adjust near the entrance. The blue light reveals edges before surfaces, which means an apparently empty space may contain a transparent leaf, a film of water, or an animal pressed flat against vegetation.

Use a low-heat lamp at changing angles. A second angle is especially valuable around the pool: one view can make a leaf look like a bottom.

Do not use flames or improvised heaters. Your body already introduces a warm object into a cold-regulated system. Keep any additional thermal disturbance small.

Most importantly, **do not test the dark regulating leaves with your bare hand**. Their ability to remove heat without warming the adjacent space means ordinary expectations about a cold object becoming less cold on contact may not apply. There is no reason to find out how that behaves against human skin.

Avoid the leaves, their margins, and nearby vegetation. Do not sever a sample. The description makes clear that the ability belongs to an intact living network; a detached leaf would give you damaged tissue while leaving the hollow with an injury.

## Learn to walk again

The floor is not ground in the ordinary sense. It is living structure under compression, with animals below it and germinating seeds at its surface.

Move slowly and place each foot deliberately. Do not jump between ridges or stamp to test their strength. If you use a probe, make it blunt and use it lightly; piercing the floor would tell you something only by damaging it.

Inspect your soles frequently, particularly after pausing. A seed that has merely caught in the tread is different from one whose threads have begun fastening themselves around it. Do not assume you can wait until the end of the visit to deal with attachments.

At the same time, do not let the fear of standing still drive you into hurried movement. The sensible rhythm is a short observation, a sole check, and a careful repositioning—not constant pacing.

Avoid sitting or kneeling directly on the floor. You would expose more clothing to water and attachment while putting a broader load on uncertain support.

When you feel the soft upward pressure of the large animal beneath the roots, stop advancing. Give it room. Do not follow the movement or attempt to provoke a second footfall.

## Treat navigation as observation, not measurement

A compass may be useful outside and misleading inside. Pace counts, straight sightlines, reflections, and a map drawn from apparent distances deserve similar caution.

Record sequences of local features:

> Past the split seed, around the thick runner, with the hanging leaf on the left.

That is more useful here than “six paces west.”

Photographs can help establish what you actually passed, but they are records, not guarantees that the route remains unchanged. Keep your first visit within a short, readily reversible route from the seam. There is no need to reach the far wall.

The animals’ curved trails are evidence worth respecting. Do not straighten a route simply because it looks inefficient. But do not assume that following a snail exactly makes a path safe for a human: you are larger, heavier, warmer, and apparently unable to smell the distinctions it uses.

Avoid the visible faults altogether. A leaf with coincident tips or water accumulating in a shadow is not an invitation to conduct a quick experiment. The surrounding new growth suggests that even the hollow has not settled the problem.

A guideline might help in an ordinary cave, but here it can snag, acquire attachments, or cut against living tissue. Do not regard a rope as proof that the return path is secure, and do not tie one to a root whose role you cannot assess.

## Leave the central pool alone

Do not wade, reach beneath the transparent leaves, or lower equipment between them.

Its apparent bottom is not a bottom. Its inhabitants occupy narrow spaces you could easily crush, and the liquid presents both a cold-exposure hazard and an unknown contamination risk. If something falls in, be prepared to lose it.

Observe the central bud from the edge, if it can be seen without leaning over the water. The animals consistently leave space around it. You need not decide whether that is caution, instinct, or part of their development to take the hint.

Do not disturb the bubbles, touch the membrane, or try to open the seam. Nothing suggests that the bud is waiting for a visitor to help it hatch. The chamber is making room over many winters; your curiosity has no claim on that timetable.

## Expect the sound to affect your judgment

The drumming, ringing, sucking water, and occasional wooden reports will eventually become background. That is precisely when you may stop noticing changes.

Establish simple hand signals with your companion before entering. Keep checking one another rather than shouting over the hollow. Avoid headphones or heavy hearing protection that cuts you off from structural sounds.

A sudden change in the established soundscape—especially together with shifting roots, falling vegetation, or altered water levels—is a reason to withdraw. It may be harmless growth, but a visitor has no basis for distinguishing that from a developing hazard.

## Leave early, and leave clean

Turn back while your hands are still dexterous, your boots still flexible, and the entrance still easy to recognize. Do not use the first sign of hypothermia as your scheduled departure signal.

At the surface, inspect every seam, tread, cuff, and strap. Keep suspect gear contained until it can be examined safely; do not carry attached seeds or filaments into another landscape. Do not apply disinfectants or scrape debris into the entrance. Plan your cleaning and containment beforehand, with the same care you give to avoiding the introduction of foreign organisms on the way in.

Take no animals, water, leaves, or seeds as souvenirs.

The right preparation is partly equipment and partly temperament. Go ready to see very little, touch almost nothing, and leave with unanswered questions. This place is not an abandoned chamber or a finished marvel. It is a crowded, unfinished living space, and you will be an enormous warm visitor standing briefly inside its development.
