Prepare for this place as an exposed alpine site with a deceptively sheltered interior—not as a refuge that happens to be near a summit. The notch keeps most of the wind off you, but it does not keep out cold, altitude, falling stone, or the consequences of a bad step.

Its most dangerous quality may be that it feels contained. Everything is within reach; there is a seat-sized shelter and water someone once tended. Beyond that small human scale is an enormous drop, and beneath it is a landscape being slowly pried apart by ice.

## Before you arrive

**Establish whether an ordinary human can breathe here.** Clouds below you do not give a reliable altitude, particularly in another universe. Obtain atmospheric pressure, oxygen availability, temperature, and weather information from a probe or previous visitor if possible. If the atmosphere is unfamiliar, test its composition rather than relying on a pressure reading or the fact that someone once made a fire.

If conditions resemble a high terrestrial mountain, acclimatize before visiting. Arriving instantly by portal would spare you the climb, not the effects of suddenly reduced oxygen. Plan a short first visit, with an immediate route back to a breathable, lower-altitude environment. Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, or dizziness warrant ending the visit; confusion, poor coordination, or breathlessness at rest demand urgent evacuation.

**Know exactly how you will leave.** A portal or retrieval device should be usable with gloves, poor visibility, and an injured leg. Its return point must not depend on standing on one particular loose flake or squeezing between shifting slabs. If you are approaching on foot, the route requires its own mountaineering assessment. The description offers no evidence of a safe way up or down.

Do not go alone. A companion can notice impaired judgment before you do, although a notch this confined is no place for a large party.

## What to bring

Pack for an unplanned cold wait, even if you intend to stay only an hour.

- **Layered insulation and a windproof, waterproof outer shell.** Sunshine may warm your chest while the stone behind your back remains freezing.
- **Warm gloves with enough dexterity for buckles and controls**, plus a spare pair. Wet gloves become a serious liability here.
- **Sturdy boots with reliable grip.** Bring traction equipment only if reconnaissance indicates it is appropriate; spikes do not make loose stone stable.
- **A climbing helmet.** The likely falling objects are small chips and flakes, but a small impact can also startle you into a fall.
- **High-protection wraparound sunglasses or glacier glasses, sunscreen, and lip protection.** Light reaches you from the sky, clouds, and pale walls. Keep clear eye protection available for dimmer conditions.
- **A headlamp and spare power.** Reflected evening light is not a dependable departure plan.
- **All your drinking water, in an insulated container, and easy-to-eat food.**
- **An insulating sitting pad and emergency bivouac protection.** Neither the stone shelter nor a shiny emergency blanket alone is an adequate overnight plan.
- **A first-aid kit, communication or retrieval equipment, and protected spare batteries.**

Keep your pack compact and free of dangling straps. In shoulder-wide passages, a caught strap can turn an ordinary movement into a loss of balance.

If ropes are needed, bring someone competent to assess anchors in fractured alpine rock. Do not assume that an upright slab is secure because it is taller than you. This is not a suitable place to learn ropework.

## Moving through the notch

On arrival, stop in the safest available position and let your eyes adjust. Identify the open edge, overhead slabs, wet rock, frost, and your exit before exploring.

**Look at every foot placement.** The neat stacks in the hollows are loose debris, not steps. A broad slab may be undercut at its base. A handhold may be a thin plate resting against another plate. Avoid pulling outward on the rock or bracing yourself by pushing opposing slabs apart.

The brightness can make nearby and distant surfaces appear to merge. Use small changes in viewpoint to recover depth before reaching or stepping. Do not walk while looking upward at the summit or outward across the clouds. Stop first.

Treat the open end as a cliff edge even where the cloud surface makes it look as if there is something below to land on. Stay well back; the description does not tell you how sound the lip is.

Fresh blue fractures deserve attention, but they are not a complete stability map. They indicate comparatively recent breakage, not necessarily a break you can date precisely. Old gray or yellow surfaces can still detach. Do not test loose pieces by kicking them, and do not throw stones over the edge—there may be people or routes below the cloud.

## Water and shelter: admire them, do not depend on them

The basins are evidence of patient work, not a guaranteed water supply.

Their contents may be frozen, nearly absent, or rich in dissolved minerals. Clear water is not necessarily safe water. Boiling or ordinary filtration would not reliably remove dissolved contaminants, and neither should be assumed to make unfamiliar multiverse water potable. Bring your own.

Do not chip through the ice, scrape the mineral rims, or move the wedges in the fitted wall. That little structure may now depend on a few contacts that are difficult to see.

The roofed recess should likewise be treated as an old structure beneath a heavy slab, **not automatically as a safe shelter**. Freeze–thaw action may have loosened its supports. Inspect from outside without disturbing it; if its stability is uncertain, stay out.

The smoke stain tells you that someone burned something there. It does not establish that a fire is safe now. Local heating could stress cold stone, and fuel would need to be carried in. For a brief visit, bring insulation rather than planning to recreate the fire.

Leave the grooves alone. Six marks and a possible seventh are not enough to tell you whether they counted days, visits, people, or something else.

## Choose your hour carefully

For a first visit, aim for a verified clear-weather daylight window, after the morning mist has lifted and well before the floor loses sunlight. That is a planning preference, not a guarantee: warming can also release ice and loosen fragments.

Before dawn, mist can obscure the edge and coat useful footholds with rime. Later, liquid water creates slick surfaces. After sunlight leaves, cooling and freezing begin another round of small movements within the stone.

The clicks are not necessarily signs of imminent collapse, but they are reminders that the notch is active. Increasing debris, visible movement, or a newly opening crack is reason to withdraw—not to stay and identify the sound.

Set your departure by a clock and conditions, not by how bright the clouds remain. There may still be beautiful light long after it has become too cold to handle equipment comfortably.

## What visiting will feel like

Expect to move slowly and to spend more effort on ordinary actions than you anticipated. Taking off a glove, finding somewhere to sit, or turning around with a pack will require decisions. There is nowhere to stop attending to your body.

Once you are safely stationary, the place will reward stillness. A drop, a shifting ice plate, or a flake settling into a hollow may be the largest event you witness. The worked basin and cramped recess will make the absence of their builder unusually tangible.

You do not need to stay until night to understand that absence.

The best visit is one in which you arrive self-sufficient, touch very little, and leave while departure is still easy. The notch can offer a few minutes of shelter. Do not ask it to promise more.
