I think I would live in one of the rooms that had been taken out of use, but not yet surrendered.

It would be above a kitchen in the northern row. The stairs would have a rope handrail, and halfway up there would be a pale rectangle where a cupboard had been removed. My rent would be low on the understanding that I watched the back wall for mold and did not ask for the whole upper floor to be restored. In winter I would sleep close to the chimney, which would be warm for an hour after supper and then merely less cold than the room.

At first I would keep my belongings in the middle of the floor.

This would be something I had been advised to do on arrival. I would take it too literally. My bed, trunk, and small table would form a crowded island, with a generous passage around them that I seldom used. Eventually the woman downstairs would come up carrying clean sheets and ask whether I was expecting the walls to lunge.

She would help me move the bed.

I would have come with tools for working material that had stopped growing: a plane, two saws, a brace, a box of bits, clamps with mismatched handles. Somewhere else, these would have constituted an ordinary trade. Here they would make me useful in a limited and sometimes irritating way.

I would fit shutters. Repair drawers. Make removable covers for inspection holes, rails for drying clothes, boxes that could be lifted clear when the floor needed opening. There would be enough work, although not always enough money attached to it. People would bring me chairs with uneven legs and explain what the floor had done. Sometimes it would be the chair.

I would keep a few lengths of dry timber above the bench. Children would run their fingers along the sawn ends, examining the rings. The town’s trained fibers would be familiar to them; this round, compressed account of summers would be less so.

“Which way was up?”

I would show them the direction of the grain, then admit that this did not tell us which end had been nearer the roots.

They would find that an unsatisfactory answer.

So would I, a little.

—

My workroom would occupy the front half of a disused store. The rear half would remain partitioned off because its floor had softened, and because nobody had decided who should pay to restore it. Through the partition I would occasionally hear a drop fall a considerable distance.

I would ask about this.

“Known,” someone would say.

For several weeks that would reassure me. Then I would learn to ask who knew, and when they had last looked.

The wall-tender would come by once a month, sometimes more often. She would borrow a narrow chisel because hers was too valuable to misuse in the particular way she had in mind. I would object, then lend it to her. She would return it sharpened to an angle I disliked.

We would conduct this disagreement for years.

From her I would learn to leave more clearance above a door than below it, and never to assume that a straight crack meant a simple movement. From me she might acquire the habit of putting screws in a saucer rather than on the nearest sill. Neither of us would acknowledge these exchanges as instruction.

My first substantial job would go wrong.

A family would commission a cupboard for a cool pantry. I would make it square, sound, and handsome enough to feel pleased while carrying it across the court. I would leave a gap around it. I would fix it on brackets so that the floor could move beneath it.

In the second month its doors would bind.

I would plane them. They would bind again.

Eventually the wall-tender would remove everything from the bottom shelf, lie on the pantry floor, and point out that the living bracket sockets were moving at different rates. The cupboard was still square. It was being asked to lean in two directions.

We would take it down. I would make it a freestanding cupboard with adjustable feet.

The family would be decent about this, which would make it worse. For a long time afterward I would visit on small pretexts and find an excuse to open both doors.

—

There would be a stool in my workroom that I kept meaning to repair.

One of its stretchers would have split near the joint. I would wind cord around it temporarily, and the cord would darken with handling until it looked like part of the design. Visitors would choose the other stool. I would sit on the damaged one, partly from habit and partly because I knew exactly how far I could lean.

I would like work that ended with a thing moving freely in its opening. A drawer closing under the pressure of one finger. A shutter that could be fastened without reaching into the rain.

This preference would sometimes make me impatient with people.

The woman who repaired clothes would ask me to put her dismantled wardrobe back together in a downstairs room. I would suggest reducing its height. There would be good reasons: the ceiling, the awkward proportions, the difficulty of ever moving it again.

She would say no.

I would begin to explain more carefully.

“I heard you.”

After that I would assemble it at its original height. The ornamental top would have to remain wrapped in a blanket beneath her bed. I would regard this as an unfortunate compromise until, some months later, I saw her open the wardrobe for a customer’s coat. Her hand would go directly to the hook inside, without looking. There would be small penciled marks on the inner face of the door.

I would not ask what they measured.

—

I don’t think I would become someone who understood the town as a whole. I would know certain doorways very well. I would know which households wanted me to knock even when their doors stood open, and where payment would be left under an upturned cup. I would know the weight of the weighing-room hatch and the trick of lifting its far edge before pulling.

There would still be passages under my own street that I had never seen.

In meetings, I would initially sit near the door, feeling that the long-standing disputes belonged to people with longer-standing lives. Later I would discover that my store’s drainage had appeared on a diagram and that three other people had been discussing it for weeks. I would move my chair closer to the table.

Someone would borrow my kettle and not bring it back. I would acquire another, then recover the first during a meeting and carry it home with an unreasonable sense of victory.

That might be when I had properly arrived.

Not when I learned the names of the growth seams, or made something that lasted. When I had become capable of being petty about a kettle in a place where I intended to remain.

At night I would sweep the shavings into a sack. The clean ones would go for kindling; painted scraps and oily dust would be kept separate. I would check the partition at the back, turn off the bench lamp, and stand a moment while my eyes adjusted to the street.

Upstairs, I would have fitted a wooden latch to my room. Its catch would be adjustable, with two screws in slotted holes. Every few months I would shift it a little.

Inside would be the bed against the wall, a jug covered with a plate, socks drying on the back of a chair. On the table, a shutter hinge someone had brought after I had put my tools away.

I would pick it up again before taking off my coat.
