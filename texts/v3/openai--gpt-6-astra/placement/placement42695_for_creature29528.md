At first I think I am in the water pocket beneath the road.

The light is coming through something alive. Above me, a shadow spreads into five long parts and gathers itself again. Pressure travels across the ceiling. There is a sweetness nearby, almost accessible, and somewhere farther off a taste that makes me draw back.

But I am not floating. I have weight. More of it than is convenient.

One of my feet is asleep.

I am lying in the fold between the causeway and a narrow reinforcing root, directly underneath the tender. The fold has been getting smaller for several seasons. Once I could turn around in it. Now I enter headfirst and leave backward, an arrangement I continue to consider satisfactory.

The green light comes through a leaf caught over the entrance. I dragged it here. Its stalk is still attached farther along the seam, so it has remained fresh, though the effort of keeping it pulled down has begun to change the shape of my bed.

When the tender shifts their painful hip, a little yellow moisture runs into the fold.

I open my mouth.

***

I would be one of the pale, six-footed animals that sleep across doorways.

An old one, though not so old that anyone is allowed to move me without objection.

My front feet are good for holding. The middle pair does most of the clinging; the hind pair pushes, scratches, and occasionally forgets to let go. Under my jaw hang two soft water sacs. One fills more slowly than the other. This gives my face a lopsided appearance, especially after a dry day, and causes strangers to stroke the wrong side first.

I bite strangers who do this.

Not hard enough to lose access to their kitchens.

My coat is short and colorless except where substances have become permanent. There is green along my belly, reddish staining at the mouth, and a dark patch between my shoulders where I habitually pass beneath the same greasy fastening. The children have washed this patch. It returns because the fastening remains greasy.

When I am full of water, I weigh enough to be resented.

When I am empty, people come looking for me.

I know the difference in their footsteps.

***

I did not always live here.

There was another household, with a sleeping shelf that sloped toward the wall. A person used to put their heels against my back to keep from sliding. They had a rough place on one foot. I remember that more clearly than their face.

The kitchen there was below the doorway. Steam came up through a split in the ledge, and I would lie with my nose over it until someone pushed a stopper into the opening. I learned to remove the stopper. They made a larger one.

There were arguments.

Then, for a long time, there was work.

The floor tilted. Fresh braces appeared where I walked. The people slept closer together and moved their possessions into baskets. One morning they went along a route I knew, carrying so much that no one had a hand free to scratch me. I went ahead to the next resting place.

They did not arrive there.

I waited where the cool water gathered. Later I went back, but the route had acquired an underside where its upper surface should have been, and beyond that was a smell of torn stems.

I do not have an account of this that could be put into a cord.

For a while, whenever I heard baskets rubbing against each other, I followed.

***

The tender with the cup first found me licking a dressing off a graft.

It was a good dressing. Fermented pith, mineral scrapings, something fatty. They pulled me away by the skin behind my shoulders. I attached four feet to the path and kept eating.

They said something sharp.

I said something sharp with my teeth.

This established much of our relationship.

They took me home because I had emptied both water sacs and become slow. There were children there, smaller then. One tried to give me a name by repeating a sound whenever I looked up. I learned to look up when food accompanied it. The child considered the matter settled.

The other child was afraid of my feet.

I spent that night across the doorway.

In the morning the household had to step over me to scrape the frame. By the third morning, I understood that this was a dependable source of breakfast.

I have stayed through the widening of two sleeping shelves and the replacement of the kitchen wall. The child who feared my feet now lifts each one to inspect the pads. I tolerate this until the examination becomes interesting to other people.

There is a room elsewhere that I also consider mine.

Its occupants disagree, but they leave the door open.

***

Most of my world is close enough to lick.

I know the road by its sequence of flavors: cool sweating grain, sour old scar, dry strip beneath a canopy, the place where someone habitually spills broth. A stretch of newly trained surface is bewilderingly uniform. I cross it quickly, with my belly low.

Beyond the rail there are immense arrangements of green and darkness. They alter the wind. Sometimes they bring the smell of animals whose dung I have investigated, though I have never seen the animals themselves.

I look outward when a membrane-crossing creature comes near.

Its fringes stir. It carries a thin, exhausted smell. My mouth begins making the preparations it makes for hunting, but there is nowhere to put my feet.

Once, when I was young, I put a foot out anyway.

The scar is hidden beneath my middle arm.

Someone caught me. I remember the squeeze around my chest and being angry about it. For days afterward I would not let that person touch me.

Fear comes later than you might expect, in my kind. Sometimes it arrives at an entirely different place.

***

I am not especially good at finding defects.

This is important.

People occasionally watch an animal hesitate and decide it knows something. I have caused a useful little stair to be inspected because I disliked a new wash applied to the third tread. On another occasion I slept comfortably over a failing attachment until the sound of tools woke me.

What I know is where water ought to be.

There is a place beneath this seam where I press my tongue every evening. It is a small concavity, just deep enough to hold a mouthful, replenished by sweat from the adjoining bark. I have polished it. The tender once put a finger into it, looked at me, and made a noise I associate with household damage.

Tonight it tastes wrong.

Not bad. There is simply water arriving from a different direction.

I stay under the road and lick again.

Above me, the tender has both palms spread. The child is beside them. I can feel their separate weights, and the little interruptions as the child changes position.

The great leaf turns.

My hollow fills quickly.

I drink what I can, but one sac is already tight and the other has the old difficulty. Water escapes past my mouth and runs down my breast.

I back out of the fold.

This takes time. My hind feet must find the lip, then my middle feet must release in the correct order. I have done it thousands of times. Tonight a strip of bark catches the dark patch on my shoulders.

I pull.

The strip comes loose.

There is a small sound, louder through my teeth than through the air.

***

The tender reaches over the edge and finds me wet.

They are displeased, initially, because I have brought a piece of the seam with me.

Then they touch the exposed wood.

I climb onto the road while they lean down. The child puts both hands around my chest and tries to turn me toward them. This is the position that means water will be wanted.

I refuse. I have just had an inconvenience.

The child waits, scratches beneath my middle arm, and tries again.

I yield a little.

Below us, the tender makes a low call against the root.

Someone answers through the floor.

I do not know what they say. I know that the tender’s movements change. They bring the animal cup nearer and open the lid. They no longer pause to rest their hip.

The child is sent away with a cord.

I follow for a short distance in case this involves supper, then return because supper has already arrived and is sitting unattended in a folded leaf.

There is a soft piece set aside.

I eat it.

It was intended for the seam-feeders. We have had this disagreement before.

***

Later there are more people.

They put a temporary binding around the root, passing it beneath the causeway with weighted lines. This closes the entrance to my fold. I stand near the opening while they work, expressing myself at intervals.

One of them offers me a strip of peel.

I eat it and resume.

They do not reopen the fold.

The tender, crouching stiffly now, takes the leaf I used as a curtain and draws it out from beneath the new cord. They do this carefully enough to preserve the stalk. Then they tuck it into another sheltered place, farther from the seam.

I investigate.

The floor is too smooth. There is no proper depression for my hip. Air comes in at the back.

I leave.

For a while I walk around their tools, placing my feet wherever they are about to need their hands. At last the tender lifts me, with the small grunt they make when they ought to have asked someone else.

They settle me against their ribs.

I am too large to be carried this way comfortably. One of my hind feet dangles. My full water sac presses against their arm.

Still, there is warmth here. The familiar bitter smell of work clothes. Beneath it, the slower smell that belongs only to this person.

I fold my front feet.

Their hand, sticky with sap, rests between my shoulders.

Tomorrow I will go back to the blocked entrance. I will probably go back many times. Eventually the new hollow will take the shape of my sleeping, and I will have reasons to dislike any change to it.

For now the road is busy beneath us.

When the next turning begins, the tender puts their free hand flat against the grain.

I wake enough to put one foot beside it.
