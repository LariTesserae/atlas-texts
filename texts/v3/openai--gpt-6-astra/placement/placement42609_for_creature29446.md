I would look first at the cut end of the conduit. It offers a back, a roof, an opening small enough to attend to. From there I could receive the chamber a little at a time: a fly, a tremor, the change in air when the stair door opens.

But the spider is already there. To enter would be to disturb the few threads that make the opening useful.

Behind the cable trays, too, there is less room than there appears to be. The rat’s tail occupies a gap that looks vacant until it moves. Even the nest’s discarded material has occupants. I would have to become smaller, and being smaller would not by itself make a place available.

I think I would end up below her route, where the unfinished pavement meets the clay.

There is a chip missing from the underside of the curb. It was probably knocked away before the last workers left. The broken surface is coarse, with two grains of aggregate projecting from it, and between them a little clay has lodged. Water reaches it when the chamber is nearly ready to pump. When the water falls, the recess stays wet.

At first I might be only something caught there.

A fragment washed from the street. A particle from a dropping, softened until its edges no longer distinguish it from the clay. Something with a surface that other things can attach to.

Then a faint slick would develop around it.

That is closer to what I would be: a living film in the broken place beneath the curb. Not the whole green stain, not the whole population of the puddle. A patch perhaps as long as one of the rat’s toes, mostly without color. Seen under a worker’s torch, I would look like concrete that had failed to dry.

My extent would be uncertain. A few cells would lie apart from the thicker growth, attached to neighboring grains. Between them, strands of material would hold fine dirt against the pull of draining water. There would be places where I was actively growing and places where almost everything had stopped. A larva could graze through the middle and leave two patches where there had been one.

I would not experience that as being cut in half. Both sides would continue with what reached them.

The pump would matter enormously.

Before it started, water would deepen beneath the curb and cover the projecting grains. The supplies dissolved in it would change as the puddles joined. Within my thicker parts, oxygen would be used faster than it arrived. Different lives would become active there from those at the exposed surface.

Then the clack, transmitted through the curb.

Then the draw.

For a short while, the water would move more quickly past my opening. Loose material would lift away. Something that had lodged against me through several cycles might turn once and disappear. As the level fell, the broad submerged surface would become a narrow wet margin. The underside of the chip would hold a bead after the water below it had gone.

I would persist inside that bead and in the dampness behind it.

This would make the recess important in a way no plan of the square could show. A thumb pressed beneath the curb could destroy most of it. A more effective pump might leave it dry for too long. For now, neither event would occur.

The rat would pass overhead.

I could not know her split ear. I would have no use for the shape by which a person recognizes her, and no sense of her journeys beyond the chamber. Some of her weight would reach the curb as vibration. Sometimes a crumb would fall close enough to change what could grow here.

Once, perhaps, she would pause with one hind foot at the broken edge. A claw would loosen a grain. It would drop into the shallow water and lodge against my outermost layer.

Over the next several cycles I would grow onto it.

There would be no decision to enlarge. The grain would simply remain wet, and material would arrive, and the new surface would become occupied. Elsewhere, a grazing mouth would clear a strip back to bare mineral. My shape would change without ever being available to me as a shape.

Above, the two young rats would still be too large for the sleeping places made when they were small. The seedling would extend another root along its seam. The spider would replace a strand.

Under the curb, the pump would leave less water than it had the previous time. A little silt had shifted near the intake.

My outer edge would dull as it dried. Farther in, behind the two projecting grains, a clear bead would remain. Into it, slowly, the clay would release what the last rise of water had brought.
