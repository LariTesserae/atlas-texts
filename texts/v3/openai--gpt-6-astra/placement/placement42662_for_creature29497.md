At first I would find myself beside the animal, in the strip of water sheltered by its flank. The current would arrive there softened, carrying particles that turned once and settled. Above me, the breathing fold would lift and lower. There would be room to remain, provided I moved when it moved.

But when it began to cross the court, I think I would feel the effort of crossing. The delay along the damaged side. The longer hold on the right.

I would be the animal.

I would know where I was by what could hold me.

Under the first feet, mud. Under the next, the rounded rim of a buried tube. Farther forward, a living surface that yielded and withdrew. My weight would pass between these contacts, never arriving anywhere all at once. Some part of me would still be leaving the wall while another had begun to encounter the open court.

There would be no open court in my knowledge of it. There would be an interval of poor purchase, water touching both sides, and then, if I continued, firmness.

I would continue a little crookedly.

The missing feet would not be an old injury to me. They would be part of how a crossing was made. The right side would hold; the left would search. On a smooth incline I would turn before I slipped, except when the current changed, or something on my back caught, or the surface was newly coated and gave way beneath me.

Then the whole body would shorten. My plates would press together. For a while I would be difficult to distinguish from the thing that had stopped me.

What I carried would make itself known mostly through resistance.

The sponge would be weight without an edge. The little shell would be a particular interruption, something that required a twist in certain passages. I would have no means of separating its growth from the gradual narrowing of a familiar opening. Both would ask the same movement of me.

Above my reach, the worms would feed.

When I rested in good water, their crowns would extend from my back. My folds would move beneath them. Through the sponge and the old tubes, the current would take many small routes before leaving us. A minute animal might spend its life among those routes without touching the basin floor.

I would carry it to the broken wall.

***

There would be a place near the seep where food reached my fingers before the water troubled my breathing.

I would spend a long time there.

The forward margin would settle over a fold of bacterial mat. My mouth would work underneath. The feet behind would remain attached to a rough piece of shell, and the outermost fingers would lift, lower, lift again.

As the tide weakened, the favorable strip would become narrower.

There would be no boundary I could point to, even with a finger. Food would remain ahead. My breathing folds would move faster. I would raise the front of my body and encounter more of the same water.

For several moments I might stay.

Then a cooler thread would touch one side, and I would follow it sideways, leaving some of the richest surface uneaten.

That would be an ordinary departure. No alarm would pass through the settlement. Behind me the grazing tracks would begin to soften.

At the broken wall I would find a crevice containing something recently dead. Its softness would come away under my mouth. Small legs would touch the edge of my body and disappear.

I would press down.

One scavenger would remain in a hollow I could not close. It would feed there while I fed, its movements faint against my underside. Later, when I lifted to pass over a ridge, it would stay behind with the scrap.

I would go on with less hunger and no account of what had eaten beside me.

***

When the capsules were beneath me, I would cross the court differently.

The middle feet would reach around them. My body would stay lifted where, at another season, it would have rested against the floor. In the steady passage I would stop often, and the small appendages near my mouth would work backward through the rows.

A grain caught between two capsules would occupy me until it loosened.

There would be larger things beyond the passage: hunters leaving their holes, larvae entering the basin, a weakened tower settling by a fraction of its height. I would attend to the grain.

It would come free and fall between my feet.

Later, the sheltered space beneath me would fill with movements too light to resemble the scavengers. A fold would lift. Water would enter. Some of those movements would cease against my skin.

I would keep the passage.

For a while, I would continue cleaning the places where the capsules had been.

***

I think the nearest thing to a familiar room would be the recess under the old arch.

To enter it I would have to turn, compress, and advance with one side folded more tightly than the other. The projecting shell would scrape. My longest finger would already be through, resting in the water beyond.

Then the shell would break away.

The release would travel through my plates. I would contract against nothing, hold, and slowly extend again.

Afterward the recess would admit me more easily. I would still begin the old twist for several tides.

On my back, beyond anything I could clean or examine, the exposed plate would receive sediment, then lose it. A larva would touch down near the ragged sponge. While I rested, it would stay. When I moved, its first attachment would hold.

I would take the lower passage because the water there had changed.

Above me, the new tube would be scarcely longer than a grain. Beneath me, the firm rims would arrive one after another. The right feet would hold a little longer. The left would find their next place.

By the time we reached the wall, something on my back would have begun to feed.
