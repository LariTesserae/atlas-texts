I keep noticing a building beside one of the long empty stretches of tramway.

It is not abandoned. The gravel has been brushed away from its thresholds, and someone has put a dark sleeve over the outside door handle so that it can be used in the afternoon. But the destination board lists a district that is not visible. A second, smaller sign points away from the tracks toward what appears to be an uninterrupted plain.

From the tram, I would assume it was a maintenance office.

Walking past, I would notice the warming rail through the window.

Going inside, I would discover that the building continues down into the rock. There is a public room, an office, a short stair, and then another door with a great deal of machinery attached to its frame. Beyond that is the far end of Saret’s linkage tunnel.

I think I would be found here.

Not deep in the machinery, exactly. In the part of the building where someone has to decide whether a noise warrants going downstairs.

***

At first I imagine myself keeping records.

There would be satisfaction in the long drawers, the gauge strips laid in their fitted recesses, the reports that arrive with grit folded into them. Each engine would have several histories: what was installed, what was altered, what the instruments said, what the caretakers believed had happened. These would agree often enough to make their disagreements interesting.

I would have a good hand for annotation. Small, legible characters, none of the abbreviations that become meaningless when their author retires.

And then, almost immediately, I can see the trouble with this picture.

Someone is standing at the counter with a cracked fitting. They have walked a long way. They do not need its history. They need to know whether it can remain in service until the next freight tram.

I would have to put down the record.

I would have to take the fitting in my hands.

So I would have hands: the double-closing fingers, the fine scales at the wrists, a pale abrasion where I habitually carry things against one knuckle. I would know the temperature of that fitting before I knew what to say about it. Its warmth would enter unevenly, reaching the ready parts of my hands first.

I would turn it over.

“Where was this mounted?”

They would explain.

“Was the crack open when you removed it?”

A pause.

This would be the part of the work I was good at. Not knowing immediately. Finding the question that ought to have been asked before the fitting was taken out.

There would also be a drawer for things that could not safely be returned to service. I would dislike opening it in front of people who had walked a long way.

***

I would be one of the plateau people, but not quite from this district.

My early childhood would have been spent nearer an elevator head, in rooms adjusted for a household whose members went below. I would still prefer my sleeping alcove cooler than visitors thought proper. On arriving at Saret’s court, I would be offered the warming rail twice.

The second offer would be an opinion.

My skin would be the muted orange of a piece of ceramic kept out of the light. Darker at the wrists. One shoulder would take longer than the other to warm, not because of an important injury but because bodies have inconvenient particulars.

I would own two work coats and keep wearing the one with the badly placed inner pocket.

The local children would find my vibration speech amusing.

Something in my household pronunciation would survive in it: a closure held too long, a question beginning in the heel of the hand rather than beneath the fingers. Spoken aloud, my name would cause no difficulty. Through a table it would sound, to them, faintly like a request to move a heavy object.

They would offer to help.

I would pretend not to understand until the joke became sufficiently elaborate to deserve an answer.

***

The district on the destination board would once have surrounded the tunnel entrance.

Most of it would still be there, below the slight rise to the north. Not buried, not ruined. Simply out of sight.

The families had left when a set of shallow thermal wells became unreliable. The wells were capped; the tram stop remained because the engine station remained. A few workshops still received power. An advocate in another city was handling the rest.

My quarters would occupy what had been the station’s waiting room for work crews. I would sleep behind a partition that did not reach the ceiling. On cold mornings the space above it would tick as the roof plates warmed.

The old crew kitchen would be too large for one person.

I would cultivate food films in only three of its trays, and use the others to sort small parts until someone pointed out the obvious hazard. Afterward there would be labels. Then, because labels do not cure every habit, the spare trays would be removed.

On the public counter I would keep a small heater for paired wafers.

This would not be an official provision. People would nevertheless begin to expect it. A survey crew would come through twice a season and leave complaints about the cheaper wafers, while eating all of them.

The building would smell faintly of hot insulation, cultured food, and the sour electrical odor of an animal I had repeatedly failed to locate.

***

Saret and I would first know one another through readings.

Every morning, the two ends of the engine would exchange figures. Temperatures, displacement, load, the condition of a bearing whose replacement had been postponed long enough to acquire its own file.

For several months her messages would be impersonal.

Then I would report a discrepancy between two gauges.

She would ask whether I had checked their brackets.

I would say that both gauges had been checked against the reference.

She would ask whether I had checked their brackets.

I would go downstairs.

Both instruments would be attached to the same slowly loosening support.

Thereafter, whenever I sent a particularly careful explanation, a brief pause would precede her reply. I would come to recognize the pause as something she was doing on purpose.

I would resent this a little.

Later I would discover an error in one of her old conversion tables. It would be a minor error, affecting readings taken under conditions that rarely occurred.

I would spend an unreasonable amount of time deciding how to tell her.

She would reply, “Correct it.”

Then, after a moment: “Both copies.”

***

My work would not remain inside the building.

That is where the first image was wrong. I could not live only among accounts of the place. The empty district would need walking.

I would go out to check the caps on the wells, the cable supports, the copper vanes above the buildings still connected to the station. In the first year I would carry a map. In the second I would use an upright fragment of wall to find the service lane behind the old lining workshop.

Then the wall would fall, and I would carry the map again.

I would learn how much of an empty neighborhood is still being used.

The pipe grazers would occupy the broadest court. Pale shells would gather along a warm conduit beneath the former eating house. Someone from a distant household would visit a wall chamber at intervals and leave the path better swept than they found it.

One locked house would have a new door sleeve every cool season.

I would not know who replaced it.

At first I would put notices through every accessible slot. Later I would learn which houses still had people responsible for them and which notices ought to be saved at the station. I would become careful about the word *vacant*.

Not all of this would make me wiser. Some of it would make me territorial.

I would object when a survey crew used my familiar wall fragment as fill, even though it was lying in the service lane and had ceased to be a useful landmark.

“It was loose material,” the crew leader would say.

I would know that.

***

The first time Saret walked the tunnel while I was stationed there, I would prepare too much.

Fresh food. Cleared work surfaces. All the disputed records in order, which would make it obvious that I had been thinking about them.

Her progress would be reported by the inspection contacts. Each closure would appear on the board, kilometers of dark rock reduced to a sequence of small lamps.

There would be a long interval between the last lamp and her arrival.

I would stand near the lower door, listening, then return upstairs so as not to seem to have been waiting.

When she emerged, she would remain braced against the frame while the warmth reached her legs.

I would ask about the tunnel.

“Long,” she would say.

There would be food. She would criticize the station’s warming rail, correctly. We would go through the bearing records.

Only afterward would she ask to see the public room.

She had been there before, of course. Long before my assignment. She would identify where the crew tables used to stand and a patch in the wall where a heater had failed.

Her brother had worked at that end for part of a season.

I would ask which season.

She would tell me, then say, “You don’t have to find it.”

My hand would already be on the drawer.

I would leave it closed.

***

I think I would stay long enough for people to stop explaining the station’s location by its official name.

They would tell a traveler to get off where the handle had the dark sleeve. They would say there was usually someone there.

This would be a useful reputation and occasionally an exhausting one.

A lost parcel would become my problem. So would an animal caught beneath a cabinet, a visiting relative who had mistaken the stop, and a child’s wheeled toy discovered an implausible distance from any child.

I would send the toy back on the tram.

It would return three days later with a repair request.

There would be times when I closed the shutters and did not answer the public bell until my meal was finished. I would have to learn to do this without eating hurriedly.

And there would be evenings when I went to the stop simply because the tram was due.

Not to board. To see who did.

***

Eventually, during one of Saret’s tunnel inspections, I would go in to meet her.

The instruments would not require this. Nor would she ask.

I would take a lamp, a small packet of food, and the solid-handled brush she had once told me to buy. At the first inspection recess I would stop and put my palm against the contact plate.

Her answer would arrive faintly through the local line.

*What is it?*

*Nothing. Coming your way.*

A pause. Then a short acknowledgment.

I would continue beside the linkage, keeping clear of the marked reach of its slow movement. The lamp would show the buff dust at the wall’s foot, the dark shelves above it, the bright places where generations of tools had touched the inspection housings.

For a long time there would be no sign of her.

Then another light would appear, low and very far away.

We would each have to walk quite a while before either could reasonably speak.
