At first I put myself beside the keeper in the western building. The blue light is on her hands; mine are waiting just behind them. I imagine that if I stand still long enough, the two clicks will become distinguishable, and after enough evenings I will understand why she reaches where she does.

But I am attending too much to the doorway. Each time its distance changes, I look up. Once, while I am looking, she gives an instruction through the floor and I miss it.

She does not take me inside again that evening.

I think I would continue to accompany her for a season, perhaps two. I would learn some useful things. I would also begin arriving with reasons to remain on the terrace: a frame to restring, a carrying cloth to mend, something that could quite properly be done outside.

Eventually she would stop asking whether I was coming in.

This would be a relief, and the relief would shame me enough that I would become irritatingly diligent about the work I could do.

My place would be a shallow room near the bottom of the settlement, where the path widens before descending into loose stone. Not a secluded room. Someone carrying a carcass would put one end of a pole through my entrance while making the turn. Children would come through because a gap behind my sleeping ledge leads into the next dwelling, although both households have told them not to use it.

The room would receive a narrow piece of afternoon sun. I would need that for drying membranes.

I would mend listening tools.

There would be more to this than stretching a piece of grazer skin over a frame. The skin has a direction. So does the frame, especially after years of being pressed against warm walls. A repair that sounds clear in the dwelling can grow slack beside a cold waterwork. Some keepers want the membrane tight enough to answer the smallest movement. Others find that exhausting. One wants the handle to rest against an old swelling in her palm and will reject a better-made tool because I have smoothed the wrong place.

I would learn to leave that place alone.

My hands would be good at the work without being graceful. One finger would not close fully. To hold a fine cord taut, I would catch it in the crease of the next finger, a motion apprentices would copy until I noticed and made them stop.

The room would smell of scraped hide, resin, and food I had set aside while working and then forgotten. Beneath the front of my body, where the stone stays shaded, I would keep a shallow tray of offcuts. Children could take from it without asking. This arrangement would have been established after a prolonged dispute about what counted as stealing something I had already thrown away.

Most of their inventions would fail noisily.

One would make a listening frame that could hear through a sleeping adult. For several nights there would be complaints.

I would not be old at first. I would be at the age when people begin bringing things to be repaired without explaining how they were broken. A cracked frame would arrive wrapped in cloth. A carrying strap would have been “like that” when its owner found it. I would turn these objects over, wait briefly, and mostly let the account stand.

Not always. I would have a weakness for finding the precise place where something had gone wrong.

This would make me useful with tools and less useful in arguments.

There would be someone in the adjoining household who had stopped bringing repairs to me because I once explained, in front of others, how her son had damaged a harvesting blade. I would consider this an excessive response. I would continue considering it excessive long after I understood why she had done it.

The gap behind the sleeping ledge would remain open. Her son would still come through.

Along the back wall I would keep another sort of unfinished work.

A loop of fiber whose knots had hardened. Three pieces of pink stone, only two of which belonged together. A flat object worn smooth on one edge by a thumb. These would have come to me because I repair small things, and because people find it easier to say *the cord needs replacing* than *there is nobody left who knows all of this*.

I would not know all of it either.

An old neighbor would come after midnight to teach me the turns of one stone. She would correct my grip before each account. The notch goes beneath the hand, not toward the body. No, the other hand first.

On one visit, the account would concern a winter when a seam failed. On another, with what seemed to me the same motion, it would concern a household leaving the eastern shelves.

I would ask which was right.

She would become angry, then tired. We would eat. Later, while I cleared the food tray, she would take up the stone and make a small additional turn that I had not seen before.

I would spend a long time trying to recover that turn after her death.

There would be no sudden evening when my hands completed it and the missing account returned. Sometimes I would think I had found it. I would ask someone who had known her, and they would say perhaps, or no, or that she had always held that piece strangely because of an injury.

Meanwhile the stone would acquire the polish of my own handling.

That would worry me enough to put it away for a while.

Then someone would bring a child and ask to hear what remained.

I would tell the part I knew, stopping where I had to stop. The child would ask whether the household ever came back. I would say I did not know. This would disappoint us both. Afterward we would examine the notches, and the child would discover that one of them held a seed.

There would still be the waterworks.

The scarred keeper would bring me her frames. She would eat while I worked, getting crumbs among the cord and pretending not to notice. We would have known each other long enough by then that neither of us mentioned my season of apprenticeship.

Once she would ask me to come back to the western terrace. Her tool would be giving an unfamiliar response, and she would want me to hear it against the wall before changing anything.

I would go unwillingly.

Outside, I would find that the frame had warped so slightly I could not see the difference. I would have to lay it against the building, then against a flat stone, then against the building again. The keeper would wait without helping. Behind the entrance, the blue light would deepen.

The fault would be in my repair. I would have replaced a wedge with material that swelled in the damp.

We would sit on the terrace while I cut it out.

I would be embarrassed, but less embarrassed than I had once been about staying outside. It would take most of the evening. The keeper would divide her food with me. When the seams darkened, we would have to move our tools because small animals were coming through the place where we sat.

I think I would live long enough to become particular about where things were put.

A strip of warm stone beside the entrance would remain clear for visitors, though I would deny having arranged it for that purpose. There would be one good frame hanging beyond the children’s reach and several bad ones within it. In a cool recess I would keep the old neighbor’s stone wrapped separately from the others.

I would want to see trees. I might never go.

Instead, I would cultivate some unimpressive lower-slope plant in a cracked food tray, carrying it into the light and back again. It would be susceptible to mites. My neighbor would point out that perfectly healthy plants grew outside without this attention.

When I was away, she would water it.

Near dawn, after the last repair had left the room, I would go down to the basin with a blade and a cake gone hard at the edges. I would know a stone that could be lifted without disturbing the thread beneath it. There would usually be enough film for a small meal.

Coming home, I would hear the children through my feet before I heard them in the air.

Someone would be in my room. Something would have fallen.

I would pause below the entrance, holding the warm underside of my breakfast against my body, and send three firm clicks into the stone.

Then I would go up to see what they had made.
