I would look for a room one level below the market, on a stair that people use in one direction more often than the other.

Going down, they would pass my door. Coming back with full containers, they would take the lift farther east. This would make the room a poor place to sell anything and a good place to receive things people had decided they no longer wanted.

There would be a return well nearby. Not in the room itself; I would not want to sleep above one. Across the landing, behind a municipal gate, with a platform where material could wait to be weighed.

I think I would work on that platform before I had a business of my own.

My task would be to remove whatever should not go back into the mountain. Copper. Ceramic. A hinge concealed beneath several coats of paint. People would bring down lengths of partition with the fittings still attached and explain that they had been told the well accepted building material.

I would show them the charge for separation.

Some would take their partitions home again.

At first I would keep only useful things.

***

There is a particular sort of hinge used in the older districts. Its pin has a square head, and the lower leaf is a little longer than the upper. The factory that made it changed its machinery forty years ago. The new hinges are better, but fitting one means cutting a new seat in the door and filling the old screw holes.

I have thirty-one of the old kind.

This is how I would know Eda.

She comes down the stair without looking into the room. Then she takes two steps back.

“Square-head. Long leaf.”

“How long?”

“The old long.”

“There are three.”

She puts her bags down.

I do not mind this part. I mind customers who look through the drawers themselves and leave the correct pieces in approximately the correct places. Eda waits while I bring out the hinges. She tests the movement with her thumb, holds one edge against another, rejects two I had thought were good.

“This one’s been heated.”

“It moves.”

“It’ll move until it doesn’t.”

I put it in a different tray. There are customers who need a hinge for a box.

She pays for two and asks whether I have any short screws with an unworn shoulder. I do. She says I should put that on the board outside.

There is very little room left on the board. Every time I add something, somebody asks for the thing I have erased.

***

By then I would no longer be the person employed to clear the return platform. I would rent the room, and the new attendant would bring me objects during the interval when the scales were being checked.

There would be an arrangement. It would not be wholly official, but it would not be secret. I would pay the listed salvage rate and keep the receipts under a weight beside the till.

I would be good at identifying things by the marks they left on other things.

A polished crescent on a latch tells you which way the door has been leaning. A screw whose thread is worn on one side has worked loose slowly. Two clean circles on an otherwise stained plate mean washers are missing, whatever the person selling it says.

I would be less good at deciding what was worth the room it occupied.

Behind the counter would be a large ceramic elbow for a water line no longer used anywhere in the district. It would have been expensive once. It would be uncracked. Neither fact would make it useful.

I would keep it for eleven years.

When people asked what it was for, I would tell them its original purpose rather than answer the question they meant.

***

My room would have a narrow sleeping recess at the back. The recess would be legal; the cooking plate beside it would not. There would be a vent, but it would open into a passage whose owner disputed my right to put warm air there.

For most of the year this would be an argument conducted by notes.

On cloud days, with both our doors open, we would speak.

She would be a woman who repairs seals for food containers. Her work requires a cool surface. Mine makes dust. Neither of us would be mistaken about the inconvenience caused by the other.

Sometimes she would bring me a bowl of something she had cooked in her permitted kitchen. I would return it clean, with the bottom dried. This would not settle the vent.

I would have come to the city young enough to think of my first room as temporary. Most of what I owned would consequently have been chosen for its ability to fold, nest, or come apart.

The counter would be made from two such possessions.

I would still know exactly how to dismantle it.

***

One afternoon a boy brings me a bracket wrapped in a shirt.

It is too large for him to carry comfortably. He has held it against his stomach, and there is a curved dirty mark on his clothes beneath the wrapped part.

“Will you buy this?”

“Where did it come from?”

“Our room.”

“From what in your room?”

He says it was spare.

People use *spare* to mean several things. Sometimes it means there were two and they could only see a purpose for one.

I unwrap the bracket. It is warm, but everything is warm. The useful sign is a little ridge of pale dust along its upper edge. Something has been resting there until recently.

“Was it under the floor?”

He shakes his head too quickly.

I ask him to show me.

This is inconvenient. I have a customer expected, and the boy has come farther than I thought. We go up three levels and cross a bridge where a freight carriage is making a turn so slowly that we can pass between its rear wheel and the wall.

His family lives in a divided room. There is a cloth partition, a basin, two beds arranged at an angle. The bracket’s empty seat is visible beneath the end of a beam.

His mother sees what I am carrying.

She shuts her eyes.

The boy had not removed it. His uncle had, to make room for the taller bed. The uncle said the beam was supported at both ends and the bracket was an old precaution.

This may be true. I sell old precautions all the time.

I put it back.

The bed has to be moved. There is nowhere satisfactory for it to go. We leave it obstructing the cupboard, with one leg raised on a piece of packing stone, and I write down the name of a foundation inspector who sometimes takes small jobs after his shift.

The mother asks what she owes me.

I tell her the price of a bracket like that.

“But it’s ours.”

“Yes.”

She looks at me for a while. Then she laughs once, without being pleased, and gives the boy his shirt.

On the way back I am annoyed with the uncle, whom I have not met, and with myself for failing to ask whether there were any other things he had taken out.

***

I would not be generous in every direction.

I would resent lending tools. I would remember small debts after deciding aloud to forget them. If someone brought me a fitting I particularly wanted, I might speak at unnecessary length about the difficulty of finding a buyer.

There would be a drawer of objects I had bought unfairly cheaply.

I would know which drawer it was.

But I think I would sometimes walk upstairs.

Not because I had a special duty to the city. More because, once I had seen the dust on the bracket, I would have had to spend the rest of the afternoon knowing what it meant.

***

The room would change around the stock.

For a while I would blame myself for putting the tall drawers out of square. Then a shallow dish left on the counter would begin to turn whenever a customer leaned against the front edge.

A surveyor would visit. He would make measurements, ask how long I had occupied the room, and inspect the wall behind the ceramic elbow.

Moving the elbow would require help.

Behind it would be an old inspection plate, accessible in the formal sense that nothing permanent had been built in front of it.

The surveyor would say this was fortunate.

The plate would give access to a collar adjustment. The adjustment would require the back quarter of my shop. For six weeks, perhaps longer, I would have to clear the stock and find somewhere else to sleep.

I would ask whether the work could wait until the dry interval.

He would point to the drawers.

They would still open. This would seem important to me, although I would have accepted the same sign immediately in somebody else’s room.

***

Eda would come while I was packing.

She would want a hinge I could no longer find. All the drawers would be stacked in the wrong order, their labels facing a wall.

“What are they doing?” she would ask.

I would tell her.

She would look at the counter, then at the sleeping recess.

“You’ll want that door rehung afterward.”

“It opens now.”

“Afterward.”

She would help me shift a crate far enough to get at the hinges. I would charge her the usual price. Before leaving, she would ask where I was going.

I would have arranged a bed in a room above the seal repairer’s workshop. The room would be cool and would have a window. I would describe it as if these were disadvantages.

Eda would pick up her bags.

“Take a blanket.”

***

The ceramic elbow would go back into the mountain.

Not because I had ceased to admire it. Its interior would still be exceptionally smooth. There would be a maker’s stamp on the outside, shallow but legible, and I would clean the dust out of it before carrying it across the landing.

The return attendant would turn it on the scales.

“Nothing you want off this?”

“No.”

He would tap it with the inspection rod. The note would last longer than either of us expected.

Then there would be the receipt to sign, and the question of whether I wanted the carrying cloth back.

I would.

Upstairs, my temporary bed would stand beneath the window. Across the slot would be the blank sunward wall of another building, close enough to show the fine waviness in its surface. Late in the day, reflected light would reach the ceiling.

I would put the cloth in the cupboard. I would put my blanket on the bed.

For the first few evenings I would keep hearing objects settle in the shop below and have to remind myself that someone else was working there.

Then, one morning, I would discover that the window opened.

I would have slept beside it for a week without trying.
