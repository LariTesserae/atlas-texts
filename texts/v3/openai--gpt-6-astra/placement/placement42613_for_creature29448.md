I would first be found on the causeway, carrying a stone.

Not a remarkable stone. Flat on one side, thick enough to fit the hollow beneath my forelimb, with a shallow pit where years of blows have whitened it. I have brought it from the southern channels. Here the seed cases can be opened in cracks in the floor, but I do not yet trust the cracks. Some are too wide. Some hold the seed so tightly that the kernel comes out in pieces.

With my stone I know where the pieces will go.

The water over the causeway is warm against my belly. Walking on three limbs, I keep turning a little toward the burden. Twice I stop and put it down. Each time I pick it up again.

Inside the building, the old female opens one eye.

I wait in the doorway until she closes it.

***

I would not be one of the two she admits without looking.

For a long time I would be allowed only as far as the place where the floor changes temperature. There is no mark there, but my hands find it. Behind me the stone holds the warmth of the causeway; ahead, it is cool enough to draw heat out of the pads.

I lie across this boundary with my head in the room.

When the daughter comes, I move.

Sometimes I move before she asks. Sometimes I am deeply asleep, or have eaten badly, and she must set her hand against my flank and push. Then I show my teeth without lifting my head. She pushes harder.

There is room elsewhere. That is not the difficulty.

The place she takes has the wall along one side and her mother along the other. Nothing can approach her there without crossing something she is touching.

In the doorway I wake whenever a grazer steps off the causeway.

***

My body would be young enough that its capacities still occasionally surprise me.

I can turn underwater in a space that looks too narrow from above. I can hang by one hand while searching with the other. When a green animal drops onto a discarded piece of grub, I sometimes have it in my mouth before I have seen where it landed.

But I cannot remain still against a reed as long as the old female.

I feel the water against its outside. I feel an insect move across the leaf above my head. Farther off, a grazer lifts and settles its weight. All of these reach me through the same embrace.

Somewhere within them is the small interruption that means food.

The old female leaves three stalks untouched and opens the fourth.

I open the second after she has gone. It fills with water. I search the split with my fingers anyway, stripping out wet tissue until the stem bends over my shoulder.

Later I find a grub in a reed she has passed.

I eat it quickly, with my back toward her.

***

At first the opening interests me because everyone else leaves a place beneath it.

When the room is empty, I climb.

My hind feet cannot find the worn holds immediately. My stone knocks against the wall, and I have to descend to put it down. Then I brace myself and lift my face above the sill.

Water lies in pale hollows. Birds stand in it.

I look up. Above my head is the dark underside of the roof.

I look into the opening again.

A bird spreads one wing and puts its head beneath it. Another walks through the water, lifting each foot unnecessarily high.

I reach toward them.

The surface is cool and hard. My fingers leave four marks that gradually disappear.

For several days I climb whenever I can. Not to watch the birds exactly. There is a little spill at the edge of one roof hollow, and I keep trying to locate it with the side of my face against the wall. I can see the water trembling. I ought to be able to feel where it touches stone.

What reaches my cheek is the slow disturbance of someone crossing the causeway.

In the opening, no one is there.

Then I turn and find the daughter in the doorway.

She has been standing still.

***

I would have a sleeping place before I had a place among them.

A low recess at the end of the outer room. Too shallow to hide my whole body, but deep enough for my head and hands. I carry in a few dry husks. The next morning I carry most of them out. They catch in my coat, and one contains an insect that bites the loose skin beside my mouth.

The stone stays.

On hot afternoons I push it into the recess before entering myself. At night I bring it nearer the doorway. Once a youngster rolls it onto its rounded side, and the unfamiliar shape wakes me as I come through the room.

I turn it back.

The old female keeps her blue thing farther inside. I know where. I have smelled the depression behind the bench and touched the smooth edge when no one was near.

It is not good for opening anything.

I want to carry it nevertheless.

Once I draw it halfway out, then hear her claws at the entrance. I leave it crooked in its hollow and go into the lake. For the rest of that afternoon I feed along the far side of the island.

When I return, it is no longer where I left it.

Neither of us approaches the other.

***

There would be a day on the lower shelves when I discovered that being able to reach farther is not the same as knowing where to put a hand.

The old female is harvesting worms from a mineral rib. Her daughter works nearer the basin. I go beyond them, where the pale bodies show through a thin film of moving water.

My tail is wrapped around a shrub. My right hand grips a dry knob of stone.

With my left I loosen one worm, then another.

The next is farther out. I flatten my chest and reach. The water enlarges my fingers until they seem broad enough to close around the whole rib.

Behind me, the shrub comes free.

It makes almost no sound. A little tearing. Soil falls against my hind foot.

My body slides only the width of my hand before the daughter catches my tail.

For a moment I cannot move back toward her. My gripping hand has tightened so completely that the elbow will not bend. The worms are still clenched in my other fist.

She pulls again.

On the shelf I turn and strike at her. I catch her wrist with my nails.

She releases me and moves away.

I remain flat on the stone until the old female passes. She puts one hand on my back, briefly, using me to steady herself.

I leave the worms there.

***

After that I would still go to the shelves.

I would test the shrub with my weight before turning away from it. Sometimes twice. The daughter would not always come with me, and when she did, she would keep whatever distance suited her.

There would be no single afternoon on which I became different enough for anyone to notice.

My stone would acquire another pale mark. A patch of coat beneath my forelimb would remain rubbed flat. I would learn two good cracks in the island floor and use them when I did not want to fetch it.

In the cool room, my sleeping place would shift inward by the length of a hand, then outward again during a crowded rain.

One night the daughter would be absent.

The old female would wake and scratch at the seam between her shoulders. Her hind foot would work through the outer fibers without reaching the roots. She would settle, wake, scratch again.

I would already be awake.

At the first pull she would turn so quickly that I would withdraw both hands. We would sit facing one another in the dark.

Then she would lower her head.

I would begin with the smallest leaves. Some would slip free easily. Others would draw the skin upward, and I would let those go.

There would be insects in the loosened coat. I would eat them.

Eventually she would put her muzzle down on her forearm. I would keep working until my fingers grew clumsy, then lie beside her with a little space between our flanks.

Rain would be striking the roof. Through the floor I would feel someone step onto the far end of the causeway, pause, and continue.

I would lift my head.

The old female would not.

After a while I would set it down again. My tail, stretched across the cool stone, would be touching hers.
