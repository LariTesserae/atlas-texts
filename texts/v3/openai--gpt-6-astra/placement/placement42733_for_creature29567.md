I think I would live in the maintenance house, in the room above the storeroom, where the window opens inward because there is no dependable way to reach it from outside.

There would be a bed, a stove, and a table shortened on one side to fit beneath the sloping roof. In wet weather I would have to move the bed away from the chimney. In windy weather I would move it back. The floor would bear pale scratches describing this small seasonal journey.

I would have come to repair the cable crossing.

That seems important: I would not have arrived intending to stay, or knowing much about water. I would know bearings, wire rope, the particular wear left by a pulley that no longer sits square in its housing. I would have brought things useful for a job expected to take eleven days.

The replacement assembly would still be under a tarpaulin at the abandoned station. There would be reasons we had not fitted it. At first, particular reasons: a cracked mounting plate, a missing shipment, the crossing flooded before we could take measurements. Later, just the weather.

Meanwhile, there would be work at the house.

***

I would be fairly good with my hands and less good at understanding what I had disturbed.

Early on, I would clear a channel beside the footbridge because the water had almost stopped moving through it. An afternoon’s work: cutting roots, lifting black slabs of turf with the rake, pulling out an astonishing quantity of pale, closely packed stems.

By evening the channel would run clean.

By morning the bank beside it would be gone.

The old female’s chamber would show in the broken face of the peat, a dark oval with a little shelf of lining still attached. I would stand there holding the rake. Something small would have slept on that shelf; the hollow in the grass would be plain.

Nobody would need to explain it to me.

Afterward, I would clear less at a time. Sometimes I would leave a troublesome obstruction overnight and look at it again in the morning. My colleague would complain that I was making a ceremony out of drainage.

Sometimes he would be right.

***

The house would have two inhabitants officially and more than that in practice.

My colleague would take the room nearest the stove flue. He would sleep through wind but wake whenever the pump changed speed. I would wake at all the wrong noises: a shutter knocking, birds landing on the roof, the expanding click of cold metal when we lit the stove.

The bank bears would occupy the space behind the storeroom wall.

At first I would hear only scratching. Then chewing. Then a sound like someone gently sorting small nails.

I would take a board off to investigate.

The old female would be directly behind it.

We would look at each other through a rectangle scarcely larger than her head. Her good eye would be on my side. Behind her, something would stir in the sacks.

I would put the board back.

Thereafter, that end of the storeroom would cease to be especially useful for storage. We would still call it the storeroom. Buildings keep their names longer than their purposes.

I would fix a strip of tin over the bottom of the door, because we needed the door. I would leave the damaged sacks where they were, because recovering them would require taking apart a wall occupied by a large animal with young.

These would be separate decisions. On difficult mornings I would remind myself of that.

***

I do not think I would be the worker who first used the fish box.

I would be the one who found it afterward, washed the coat, and hung it too close to the stove. One sleeve would stiffen permanently. The next time we needed it, it would have to be folded with that arm sticking up along the side of the box.

I would acquire an unreasonable attachment to this coat.

Not to its history. To the object: its remaining pockets, its broad back, its ability to be put under a dripping thing without any further deliberation.

Most of my possessions up there would become valuable in that way.

A spoon narrow enough to clean a fuel strainer. A wooden wedge with a cord through it. A pair of boots that leaked at different heights, allowing a brief, ridiculous interval in which only one foot was wet.

I would miss dry socks more often than the world below the clouds.

When the clouds opened, however, I would stop what I was doing.

The foothills would surprise me every time. I would have been imagining the plateau as the whole country again.

***

My work would gradually become a round of inspections.

The bridge first. Then the gauge. Then the fuel, the intake, the fastening on the shed door. Across to the house, where I would check the broken drain without repairing it.

I would keep a notebook in a flour tin.

Water level. Fuel used. Wind direction when I could judge it. Things to fetch if the crossing reopened.

On one page:

*Female carrying lining to house. Island by third post loose.*

Then, below it:

*Move flour.*

I would learn her routes without supposing they were permanent. She would abandon a passage I had finally recognized and appear from beneath a different bank. Once, carrying a board across the shallows, I would step into a hole where she had passed quite comfortably all week.

From the kitchen window my colleague would watch me retrieve the board.

He would wait until I reached the door before asking whether the water was deep there.

***

What would make the house mine, I think, would happen during a stoppage.

Not a great failure. A clogged fuel line, perhaps. Something repairable, at an inconvenient hour.

The pump would falter twice and stop.

For several seconds I would hear its rhythm continuing in my head. Then the other noises would separate: rain against the shed roof, the railing trembling under caught grass, water sucking at the concrete plinth.

From the islands, splashes.

I would cross to the house for a clean rag and hear the ticking inside the wall.

Usually the engine would cover it. Now there would be several sounds: the adult’s teeth, a youngster answering, stems being dragged across something rough. I would remain in the storeroom with my hand in the rag bin.

A little water would be coming through the drain.

Not much. Enough to spread across the floor toward the sacks we had not yet raised.

Behind the boards, the animals would be moving upward.

I would fetch two bricks and lift the lowest shelf. Then another two for the bin. I would take the good rope upstairs and leave the rotten piece because it was already disappearing into a gap beside a stud.

Then I would go back out and mend the pump.

There would be no moment when the female understood what I had done. By morning she might have blocked the drain again. I might have to undo half her night’s work to keep water out of the kitchen.

But before starting the engine, I would stand a moment with my hand on the switch, listening across the basin.

***

So I would be a repairer, though the cable would remain unrepaired longer than I liked.

I would live above an occupied wall, beside a road I could follow only in calm weather. I would become stooped from working under low eaves. There would be a burn on one wrist from the exhaust pipe, and a place on my thumbnail that never grew back evenly.

On the back of the storeroom door I would pencil the heights the water reached.

Some marks would have dates. Others would have whatever I remembered afterward:

*During the west gale.*

*Night the bridge shifted.*

And one, lower than I would expect when I looked at it years later:

*First ticking heard.*

Before bed I would put the kettle on, hang my socks, and go downstairs once more to make sure nothing heavy had been left in front of the broken drain.
