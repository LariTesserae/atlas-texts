I would have a room beside a passage that had ceased to be a passage.

At one end, a pressure door would have been sealed into its frame. At the other, people would still come in occasionally, expecting to get through. There would be room to turn around if I pushed my chair beneath the desk. I would learn to do this at the sound of footsteps, without looking up.

The room would contain a warm pipe, two cabinets, and a cupboard installed too low to open without moving the bed. I would keep seldom-used things in that cupboard. Eventually I would forget some of them, and be pleased and inconvenienced when a leaking cup or a missing fastener obliged me to look.

I think I would come to this room because of the cabinets. Only later would it become the place where I slept.

***

My work would begin with a question someone else had stopped having time for.

A fitter wants to know whether a valve used to have a second handle. There is a socket in its housing, but a socket is not proof. A household has found a number beneath successive layers of cupboard paint. Someone remembers that the food chamber beneath their building was once fed differently, and would like to know whether this was an improvement or a mistake.

I look through records. When a record refers to another record, I try to find that too.

There is an established allowance for this work, though not a large one. Most of it is paid by stations that need their instructions kept usable. Private questions arrive with other arrangements: a portion of soft food, replacement cloth for my sleeping mask, an offer to carry something the next time the person is going my way.

Some questions are free. I am inconsistent about which.

I would have a good memory for where a phrase occurred and a poor one for whether I had eaten before beginning to look for it. My hands would be narrow, with a permanent roughness along the side of one finger. Turning dry pages opens little splits there. I would have tried gloves and disliked them.

In the beginning, I would be too ready to believe that finding the oldest instruction meant finding the best one.

Someone would have to teach me otherwise.

***

She lives two landings above a disconnected laundry chamber. Her inspection manual contains a correction I cannot reconcile with any of its earlier copies.

“Have you used the earlier setting?” I ask.

She has let me recover from the stairs. She has given me a drink. At this question, she takes the cup from my hand and puts it safely on a shelf.

Then she brings me a seal in a shallow dish.

One side has folded inward. The material has become hard around the fold, preserving the failure with the clarity of a tooth mark.

“That’s the earlier setting.”

I examine it for longer than necessary.

“May I take it?”

“No. You can draw it.”

I am not good at drawing. She watches until the fold in my drawing looks sufficiently like the fold in the seal.

Afterward, I begin leaving spaces in my notes for things I have not gone to see.

This makes the notes less handsome. I dislike that more than I ought to.

***

I would be one of the narrow people who keep their coats on indoors. There would be nothing distinguished about me at a distance. A person seeing me cross a loading apron would remember the case against my hip, perhaps, because one of its hinges makes a small double knock.

I have the material to repair it.

Inside the case are paper, two pencils, a measuring cord, and a thin piece of blue enamel that I use to hold a page open. The enamel came from a cabinet door. It is blue on both sides, which still seems extravagant to me.

At home I sometimes turn it over while thinking.

I would have neighbors who regard the work as useful and the objects it accumulates as a nuisance. One of them would be right about the loose sheets beneath my bed. Another would borrow a record and return it with a food stain, then insist the stain was already there.

We would continue sharing portions.

There would be a child who visits because I let her use the backs of discarded copies. She would draw entrances much larger than the buildings around them. When I ask what passes through, she says the buildings are small, not the entrances large.

I would have no useful reply to this.

***

The keeper of the winch would come to me about the second gauge.

Not the cable, not the darkness beneath it. She has a particular question: do the two gauges measure from separate points, or does one line divide somewhere inside the wall?

She has checked both after the small change in tension. Now she wants to know how much reassurance the second reading ought to give her.

I ask whether she has the installation sheet.

“If I had it,” she says, “I would have brought it.”

Then she rubs her split thumbnail. She has walked a long way, and I have made her spend some of her recovered breath on this.

I move the case from the other chair.

The drawing I eventually find shows two lines continuing beyond the edge of the sheet. Its continuation is listed under a building name I do not know. For several days I believe the name belongs to a demolished station. Then someone tells me it was the former name of the winch house itself.

There are three cabinets there I have not searched.

When I return, I take the eastern passage. The keeper notices this before she asks about the drawing.

***

We spend part of a watch with the cabinets open.

One holds clamps, packed in dark cloth. One contains ledgers arranged in an order neither of us can explain. The third cannot open fully because a pipe support was installed in front of it.

“You could have told me about that,” I say.

“You didn’t ask about the doors.”

She says it without satisfaction. It is simply what happened.

We get the upper drawers open far enough to remove their contents a few sheets at a time. I kneel. She sits on the floor rather than bend over me. After a while she puts a folded cloth beneath one knee.

The continuation is not there.

We do find an instruction for reading an instrument from the lower landing. The instrument is no longer on the inventory. She remembers the covered recess where it must have stood.

Beside the instruction, someone has written:

*Allow time after arrival.*

I have copied similar cautions before. Gauges may need time to settle after a valve is opened. A chamber may be disturbed by entry.

The keeper reads over my hand.

“That means the attendant,” she says.

There was a stair. People arrived breathing hard enough to move the fine indicator when they leaned close.

I add this to my copy. For a moment I am annoyed that the original writer could not have been clearer. Then I picture the instrument in place, the stair directly behind it, someone standing where we cannot now stand. Perhaps there had been very little room for misunderstanding.

We put the papers back.

She keeps the folded cloth under her knee until the last drawer is shut.

***

I would not find the answer that watch.

I might not find it for a year. There would be other work, and the next cabinet might lie beyond a route I could manage only with a place to stay at the far end. The keeper would continue comparing the readings. I would give her a note explaining that their independence had not been established.

She would read it twice.

“You could have said that.”

“I wanted you to have it.”

She folds it inside the ledger, near the open hook.

I do not ask her to stop using the second gauge. An uncertain comparison is still a comparison. I have learned enough by then not to turn the limits of my search into instructions for her work.

***

Over time, the questions in my room would acquire smaller notes.

*Ask when her brother returns.*

*The lower door opens inward.*

*Do not carry the sample uncovered.*

And, increasingly:

*There is a seat halfway.*

I would begin copying those last details onto separate slips. At first they would be for my own journeys. Later someone would ask to borrow one, and return with a correction: the seat is still there, but the room is locked now; the rail has been repaired; the household beside the stairs does not mind visitors resting, provided they knock before entering.

These slips would need checking more often than the old drawings.

I would go out more.

Not far every time. Some watches I would reach only the next occupied building and sit with the person who had let me in. I would hear about a troublesome hinge, a birth, an inspection that had become difficult. Sometimes I would forget to ask the question written in my case.

Back in my room, I would have to decide what belonged in the records and what someone had merely told me while we were sitting together.

I would not always decide correctly.

But I would leave space to make corrections.

And when footsteps entered the blocked passage, I would push in my chair. If the person needed to turn around, there would be room. If they needed to sit before going back, I would move the case from the other seat.
