I would be under the third stair, where the stair has parted from the wall.

Not in the opening you can see. That leads to a pocket of stale water, a drowned root, the soft remains of something carried down in the last rain. Behind it, a seam turns upward. There is room to pass if I put one shoulder ahead of the other. Beyond the turn, the stone is dry for the length of my body.

I sleep there with my rear feet in the water.

A change reaches them before it reaches the rest of me: cold rain from the upper court, warmth from an exposed run of paving, sometimes the slight drag of water beginning to go the wrong way. I wake, but do not necessarily move. Many changes undo themselves.

Above me, the stair supports nothing anyone would call a destination. Four steps survive. The fifth is a rough rise into air. But beneath the fourth lies a crossing between two occupied seams, and the weight of the stair keeps their roofs from shifting.

I know its weight without knowing the stair.

*

I am not one of the pale ones.

My back still folds easily. There is a notch in the margin of one plate where I caught it while retreating with a shell beneath my chest. For a season, grit lodged there and irritated the skin. Now the edge has worn smooth. A young one sometimes grips it when I carry them. This is inconvenient, but secure.

I can fit through a channel that most of the adults avoid. Near its middle the ceiling dips, and I have to turn my head sideways and push with the rear feet. The passage is easier coming back. Once, after feeding, I had to wait outside it for a long time.

There are perhaps twelve bodies whose approach I recognize through familiar stone. On other stone I may fail to know them. One walks with a middle hand held up until the last moment. Another pauses twice at every opening, even when following someone who has just gone through. The heaviest of them barely disturbs the floor. I recognize that one by the long quiet interval between the first contact and the next.

I do not know how many of us there are.

There are some I have touched beneath the jaw and never encountered again. There are others I have never touched whose movements arrive beneath my resting place every wet season.

*

For a while, I think I would be a keeper of this crossing.

There is work enough. Fine silt gathers where the seam turns. A sliver of limestone must be taken out before strong rain and replaced afterward, or the resting pocket drains completely. Small bodies can be drawn into the stale pool at the entrance. I have pushed two of them out with my head.

But I leave the crossing for days. When I return, the sliver has sometimes been set differently. Once it was gone and three grains of gravel were doing its work better.

I tested the flow with a hand. Then I went inside.

What I keep, more often, is a little distance between things: between water and a sleeping body, between one stone and another, between a familiar contact and the answer that ought to follow.

There is a place below my stair where that answer no longer comes.

*

The break happened during dry weather.

A slab settled onto its bed of silt. Nothing fell into the street. The water still passed underneath. I could travel the route as before, but a signal made at one end scarcely reached the other.

For several days I answered too soon.

I knew what usually came next. I had felt it often: a short contact, a pause, then two closer together from beyond the slab. So when the first movement reached me weakly, I supplied the rest with my jaw and went on.

Once, another creature followed me into a run that had become dry.

We waited together beneath a lintel. It kept one front hand against my throat while I tested the floor again.

This time there was only the first contact.

We returned by the longer way.

Afterward, I began crossing the settled slab whenever I could not feel the whole exchange. It was a small journey, scarcely more than three body lengths. On the far side I would stop, raise my belly clear of the water, and wait.

Sometimes there was no one there.

Sometimes what I had taken for a call was a drop falling inside a buried room.

*

This is where I find myself most clearly: not in the sleeping pocket, but on that short stretch of uncertain stone.

I go back and forth.

At first I carry news badly. I repeat too much, or hurry, or continue to press my jaw down after the other body has turned away. The old one beyond the lintel puts a hand over my head. I wait beneath it. When the hand lifts, we begin again.

I bring food there too. Listening takes time, and by the end of it the good feeding water may have passed.

I learn which contacts must remain close together. I learn that a long pause cannot always be shortened merely because I am cold. I learn to return to the source when another creature asks something I cannot answer.

Some days I do little except carry softened roots and eat.

The break does not make me indispensable. Others cross it. During heavy rain, the wet joints sometimes carry well enough that no one needs to cross at all. On those days I feel exchanges passing beneath me while I am still arranging my feet to listen.

I remain there a little longer than necessary.

Then I go looking for larvae.

*

I would have a dry pocket with objects in it.

A shell too broad to take through my narrow passage. Two flakes, one good for scraping and one I retain because it fits well beneath my chest, though its edge is poor. A lump of coarse sand held together by dried slime.

And a dark pebble with no useful flat face.

The pebble comes from farther down than I usually travel. I brought it back during a season when the lower channels were warm. Wet, it is almost black. As it dries, a pale ring appears around it.

I take it out sometimes and turn it.

There need not be anyone nearby.

*

When you enter the room above, I feel you through the stair.

Your first step is too large to locate. It reaches me through several surfaces at once. I draw in my feet. Silt continues drifting past the opening.

Then your weight settles in the middle of the floor.

After a while I come forward far enough to place my front hands against the threshold. You move, and I withdraw. Later I try again.

I cannot tell whether you have heard me. Your body makes so many movements that do not lead anywhere.

But when I cross beside your foot, you keep still.

I carry a flake down to the settled slab. There is a grain beneath one edge that I have been trying to loosen. The work has gone slowly; I cannot brace myself properly, and the grain may be part of the bedrock.

Behind me, your weight shifts away from the wall.

The next faint contact comes through more clearly.

I set down the flake and spread both hands.
