I would first look for a place inside the granary, on the beam where the old measurements are kept. It would be dry there, and occupied without being crowded. People would come close to look at the notches, then go away again.

But the beam is brushed regularly. There are baskets passing beneath it. Whatever lived there would need to withstand being mistaken for something that ought to be removed.

I think I would be farther down, behind the hooks where the wooden slips hang during the day. There is a little space between the mounting board and the wall. One end is packed with dust; the other admits a narrow light. From within it, I could touch wood with my back and stone with my feet.

I would know a great deal about those two surfaces, and very little about the granary.

My body would be longer than it first appeared. At rest, most of it would be folded beneath a hard upper plate the color of old stalks. The plate would have an irregular edge, easily mistaken for a splinter lifting from the board. There would be two small gripping limbs near my mouth, and farther back, softer feet arranged in pairs. These would come loose reluctantly, one at a time, as though each had to be persuaded that the next place could bear my weight.

I would not be one of the animals that grazes the fresh stone. I would arrive later, when the first green had lost its shine. There is a stage between a sheen and a cushion of moss, a nearly invisible felt that grows best where two unlike materials almost touch. I would eat that.

A bracket against a wall. The underside of a jar lid. Wood lying loose on stone.

The town would provide a great many places suitable for me, but no continuous route between them.

For much of the morning, I would remain behind the board.

The measuring slips would come back smelling of other cracks. Some would carry traces of growth I had never tasted. When the young woman hung them up, I would extend the front of my body through the narrow opening and test the nearest one.

This would be how she first noticed me.

She would lift the slip again, believing it had caught on a roughness in the wood. I would go with it.

For a moment we would inconvenience each other. She would need her instrument. I would need the surface beneath my feet to stop moving.

She would set the slip on the granary threshold and wait.

I would not hurry. There would be no useful distinction, in my body, between a patient person and a very slow disaster.

Eventually she would fetch a brush.

After that, she would tap the board before hanging up the slips. The shock would pass through my upper plate. I would withdraw, and she would finish her work. Sometimes she would forget. Sometimes I would be too far out to withdraw quickly. Our arrangement would improve without ever becoming reliable.

I would have eyes, but they would not give me the town as it has been described. I could distinguish the bright opening above from the dim square below. A person crossing the hall would obscure one and then the other. Near objects would become clear only when I moved my head sideways: a hook, the edge of a sleeve, the separate hairs of a brush approaching.

Much of what I knew would reach me through my feet.

The granary keeper’s step would travel strongly through the wall. The children would arrive as several interruptions that were difficult to separate. A basket set down carelessly would make me flatten myself before its sound reached the opening.

I would learn the young measurer by the sequence of her work. Hand on the board. One slip lifted. A pause. Another.

I might come out for someone else who performed those movements in the same order.

She would probably believe I knew her.

Perhaps, after enough mornings, I would.

There would be a season when the growth behind the board became thin. I would scrape the same places repeatedly, leaving small pale crescents on the wood. My body would begin to fit too easily beneath its plate.

Then I would venture down.

Crossing the bare wall would take most of an afternoon. I would stop at each old fixing hole and put my head inside. One would contain a tightly folded animal whose legs moved against my face. One would be empty but smell inhabited. At the third I would turn around, back into it, and remain until evening.

The next day I would reach the lowest step of the hall.

Here the shell-backed procession would pass close enough for me to feel every contact through the stone. I would find food beside its groove, where their passage kept the growth cropped and damp. I would feed with my rear feet anchored beneath the step and my plate projecting just above the moving shells.

For a while this would be a good place.

I would grow. The pale edge beneath my plate would darken and harden. I would begin to leave the shelter before the granary opened.

A child would notice that if she touched the step twice, I withdrew, and if she waited, I emerged again.

She would spend a considerable time discovering this.

On the fourth day I would cease to withdraw. On the fifth she would touch my plate.

I would grip her finger.

My mouth would be too small to do much harm, but my front limbs would close firmly. She would lift her hand with me attached, astonished that an experiment could acquire weight.

Someone would tell her to put it down.

She would put down her hand.

For a little while we would both stand on the step.

I would release her when the stone beneath my hind feet became more convincing than the skin between my claws. Afterward, she would show the marks to other children. There would be arguments about whether I had bitten her. She would return to look at me, though not as closely.

I would have no knowledge of my reputation.

During a strong breath from below, the procession would change its route.

I would feel the first shells before I felt the cold. Their new line would pass directly beneath my shelter. I would draw myself in, but the creatures would keep coming, pressing against my folded feet, flowing into the space I had left behind my body.

I would have to come out.

The hall would be moving everywhere around me. I would reach toward a dark edge that proved to be a lifted leaf. It would rise beyond my grasp. Something pale would strike the paving beside me, open, and leave.

I would cross an expanse of stone I would never have crossed to find food.

Near the middle of it, the air would weaken.

I would stop there.

This is where I can most clearly find myself: a little animal badly placed, holding very firmly to a surface that offers no shelter.

The young measurer would come down the steps carrying an empty basket. She might recognize the broken-looking edge of my plate. She might simply see that I was alive.

Her brush would be upstairs.

She would lower the basket on its side and put the rim beside me.

I would remain still.

She would move it closer, and I would grip the stone more tightly. Only when she took her hand away would I extend a limb toward the wicker.

Inside the basket, the light would be divided into small manageable openings. There would be dust in the bindings and a dry fragment of leaf caught beneath a rib. I would climb toward that.

She would leave the basket beside the granary wall.

By morning I would have gone underneath it.

For several days she would use another basket. Then the granary keeper would need both, and mine would be lifted, examined, and brushed.

I would be returned to the wall on a piece of bark.

There would be no permanent provision for me. I would still have to find somewhere to live.

But a new packing strip would have been fitted beneath the board that held the hooks. One end would stand slightly clear of the stone.

I would enter headfirst, turn with some difficulty, and settle with my plate toward the opening.

Above me, the slips would knock together as they were taken down.

I would know the board. I would know the stone. The gap between them would be unfamiliar, and wide enough.
