I would be the one whose hands arrive second.

Not because I had been waiting to help. I would have been on my way past, carrying something awkward, and seen that the gardener could not change their grip without letting the root spring back. I would put my bundle down where it obstructed the stair.

One palm beneath the bend. The other against the wall, to keep myself steady.

For a while the gardener would continue working as though the extra hands were their own. Then they would look at where I had put my thumb and make the small, irritated movement that means *there, not there*.

I would move it.

The root would be warmer than either of us. Its outer skin would have fine transverse wrinkles, packed with pale dust. As it yielded, something farther along the wall would shift: a leaf turning its hard edge against a window frame. We would both look toward it.

This would take much longer than I had intended.

My bundle would contain three channel covers, a wedge, and a cover I had brought because I could not remember which of the others needed replacing. Someone coming up the stair would have to step over them. They would express an opinion about that with one foot.

***

I think I would work on the covered channels through which the clay messages travel.

Not writing the messages. Not deciding where they ought to go. Keeping the little descending ways clear, easing their curves, replacing the covers where roots had pushed them up. Much of the work would involve lying on a landing with one arm inserted into a wall.

There would be a polish along the outside of that arm. My coat would have one short sleeve and one long one, and the short sleeve would keep catching on exactly the projection it had been shortened to avoid.

I would own a good wedge. People would borrow it without asking because I had once said they could.

The channels would not form a single orderly system. Some would have been laid when a house was two houses. Others would empty into rooms no longer occupied, or pass through a sleeping cell whose occupant objected to deliveries during their rest. A strip sent from the upper gardens might need to be taken out at the old market and carried up six steps before it could continue.

That transfer would be somebody’s responsibility.

There would be considerable disagreement about whose.

I would know enough of the routes to seem indispensable during a blockage and enough of their history to know I was not. Inside the walls, my repairs would meet older repairs: a curved section held up by a pebble, a seam sealed with fruit skin, a magnificent little bridge built to cross a root that had since died and crumbled away.

I would leave the bridge.

I would also take one of its supports when I needed a support, and later feel obliged to go back with another.

***

My hooked nail would be filed almost straight, with a shallow notch near its tip. Useful for catching the edge of a strip without damaging the clay.

Less useful for making my own name.

I would have altered the nail when young, for the work, and discovered afterward that my name now looked faintly like an instruction to lift something. People who knew me would make the necessary allowance. The child would exaggerate the ambiguity whenever it wanted carrying.

There would be a pale repair below my left knee. No impressive story: a cover slipping, an ill-placed leg, my own refusal to put down what I was holding. In a draft the repair would stiffen before the rest of me did.

I would stop to inspect perfectly sound masonry until the stiffness passed.

Everyone would know.

One of the others would occasionally stop beside me and inspect it too, with an expression so carefully neutral that I would have to turn away.

***

The western passage would be on my route, but I would not be its interpreter.

I might deliver food there. I might remove an empty dish. Once, finding fresh marks on the receiving strip, I would carry them out into better light and spend too long looking.

They would resemble marks I had seen before.

That would tell me very little.

The next time I came, I would put the strip back beside the dish. Whatever was beyond the wall might want it. I would have no reason to keep it merely because I could not understand it.

I would be more concerned about the ledge. One corner would be breaking away. A bowl set there carelessly could fall into the narrow space below, where neither a hand nor a root hook could reach.

Repairing it would mean working close to the sealed opening. I would scratch a notice on clay and leave it with the food: the gesture for *ledge*, the mark for *work*, then two broad intervals to indicate that I would wait.

The food might disappear. The notice might remain.

I would wait another interval.

Eventually, perhaps, I would find the bowl set on the floor instead.

That would be enough permission to repair the ledge. It would not be permission for anything else.

***

I would have no settled sleeping cell, though I would have preferences so definite that other people would call them a household.

There would be a warm recess behind a disused sorting room. A place beneath the eastern stairs where the roar lost its tearing edge. A narrow bed in someone else’s house that I claimed to use only when working nearby.

My belongings would accumulate in all three places.

I would spend a good deal of time searching for a particular scraping tooth while confidently remembering where I had left it.

The child would know some of these places. It would bring me objects for which it wanted openings made.

A splinter should have a doorway. A folded fruit skin should have two. One of its six shutters would need a window, and this request would prove harder to satisfy than the others.

I might offer to fit it into a channel cover.

The child would refuse. Messages would strike it.

A box, then.

No.

I would ask what the window was for.

The child would make the sign for a window again, very distinctly.

***

For some time I would carry that little shutter in a pocket.

It would not be well made. Its two pivots would be of unequal size. Held one way up, it would stay open; reversed, it would fall shut. The child would insist that the first way was correct, although a notch in the frame suggested otherwise.

I would begin noticing places where it might fit.

This would interfere with my work. I would be clearing grit from a channel and find myself measuring a gap above it with two fingers. I would climb stairs I had no business climbing, looking at old facades. Most of the possible windows would open onto packed roots or solid brick.

At last I would find a room behind the abandoned market.

It would be scarcely a room at all: enough floor for one adult sitting with knees drawn up, and an old opening that looked across the backs of two counters toward the central hollow. There would be no receiving channel, no fruit to gather, no shutter mechanism to maintain. The wall would be warm.

The opening would be far too large.

I would spend an unreasonable amount of good material making it smaller.

Someone would notice and ask whether I meant to live there.

I would say no.

They would ask what I meant to store.

Nothing.

They would look at the quantity of clay I had mixed.

I would dislike that look because it was justified.

***

I would take the child to see the shutter fitted.

It would open it. Close it. Open it again. Test the catch with its smallest finger. Then it would leave to fetch something, without telling me whether the work was satisfactory.

I would remain sitting inside.

Through the little frame, the central dust would occupy most of the view. A strip of distant terrace would cross the upper corner. If I leaned to one side I could see a balcony where somebody had hung skins to stiffen; if I leaned the other way, only the bare slope.

There would still be noise. My knees would ache from being folded. I would notice a crumb of mortar stuck to my wrist and pick it off.

After a while I would close the shutter.

The room would become dim enough for my eyes to open.

I think this is where I would find myself—not at first, and not every day. A channel worker with an unfinished repair elsewhere, sitting behind a shutter made by a child, discovering that I had wanted a door I could close without going to sleep.

When the child returned, it would have two slivers of amber fruit and something sharp it considered furniture.

I would move my legs to make room.
