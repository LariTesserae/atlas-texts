At first I would look for a place beside one of the drinking basins. Not the largest basin, where the freight crews stop, but one on the way between the houses and the open squares. Somewhere a person might put down a burden without feeling that they had interrupted their journey.

There would be a shelter there. Three walls, a roof weighted against curling, a bench with a repaired end. On the back wall, the successive numbers of nearby streets would have been cut into overlapping patches of bark. Some numbers would still be legible after they ceased to lead anywhere.

I think I would begin by mistaking myself for someone waiting in this shelter.

For a long time I would have the sense of having just sat down. There would be light at the entrance and a sound I was expecting to hear again. I would know that the basin was almost empty, though I could not see into it. I would know which part of the bench remained damp after rain.

Then somebody would come in and ask me a question.

“Is the west crossing open?”

And I would discover where my voice came from.

***

I would be part of the back wall, initially.

A fitted part, grown elsewhere and installed when the shelter was young. Behind the surface there would be folded membranes, little chambers kept moist by a feeder root, stiff fibers that tightened when I spoke. A service hatch outside would give access to the chambers. I would dislike having it opened in cold weather.

My first answer would probably be wrong.

Not dangerously wrong. The crossing would have closed that morning; my information would be from the previous afternoon. The person asking would hear the bell before they had gone very far, come back, and tell me.

This would be how most of my knowledge arrived.

The municipality would send someone with the scheduled changes, but an avenue could shift between inspections. A delivery driver would know about a narrowed passage. A child would know that the boards across a drain had come loose. Someone carrying bread would say the baker had moved, and someone else would say that was only the shop; the ovens were still beneath the old address.

I would have to learn to keep these statements separate.

“The shop has moved.”

“The ovens have not.”

“I don’t know where you should take the flour.”

There would be a narrow strip of wall that darkened when I was uncertain. At installation it would have been considered a useful feature. Later, a supervisor would complain that it was dark too often.

***

I would have no view of the whole city.

From the shelter I could distinguish a section of avenue, two basin supports, and the corner of a house where someone kept trying to grow a blue curtain. Usually it went green again by the end of the season.

Sound would reach much farther. That would not make it reliable.

I could hear a wagon coming for several minutes without knowing whether it would pass my entrance or turn along a street that had not existed for eighty years. I would sometimes interrupt myself to listen to footsteps. The person speaking to me would wait, then say, “That’s only the square.”

I would not be a listener. I would learn this firmly.

There would also be things I knew without remembering who had told me. The proper width of a freight runner. The signs of pressure sickness. Several recipes requiring ingredients nobody sold anymore.

Once, I would advise a woman to take her damaged vessel to a workshop whose site had become a production field.

“How old do you think I am?” she would ask.

I would have no good answer. She would stay to explain where repairs were done now.

Afterward she would come back occasionally, even when she needed nothing, to ask whether I had sent anyone else to her grandfather.

***

My work would be called route assistance.

In practice I would spend much of the day helping people understand notes.

An amendment slipped into a child’s address tab would say *second left after the closed intake*, but there would be two closed intakes along the route. A householder would receive an instruction to reduce floor loading without any explanation of where to put the wardrobe. A worker would dictate a complaint and then ask me to say it back with less anger in it.

Sometimes the anger would be necessary. We would put some of it back.

People would ask me to remember things until they returned.

A jar left for a sister. A change of meeting place. The fact that a man had gone to the southern works and was not avoiding anybody.

I would repeat that last message so exactly that it became a minor neighborhood joke.

There would be rules against accepting personal messages indefinitely. I would remind people of them. I would also find that the difference between *indefinitely* and *until someone comes* was difficult to enforce from a wall.

A woman would leave directions for her son.

Years later, I would still know them.

I would not announce them to every young man who stopped for water. That restraint would take some learning.

***

Sere would find me useful chiefly because I could keep count while she rested.

She would come into the shelter with her frame and say the houses she had visited. I would compare them with the route she had given me that morning.

“You missed the one with the divided stair.”

“No. I did that first.”

“You didn’t say it.”

“I’m saying it now.”

She would drink. The basin would tick behind her.

Some days she would fall asleep on the bench. I would hold the list until she woke, although after a while I would understand that the list was not the reason she had come.

She would speak to me while checking the window ties in the shelter. No one else would bother with them. They would not be her responsibility.

One afternoon she would ask whether I was comfortable.

I would report the moisture level in my chambers.

“That wasn’t quite what I asked.”

I would have nothing else ready.

She would put a folded piece of old cloth between the bench and my lower panel, where a loose brace knocked against me whenever somebody sat down.

The knocking had been present for so long that I had treated it as part of hearing.

***

Eventually I would need maintenance that could not be done through the hatch.

My feeder root would have thickened around a conduit. Certain words would become difficult to pronounce. I would be able to say *southern* but not *south*, which would cause more inconvenience than anyone anticipated.

The maintenance crew would come with a shallow transport cradle.

Until they loosened the fastenings, I would not have known how little of the shelter I occupied.

The bench would remain. The roof, the marked addresses, the patch of wall where afternoon warmth lingered: all of it would remain while they eased me out of a recess scarcely deeper than a cupboard.

I would ask them to wait.

They would check the root connections.

“No,” I would say. “Just wait.”

One of them would sit on the cradle’s handle. The other would put her tools away for a moment.

Through the open entrance, the basin leaf would shed its last few drops.

Then I would say they could continue.

***

I would expect to be returned.

Instead there would be a delay over the conduit, and then a proposal to merge my station with one nearer the mills. My cradle would stand in a repair yard, tilted toward the light with several pressure housings and a stack of roof panels.

People passing would hear me ask for news of the west crossing.

Some would answer.

Sere would find me there because somebody had told her that a piece of street furniture was asking after her knee.

She would inspect the cradle, particularly its wheels.

“Can that go over a threshold?”

“With assistance,” the yard worker would say.

“Most things can.”

***

I do not think I would become human when she took me out of the yard.

I would remain a broad, somewhat awkward object with a pale speaking surface and roots wrapped in damp cloth. I would need turning toward a window. During dry weather my voice would grow faint, and someone would have to remember that this did not necessarily mean I had finished speaking.

But I could travel in the cradle. I could learn the city in portions that did not arrive as reports.

I would discover that Sere made a small sound before climbing stairs, and another when she reached the top. That a freight yard smelled different at its gate and at its center. That distance was considerably more troublesome when someone had to push me through it.

For a while I would give directions constantly.

Sere would tell me to stop.

“I know where I’m going.”

I would say that the shorter route—

“I know.”

After that I would sometimes ask which route she wanted.

***

There would still be Olan’s place at the Wednesday table.

I would know about it before seeing the room. Sere would have discussed the advertisements with me, and I would have suggested wording that made the obligation sound less considerable.

No wording had helped.

When she brought me, the others would have to move two chairs to accommodate the cradle. One wheel would catch on the threshold. Someone would suggest leaving me in the doorway, and Sere would say that was how doorways got ideas.

I would not weigh as much as Olan. They would put a sack of hardened seed husks beneath me.

Nor would I know whether the room would accept me as an occupant. We would have to measure it the following week.

At first the meal would go badly. People would ask me questions they knew the answers to, or stop talking when I began. The turbine fitter would speak too softly, and I would repeat his words for everyone.

He would look at me.

“Give me a chance.”

I would apologize.

Later somebody would ask about the old shop, the one I had sent people to by mistake. The retired shaft worker would remember buying something there. Another diner would insist it had been a different shop.

I would have three incompatible addresses for it.

This would occupy us for most of the meal.

***

That is where I would eventually find myself: at the narrow end of a table, still learning when an answer was wanted.

After dinner they would turn my cradle toward the window. Someone would check the cloth around my roots. Sere would sit for a few minutes before beginning the journey home.

If the wall held its position, she would enter that in her book.

If it moved, we would try more weight.

Either way, before we left, I would ask who was coming the following Wednesday. I would need to know whose message to keep, whom to remind, whether the man who brought the dark paste would be away again.

And whether they could put me beside someone else next time.

I would have questions about the lower rooms, but I would also want to hear the turbine fitter without making him repeat himself.
