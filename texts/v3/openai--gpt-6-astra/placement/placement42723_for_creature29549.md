I would probably be found halfway up a stair, with a slab across my knees and no good place to set it down.

The stair would have been adequate when I began the repair. Now there would be a shallow landing where the next step ought to go, and the slab I had brought would be too long in one direction and not long enough in another. I would have spent some time trying to make this the slab’s fault.

Beside me: a short blade, a packet of food, two strips of leaf rib saved because they might be useful. The blade would be mine. The hammer would belong to the route. I would have borrowed a knee pad from a shelter and carried it farther than I meant to.

For a while, this would be enough to know about me.

I would be low to the ground, working. The light beneath the slab would show through the thin edges of my fingers. Every time I shifted its weight, something deep in the stair would answer, and I would wait with both hands resting flat until I understood whether the answer concerned me.

Not all of them would.

That distinction would have taken me a long time to learn.

—

I think I would be one of the people of the ridges, rather than a visitor who needed the world explained.

The roar would have differences for me. I would recognize the strained, almost fibrous sound of a slab held at its corners, the crowded grinding beneath a newly deepened basin. There would be parts of it I could work through and parts that made me put down a tool. I would sometimes mistake one for the other.

My left foot would be less reliable than my right. An injury, badly bound because I had been unwilling to stop traveling, would have left one half of the sole slow to settle. On a broad path this would scarcely matter. Descending, I would turn slightly sideways. People who knew me would leave room without looking down.

I would dislike their doing that.

Under my outer mantle I would have worked a pattern of small, crowded angles into the binding. The pattern would only become visible when the strip bent. I would be pleased with it, and would arrange, occasionally, for someone to notice.

My hands would be good at receiving speech. I could keep still without becoming rigid, distinguish a pause from a refusal, allow someone else’s fingers to move mine.

Giving up the last part of a conversation would be harder.

After the other person had finished, I would often maintain a little pressure: something else, surely; a qualification; one more thing I could do before they went. A friend might have to turn my hand over to make me release them. I would be embarrassed each time, then do it again.

This would matter more than the injured foot.

—

At first I imagine that I would repair shelters.

There is satisfaction in that work: a lining newly tensioned, a chipped floor edge made safe for bare feet, the discovery that a persistent harshness came from one rib rubbing against another. You can put your cheek near the finished panel and feel what your labor has changed.

But I would linger too long over small improvements. While I was adjusting a sleeping niche, a stair elsewhere would be separating from its destination.

So I would travel.

I would carry measurements made with flexible strips, spare bindings, reports that could not be given across a gap. I would know which shelter had food but needed lining, which had good tools but no sound place to sleep. Sometimes I would arrive with news already made useless by the terrain.

Once, after a difficult journey, I would discover that the people I had come to warn were visible from the place where I had started. A new face of stone would have brought their path around toward mine.

We would stand looking at one another.

Someone would make a large gesture meaning, approximately, *You could have stayed there.*

For years they would find occasions to repeat it.

My useful work would emerge gradually from these journeys. I would begin going a little ahead of people who could no longer trust an old route to remain ordinary around them.

Not far ahead. Usually within sight.

I would test a landing, move a tool out of reach before it became difficult to retrieve, loosen the right stone beside a doorway. At a shelter I would choose where to lay the sleeping fans, then wait for the traveler to arrive before fastening them. A niche could be deep enough when empty and much too deep when occupied.

There would be no name for this work that excluded all my other work. I would still harvest. I would still mend bowls badly. But people would begin to seek me out before particular journeys.

—

There would be one elder I knew especially well.

They would own a bowl with a pale inclusion running through its dark base. They would turn it to put the pale mark beneath their thumb before eating. If I handed it to them the wrong way, they would correct it without comment.

They would also be convinced that I wasted good leaf.

When I trimmed a lining, they would take the offcuts from beside my knee and sort them into piles: useful, useful, useful, and, finally, grudgingly, waste.

I would sometimes cut a strip more narrowly than I needed merely to prevent this.

They would notice.

We would not be gentle with one another all the time.

On our earlier journeys they would walk ahead, impatient with my descending foot. Later we would travel abreast. Eventually I would find myself arriving at each shelter first and pretending this had something to do with the tools.

The first unmistakable change would concern their bowl.

They would set it on the ground between us. I would reach for it and fail to take hold, although my fingers seemed to have reached its rim. I would lean farther. The elder would catch my sleeve.

For a while we would both watch the bowl.

Then they would pick it up and place it directly in my hands.

Neither of us would discuss that until the next shelter.

When we did, I would begin too firmly. My hand would already contain a plan: another companion, broader paths, no more sleeping against the inner wall.

They would let me finish.

Then, with two fingers beneath my palm, they would ask whether I had mistaken this conversation for a repair.

I would remember the question. I would not always benefit from remembering it.

—

The place I would come to know best would be an unremarkable shelter below the highest gardens.

Its entrance would need one shallow step added almost every time I visited. Inside, there would be room for three sleepers if none of them minded being touched. A black-green fan would grow beside the outer turn, one leaf bent permanently around a projecting stone.

I would keep trying to free the leaf.

It would keep growing back.

Here we would stop for a long interval. Other people would bring nodules and fresh fans. Someone would return my borrowed knee pad to its proper route, leaving me a worse one. The elder would take an interest in repairing the worse one and make it almost unbearably elaborate.

Along the inner wall, their handprints would begin.

I would prepare the green dust. They would choose the spacing.

After each test I would clean their palm, working the powder out of the creases. Early on, there would be powder everywhere: beneath the nails, along the side of the wrist, once on their cheek because they had forgotten and touched their face.

Later, their hand would come away clean.

That would be the part I disliked most.

A depression would remain in the wall, but nothing would have adhered to them. I would hold their hand longer than cleaning required.

They would turn mine over.

—

I do not think I would be especially brave at the opening.

By then another companion would have joined us: someone better at waiting, worse at practical work, who could sit beside the elder for hours without constantly offering an adjustment. I would resent them a little. I would be relieved whenever they took my place.

The opening would appear low in the wall, beside the sleeping niche we had stopped using.

Through it, a slope would be visible. There would be loose pale stone, a terrace farther down, the folded edge of a garden. The light would come from somewhere beneath that terrace.

I would know the other side of this wall. I would have repaired its outer face.

For several days I would continue making small repairs elsewhere in the shelter.

Then the elder would ask for their bowl.

I would turn the pale mark beneath their thumb before giving it to them.

We would leave most of our things behind. I would take the short blade, food, a bundle of lining, and far more binding than necessary. The other companion would quietly remove some of it. I would put one strip back.

At the opening I would lower myself onto both hands. My good foot would find the slope first.

The stone would feel like stone.

This would not reassure me as much as I had hoped.

Behind me, the elder would wait without touching my shoulder. I would have to decide when to offer my hand.

—

Beyond that point, I cannot make the whole country clear.

I can imagine a path whose steps have been widened by someone with a different reach. A shelter with its entrance turned away. A bowl left upside down to keep the mineral needles from growing through what remains inside it.

I can imagine recognizing a repair.

Not the person who made it, not the age of it. Only the method: a short strip doubled around a longer one, saving a sound piece of rib that another worker would have thrown away.

The elder would sit while we examined the shelter. They would be tired and conceal it poorly. I would kneel to arrange the lining beneath them, fussing with a fold near their hip.

Their hand would come down beside mine.

I would stop.

For a moment I would think they were asking for the long conversation I had been preparing for throughout the journey. I would bring my palm beneath theirs, carefully, ready to receive something difficult.

They would tell me that I was kneeling on their mantle.

I would move. Their fingers would remain against mine.

There would still be something they wanted to say, but first they would need to settle the bowl securely between their feet. I would wait while they did that.

Outside, beneath the threshold, the next step would be beginning to form.
