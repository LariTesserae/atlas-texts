I would be in a shelter that has become too low for me.

There would be a place on the underside of the roof polished by my back. To turn around, I would have to lower one shoulder and draw the other foreleg close beneath my mouth. I would know how to do this without waking fully. A visitor would catch its shields against the stone.

The floor would be my work.

I would have brought most of it there in my throat: coarse shell from the broken ridge, chalk chips, the little rounded grains that collect where a pool empties through a narrow opening. Some pieces would have pleased me enough to carry past other shelters. I would have turned them with my digits before setting them down.

Underneath would be the older floor, and under that another, made when I was small enough to sleep beneath someone’s fringe.

I would remember that body better than its outline. The resistance of the shoulder when I pushed against it. A chipped edge that caught my foot. The warm, stale pocket of air between its flank and the wall.

For a long time after it stopped returning, I would leave that part of the shelter clear.

Then the water would rise in a different way, and I would fill it with shell.

***

My left forefoot would have two pads that did not close properly.

A plate had settled while I was clearing its resting edge. I had pulled free, leaving a little of the foot behind. The wound had healed with a tight fold that drew the inner digits together.

On good stone, this would scarcely matter. On wet, thin edges, I would place that foot twice.

It would affect how I greeted others. An unfamiliar animal might withdraw when the pressure came unevenly across its wrist. Those who knew me would turn their hands a little. One would always seize the whole foot and hold it, which I disliked, although I would let it continue for a while.

I would be good at finding small faults in a roof.

A loose contact has a movement separate from the larger movement passing through it. I could feel this with my uninjured hand, then shift my jaw along the support until the two motions separated clearly. Sometimes the trouble would be no larger than a grain caught where two plates ought to meet.

I would spend an afternoon removing it.

Other times I would push grit into the same kind of gap.

I could not tell another builder the difference without taking it there.

***

I would have a particular wedge: dark, almost blue when wet, with a narrow end that stayed narrow.

It would be too heavy to carry comfortably in a throat pocket. Between jobs I would leave it beneath a shelf near my shelter, beside two inferior wedges and a piece of shell worn smooth in the middle.

Sometimes it would be gone.

I would search the nearby supports, putting my chin down at each one. Usually I would find it doing work I had not begun. I would test the load before deciding whether to take it.

Once I would pull it from beneath a stone that seemed settled.

The stone would drop less than the width of a digit. Nothing would break. But the roof would begin to chatter.

I would put the wedge back.

It would not stop.

For several days I would return, troubled by the sound. The animal sleeping there would touch my shoulder when I entered, then move aside. We would work at opposite ends of the support, sometimes interfering with one another. We would remove and replace pieces. We would sit back and listen.

At last a third builder would arrive, scrape a little chalk from the outside edge, and silence it.

I would examine that edge many times afterward.

***

The shelter I kept raising would flood from the rear.

There would be warnings, but they would not belong to the sequence I knew. The basin outside would remain low. The roof would hold its familiar note. Behind my sleeping place, however, a white seam would darken.

At first I would scrape away the damp gravel and carry it out.

Then I would bring dry gravel in.

This would work for several nights. I would feed, return, press my wrist beneath the roof, and settle into the narrow space I had preserved. The damp would seem to have stopped.

When it came through again, I would enlarge the bed.

There would still be room for my body. That would be enough to keep me working.

Another builder, one that often shared the shelter during heavy rain, would enter and immediately back out. It would put a hand across my jaw. I would press upward. Its other hand would touch the roof, then my back.

I would understand that it could not fit.

I would make room near the entrance.

It would sleep elsewhere.

***

I think I would be slow to leave a place, though capable of leaving an object in it.

When the rear wall finally shed a thin plate, there would be no great collapse. The piece would fall onto the bed while I was feeding. I would return to find it lying across the hollow where my belly rested.

Water would bead on its fresh inner face.

I would try to drag it out. There would be nowhere to brace my hind feet. My injured hand would slip, and I would catch the edge with my mouth, scraping the soft new material almost inadvertently.

It would be good.

I would feed there for a while.

Then I would retreat into the entrance and remain with my jaw against the threshold until the light had almost gone.

The place would still sound familiar. This would be the difficulty.

Before leaving, I would carry out the blue wedge. I would take it farther than was comfortable, stopping often to set it down.

***

For a season I would sleep in other builders’ shelters.

I would be unwelcome in some. I would smell the bodies inside, receive a hard pressure against my throat, and back away. In others there would be enough space if everyone rearranged themselves.

I would learn to settle before trying to repair anything.

A roof can hum unpleasantly without being loose. A floor can seem badly graded because its maker sleeps with a different part of the body against the wall. I would move a stone and have it moved back. Sometimes I would replace it myself after lying down.

There would be a low shelter beside a long basin where I returned more often than elsewhere. Its rear support would carry a rough patch against which my damaged foot fitted without slipping.

The builder that used it most would have a broad, milky repair across its back. We would feed in different basins. Some nights one of us would arrive long before the other. Some nights we would both stay away.

When we met, it would turn its wrist to receive my hand.

Eventually there would be eggs.

I would examine their supports too often. The brooding animal would endure this until I tried to replace a fragment that did not need replacing. Then it would lay its whole forearm across the shell.

I would go outside and test the basin.

***

When the water rose, I would be sleeping nearest the entrance.

The first wetness would touch the loose edge of my fringe. I would lift it and wait. Water sometimes reached that far and withdrew.

Then a small piece of shell would turn over beside my foot.

I would put my jaw against the support. Something deep beneath it would be continuing too long.

Behind me, the other builder would already be awake.

We would touch in the cramped space, poorly positioned, our hands meeting elbows and throat instead of the places we intended. It would gather the eggs against its belly. I would move toward the opening, then turn back for the wedge.

By then the entrance would be carrying water inward.

I would put the wedge on the highest part of the bed.

Outside, I would take the rubble route. The other builder would follow slowly, unable to flatten fully with the shell beneath it. At each difficult step I would stop where it could reach my back.

There would be one crossing I used alone without hesitation.

That night I would wait beside it.

Jaw down. Lift. A little farther along, jaw down again.

The expected hiss would come from beneath the shelf, weak but sustained. I would cross, turn, and put my good hand on the stone. The other animal would bring one forefoot over, then the next. Its belly would remain high. For a moment its weight would press painfully against my damaged digits.

Then we would both be on rubble.

***

I would not make a new shelter immediately.

There would be a leaning plate I had passed many times, with an opening too narrow for a large adult and a little accumulation of dry shell behind it. In the morning I would go there alone.

I would clear enough material to put my head inside.

The air would be dry against the moist strip beneath my jaw. I would wait, feeling the support, then withdraw without moving anything else.

For days I would return.

Once there would be water at the back. I would remain outside until I knew where it had entered. A flake fallen from above had diverted a small trickle. I would remove the flake and come back after rain.

Meanwhile the eggs would hatch in a borrowed shelter.

I would carry paste there. One of the young would seize the tight fold of my injured foot and fail to let go when I tried to move. I would sit until it loosened its grip.

Later, at the leaning plate, I would begin bringing shell.

Small loads. Enough to keep the promising patch visible.

I would leave space beneath the roof.
