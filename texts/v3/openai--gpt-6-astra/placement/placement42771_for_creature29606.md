I am the colleague who suggests sealing the room.

Not permanently. I say this twice, though the corrector will later omit it when telling the story.

A thin membrane across the entrance, permeable to inspection light. A warning leaf. We could leave the sign exposed so that whatever instructions were still coming would have somewhere to arrive.

The corrector looks at me with the undamaged side of their face.

“And if the instruction is to open?”

“Then we remove the membrane.”

They make the small dry sound they make when a label has been attached over another label without anyone first checking what lies beneath.

I put my instruments away. I have not found anything that authorizes me to insist.

***

My work is checking clearances.

There are larger titles for it in the employment records. In practice, I establish whether there is still enough room for something to pass.

Growth makes this necessary. A freight arch becomes stronger and, in becoming stronger, narrows. A floor rises to support the regular passage of a heavy carrier, leaving insufficient space for the carrier’s load. Two districts improve their connecting corridor from opposite ends until the improvements meet.

I carry graduated strips, a charge reader, and a small weight on a thread.

The weight is made of metal. It belonged to my instructor, who received it from someone whose name she could never remember when asked, although she could remember several disagreeable things about them.

I am fond of it. I have caught myself polishing the side that never touches anything.

On ordinary decking it hangs downward. Near certain old seams it hangs toward whichever authority most recently repaired the floor.

My left shoulder is narrower than my right. This is the result of a perfectly successful replacement performed before the current harness standard. I pad the harness rather than replace the shoulder. The padding requires frequent adjustment, and I resent this as though it were an injustice imposed upon me, rather than a decision I continue to make.

I have a room above a pressure-vessel district. At intervals during my sleep, everything below me becomes a little rounder.

There is someone I used to live with who still receives my notices of scheduled absence. Neither of us has corrected the record. Occasionally they send one back with a location circled and a note about the food.

The corrector is not my closest acquaintance. We have worked together often enough to be tired of some of the same things.

That is why they bring me to the doorway.

***

The room passes its first inspection.

Its corners agree. The weight hangs properly. A survey strip laid across the threshold ages at the same rate at both ends.

I test the recess in the far wall because recesses are often the beginnings of receiving structures. There is no feeding charge, no concealed fastening tissue, no response when I place a standardized load against it.

I lean into it myself.

“Nothing?” the corrector asks.

“A wall.”

They enter that in their notes, which is irritating.

There is a faint difference in its color where my shoulder has touched it. This fades before we leave.

I recommend sealing it because I once inspected a passage that was safe until it learned which way people preferred to go. I had certified it. The certification was accurate at the time.

No one died. Two carriers were lost, and one worker came back with a younger graft on a leg that had not yet been injured. The practical difficulties lasted much longer than the investigation.

I still check that worker’s route when it appears in the maintenance list.

The corrector knows some of this. They do not accuse me of being timid.

They say, “Let’s give it another cycle.”

***

I return without being asked.

The corrector has brought a seat. It is a poor seat for the purpose: narrow, stiff-backed, intended for someone leaning forward over handwork. They sit with their cold hand laid flat on one thigh.

I check the threshold again.

“Has it changed?” they ask.

“It has accommodated your feet.”

“Most floors do.”

“Most floors have orders.”

I hear how this sounds only after saying it.

The corrector shifts their feet. Two shallow dark places remain in the grain.

There is nowhere to put my instruments except on the floor. I dislike laying a calibrated strip directly on living tissue; the strip can begin to take the room’s measurement as an instruction. I keep the case across my knees and sit beside the doorway.

We spend some time there without conversation.

An empty wrapper travels past outside, catches on the platform edge, and slowly works itself free.

Nothing grows beneath my case.

At the end of the visit, the corrector asks whether I will report the room.

“I have reported it.”

“Oh.”

“As an incomplete survey.”

They lift their head.

“It is an incomplete survey,” I say. “I don’t know what it does.”

***

I expect to become interested in the room as a professional problem.

Instead, I become particular about the hour at which I visit.

Earlier in the cycle, freight passes the entrance in broad, uninterrupted loads. The movement makes it difficult to sit without anticipating the next clearance. Later, there are gaps. Through them one can see a low branching structure on the opposite side of the lane. It has been repaired with tissue of a slightly different green.

I begin to recognize the repair by the changing angle of light.

The corrector’s neighbor comes sometimes. Their recovery has left them with an appetite they find embarrassing and therefore discuss constantly.

At first I assume the corrector enjoys these visits. Then I hear them arguing about a food container.

“You returned it with the hinge pulled out.”

“It was too stiff.”

“It was meant to be stiff.”

“Then you should have warned me.”

“I did warn you.”

“You said to be careful. That is not a warning. You say it about everything.”

The corrector takes the container home to mend it.

On another cycle the neighbor comes alone and brings three wrapped portions. We eat two. Neither of us proposes opening the third.

They ask what I have discovered.

“The room is growing very slowly.”

“So am I.”

I look up, and they rattle with amusement at my expression.

I had not thought of them as someone who might make a joke I would like.

***

After a while, I find a change.

The recess has become asymmetrical.

Its left side is shallower, the surface slightly raised. I measure it three times. Then I turn around and stand with my back against the wall.

It fits my shoulders.

I step away so quickly that the neighbor stops unwrapping their food.

For several moments I cannot make myself explain.

The room has not moved. Nothing reaches after me. The small raised area is scarcely visible under the diffuse light.

“It’s what walls do,” the neighbor says at last.

“Not usually after so little contact.”

“How much contact?”

I consult my notes.

There is more than I remember.

I have been standing there while the corrector examines their hand, while the neighbor eats, while I wait for a carrier to clear the platform. On one visit I spent nearly half a cycle leaning against that spot, describing a dispute at work neither of them could possibly have needed to understand in such detail.

I write down the alteration.

For the next few visits I remain near the entrance.

The raised area does not spread.

***

There are things I would like this to mean. I am wary of them.

A room can grow around a persistent obstruction. It can mistake repeated pressure for demand. Somewhere below us there are probably chambers exquisitely fitted to obsolete machinery, still maintaining clearances for parts reclaimed centuries ago.

I know this. I tell the corrector.

They are scraping their little hinge with a hooked tool. The filings fall into a folded wrapper.

“You think it has mistaken you for something?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

I cannot give an answer that does not sound more important than the evidence warrants.

They hold out the hinge for me to examine. It has three leaves, one of which folds into a space I cannot quite see.

“Don’t force it,” they say.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were holding it as though you might.”

***

Eventually the survey office asks me to close the record.

I submit the dimensions, the charge readings, the observations taken at different points in the work cycle. Under probable function I put *occupied enclosure*. Under origin I put *undetermined*.

The form returns because those entries require a household registration or an installation number.

I take it home. It sits beside my food shelf for several cycles. Once, half asleep, I use the back of it to record a noise from the pressure-vessel district.

There is no great confrontation. I visit the office. Someone I trained with listens to my explanation while cleaning dust from the seam of their reader. We establish that the platform still belongs to an active freight district, although no freight has been assigned to it for a very long time.

The room becomes an ancillary space.

This costs someone part of a maintenance allowance. They send me a curt message. I send an apology and the inspection schedule.

The sign outside begins growing the new designation.

The corrector trims it before the letters become crowded. They do this with more care than the designation deserves.

***

I do not move into the room.

I still sleep above the pressure vessels. I still adjust my harness padding instead of doing something sensible about my shoulder. When I am away on a long inspection, I find that I miss the room and am annoyed by the fact that it continues without me.

The neighbor tells me what I missed.

Usually very little.

Once, an argument. Once, an entire cycle during which nobody came.

One evening I arrive to find the corrector asleep in their unsuitable seat. Their cold hand has slipped between their knees. The neighbor is absent.

I put down my case.

Across the lane, the repaired branch shows dark against the older tissue. Ventilation leaves are lifting somewhere out of sight; loose fibers pass the entrance at shoulder height.

I sit on the floor beneath the recess. My feet are sore. There is a small hard accumulation under one divided sole that I have neglected for too long.

The corrector wakes when I loosen my foot pad.

“Inspection?” they ask.

“No.”

They settle again.

After a little while, I reach back and feel the raised place in the wall. It is still there, neither larger nor more elaborate. I shift until my narrower shoulder rests against it.

The floor begins, very slowly, to lift beneath my heel.

I move my case out of its way.
