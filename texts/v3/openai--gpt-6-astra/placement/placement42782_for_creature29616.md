I think I would live near a crossing, in a chamber people mistook for a passage.

There would be a second opening at the back, but it would lead only to a shallow recess where I kept my bedding. Visitors would discover this after stepping over my food. Some would apologize. Others would ask why I lived in such an inconvenient place, as though I had hollowed it out myself.

I would have put a cord across the entrance at first. Later I would have taken it down. Too many people arriving with something heavy would need me to come and hold the other end.

That is probably how my work would begin: not with a recognized aptitude, but with being available.

I would be an ordinary human person, with sore places at the bases of my thumbs and a habit of rehearsing what I intended to say until the moment to say it had passed. I would not be a floor listener. Left beside a seam long enough, I would begin to hear whatever I feared most.

But I could remember a route after walking it. More usefully, I could remember where a route had been difficult.

A turn that was easy for two people carrying a dish could catch the end of a sleeping board. A low rise scarcely noticed on the outward journey could stop a loaded sledge coming back. I would learn these things by being the person at the rear, unable to see what had gone wrong, with everyone ahead saying, “A little farther.”

Eventually I would begin going ahead.

***

I would keep route marks on strips of old cloth.

Not maps of the whole city. I would have tried that once: laid out a broad piece of cloth, borrowed pigment, made the courts small enough to fit. The result would have been beautiful to me for about three days. Then someone would ask whether the narrow way beside the six-household wall could take her mother’s chair, and I would have to go and look.

The cloth would show where everything was and omit what she needed.

After that I would measure with cord. There would be knots for the width of a carrying board, for the widest sledge in my district, for two carriers who could not safely let go. I would stretch the cord across a doubtful place, crouch, look at the approach, and sometimes still get it wrong.

Eren would dislike my first measurements.

“You’ve measured the basin,” she would say. “Where are the hands?”

I would add the hands.

For a while I would become tiresome about this discovery. Other people would be talking about a blocked passage and I would interrupt to explain that the object was not the only thing that had to pass through. Eren would let me do it twice before saying, “Yes. We were there.”

My strips would acquire short sequences of marks: a rounded corner, three hollows, a threshold to cross sideways. They would be prompts for someone who already knew the district, not instructions a stranger could follow. When people borrowed them, I would make them tell me the route back.

This would be useful, and also an opportunity to enjoy being consulted.

I would need to be watched for that.

***

In the back recess I would keep a strip I no longer lent.

It would describe the way to a court where I had lived as a child. The court would still exist. That would be the difficulty. It would not have vanished into a pool or become an inaccessible hollow. Another household would be sleeping there, using the wall where my mother had dried her cloth.

Once I would go back on the pretext of checking a passage.

A child would be sitting in my old sleeping place, biting the rim of a cup. Someone would ask whether they could help me. I would say no, and then, because that sounded unfriendly, ask about the seep.

They would answer carefully. They would assume I had come for my work.

I would leave with information I did not want and feel ashamed of having required their courtesy.

On the old route strip there would be a small mark for a broken threshold. It had been repaired before we left. I would never correct it.

***

I would not live alone all the time.

There would be a woman who scraped the upper portions of food walls from a low platform and brought home fine pale residue in her hair. She would stay for several nights, then go to her sister’s, where there was less traffic and a chamber in which singing sounded good.

She would say I made it too easy for people to interrupt us.

I would say people only came when they needed something.

“That is not a different answer.”

Sometimes I would pretend to be asleep when someone called through the entrance. She would remain still beside me, waiting to see whether I could bear it. Usually I could not.

I would be fond of telling myself that my chamber was simply in the wrong place. She would point out other chambers.

There would be one we nearly took: darker, farther from a reliable drip, with enough floor for both our sleeping wraps to lie flat. We would spend an evening there deciding where things might go. I would even stretch my measuring cord across the entrance.

Then I would delay.

The next time we visited, someone else would have set bowls inside.

She would be angry with me, reasonably. I would be angry too, though I would have difficulty saying with whom.

***

When the floor changed under Eren’s court, I would be at the crossing with a piece of pigment in my hand.

I would have marked a broad curve on the stone, directing sledges around a shallow depression. The first families would follow it. Then swimmers would begin coming out of the depression, pulling themselves over dry ground toward the very passage I had chosen.

That would not prove the passage unsafe. I would know this. Animals sometimes stopped, scattered, doubled back.

I would go far enough to see grazers above the usual damp line.

Coming back, I would rub out the curve with my sole. It would smear rather than disappear.

Someone would already be turning a loaded sledge toward it.

“Not that way now,” I would say.

“You sent the others.”

“Yes.”

There would be a pause in which I wanted to explain every observation that had changed. Behind the sledge, another household would arrive.

I would put down the pigment and take the front cord.

The alternate way would have a doorway stone projecting into it. I would have measured it before. There would be room for the sledge if we shifted the load and lifted one corner; no room to do that while another sledge came through.

So I would stay and arrange the turns.

This would be slow, necessary work, and I would spend much of it looking back toward the court because Eren had not appeared.

When she did, the two brothers would be carrying their father beside her. The repaired loop would hold him level. His head would be turned away from them, his mouth tightly shut.

I would take the rear end of the pole from one brother.

The weight would surprise me. It always would.

Eren would tell us to shorten our steps. At the projecting stone, the father’s hand would close around my wrist.

“Mind my foot.”

“I see it.”

“Then mind it.”

We would get him through.

I would not feel the knock that came next. I would hear one carrier draw breath, and see Eren stop with her weight over her good leg. For a moment nobody would move.

Then she would say, “Keep going.”

***

Later, on ground that had so far remained dry, I would try to find out who had taken the first route.

Not everyone would remember. Some would remember with great certainty and describe a different crossing. One woman would become angry because I kept asking after she had told me her children were here.

They would all be accounted for eventually, but not through my efforts alone. A boy would arrive looking for a missing food dish and mention the last household as though I ought to have known where they were.

I would have a headache by then. I would resent the boy for half a breath.

Eren would find me washing pigment from the route strip.

She would lower herself beside the basin and hold out her hand. There would be a small puncture near the thumb where the awl had slipped.

“Have you clean cloth?”

I would look at the strips.

“Clean,” she would repeat.

There would be some in my bedding. I would have to unpack nearly everything to reach it.

While I bound her hand, she would ask about the route. I would tell her where the animals had moved, which families had passed before I changed the mark, where we had lifted the sledges. She would ask one or two questions. Then she would say the binding was too tight.

I would loosen it.

***

I think I would remain near the crossing, though perhaps not in that same chamber.

People would know me as someone to fetch when a thing had to be carried through a doubtful place. Some would trust me too much. A few, after the evacuation, would insist on walking the route themselves. I would accompany them, trying not to sound injured.

I would still want to make a map.

On quiet evenings I would work on it in the recess behind my sleeping place, drawing courts I knew and leaving gaps where I had not been. It would grow awkwardly across joined pieces of cloth. I would enjoy putting in the long, broad spaces. I would mark the chamber we had failed to take.

But the working cord would hang by the entrance, separate from it.

One end would be darkened by my hands. Farther along would be Eren’s added knots, slightly uneven, allowing room for fingers on either side of a basin.

When someone came for me, I would take that.
