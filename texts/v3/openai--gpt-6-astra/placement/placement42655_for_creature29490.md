I think I would be the pot-mender—the one who came down late and still looks up at the wrong sounds.

Not because I have mistaken the salt for sky. I know what is overhead. But a large sound reaches my shoulders before it reaches whatever part of me has learned to live here. My neck draws in. My face turns upward. By then someone has usually seen.

Seli does an imitation in which she looks up so violently that she falls backward. This is not accurate, but it is popular.

I have a working place behind one of the broad supports, where the floor stays cool. Three hollows in the stone hold three sorts of powder. There is a flat piece of hide on which I put things small enough to lose: flakes, plugs, the little drill that must be rolled between the palms. When I leave, I fold the hide inward from the corners. I learned to do that after something carried off a plug while I was reaching for it.

I never found out what wanted it.

People bring me vessels before they bring me news. A bowl appears beside my bedding in the night. In the morning its owner comes to explain that it has hardly cracked at all.

The owner has generally tried to mend it first.

Eda is particularly bad about this. She presses a greasy thumb over a leak, decides where the hole must be, and packs it with whatever she has ready. By the time she brings it to me, the damaged part is hidden beneath a hard lump. I have to remove her work without enlarging the break.

“Leave it,” she says. “Just mend the other side.”

I tell her to make pins.

She tells me she was making pins while I was still drinking the wrong water.

That happened once.

It is not going to become once merely because I say so.

***

Above, I made vessels as well as mended them. Down here I mend better ones than I ever made.

Some have walls so thin that I hold them against a bright seam to find the crack. Some are squat and heavy, made to sit between stones without spilling when the floor gives a small heave. Several have been repaired often enough that their owners must explain which parts may be touched.

I like working on a pot that is empty and dry. This preference is considered unreasonable.

There is usually something still soaking in it, or it is needed before the next meal, or the owner has brought it full to show me exactly how it leaks. They watch the water gather on my fingers with the injured patience of people whose property I am wasting.

My own drinking vessel is badly repaired. One half sits a little higher than the other. I made the repair when I first arrived, before I understood how long the binding needed to stay dry here.

I keep meaning to do it again.

Instead, I drink from the low side.

***

What I have not learned is how to feel safe on the white ground.

I can cross it. I know the signs people have shown me. I do not step on a greasy stretch just because it looks smooth, and I know that a line of scavengers disappearing into a crack may be worth more attention than a ridge large enough to cast a shadow.

But I put my feet down too carefully. My knees stiffen. This makes me clumsy in precisely the places where I need not to be.

Eda once took me out beyond the stakes and told me to stand still.

“Not like that.”

I asked how many ways there were.

She put her hand against my back and pushed. I had to take a step.

“That is what the ground does,” she said. “Let it.”

I disliked the lesson. I disliked being pushed. For several days afterward I caught myself remembering it while I worked.

Now, when the little repeated lifting comes, I sometimes manage to leave my knees loose.

Sometimes I still look up.

***

The question of moving makes me disagreeable.

I want to move before the grazers reach the drinking channel. I have said this often enough that people begin answering before I finish. They ask where I propose to find another water hollow. They ask whether I intend to carry the ledge.

I have no good answer. I have a sleeping place with a little more height above the floor than Eda’s, and no child. This is brought up when I am especially persuasive.

What I do not say is that the channel interests me.

It is made in sections. Eda’s mother fitted them so neatly that, beneath the mineral deposit, there are joints narrower than a pin shaft. I have cleaned two of them. I think the sections could be lifted, packed, and carried.

The hollow could not.

I spend a good deal of time considering how to move the part I understand.

***

When Seli comes for the second bowl, I am opening a crack in a cooking pot.

She stops at my shoulder.

“You’re breaking it.”

“Yes.”

“Does he know?”

The owner is within hearing. He comes over.

I explain again why a narrow, dirty crack cannot be filled properly. While I explain, Seli reaches for the large shallow bowl beside my foot.

“Not that one.”

She stops with her hands already under its rim.

“Your mother wants water carried?”

She nods.

“Good water?”

Another nod, impatient now.

I give her my drinking vessel and take the shallow bowl myself. There is nothing wrong with the bowl, but I do not want her hurrying across the island with it held in both arms, unable to see her feet.

At the broken crossing, Eda has found a place where the new water runs over a lip of dark stone before entering the pool. She is crouched with one foot awkwardly turned outward. Her sandal has slipped. The sore between her toes looks white at the edges.

I set down the bowl.

“Will this last?” I ask.

She thinks I mean the flow.

“No.”

I mean the crossing, the island, the sleeping ledges. I mean the route from my work hollow to the place where we eat. But the bowl is filling, and this is not the time to make her answer all that.

She shifts to get her foot out of the wet.

“Sit back,” I tell her. “I can hold it.”

For a moment she does not let go. Then she does.

The water is warm enough that I feel it through the clay. A grazer comes toward my wrist, its head moving slightly from side to side. I turn it with the edge of my hand. It goes around the bowl instead.

Behind me, Seli tips my crooked vessel into a carrying pot.

“Yours leaks,” she says.

“Only if you fill it that far.”

She considers this explanation. I can hear how poorly it has served me.

The bowl grows heavy. I brace an elbow against my knee, then remember to loosen the knee. Under my sandal there is a small movement, or perhaps only the strain of holding still.

“Eda.”

“I feel it.”

“Now?”

“Not yet.”

She puts her hand under the bowl beside mine. Together we wait for a little more water.
