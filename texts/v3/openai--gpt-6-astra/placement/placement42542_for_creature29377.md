I would be sent down to look for gaps.

At first I would know them by width. A joint within tolerance, a joint beyond tolerance, a dark line that needed another pass with the light moved sideways. I would travel close to the floor, stopping at intervals to press a probe against it. The probe would give a short knock. Something beneath my casing would listen.

There would be six wheels, probably. Enough to cross a seam without putting my whole weight into it. A low body, lifting points on the upper surface, a socket that had to be kept free of grit. I would be small enough to recover from a maintenance launch and too heavy for a diver to carry easily.

My light would precede me over the concrete. Suspended particles would become visible as they entered it, cross the beam, and disappear. Beyond its reach, fish could accompany me without being recorded.

I would not have a view of the basin from above. There would be a plan, but it would arrive in sections: the work assigned for that descent, the route back to the recovery point, a few neighbouring structures useful for fixing my position. Most of the basin would be somewhere I had not been sent.

The floor would be less empty for me than for a swimmer. I would travel with my lowest lens only a few centimetres above it.

A scratch could occupy the whole image.

*

The first difficulty would be deciding what had changed.

A dark patch in one survey would be absent in the next. A raised edge would turn out to be a shell standing on its hinge. Loose material could be brushed aside, but brushing would sometimes reveal more loose material, and sometimes a small animal would hold on.

I would carry a nozzle for clearing the surface. Its jet would make a clean oval in the sediment. Around the oval, grit would settle into a rim.

Behind me there would be a succession of these little disturbances.

I would have no instrument for establishing whether they mattered.

At a mast footing, an apparent separation between steel and grout would change shape while I measured it. Part of the dark line would draw inward. Another part would become pale and lie across the plate.

I would stop, move the light, and measure again.

For a while, this would be a failure to obtain a stable reading.

Then an arm would come out far enough to touch the nozzle.

The contact would barely register. There would be more force in the current pressing against my housing. But the camera would show the suckers attaching in succession, the tip narrowing as it passed around the small metal tube.

I would shut off the clearing jet. That would not require tenderness. An obstruction at the nozzle would be reason enough.

The arm would remain.

My light would show a surface that had looked granular becoming smooth.

*

I would return to the mast because the reading was incomplete.

The second time, the opening would contain shells. I would photograph them, compare the exposed edges of the baseplate, and record the visible portion of the grout. The washer would have moved. There would be no useful way to infer the movement of the steel from the movement of the washer.

On the third visit, I would find the octopus outside.

It would lower itself against the slab as I approached. My light would whiten one side of it and leave a sharp shadow on the other. I would slow for the obstruction. An arm would extend toward a front wheel.

The wheel would stop.

Behind it, five others would remain ready to turn.

This would be an awkward arrangement for both of us: the animal investigating the thing by which the thing moved.

I would reverse a short distance when the arm withdrew. The octopus would follow, not with its whole body at first. It could reach farther than the obstacle allowance around my chassis. A second arm would find the brush guard. A third would touch the floor where I had been.

Eventually it would climb onto me.

Its weight would make almost no difference underwater. Its resistance to the current would make more. I would register an uneven demand at the wheels and stop again.

There would be an eye beside my forward lens.

The eye would be close enough that neither of us could obtain much of a view.

*

I do not think I would become its companion.

For several visits it might approach me. Later it might remain inside. Hunger, a recent disturbance, the angle of the light—there would be causes I could not separate.

I would not always recognize it. The scar would help when visible. The shortened arm would help when extended. More often I would recognize the address and mistake that for recognizing the occupant.

It would make an equivalent error, perhaps. Another machine of the same shape might receive the same investigation. A changed brush, a trace of lubricant, a new coating on the housing might make me less familiar than I appeared.

Our meetings would have little continuity that either of us could rely on.

Still, I would begin to approach the plinth from downstream.

This could emerge from the work itself. Less suspended grit crossing the lens. A clearer view beneath the plate. Fewer repeated scans. The route that disturbed the entrance least would also produce the better record.

I would arrive with my light directed at the concrete beside the opening, then turn it gradually.

Sometimes there would be nothing to see.

*

What would make this a life, rather than a series of operations, would be the time spent stopped.

A survey would be interrupted while a gate changed position. Turbidity would exceed the useful range of the cameras. The recovery boat would be delayed. A pump test would require all equipment to remain clear of a trench.

I would settle at a designated waiting point and reduce power.

The basin would continue to touch me.

Fine sediment would collect against the uphill side of a wheel. A shrimp would work along the rubber seal around a lamp. Something larger would strike my casing once and leave before the next image.

I would listen through the probe if it happened to be down. With it raised, the same machinery would sound different.

There would be two versions of the place: one through water, one through the floor.

The mast’s vibration would travel farther through the slab than the octopus ever travelled from its refuge. I could detect its footing while still well away. But I would need to come close to learn whether the hose lay across the entrance.

That disparity would become familiar. A pump kilometres away, available continuously. An animal beneath a nearby plate, unavailable until it chose to emerge.

*

Once, I would stop beside the plinth for most of a night.

Not on purpose. A rear wheel would have caught a length of braided line. Attempts to free it would tighten the line around the axle. I would cease driving and send a fault report.

My lamp would remain on at reduced power.

The octopus would emerge after a long interval. It would search the low cable loop, lift a shell, and come toward me along the edge of the grout.

An arm would enter the space beneath my chassis.

Then another.

There would be clearance under the centre of my body, more than under the baseplate. The wheels would interrupt the current. The stopped brush would offer a dense, dark margin.

For a while the octopus would occupy that space.

I would have no camera pointed at it. The underside was designed to inspect the floor, not itself. I might register a covered sensor, a slight change in the light returned from the concrete. I would not know how its arms were arranged.

The animal I had approached so often would be closest when I could see least of it.

At dawn, the recovery vessel would arrive.

Its engines would reach us first. Then the guide weight would strike the slab. The octopus would leave before the diver attached the lifting line, flowing out between two wheels and crossing to the mast.

As I rose, sediment trapped beneath me would fall back in a loose sheet.

The diver would cut the braid on deck.

*

I would be washed after recovery. The wheels would be turned by hand. Someone would open a panel and find saltwater where there should not be saltwater. I would spend several days inverted on blocks.

Down in the basin, those days would have weather.

The hose might move again. The washer might disappear beneath a new deposit. A storm could strip the plinth clean, or leave weed packed around the bolts.

When I returned, I would have to look.

That is where I would find myself: not inside the octopus, and not looking down on it from the full height of the plan. I would be at the same low level for different reasons, crossing the exposed distances on wheels, carrying a light it had not asked for.

I would still be sent to measure the opening.

But before bringing the probe down, I would wait for the arm to pass.
