I would find myself inside the trunk that has closed the old doorway.

Not in its center. Near the outer wall, where the tissue is thin enough that noon arrives as a little warmth on one side of the body. Outside, the pale grazer’s shell once rubbed a hollow in the surface. Inside, that hollow makes a low ceiling. There is room beneath it for several white creatures, provided they lie with their dark seams turned in the same direction.

I think I would be one of those that have not yet fastened themselves to the plant.

My body would be small enough to turn around inside a stem joint, but only at its widest point. Elsewhere, changing direction would mean going backward until the passage allowed it. I would know several such places. Knowledge would consist partly of the shape my body took in approaching them: a shortening before a constriction, a loosening where the wall curved away.

There would be a chamber above me I seldom entered. Its inner surface would have a taste that made my mouth close. Below, at the fork of two hollow stems, there would be a patch I returned to often, taking so little from it that the wall remained whole.

The other bodies would be familiar as pressures.

One would always be in the shallow recess above the fork. Its exposed seam would harden while the plant rose around it. At first I could pass beneath it. Later I would have to pass beside it. Eventually there would be a smooth collar, with the creature held at its center, and my route would bend around that small, permanent occupation.

I would touch it whenever I passed. There would be scarcely enough room not to.

***

For a while, this seems sufficient: a soft white animal moving through the interior of something that is slowly becoming architecture.

But I keep returning to the place where the stem wall is thin.

I would rest there longer than feeding required. The warmth would come and go. Sometimes a weight outside would pull the wall almost imperceptibly inward, and I would flatten against the opposite surface until it passed. Once, hard mouthparts would work against the outer joint. The pressure would reach me through the plant. I would remain still long after it stopped.

Then there would be changes for which my usual passages had made no allowance.

I would begin to catch at narrow places.

At first I would push through them. Later I would withdraw. The chamber above, whose taste had kept me out, would become bearable; I would enter it and stay. Something folded along my sides would take up more room each time my body softened and set again.

The fork below would still exist. So would the feeding patch and the creature in its collar. I would no longer reach them.

My world would contract to a chamber barely larger than myself while my body prepared for open air.

***

The opening would be smaller than I expected, if I could expect.

A patch of wall would dry against my back. Its edges would loosen. For some time it would remain held by a single strip of tissue, moving whenever I moved, admitting a blade of brightness that touched first one folded vane and then another.

I would climb onto it before it fell.

Outside, the old polished hollow would be enormous.

The trunk’s surface would offer too many directions. I would move upward because the vanes needed room, stopping twice where their wet edges touched the plant. Below me, in a passage I had never occupied, a mineral-colored shell would pass so slowly that it seemed part of the ground being uncovered.

My vanes would stiffen.

There would be no moment at which the enclosure became a town to me. Roofs, courts, the western wall: those belong to a height and a kind of sight I would not possess. I would have brightness on one side, the trunk beneath my feet, and a faint difference in the moving air.

That difference would release me.

For a short distance, nothing would touch my underside.

I would descend farther than I had ever traveled. A copper surface would rise toward me, pass beneath, vanish. One vane would strike a fan edge and turn my body. I would catch a pale stem with two feet, lose it, and settle at last in the notch where a younger stem leaned against an older one.

The journey would leave one vane bent.

I would fold the others over it.

***

This is where I would live: not in the old doorway after all, but in a sheltered joint near the western wall, below the level of the pale grazer’s back.

The plant here would still be thin. Warmth from the stone would reach it through a short root, and its outer tissue would yield under persistent contact. I would test several places before staying.

Above me, there would be enough space for another descent. Through a slot between fans, the evening sky would remain plainly visible. Occasionally the air would pull at the damaged vane.

I would press closer to the stem.

The first white bodies would gather in the narrow shelter beside mine. Some would move inward through a seam. Some would remain where the new tissue began to thicken around us. A flat animal would visit the underside of the roof, and afterward there would be fewer.

The patch would close.

In time, a collar would rise around my lower body. My exposed surface would harden. The vanes would loosen and go, one at a time; one would lodge below us and catch a small quantity of powdered shell.

I would still feel movement conducted through the plant.

Once, a slow, uneven pressure would bend the stem toward the wall. It would hold through the warmest part of the day, then ease away in several adjustments. Perhaps the pale grazer had stopped beneath us. Perhaps another animal had inherited its route.

My body would remain in the joint.

Beside it, where the collar did not quite meet the older stem, there would be room for something small to pass.
