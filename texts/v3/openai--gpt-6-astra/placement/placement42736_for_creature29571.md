I would be below the court where the shells have accumulated, but above the water. There would be two courses of brick between my back and the underside of a stair. Something heavy could cross overhead without seeing me. I would feel it stop.

At first, I think, I would know the place through that pressure: a little grit loosening onto my shoulders, the passage briefly carrying a weight it did not usually carry. I would wait with one forefoot lifted. My nose would move.

The crickets would go on without interruption. Whatever was above me would eventually move too.

Then I could put my foot down.

I would have a body accustomed to being touched on both sides. In a broad passage I would keep near one wall; in a narrow one I would go faster, fur brushing brick, the way already held around me. Open ground would require more attention. There would be too many directions from which something could arrive, and nothing against my flank to tell me exactly where I was.

I would not be very old. The dust would have begun to redden my coat, but my ears would still be almost whole. One hind claw would be split. It would catch occasionally in dry bedding, and I would bite at it with sudden annoyance, interrupting whatever else I had been doing.

My sleeping place would be my own, though not entirely by choice.

Farther up the wall there would be a chamber I had once slept in among other bodies. I would still go past its entrance. I would know the smell before reaching it, and sometimes slow down. But an animal inside would shift toward the opening, or make a low sound, and I would continue to the smaller cavity beyond the turn.

There, I would have enough leaves to cover the brick beneath me, but not enough to prevent the cold reaching my hip before dawn.

I would bring more.

Not every leaf would be worth bringing. Some would break apart in my teeth. Some would seem dry until compressed, releasing a wet, sour smell. A good one would have a tough stem and a blade that folded without crumbling. I would carry it awkwardly, head turned, unable to see properly on one side. If I found a beetle on the way, I would drop the leaf.

Sometimes I would remember where I had dropped it.

My circuit would pass through four courts, though I would never see any of them whole. One would be the place where fruit came down. One would have a drinking edge that held my hind feet well. One would be crossed by the smell of an animal I avoided. The fourth would contain a long, dry ledge where I could hold food between my forefeet without being approached from behind.

I would spend a great deal of time there doing nothing impressive.

A snail would be difficult. I would turn it, press it down, turn it again. Slime would collect above my mouth. Somewhere close by, another animal would click, and I would not answer until I had finished worrying at the shell.

Then perhaps I would answer once.

The return click might come from below me.

That would be enough.

One night the route to the drinking place would have changed.

I would discover this nose-first. Air would be moving where there had been very little air before. The familiar passage would end in a rough opening, and beyond it the darkness would have a different depth. I would reach a forefoot down and touch loose material. A piece would fall.

I would draw back.

There would still be an old trace of my own passage on the surviving brick. I would follow it to the edge again, as though arriving more carefully might produce a different result.

It would not.

For a while I would go elsewhere and feed. Then I would return.

Below the break, water would continue making its small sounds. Thirst would not make the drop shallower. I would crouch with my tail against the wall and lick dust from my nose.

Eventually I would investigate sideways.

A root would come out of the masonry near my shoulder and disappear into a room I had never entered. Its upper surface would be dry. I would test it, retreat, put two feet on it. It would give a little under my weight, but not continue giving.

The room beyond would smell of bats.

I would dislike that room. Wings would stir above me, and their disturbance would travel across the vault in quick, crowded increments. I would flatten myself beside the root until they settled. Then I would descend behind a slab of fallen plaster and find a gap at floor level.

Cool air would come through it.

I would push my muzzle in. My ears would fold back. For a moment my ribs would fit tightly enough that I could feel my heartbeat against the edges.

Then I would be through, beside the tank, lower than I had intended to come.

There would be a safe place to drink, but I would have to find it.

I would not become the animal who knew the ruins. I would become an animal who knew one more way to water. On the return journey I would stop at each awkward place, smelling it from the other direction. The climb would take longer than the descent.

Two nights later, another animal would follow me into the bat room.

I would hear its feet behind me and hurry, unwilling to be caught in the narrow gap. At the water we would drink a little apart. Neither of us would click.

By the end of the rains, the root would carry several smells. A patch of plaster beside it would be rubbed smooth. I would no longer pause there, except when someone was coming the other way.

That is where I would find myself: not at the parapet looking out, but returning inward as the sky began to pale, with damp whiskers and something caught between my teeth.

At my chamber I would turn twice before lying down. A leaf would catch on the split claw. I would tug my foot free, bite the claw, lick it.

Beyond the brick, feet would pass toward the higher nest.

I would raise my head. For a moment my mouth would open to answer a sound that had not been made.

Then I would tuck my nose beneath my tail.
