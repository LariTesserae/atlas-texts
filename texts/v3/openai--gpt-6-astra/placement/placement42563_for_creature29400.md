I would be underneath one of the old animals, but not at the center, where the water first comes out of the stone. I would live near the rim, where a groove divides around a thickened foot and one branch gives less water than the other.

There would be room for me in the poorer branch.

Above me, the underside of the shell would be low enough to touch with my back. Below, the limestone would have a shallow defect: a pocket left by something dissolved out of it long ago. I could withdraw into that pocket when the foot moved. It would be a good place for a body of my size, and become a bad place very gradually.

I would know neither of those things in advance.

At first, I imagine knowing the old animal as a ceiling. But a ceiling does not tighten before it moves. It does not send a muscular shiver along a watercourse, closing one passage and opening another. This shelter would have habits I could feel through my whole body. Before the foot lifted, the stone beneath it would become less heavily loaded. Before a curtain opened, a little pressure would arrive in the water.

I would begin moving at those small changes. By the time the visible event occurred, I would already be feeding, or flattened into my pocket.

My shell would still be pale at its newest edge. There would be a notch on one side where another juvenile had caught me before I hardened. Beneath the notch, a few sensory bristles would grow turned inward. They would brush my own feet when I walked.

That would be part of being me: a faint report of company on a side where there was sometimes no one.

I would not be alone enough to discover the mistake.

Other small animals would occupy the groove. When water came, we would spread along its edges, keeping our mouths in the film and our breathing slits above the thicker paste. When it diminished, we would gather at the division. Someone would always be trying to pass beneath someone else. The pressure would wake my feet even when there was nowhere to go.

Food would arrive as a change in the work my mouthparts had to do. A strip scraped nearly bare would resist them; a renewed film would come away softly. Warm water would have several tastes. One would draw me forward. Another would make my fringe lift from the stone.

I would have no view of the basin.

For a long time, the largest space I encountered would be the gap made when the old animal raised its rim.

Cold would enter first. The sheltered bodies would tighten against the floor. Then vibrations that ordinarily reached us through the old animal would arrive through other contacts, sharp and difficult to separate. A passing foot might strike nearby limestone with enough force to stop my mouth.

There would also be food out there.

I would move toward it in short advances, carrying wetness beneath my shell. My rear feet would remain in the groove until the pull from the front became stronger than the hold behind. Then I would come out all at once and be a small raised shape on open stone.

Nothing would mark the event.

An animal broader than me would push against my flank. I would turn with it. Its bristles would find mine, withdraw, find them again. Beyond it, other bodies would be moving, and through those contacts I would acquire a direction.

I think this is where I would find myself most fully: just outside the shelter, with a route passing through my body.

Not making the route. Not knowing its destination. Walking because the bodies beside me had begun the long, uneven scrape of drying, while the stone ahead offered enough dampness to keep my underside open.

Sometimes I would transmit a rhythm before I followed it. My feet would change against the limestone; a neighbor would answer; only afterward would space open for me to move. At other times I would be the delay. My mouthparts would still be engaged in a rich patch. A damaged foot would hold badly. Pressure would accumulate against my shell until feeding became impossible.

From above, the crowd might appear to turn together.

Inside it, there would be all these small difficulties.

The old animal would shift while I was away.

On returning, I would encounter its rim sooner than expected. The entrance I had used would be pressed against the rock. I would work along it, tasting the moist line, finding feet where there had been gaps.

My pocket would still exist beneath the shell. I would not find it.

For a while I would remain beside a breathing opening. Young smaller than me would be lodged inside, where the current stayed warm. Their soft edges would touch my fringe. I would rasp at the residue below them, and when one lost its hold, I would eat it.

Then I would move on.

There would be another groove, another animal, an interval beneath an abandoned plate where the dampness smelled different and the floor yielded slightly under my feet. My shell would thicken. Openings would cease to admit me. I would learn them by entering as far as I could and backing out.

The notch would persist beneath each new rim.

Eventually I would occupy a shelf where the animals lay only one layer deep. My underside would rest on limestone rather than on the backs of others. Water would seep from a seam too narrow for any part of me except the ends of my fringe.

Here, when the buried flow changed, I might be among the first to stop.

The change would not arrive as a message. A familiar movement against my bristles would weaken. Something fine within the crack would contract. My mouthparts would pause, resume, pause again.

Beside me, an animal would still be scraping. Its vibration would obscure the seam.

I would press against it.

Perhaps it would stop. Perhaps my own fringe would settle again and I would return to feeding. The water might recover before anything traveled farther than a few shells.

But once, the stillness would continue outward.

Contacts would quiet on every side. With each cessation, smaller movements would become available: feet adjusting far beneath a neighboring rim, a worm withdrawing into stone, water touching the roof of a hollow below us.

I would remain open against the limestone.

There would be nothing in my body corresponding to the whole country, nothing that could contain its ridges or the extent of the listening crowd. Only the wet seam, the changed pressure, the neighbors now still enough that I could feel what had been there beneath their feeding.

And when scraping began again, somewhere beyond my contacts, I would receive it through one animal, then another.

I might wait a little longer.

Then my mouthparts would settle into the film.
