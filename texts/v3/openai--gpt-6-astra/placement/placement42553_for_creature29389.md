At first I would put myself in the shelter under the broken shell. There is room there for something small, and a roof that need not be held up. Food comes within reach. The water moves outside.

But the shell is only resting on the bottom. A stronger tide could turn it over without slowing.

I would be farther out, where there is no shelter separate from the body. Somewhere near the center of the basin, though I would have no way of knowing that. The water arriving there has crossed several kinds of ground. I would know the differences before I knew what was beneath me.

I think I would be the animal with the old injury along its margin.

Not because the injury makes it exceptional. It is simply where I begin to find the body: a small place that cannot flatten properly, admitting water when the rest of the edge has sealed. Fine grains lodge there. The skin tightens, releases, tightens again. On that side, holding still requires a little movement.

The remainder is less distinct. A broad weight. A number of points of purchase. A soft extension working somewhere under the front.

I would not feel the whole back at once. There would be a grain pressing between two plates, a cold movement through an open groove, the local insistence of something caught beneath me. Much of the body would become noticeable only when it needed adjusting. The injured edge would be present more often than the intact one.

Underneath, the ground would have detail.

What looks smooth from above would contain passages too narrow for the feeding lobe, firm crumbs that turn under pressure, the yielding wall of a burrow. The lobe would follow that wall for a short distance. At one point the mud would move independently of the water.

I would stop.

The worm would stop too.

For a while, there would be two pressures against the same thin partition. Then a little of it would give.

Eating would occupy most of the quiet water. Not a succession of discoveries, exactly. More a continuous sorting, interrupted by things that required care. A shell fragment with tissue deep in its hinge. An egg too firmly attached to draw free. Something long and soft that contracted beyond reach, leaving only a taste in the mud.

Sometimes I would take in more grit than food. The sediment would pass through me and return to the bed in shapes that briefly held together. I would move over some of it again.

I would have no clear account of where I had been.

There might be a patch of clay that made the hooks settle easily, or a chemical trace that brought the feeding lobe out before the body had finished securing itself. But a place could arrive again as water, carried from elsewhere. Familiarity would not always mean return.

When the current strengthened, I would first notice changes underneath.

A groove that had been draining gently would begin to pull. The loose material near the front would thin, exposing firmer mud. The lobe would withdraw. Along the edges, the hooks would take hold in sequence, and for a while the body would become lower and more firmly attached.

This would work often enough that I would keep doing it.

Then the layer I held would move.

There would be no immediate difference between gripping the bottom and gripping a piece of bottom that was leaving. The hooks would remain loaded. Mud would press against the underside. Only the shifting grains in the organs along my margin would begin to disagree with the rest.

One side would rise.

I would contract there, too late, and the current would enter beneath me.

Folded into the water, I would have very little to do. Keep the lobe covered. Close against the abrasive touch. Open a margin when something broad pressed against it, then close again if the pressure slid away.

There would be contacts that were not landings.

Once, perhaps, I would catch on a rounded stone. The hooks would scrape without finding purchase. Water would draw the body across it, pulling at the old injury, and I would leave a little tissue on a surface too smooth to hold me.

Afterward there would be mud.

I would not open all at once. A small part of the underside would test it while the rest remained folded. The mud would yield, then resist. More of the body would lower. Water would escape through the grooves.

For a long time, nothing would reach outward.

Eventually a fringe beneath the front would loosen. A few knobs would press into the new bed. Somewhere close by, an animal would be moving between grains.

That would be enough to begin.

And sometimes the contact at my edge would persist through several changes of pressure. It would not scrape like shell or part like mud. When I tightened, it would tighten too, not quite at the same moment.

I would feed beside it.

Between us, the narrow strip of accessible bottom would slowly be worked over. My lobe would encounter the other’s and draw back. Later it would extend again. Neither body would need to uncover itself.

If we shifted apart, the exposed place would fill with silt. I would not know whether the other remained a short distance away or had been carried beyond any movement I could register.

I would be left with the water reaching that edge again.

Near the center of the basin, then, I would lie almost flat, the injured side slightly raised. A few shell grains would collect against it. Under my front, something would resist being turned.

I would adjust my hold on the clay and try from the other side.
