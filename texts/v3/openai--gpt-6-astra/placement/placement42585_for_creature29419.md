I think I would first mistake one of the air chambers for the place I belonged.

A small room inside a room, darkness overhead and green light underneath; the noise of the falls coming through the beam against my shoulder. I could imagine being useful there. Someone would arrive with a question about a fastening, put a wet hand on the wood, catch their breath.

But no one lives in those chambers. After a while the air has been breathed too often. You have to put your face back into the cold and leave.

I would need somewhere to hang my clothes. I would need to have quarreled with someone about where they drip.

My house would be near the eastern end of the town, on a raft that receives the morning sun and loses it early. There would be another shelter close enough that I could hear a spoon scrape the bottom of a bowl. I would have thought this comforting when I first arrived. Later I would know who was eating, and what, and whether they were deliberately waiting until their mother had gone out before taking more.

I would have come from somewhere with a bank you could step onto.

Not far away. No extraordinary journey. My family would still live within two days’ travel, and would ask, whenever I visited, whether I meant to stay in the roofs. After enough years the question would become irritating chiefly because I sometimes asked it myself.

I would be good at handling clay.

That would not mean I could make a living firing pots here. There is too little dry working space, and fuel has to be brought up or brought in. But I could tell which jars were worth buying. I could hear certain faults when a vessel was tapped, and distinguish a crack through the body from a mark left in the making. For a while people would bring me every damaged bowl in town.

I would explain that knowing why a pot leaked did not mean I could make it stop.

They would continue bringing them.

***

The task I would gradually acquire is looking after some of the jars used to carry air down.

Not all of them. Different households keep their own, and there would be a woman in the western street who considered my way of padding a rim wasteful. We would both have evidence.

Mine would stand mouth-down on split reeds beneath the sleeping platform. I would dislike finding other things put inside them. A jar with an onion in it is easily mistaken for a jar available for work, especially by someone reaching under a platform before there is much light.

For a long time I would be better at preparing them than taking them below.

Swimming across water and descending into it are different accomplishments. I could cross from raft to raft readily enough, but halfway down a ladder one ear would begin to hurt. I would keep trying to swallow. The bright opening above would remain distressingly near.

At first I would say I had forgotten something.

Eventually Eda would notice that I forgot things at the same depth.

She would not give me a speech. She would make me descend less far, then come back up. We would do this on a day when she had something else she wanted to finish, and I would know it.

“Again?”

“Not yet,” she would say, holding my elbow.

Waiting would be the part I disliked most. I would want either to succeed or to be excused.

***

Years later I could manage the nearer chambers. I would still have one ear that needed time.

On a working morning I would turn a jar over in the floor opening, trapping air inside it. There would be a little skill in keeping its mouth level as it went under, though from above it would look like very little happening.

Below the raft, the jar would pull upward.

I had expected it to feel empty. Instead, it would insist on going home.

One hand would hold the netting around its belly. The other would take the ladder, then the rope. I would carry it close against me rather than at arm’s length; I would have learned that after losing one. For several weeks afterward a neighbor would occasionally ask whether I had considered making jars out of fish.

My partner would go slightly ahead.

I would know the soles of her feet very well. One would have a thick patch beneath the second toe, where she worked a steering pole barefoot. Above water she would generally be looking past me toward whatever needed doing. Below, she would turn frequently to make sure I was still there.

This would annoy me on good days and reassure me on bad ones. She would not always know which kind it was.

At the chamber entrance I would feel for the toggles. The last two would be close together, because there was not enough clear rope beneath the roof to space them as generously as the others. I would have complained about this before learning that everyone else recognized the chamber by that very inconvenience.

We would push the jar’s mouth under the trapped air and tilt it. The released bubble would strike the darkness overhead with a blunt, startling sound.

Then our heads would come up.

There would scarcely be room to turn without touching.

“You’ve scraped it,” she would say.

“Where?”

“The shoulder.”

“I scraped it last time.”

“I know.”

She would put my hand on a splinter projecting from the entrance. We would have to come back with something to bind it down.

Work here would so often produce another piece of work.

***

I would not be the person who remembered every repair.

I would remember a binding because I had made it badly and someone else had needed to cut it free. I would remember a beam because I had struck my head against it. Other places would remain oddly indistinct, however often I passed through them.

I would keep short lengths of cord beneath the jars, with knots for things I meant to do.

This would work until I could no longer remember whether three knots meant three jars or the third chamber. After that I would ask my partner to remind me, and she would ask what the cords were for.

Still, people would begin to wait for my opinion before loading a new jar with weights. I would be asked to go along when a household first tried an old room as a resting place. Someone would send a child to fetch me, saying only that they needed the person who knew about the clay.

I would complain about being interrupted.

I would go.

***

There is one afternoon I can place myself in more clearly than the others.

I have come back early. My ear would not clear, and I was cold before we reached the second rope. Nothing has gone wrong, but the unfinished task has followed me up the ladder.

I sit with my belt beside me, turning a jar to examine its scraped shoulder. Water runs out of my hair and onto my knees.

Through the open side of the shelter I can see Eda’s brother crossing a neighboring raft. He is carrying a bundle of greens against his chest and walking with the offended care of someone whose hands are full while other people’s are not.

My partner has gone down again with someone else.

I listen for them.

There is no chance of hearing their movements through the roar beneath the town, but every small knock against our ladder makes me look up.

A child from next door comes to ask whether I have finished with the shallow bowl. I have been examining a line in its base.

“Not yet.”

“Is it broken?”

“No.”

“Then can we have it?”

I turn it once more. The line is shallow, an old scratch stained dark with use. I knew that before the child arrived.

I give it back.

For a little while I sit without finding another thing to pick up. Sunlight reaches through the floor opening and trembles on the underside of a roof pole. Then a hand closes around the ladder.

I move the belt out of the way.

My partner comes up first, breathing hard. She remains on the top rung while the other swimmer climbs into the neighboring house.

“Done?” I ask.

She shakes her head. The binding we meant to replace supports another root we could not see from the doorway. Eda will have to look at it.

I reach down for the jar.

It is awkward to take its weight while she is still holding it from below. For a moment we pull against each other. Then she lets go, and I lift it through the opening, careful of the rim.
