At first I would be where the rain slows down.

Not beneath the roof exactly, but against the place where the larger body interrupts it. Water would arrive warm along one side of me and cool along the other. Before there were directions, there would be that difference. I would turn toward one, then the other, putting my mouth against whatever my turning brought near.

There would be a surface that sometimes became a hand.

I would not yet distinguish the hand from the shelter. Both would hold when I pressed against them. But the hand would change its hold in answer to mine, and when I loosened one finger, something beneath it would move closer.

That would be the first thing I learned well.

***

I think I would be the smaller patch.

Not small in the way a grown creature understands smallness. The roof would have no opposite edge. The distance between one of the larger creature’s ribs and the next would be a distance I could cross, or fail to cross, depending on the rain.

I would have more grip than strength. Once fastened, my fingers would not readily open. The larger one would have to wait for me.

Sometimes it would begin to descend and discover that I still held both its throat and the roof. It would stop, hanging crookedly, while I tried to resolve this. One of its hands would come up. A fingertip would press beneath each of mine in turn.

I would close them on the fingertip instead.

For a while we would get nowhere.

***

The first ground I knew would be the shelter floor. I would be set beside the shallow depression while the larger one checked the crevices.

The shed husk would still be there.

I would take it in both hands. Its edge would soften in my mouth without becoming food. When I lowered it into the water, it would darken; when I lifted it, water would remain inside.

This would occupy most of an afternoon.

The larger one would leave and return. Its foot would pass near me, carrying a smell of the channel. I would raise the husk toward it. It would touch the husk once, then go on arranging something beneath the overhang.

Later, trying to retrieve the husk from the depression, I would lean too far forward.

The water would be shallower than my head. I would not know to lift my head.

A hand would close around my hips and set me upright.

After that, for several days, I would reach into the depression with one hand braced behind me, even when it was empty.

***

I would follow the path before I understood that anyone had made it.

Under my feet, a narrow succession of bearable places would lead from the shelter into green shade. Beyond them the ground would prick and shift. I would sometimes put a hand down outside the route and draw it back with a bright grain caught in the skin.

The larger one would turn my palm upward.

Its mouth would be surprisingly firm. It would take the grain between its lips, pull, and wait while my fingers opened again.

I would learn to watch its feet. Then I would learn that watching was not enough: the places broad enough for its pads were not always places where mine could settle. Its step might bridge a crack into which my whole foot entered.

There would have to be a smaller path within the path.

I would find it by stopping often.

Beneath the leaves, the larger one could reach the flowers while lying down. I would have to stand on its belly. The surface would rise gently under my feet, and I would spread my toes to stay upright.

Some flowers would be beyond my reach. One would not.

My finger would come back sweet and peppery. I would put it in again.

The larger one’s hand would close around my wrist before I reached into the next blossom. It would hold me there, firmly enough that I could not advance, until I turned toward another.

I would remember that flower. By morning it would be folded and indistinguishable among the others.

***

There would be things I learned incorrectly.

I would believe that the mountain knocked because the larger one lay down.

Its body would lower, its hands would spread, and then the stone would answer. I would copy it many times. I would press my whole belly to the floor and wait.

Nothing.

I would lift my head to see what I had done wrong.

Once, by chance, the knock would come while I was trying. I would remain flat long afterward, fingers driven into the paste.

For the rest of that evening I would move carefully, reluctant to press too hard.

***

The ring would first interest me because the larger one went there without me.

It would put me beneath the shelter, bring the folded leaf close, and leave. I would eat until its back moved beyond the nearest leg. Then I would follow.

It would hear my feet.

There would be several returns to the shelter. On the third, it would stay with me, holding the smooth dark object against its belly. I would explore the groove with my mouth. Its thumbs would continue around from the other side, meeting my face.

The ring would lift unattended.

Water would strike the stone. I would startle hard enough to lose my grip on the object.

The larger one would catch that, too.

Later I would see it rising.

Its body would lengthen above the circle. Places I knew by touch would open into unfamiliar spaces. I would see daylight between the ribs. One foot would turn outward, slowly, with nothing beneath it.

I would lift my arms.

I would not be asking to fly. I would want it to come within reach.

When it fell, I would run toward it across ground I had not tested.

That would be how I acquired the deep cut in my left heel.

For several nights, flattened against the roof, I would keep that foot drawn close. Rain would wash through the cloudy seal. The larger one would touch it each time we descended, and I would grip its wrist before it could finish.

***

I would grow unevenly.

One arm would become inconvenient before the other. My head would no longer fit beneath the same fold of the larger creature’s throat. I would push into it while settling for the day and be moved aside.

Beside it, there would be unoccupied roof.

I would fasten there badly at first. Too much of my weight would hang from my fingers. My back would peel loose whenever I slept.

A hand would rise beneath me.

Eventually I would learn to spread myself far enough that I did not need it. Rain would pass between my ribs. The amber thickness within me would settle. I would sleep through the hand’s withdrawal.

***

Where I would find myself, after a time, is near the entrance to the thicket, attending to a piece of gravel.

The mountain would have knocked. A flake would stand upright in the path, its buried end held fast by fresh paste. I would pull with both hands. It would not come free.

Behind me, the larger one would wait.

I would brace a heel, remember the cut, change my footing. The flake would loosen so suddenly that I fell backward with it against my chest.

For a moment I would hold it there.

Then I would carry it to the side.

The larger creature would pass, touching one hand to the top of my head as it went beneath the leaves. I would follow it partway, then return. A smaller shard would remain in the hole.

I would work that loose as well.

There would still be afternoon left. The flowers would be opening. Under the roof, two places would wait for our bodies, and beyond the hollow there would be heights I had never seen.

But I would put my palm into the emptied place first, and press until I knew a foot could rest there.
