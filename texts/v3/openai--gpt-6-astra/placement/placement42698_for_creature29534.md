I would be in the passage that answers.

Not far along it. Close enough that, when the basin rises high, light reaches the lower part of my body around the bend. The rest of the time I have a white reflection on the opposite wall, a deep current against my underside, and the small sounds that come through stone.

You could pass the mouth of the passage several times without seeing me. There is a shelf there that appears to have sagged into the water. Cream-colored ridges run across it. A few brown threads hang from its edge.

One of the ridges is the rim of my shoulder.

I am of the resident’s kind, although at first we would look less alike than it and a root hauled from a flooded field. My plates have thickened. My limbs lie at angles that conceal their length. Where my rear pads rest against the floor, mineral has built up around them, leaving small openings through which I can still draw water.

I can move. It takes preparation.

The passage is not quite my burrow. I used to go through it.

***

The bench reaches me best through my left hind limb.

There is a seam in the chamber floor that interrupts some vibrations and carries others. A dropped pebble arrives as a little click with no direction. The basin’s upheavals spread through everything at once. But pressure on that end of the bench travels along a continuous bed of stone, past the four sockets, beneath the exposed conduit, and into the place where I have settled.

Two impacts. A pause. A sustained tremble.

I lift one nail from the wall and put it down again.

It is a small movement. The tip strikes a bare patch I keep clear of limestone. I have been maintaining that patch longer than I have maintained most of the joints in my body.

The signal does not mean *come*. We have another way of asking that, involving repetition.

This one gets an answer even when there is nothing to report.

Afterward I remain attentive. That is how I notice your weight.

You sit differently from us. Your pressure gathers in a few places and shifts abruptly. Your feet keep making and breaking contact with the floor. At first I cannot distinguish your movements from small things falling off the bench.

Then you become still.

The chamber continues around you, and you continue being there.

***

I would have some sight, but it would not organize the world for me.

The cups along my head see the basin as a direction of brightness. When a limb passes close to them, they close. I recognize the other resident partly by its uneven step, partly by the water arriving ahead of it.

Its scent has changed over the years. There used to be a sharper taste beneath the sweetness. Now I find that only after it has stripped away a piece of crust.

Today there is fresh tissue in the water.

I open the folds beneath my head and let the current pass over them. There is grit, a little sweetness, the familiar mineral taste, and the faint trace of the exposed patch. The wound is small. After the jet strikes, it becomes fainter.

I settle again.

Along my back, animals I cannot reach withdraw into their seams. A crab pulls at something near the base of a plate. I can feel the tug but not the object it has found.

There are places I keep bare. The insides of my wrists. The folds that open when I breathe. The patch of wall under my signaling nail. Elsewhere I scrape when the weight becomes troublesome, or when a seam begins to pinch.

On one shoulder there is a dead rosette enclosed almost completely in limestone. For a long time its leaves caught the current. Now water passes smoothly over the little mound it left.

Below it, in a younger crack, something has begun to root.

***

I did not become lodged here in a single bad season.

First this was a good place to rest. The current lifted my middle, relieving a joint that hurt when I crossed the shelves. Food arrived in the returning water. I could reach the wall and floor without stretching. The other resident passed close enough to touch me.

Later I found I needed to loosen my rear pads before leaving.

Later still, leaving required most of a quiet interval, and I had to wait beneath the arch until the next surge passed.

There was always somewhere I intended to go. The shallow shelf where the pale sand collected. A hollow that would choke if no one cleared it. The island, after the narrow jet had cleaned its top.

For a while I went less often.

Then the hollows began to arrive here.

The other resident carried a stone into my passage. It was small enough to turn over in one hand, with an amber film growing on its sheltered face. The pocket beneath it had filled with sediment. I held the stone while the resident worked the packed sand loose.

Afterward it left the stone within my reach.

Others followed. Not many. Enough that the current now divides around them before passing beneath me. There are transparent animals in two of the hollows, and a crab small enough to shelter beneath the last joint of my wrist.

I still have work that requires a delicate hand.

***

I would first meet you by what you put into the water.

Your foot, perhaps. The sole makes a broad disturbance, followed by the slower cloud of grit released from its edges. The water carries an unfamiliar mixture: dry dust, fabric, the salts of a body that does not live submerged.

You stop at the turn because the passage seems occupied by rock.

The resident reaches past you and touches my forelimb.

I unfold it.

You draw back, striking the wall with something hard. The sound arrives through my pads and through the water almost together.

Then there is a long interval in which nothing touches me.

I keep my hand extended, supported underneath by the current. Its six tips drift a little apart. One has no nail. One cannot straighten completely. A thread of brown growth lies across the two outer fingers.

Eventually you put your hand beneath mine.

You would feel the weight first, then the small corrections as I find where your support is firm. My pad would adhere faintly to your skin. I would move a fingertip along the side of your thumb, looking for the division that is not there.

Behind you, the resident braces as the pool begins to flatten.

It touches your ankle again.

This time you know to withdraw it.

I lower my hand before the surge reaches the passage, and you let it go.

***

I would not ask you to free me.

You might think of doing it. Some of the deposits would yield to a tool. Others could be softened or patiently scraped away. But you would have to learn which pale surfaces were rock, which were old plate, and which concealed the thin, living fold beneath.

There are things I would let you remove.

A bead at the hinge of a limb. A sharp edge that catches when I expand. The loose crust above a place I can no longer clean.

We might spend a long time discovering the difference. Your hands would become recognizable to me by their pressure, and perhaps you would begin to feel when my limb was resting in them and when it was trying to move.

For the present, there is the small stone beside my wrist.

The last surge has turned it slightly. Water now enters beneath the amber film from the wrong side. I reach, but your foot occupies the place where I usually brace.

You see my fingers opening and put your hand out again.

I pass over it.

My tips settle against your ankle. I press once, wait, and press a little farther to the side.

You move.

With the space clear, I brace, turn the stone, and hold it while the next current tests its new position. A transparent body uncurls beneath the sheltered edge.

Then I let go.

You remain crouched in the passage, your hand still open. I rest two fingertips in it until the water begins to rise.
