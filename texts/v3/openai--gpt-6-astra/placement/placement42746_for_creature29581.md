I would live above a passage that people used before dawn.

The room would have been built to join two parts of a household that no longer belonged to one another. One doorway would be blocked with rubble, the other reached by an outside stair. In winter, the floor above the passage would be so cold that I would keep my boots beside the bed and put them on before standing. Whoever gave me the room would describe it as well situated.

It would be. I could reach the spring without climbing, which is different from being able to return from it without climbing.

For the first few weeks I would wake whenever someone passed below. A cough, the scrape of a basket against stone, two people deciding which way to go. Later I would sleep through most of it. I would learn the voices of people whose faces I rarely saw.

One woman would stop beneath my room every morning to shift her load. She would make a small, angry sound as she lifted it again. I would come to depend on hearing her. When she failed to pass for several days, I would ask after her too casually and be told that her daughter had finally come to help.

That would be my first attachment to the district: relief concerning someone I had never properly met.

***

I think I would have come there expecting to leave.

There would be a reason for that which sounded better when told to strangers. Work, perhaps, or a place on a caravan that had not yet been arranged. In the household where I grew up, people would say I had gone because of an argument. Both accounts would be true, though neither would include the part where I had stayed awake listening to my brothers breathe and felt that, unless I went soon, I would spend my whole life trying not to wake them.

I would have brought a blanket, a narrow cooking pot, a change of underclothes, and a small quantity of money sewn into a place that made it troublesome to reach. For some time I would regard this inconvenience as evidence of good judgment.

My first work would be carrying stone.

I would not be especially good at it. I would choose pieces that looked manageable and discover, halfway up a stair, that there was no comfortable way to hold them. A woman younger than me would show me how to turn a slab before lifting it. She would do this twice. The third time she would watch without helping, and I would get it right.

At the end of each day I would wash my hands in very little water. The pale grit would remain in the splits beside my nails.

I would like the work more than I had expected. There would be a wall where there had not been one. But I would dislike the bargaining over payment, and I would discover that being tired made me neither generous nor easy to live with.

For a while, my chief distinction in the household would be that I went quiet when hungry and denied it when asked.

***

The work I kept would begin with a missing tool.

An old channel keeper would leave a hooked iron under a cover slab. I would be sent to fetch it because I had helped close that section and knew which slab we had moved.

I would remember the wrong one.

By the time I found the tool, I would have opened three covers, loosened a stone that had been sound before I touched it, and noticed a trickle going somewhere it should not go.

The keeper would be more interested in the trickle than in my excuse. The following morning he would take me back.

Under the paving, the channel would be narrower than I expected. Its importance would have led me to imagine something substantial: a dark watercourse, room for a hand at least. Instead there would be a shallow groove with water slipping along one side, no deeper than the folded edge of a piece of cloth.

We would crouch over it while people stepped around us.

“Is that enough?” I would ask.

“For what?”

I would find this irritating. Later I would learn that he asked it of everyone.

He would have a bad knee and no wish to train a successor. What he wanted was someone to lift covers, crawl into low places, carry grit out in a basket, and remember where the tools had been put. I would be available and already owed him a repair.

That would be how I became useful.

***

I would not have his ear for water.

He could rest the tip of his staff against a covered conduit and sometimes hear a change. I would hear my own fingers moving on the shaft. On days when I thought I had detected something, I would be wrong often enough to stop announcing it.

I would be better at remembering how things fitted.

A slab with a broken corner had to be lowered from the uphill side. Beneath a certain stair, the passage widened just enough to turn a shoulder. The owners of one storeroom stacked their fuel over our access stone every autumn, having sincerely promised every summer that they would not.

I would begin making little drawings.

At first these would be for myself, scratched on flat scraps of stone. Then someone would give me a few spoiled sheets from a merchant’s accounts. I would draw between columns of figures, putting a doorway where there was room for it and a bend in the channel over somebody’s debt.

The keeper would object to the first drawing I showed him.

“That wall isn’t straight.”

“It doesn’t need to be straight. It shows where the opening is.”

He would turn the sheet, trying to make it agree with the room.

For a season I would think he simply did not understand drawings. Then I would take one of mine into a house I had visited only once, enter from a different door, and spend a long time looking for an opening in the wrong wall.

After that I would mark the stairs, and which way was uphill.

***

There would be a household I disliked entering.

The man who owned it would follow us through every room, counting our tools aloud as though this protected his possessions. His sister would ask whether we had wiped our boots while watching us wipe them. They would have a good water share, a dry store, and a covered court with a beam so long I would find myself looking at it despite my dislike.

One winter their flow would diminish.

We would find no obstruction. We would clear the little settling hollow, inspect the joints, and go uphill through two neighboring properties. The water would be low there too, but not as low.

The man would suggest theft.

He would say it quietly, which would make the accusation worse.

I would want him to be wrong. This would affect my judgment. I would waste half a day insisting that the fault must lie in a cracked section we had already examined.

In the end, the keeper would send me into the cellar of the house above.

Someone had pressed a tuft of wool into a small opening beside the channel. It led to a storage jar sunk in the earth. The wool slowed the diversion so that it made almost no sound.

The jar would be less than half full.

I would remember that particularly. I would have preferred to find a great reserve, enough to make the matter simple.

A woman would stand behind me holding the lamp. There would be washing hanging in the cellar, stiff at the edges, and the smell of someone long ill.

“Will you tell him?” she would ask.

I would remove the wool. I would look for somewhere clean to put it, an absurd concern which would occupy me for several moments.

“Yes,” I would say.

But I would tell the keeper first.

There would be witnesses, an argument about shares, an argument about what had been taken, and an older argument that everyone except me already knew. Some water would be allotted for the sick person. A penalty would remain. Neither household would consider the outcome fair.

For months afterward, the woman would turn her face away when she saw me. The man below would welcome me with a satisfaction I found harder to bear.

I would still have to go into both houses.

***

By then, there would be things in my room that I had not brought.

A low stool with one leg wedged. A second bowl. A shutter repaired with wood of two colors. Under the bed, a sack of straight scraps saved because straight wood could not sensibly be thrown away.

My money would no longer be sewn into my clothes. Some of it would have become the shutter. Some would be owed to me. Some I would have sent home in a parcel without a letter, then spent weeks wondering whether that seemed cold.

I would know a person who came occasionally to sit in the room.

At first I would tidy before their visits. Later they would move the drawings themselves to find a place for the bowl they had brought. We would have a disagreement about whether this meant they were welcome whenever they chose.

They would be. I would nevertheless object to the way they tested the latch before knocking.

I would still speak of going to the lower valleys. I would want to see rain that continued through a whole afternoon. I would want to walk under trees without feeling I had entered somebody’s carefully defended property.

Once, this person would ask what work I thought I would do there.

I would have an answer ready, and hear, while giving it, how old the answer had become.

***

Years later, I would be standing in a passage before sunrise with a younger worker beside me.

Behind us, someone would be complaining through a shutter that we had come too early. The cover stone would be frozen along one edge. We would have to loosen it without breaking the lip beneath.

The younger worker would be impatient. I would recognize the look: the conviction that the awkwardness of a task must mean someone had failed to devise a better way.

There might well be a better way. I would ask what they proposed.

While they explained, a woman would come down the stair carrying two vessels on a repaired yoke. We would draw back. The younger worker would shift our basket of tools out of her path, but leave its handle leaning toward the steps.

I would turn the handle inward.

The woman would pass without looking at either of us. Beneath my room she would stop to adjust her load.

I would wait until she had lifted it again. Then I would crouch beside the stone, put my fingers where they would not be caught, and ask the younger worker to take the other end.
