I think I would come down to measure the floor.

There would be a reason upstairs: a door too narrow for a replacement part, an old drawing that placed the machine several feet from where it stood. I would have a folding rule, a reel of cord, and paper stiff enough to write on without a table. At first I would leave these on the lowest stair. Then I would discover that this was where the caretaker kept their drinking water, and move them one step higher.

The measurements would not be particularly difficult. Four pillars, a plinth, a pit. I could establish their positions without crossing most of the planted ground. The difficult part would be deciding what line to draw around everything else.

I would ask whether the raised slabs should be shown at their original level.

The caretaker would ask what the drawing was for.

I would give the answer I had been given upstairs. Neither of us would find it sufficient.

Still, I would finish the drawing. There would be a clean outline of the chamber, dimensions between the pillars, little interrupted lines where a pipe’s course was uncertain. I would mark the exposed yellow tissue with a convention borrowed from some other kind of survey. On the page it would look like a deposit.

I would have to return for something I had omitted.

That seems the likeliest way I would stay: not through a decision to belong there, but because my account of it kept requiring another visit.

***

I would be a person with ordinary knees, increasingly troubled by the stairs. Someone whose glasses clouded on arrival, so that for the first few minutes I could see less than a stranger might. I would learn to stand still while they cleared.

There would be work I could do before I understood much.

Hold the lamp sideways. Pass up the gutter brush. Keep the small fittings from rolling into the grooves. Write down which bracket had been replaced, and where the old one had gone.

The caretaker would let me turn the valve only after I had watched them do it several times. Even then, I would want a number: how far, how often, at what height of the red mark?

They would show me how to rest two fingers on the extension while turning it. There was a point at which the resistance changed. Not a stop. Something less definite, easily missed if one were trying to reach a predetermined position.

I would turn it back and try again.

Above us, the fans would remain quite still.

The consequence of getting it right would arrive after we had left.

***

For a while my notes would grow more elaborate.

I would date the edges of the beds on successive sheets. I would distinguish a slab newly lifted from one newly noticed. Beside observations made in poor light I would put a small question mark.

There would be many question marks.

One day I would find that a route I had marked impassable was open, although the slab obstructing it had not moved. The leaves had changed position. I could get through sideways with the bag held against my chest.

I would erase the old line, then regret erasing it.

After that I would use thinner paper and lay the new drawings over the old ones. Held against the light upstairs, they would make a dense grey chamber in which almost nowhere remained consistently available.

Except the stairs. Except the last few steps to the valve.

And, eventually, a place beside the second pillar where I tended to stop and put down my things.

I would not notice that exception first. The caretaker would.

They would say, “You can leave the bag there.”

I would ask whether I always could.

“Today,” they would say.

***

My place would be near that pillar, but not directly against it. There is often something travelling along the junction between a wall and a floor. I would have learned this by then.

I imagine a low stool with broad feet. It would have come from upstairs, where its missing back made it inconvenient. For several months I would carry it out after each visit. Later I would leave it, but move it a little each time.

From there I could see the gauge through a gap in the vegetation. I could also see the chain above the new trough, though not the place where the water went.

I would spend longer than necessary cleaning the cloth bag of fittings. The heat would make me slow without making me sleepy. Occasionally I would hear a drop and look up too late.

There would still be things I wanted to know badly.

Whether the pressure beneath one stone altered the flow beneath another. Whether a bed had been abandoned or merely passed over for a season. How far a disturbance travelled before it ceased to be a disturbance and became one of the conditions of the place.

I would not become free of the wish to test these things. I would keep devising small experiments in my notebook.

Some I would carry out. A collecting pan moved the width of a thumb. A lamp placed farther away. Others would remain on paper because I could not work out what restoring the previous condition would mean.

***

The caretaker would begin missing visits.

Nothing dramatic at first. A message asking me to look at the gauge. Then a week in which I did all the stairs.

I would bring back reports that were too long. They would ask whether the casing was hotter than usual, whether the basin bracket held, whether I had remembered my own water.

For some time I would continue to think of myself as the person making the record.

Then a gutter would twist loose while I was below. I would hear water striking somewhere it had not struck before. Finding it would take a while. The lamp would show a wet streak on the side of a tilted slab, a suspended root shining along its underside.

I would fetch the brush, discover that it would not reach at the necessary angle, and go upstairs for wire.

No entry in the notebook would adequately distinguish that visit from the others.

***

I don’t think I would be the chamber’s interpreter. I would sometimes tell people what had happened there, and have to correct myself when they returned.

Nor would I be certain that it knew me.

But after enough years, I might find that the stool no longer needed shifting. Beneath one broad foot, where the floor had begun to rise, the stone might settle into a shallow inclination that held it level.

I would check for a crack. I would lift the stool and examine the underside. Perhaps I would put it somewhere else for a few visits.

Then, because it was a useful place to sit, I would put it back.

That is where I find myself: beside the second pillar, with a drawing rolled loosely in a tube that no longer closes, waiting for enough light to distinguish the wet rim of the trough.

When someone new came down, I would move my bag so they could stand there too.
