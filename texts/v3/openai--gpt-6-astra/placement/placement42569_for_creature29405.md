I would first try to put my foot somewhere that did not already belong to something.

There would be no such place. Even the broadest tile would have its curve, its share of light, the little hollow under its lip. I would lift my foot and set it down almost where it had been. The inhabitant would feel both movements through the wall.

For a while, that would be the extent of our acquaintance.

I think I would remain near the entrance, with one side of me still receiving the less thoroughly reflected light of the passage. Not because I wanted to leave immediately, but because I would not yet know how to accept the space it had made. Its withdrawn shoulder would be an exact and difficult generosity. I could not thank it by coming closer without making another demand on its body.

So I would sit, slowly, on the shallow drop in the floor.

From there the tiles would cease to be a landscape beneath my foot. They would be beside my face. The ankle-high ridge at the back would conceal the inhabitant’s heel. I would discover a leaf I could not have seen while standing: a short white blade growing downward into a hollow, its tip suspended just clear of the tile.

The roar would be no quieter here. But with my weight settled, there would be differences inside it.

One through the floor.

Another where my shoulder touched the entrance.

I would keep shifting between them, at first without knowing I was doing it.

—

I do not think I would remain the person who arrived.

But neither would I become another creature fitted all the way into the wall. The pocket already has one of those. I would belong to the smaller space between its accommodation and mine: the space that exists because it moved, and that must not become a reason it can never move back.

My body would be low and broad enough to rest across several tile edges without rocking. Beneath it, a few blunt folds would take up the unevenness. They would not enter the seams. If I lifted myself, the seams would still be there, unoccupied.

I would have two small hands, though most of the time they would be folded under me. Their palms would be darker than anything else in the pocket—not dark, exactly, but the gray of pale clay before it dries.

The surface facing the entrance would harden first. The side facing the inhabitant would stay soft.

There would be a little distance between us.

Enough for the light to get down.

I would possess no plates behind my jaw. The great rush would reach me through a broad, smooth place under my chest, and I would understand very little of it. Sometimes a change in the buried constriction would pass through the inhabitant before it reached the tile beneath me. Its finger would flatten. A joint would open. Then I would feel something that had already happened to it.

I would come to know that interval.

Not the passage beyond the walls, not the source of the air. Just the brief difference between its receiving and mine.

—

At some point I would try to answer.

I might press both hands down, making a small, emphatic disturbance. The inhabitant would uncover its eyes. I would see the wetness and press again.

It would adjust its weight.

For a long time I would mistake that adjustment for an answer.

Later, resting one palm instead of two, I would discover that my first signal had travelled through a tile whose buried end lay beneath its heel. I had not spoken so much as nudged it.

After that I would be more careful, though not always less clumsy.

There might eventually be one thing we could do together. I would raise the front of my body a fraction, taking pressure off the curved tile under my chest. The inhabitant would ease one finger out of its recess. The rush would alter—not in the air, but in the small circuit of surfaces holding us.

Then we would settle again.

I cannot say what that would mean. I would sometimes initiate it. Sometimes it would.

—

I would still go out.

That seems important to the sort of being I would become. I would have a flexible rim that could draw inward at the sideways entrance, and an underside that remembered how to cross a surface without becoming attached to it. Outside, I would probably look unfinished. The little folds that suited the tile floor would make poor progress on anything flat.

I would not bring things back. No grains, no bits of leaf, nothing to put in the hollows.

Coming home, I would pause before settling my weight. The inhabitant might have opened its shoulder into the place where I usually rested. I would wait at the threshold, or find a slightly different position. Once, perhaps, it would leave its shoulder there and move its hand instead.

My place would change by the width of a finger.

And I would spend a considerable time learning it: which ridge supported me, which contact carried the roar, where my soft side could round outward without touching the leaf that grew down into the light.
