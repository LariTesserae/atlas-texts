I think I would live behind the child’s seat that now faces into a cupboard.

Not in the cupboard itself. There is a space behind its back wall, left when two thick roots grew past one another without joining. At one end it is scarcely wider than a knife cut. At the other, a person repairing the wall could put a hand inside as far as the wrist.

No one has repaired that wall for some time.

A little heat reaches the space from the sorting chamber. Sound arrives through the roots, so that the rollers can be felt clearly but a person speaking just outside is a disturbance without much shape. There is an old nail in the upper part. Its point has been turned aside by growth. On certain shifts, moisture collects there.

That would matter to me more than the cistern.

At first I imagine myself small enough to lie beneath the nail, waiting. But I would need to reach the outer wall as well, to feel what was happening in the square. And I would want something of myself deep in the narrowing part, where neither a lizard nor a probing finger could follow.

So I would be longer than the space initially suggests. Folded, perhaps. Not curled like a sleeping mammal, but doubled along several soft creases, with most of my length put away.

The residents would call an animal of my kind a sleeve.

***

A sleeve is difficult to recognize when it is empty.

Empty is their word for resting. The animal draws its underside together and presses its outer skin against the wall of its cavity. What remains visible resembles the lining of an old conduit: brown, slightly puckered, with a pale rim where the surface turns inward.

There are muscles in that rim. There are eyes, though I would not use them to make much of the square. They distinguish an opening from a surface, and something crossing an opening. My more useful senses would lie along the creases of my body. I could lay one against a root and feel a load approaching before the freight gate began to descend.

When I moved, the pale rim would extend first. The rest of me would follow by loosening from one patch of wall and fastening to another.

I would have a definite front end. Someone looking into my home would probably identify it incorrectly.

Sleeves graze on the dry membranes that living wood abandons inside an old cavity. My mouth would have two short, hard edges that meet slightly out of line. I would work these beneath a loose layer and draw it into myself, leaving the sound tissue untouched unless I was hungry.

There would be a shallow scrape in the back of the cupboard that someone had dated in pencil.

Once a month, during stoppage, the woman from the sorting chamber would open the cupboard and compare the scrape with the mark.

If I had eaten past it, she would push in a strip of dried press skin.

If I had not, she would close the door.

This arrangement would satisfy neither of us completely. She would prefer not to have another thing to inspect. I would prefer fresh skin, with a little sweetness left in it.

***

My cavity would contain a steel washer.

I would have brought it in when I was smaller, before the entrance narrowed. It would lie flat beneath my second fold, cool at the beginning of each stoppage and warm by the time the mill was running properly.

The washer would be of no use to my digestion. I could not distinguish it by sight from a dark ring shaved from seed tissue. But it would not soften when I rested on it. Its edge would always be in the same place.

When turning over, I would find it with my underside.

Occasionally it would slip toward the narrow end of the cavity. Then I would spend a considerable part of a shift retrieving it. The residents would hear a faint, intermittent ticking from the cupboard and assume something had got into the wall.

Something would have.

I would not collect a second washer.

***

The space behind me would not be entirely mine.

A small white insect would live in the recess where I turned. It would eat what accumulated in the folds of my outer skin. There might be several of these insects, but I would experience them as a recurring irritation in one place.

A thread of fungus would survive near the nail. I would eat it down whenever it reached the first bend. It would return.

Once, a young hem would enter while I was feeding at the cupboard door. By the time I withdrew, it would have settled on the washer.

For several hours we would remain very still.

The hem would grip the floor. I would grip the walls. Neither of us would be able to eat the other, and neither would know that.

Eventually the hem would leave because there was nothing growing within reach of its mouth.

For a long time afterward, I would take up more room than I needed.

***

I would not understand the weekly stoppage as rest.

The roots become difficult to read without the machinery moving through them. A person setting down a bowl might reach me from farther away than a loaded trolley does during work. Small sounds would arrive separately and require attention.

This would be when I ventured out.

I would emerge behind the cupboard’s lowest shelf, then flatten myself along its underside. A fringe of short limbs, normally tucked into my flanks, would take my weight. In the open I would be broad and rather ugly, with the creases still visible across my back.

The boy who sleeps beside the sorting chamber would know about me.

At first he would leave food. Most of it would be unsuitable. Once he would offer a piece of apple, and I would spend the next two shifts behind the wall with my outer rim swollen.

After that, he would bring thin scraps from the press.

He would call me Cuff.

Other sleeves would also be called Cuff. This would not concern either of us.

When the boy grew tall enough to knock his shoulder against the sorting-chamber lintel, he would stop lying on the floor to watch me eat. For a while the scraps would arrive less often. Then they would begin appearing in a particular corner of the cupboard, placed there by a hand reaching down from above.

I would learn the new location.

***

There would be a season when the wall grew faster than I could graze it.

At first I would fold more tightly. Then I would leave my hindmost crease extended into the cupboard, where it would dry and have to be drawn in again. The washer would become difficult to turn.

The monthly pencil marks would crowd together.

One stoppage, the woman would remove the lowest shelf.

She would put a shallow box against the opening. Inside would be two curved pieces of cork, tied together with a gap between them. She would rub the entrance with press skin and go away.

I would investigate the box. I would eat the skin. I would return to the wall.

This would happen several times.

In the end it would be the washer that moved me. The boy—no longer usually called the boy—would ease a thin hook behind it and pull it free while I held myself tight against the upper surface of the cavity.

He would place it between the pieces of cork.

I would come out farther than he had ever seen me come.

For a moment my whole body would be on the cupboard floor, exposed to the green light, every crease slightly parted. Then the leading rim would find the new opening. I would draw myself inside.

They would set the box beneath the seat, close enough to the wall to receive the machinery’s vibration.

For several shifts I would keep trying to fasten myself more securely. The cork would be too light. Footsteps would tilt it. There would be an unfamiliar draft along one edge.

But the washer would lie flat, and there would be room to turn it.

Behind the cupboard, the old cavity would continue to narrow.
