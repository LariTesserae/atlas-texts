At first I would put myself under the washer. Not at its center, where the meal is packed and the larvae keep working against one another, but near the rim, where a little loose flour sifts through whenever something passes overhead.

There would be room to withdraw there. There would also be an edge to approach.

But the shelter is not equally deep for every body. A larva fills a passage that a mite could spend a long time crossing. To choose my place I would have to move through it, find where my back touched, what my feet could hold. I might go beneath the washer and keep going, past what had seemed its deepest part, into a crack in the meal too narrow to have appeared in the description.

I think I would be one of the travelling mites.

For a while, though, I would be on foot.

A broken husk would rise in front of me, its edge lifted just clear of the flour. Beneath it, the floor would be littered with particles too large to push aside. I would go between them. My legs would reach surfaces my body had not yet approached, and each foothold would change the angle at which the next became available. Crossing the hollow would consist of these adjustments. A short distance could require most of me.

Where silk bound the meal, my footing would improve. Where the silk ended, a grain might turn under my weight. I would stop with several legs on something firm and the others reaching.

The mill would be running.

I would not have the separate sounds given in your account: bearing, belt, loose part beyond the wall. The knock would arrive through whatever held me. Beneath the husk it might be a small repeated loss of purchase. On exposed stone it would come cleanly through every foot. A loose flake would add its own movement, continuing after the larger jolt had passed.

I would cross it badly once, and then be off it.

The beetle would reach me first as a disturbance in the passage. A grain would press against another grain. The roof would lift. One hooked foot would enter, withdraw, and enter farther along.

I would draw close rather than run into the space it had opened.

There are sheltered places on a beetle that remain sheltered while it moves. Near the base of a leg, behind a hard edge, I would find one. Getting there would be awkward. The leg would move away from me; the body would lower; for a moment the flour would hold us both. Then the beetle would pull free, and I would be attached.

My place in the hollow would now walk.

I would have no use for most of the beetle’s outline. Its brown wing cases, its dusted back, the unequal reach of its antennae would be beyond the small region I occupied. Beside me a joint would open and close. A bristle would bend against my body. Other mites would be lodged farther in, close enough that I sometimes touched one without changing my hold.

Flour would rub past. A wall would bear against the beetle and leave me untouched in the recess.

For several steps we might be carrying a second beetle overhead. I would experience the slower movement, the altered pressure, without knowing what had climbed aboard.

Then a sudden fall would cover the route.

My carrier would stop. Around the leg, the available space would fill with pale grains. It would push forward again, and the grains would stream backward along its body, catching briefly where I held. One would remain against me until the next narrow passage scraped it away.

I would stay attached beyond the first fresh deposit. Perhaps beyond several. The hollow contains destinations, but arriving beside one is not the same as being able to leave. I would need a surface within reach, a pause long enough to transfer my weight, an opening that did not close when the beetle took another step.

Eventually it would wedge beneath a curved plate of insect shell.

For a moment, the moving shelter and the stationary one would touch.

I would reach out.

The underside of the plate would be rough enough to hold. I would shift onto it a little at a time, until the beetle’s next movement took it away without me.

Here the flour would be coarse with fragments. Another mite would pass behind a translucent wall of shed skin. Along the floor, a narrow gap would admit the cooler air from beneath the iron.

I would turn down into that gap.

Above me, the plate would rock once as the beetle forced its way out. A few grains would fall through the place where its shoulder had been. One would lodge against the opening, leaving just enough room beneath it.

I would go under.
