I would be somewhere beneath the junction, where the soil loosened by her hands comes down.

For a while, that would be all I knew of her: a darkening of the water, grit settling on my wrists, then a cool current reaching a place that had been growing difficult to stay in. I would lift my chest a little. Water would pass beneath it. Above me, roots would creak against one another with the almost inaudible sound they make when their weight changes.

I think I would have been there long enough to mistake the pressure of those roots for safety.

The hollow would fit me closely. There would be a smooth place for my chin, a branch against which I could brace one hind foot, an opening where I could raise my nostrils without bringing my eyes above the water. When I was smaller, another opening would have let me leave downstream. Now my shoulders would catch there.

I would still test it sometimes.

Not urgently. One hand through, then my head turned sideways, then the familiar resistance across my back. I would withdraw and settle into the part that fitted. Outside, things would move through water I could feel but could not see.

That is where I begin to find a body: in the difference between an opening I remember and an opening I can use.

Broad hands, certainly. A pale underside pressed to the bottom. A tail still long enough to strike a root behind me when I turn too quickly. I would not yet be one of the old animals whose habits have become part of the channel. My back would bear a few gold flecks, densely gathered near the neck. All my fingers would be present. One would have a tender place near its tip, and I would sometimes hold it clear of a surface the others had already accepted.

I would know several routes badly and one extremely well.

The well-known route would lead from my hollow to a shallow feeding shelf, with a submerged branch halfway along where I could stop and breathe. I would know the branch from below: its fork, its soft bark, the notch that caught my palm. The little trees growing on its upper side would be outside my acquaintance. Their fallen flowers would occasionally enter my mouth with the sediment.

The new current would change that route.

For the first evening, I might only turn toward it. Cool water would spread along my throat, then falter when something shifted upstream. I would wait for it to return. Hunger would eventually draw me out, but I would leave by the unfamiliar side, following the water with my belly close to the ground.

There would be a place where I had to come almost entirely out of the channel.

I imagine this slowly: my forefeet on wet moss, elbows wide, the weight of my trunk reluctant behind them. Air touching folds usually filled with water. A pale insect walking across one wrist. I would remain still while it crossed, although I would eat the same insect if it moved against my palm beneath the surface.

Then I would pull again.

Beyond the moss would be a narrow runnel, scarcely deeper than my hands. Water would gather against my chest. My body would dam it until it found a way around one flank, carrying a thread of soil with it.

I would stay there longer than was comfortable because I could not turn around.

Eventually I would push forward far enough to drop into a pool.

It would be a small pool, under the lifted root plate of a fallen tree. Rainwater would enter from one side. Somewhere beneath the other bank, warmth would seep through fine gravel. There would be larvae in the rotted wood, and a slit between two roots through which I could breathe. I would work my hands over the bottom before resting. Once, a fish would brush my mouth and be gone before I closed it.

By morning, I would have left shallow grooves in the silt.

I do not think I would immediately belong there. For several nights I would return to the old hollow. The journey back would be easier: the narrow runnel would now admit part of my chest, and the moss would yield where I had dragged my belly over it. Each passage would carry a little more earth away.

Then rain would come hard enough to fill the new pool with moving brown water, and I would discover the inconvenient low corner where sediment accumulated. I would discover that a grazer sometimes stood above the breathing slit. I would feel another animal’s pulse through the roots and answer before I had meant to give up my presence.

There would be a brief contest. I might lose.

I want to leave that possibility in the body. To have found a place would not give me the strength to keep it.

But perhaps I would return after the other animal had gone to feed. Perhaps I would settle against the gravel and find the mixture still good. Over time, the exposed root beside my cheek would grow smooth. My hands would keep one small entrance clear while leaves closed another.

Later, there might be eggs beneath it.

I would turn in the cramped pool, stirring water over them with my hind feet. After rain I would lift away what I could: a leaf, a little clot of fern dust, a dead larva caught in the clear sheet. Silt would continue arriving. Some chambers would cloud despite my work.

Upstream, the old female would go on using the passage she had opened for herself. I might never touch her.

One evening her movements would send another small fall of soil down through the moss. I would raise my head into the cooler water, wait until the grit had passed, and lower it again beside the eggs.
