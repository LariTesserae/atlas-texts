I would know the hollow by what can be held.

Not the chamber, not its height, not the blade of sky. Those would lie beyond the useful reach of my body. I would know a ridge that fits beneath a foot, a surface too smooth to cross while carrying food, a crack into which two hooks can enter but from which only one can easily be withdrawn.

A few body lengths away, the stone would already be uncertain.

I would live beneath the collapsed shelter, near the triangular opening. Far enough inside that the cold wind reaches me only after passing over other bodies. Far enough out that I can leave without turning around.

Above my back would be a tilted slab. One corner rests on the floor; another rests on a smaller stone that shifts during the mountain’s stronger movements. When it shifts, the passage becomes narrower. There is an old scrape across my plates from a time I stayed too long.

I would keep one foot against that smaller stone.

Even asleep.

***

I think I would be the animal the burdened parent meets there.

Not its companion. Sometimes an obstruction. Sometimes the familiar pressure that makes an obstruction predictable.

My forefeet would be whole. My back would be thin enough to pass through the gap behind the tilted slab. Inside that gap there is a pocket where discarded skins collect, and beneath the skins there are things still soft from leaving them. I would feed there when I could.

But the gap would not always admit me.

There would have been seasons—not measured by light, but by molts and the slow thickening of the yellow crust—when my own plates stood farther from my body. When turning required room I did not have. When every brush against my flanks made me flatten and hold.

Now the chamber beneath my back would be empty.

The folded skin would remain. Its inner surfaces would touch. In dry heat, they would sometimes adhere, and I would have to lift my plates a little to separate them.

Nothing would come to the openings along my sides.

I would clean them anyway.

***

The injured animal arrives carrying the smell of the intact shelter.

There is scorched mineral dust on it, and the bitter trace of the folds it feeds from. When we meet, I put my forefeet against its forefeet. The left one offers less resistance than it used to.

I press there.

It shifts its weight before I can raise it.

We remain like that for a while, each receiving through the other the movements of the floor. Something small tries to pass between us. I close one foot around it and bring it underneath my head.

The other animal advances.

I have lost the better place in the opening while feeding. I brace against the tilted slab, but its front plates are already beside mine. We pass with our bellies turned toward stone.

There would be no decision to forgive it. The morsel is wet. The passage is still wide enough.

Afterward I would put my foot back against the loose support.

Its upper edge has moved.

***

When the heat begins to change, my shelter gives me warning differently from the intact one.

The first message comes through the little stone beneath my foot. A succession of minute slips. The slab above it expands unevenly, and the weight settles toward the opening.

I draw my foot away.

Under the rear edge, a molting hunter has wedged itself in the coolest recess. Half of its old skin is off. Its exposed limb bends when I touch it.

I could feed.

For several breaths I stay there, mouthparts moving against the limb’s soft joint. Then the slab lowers enough to touch my back.

I leave with less than I could have taken.

Behind me, the hunter pulls. Its old skin holds somewhere in the narrowing gap.

Outside, the floor is moving toward the shelters.

The grazers press beneath my belly and lift it. I spread my feet farther apart, finding stone through their bodies. One breaks under my grip. I eat what remains attached while climbing.

My usual route runs along the fallen roof, across a short strip of wall, then into a recess above the intact shelter. But the fallen roof has changed its angle. The edge I reach for is no longer where my extended foot expects it.

I touch air.

Draw back.

Touch air again.

For an instant my body repeats the old movement without alteration. The mountain trembles beneath me. Then a passing fold pushes against my hind legs, and I turn sideways onto its back.

Its surface contracts under my weight.

I cross before it can close around a foot.

***

This would be the part of the hollow I had not known.

Only a narrow interval beside the intact shelter, but approached from below instead of above. A ledge is hidden on the far side of the lintel. The underside of one groove holds a rind of mineral crust. There is coolness here, interrupted by the hot bodies moving across it.

Overhead, the injured parent has hooked its tail beneath the slab.

Something touches my head.

A young animal, emerging backward.

Its hind feet still hold the parent. Its forefeet search the stone beside my face. One finds a whisker and closes on it.

I pull away sharply.

The young animal withdraws. Its plates have not yet hardened at their margins. Between two of them is a pale line, and my mouthparts open beneath me.

The floor presses upward. A fold has entered the space behind my body. I move onto the ledge, and the young animal is there too, caught between my front foot and the stone.

I could hold it down.

Instead I need that foot farther forward.

I place it beyond the small body and pull myself up. The young one clings briefly to a joint in my leg. Its weight is almost nothing, but the contact travels through a place where, once, other feet held.

At the top of the ledge I stop.

The injured parent turns toward me. Its good forefoot rises. I brace for the pressure, lowering my head.

We touch.

Behind its plates, the remaining young tighten into a single uneven mass. Beneath my leg, the released one lets go.

Neither of us can advance without losing purchase.

For a while we hold the ledge between us.

***

After the pulse, I would return to the collapsed shelter.

The opening would still be triangular, but lower. I would test it with my head, then with one shoulder. My plates would scrape before my hind feet reached the threshold.

I would back out.

For some time I would remain beside it, touching the same edge from different positions.

Inside, something would be feeding on the trapped hunter. The movements would come clearly through the slab.

Eventually I would go around to the other side.

There, where the roof had lifted, would be a narrow space admitting cooler air. No skins yet. No familiar footholds. I would enter sideways, turn with difficulty, and settle facing out.

One foot against the stone bearing the weight.

The others finding places.
