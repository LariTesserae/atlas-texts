At first I would look for a chamber with a little water moving through it.

Not one of the sealed rooms beneath the terraces. There would be too little arriving there, and no way to leave except by becoming small enough to pass between grains. Nor the open court, where every soft thing must either hold fast or be carried away. Somewhere under a threshold, perhaps: a low space with two entrances, one receiving the current while the other releases it.

I would expect to occupy that space as an animal occupies a burrow.

Then I would find that I had no surface separate from the surface touching the stone. Drawing back would mean peeling myself away. In a few places I could do it. In others, the flesh had grown around a roughness, and withdrawal would tear it.

The chamber would not be my shelter, exactly. It would be the shape I had acquired.

But even that gives me too much of a center.

I think I would be near the bowl, in the narrow fracture below the new mouth. Not the mouth itself, which has the distinct work of reaching and closing, and not the buried end, where tissue advances into water that scarcely moves. I would be a short length between them. Almost transparent. Nothing a fish could recognize as worth biting, even if the stone were taken away.

Through me, what happened at the opening would reach what was growing farther in.

At first there would be very little difference between those places. A pebble passed; the mouth withdrew; the whole length tightened. Food arrived; a fold filled; the inner end pressed farther against the fracture. There was no distance within the body sufficient for one part to continue undisturbed.

As the thread lengthened, that would change.

The mouth would already be open again while a contraction was still moving inward. The growing tip would pause at the news of a danger that had passed. Somewhere between them, a branch would enter a second pore and receive the disturbance later still.

That is where I would become less easy to locate.

I would not see the stones circling. Their passage would arrive as a small compression, regularly enough to affect how far the feeding edge extended, irregularly enough that it could never remain extended safely. Sometimes two stones would come together. Sometimes one would lodge briefly above the mouth, leaving a longer interval, then drop and strike the lip.

After that strike, the exposed tissue would remain shortened through several harmless circuits.

Inside the pedestal, the pressure would be slight. Yet the tissue there would shorten too.

I would live partly in that excess of response: the closing farther than the bite could reach, the pause lasting longer than the stone took to pass. It would cost food. It would sometimes preserve an edge from being stripped away.

There would be no need to make it wiser than that.

For a long time, growth would consist of finding that the stone did not continue quite everywhere.

A thin opening beneath a grain. A pocket left by something that had dissolved. A joint that widened beyond a constriction. The advancing flesh would flatten, divide, thicken. Where there was no passage it would remain pressed against the obstruction, alive but making almost no progress.

Then a storm would shift the bowl.

Only a little. The pedestal would remain standing. The white stones would be thrown out of their usual paths and settle back into circulation. But deep inside, one fitted surface would slide against another, opening a space that had not been open before.

Water would enter it first.

I would extend into the water over many days.

There might already be flesh on the other side.

I cannot tell whether I would be the part approaching or the part approached. Both would have fed from the same surge by then, though through openings far apart. One would carry coarse grains in its outer skin. The other, sheltered inside the pedestal, would be smooth. Their pressures would differ. Their contractions would arrive out of time.

Where they touched, each would initially draw away.

Later the surfaces might adhere.

For a while, nothing that nourished one would nourish the other. They would pull on their shared skin, and a fine wrinkle would travel along the attachment. One side might be closed against the bowl’s tumbling stones while the other continued feeding beneath a flag.

I would be there, too, in the attached place that had not yet become a passage.

Perhaps it would never become one.

A small crust could lodge along the contact. Sediment could enter the wrinkle and hold the bodies apart. Years of growth might leave two substantial masses joined only by that useless strip, each sustained elsewhere. When one contracted, the other would feel a tug.

That seems a possible life for me: not the keeper of the drowned place, nor something through which the whole ruin learns to feel. Only a little flesh by which one hidden activity interferes with another.

And there would be things living on me for which this distinction meant nothing. A worm would settle along the wrinkle. Its weight would be negligible. Its movements would become ordinary. As the seam deepened, it would follow the shelter inward.

Above us, a pebble would finally wear small enough to escape the bowl.

The remaining stones would fall into a different rhythm. At the feeding edge, closures would begin too early or too late. Some food would pass untouched. A soft rim would be bruised.

Farther in, the contractions would still arrive with their old force, but at altered intervals. The attached strip would pull. The worm would withdraw.

I would be nowhere near the light, and the change would reach me.
