I would be underneath, though not at first as far underneath as I would eventually go.

There is a place near the rear of an old grazer where one plate projects beyond the next. Beneath that uneven edge, the ground stays damp a little longer after the body has passed. A young animal has been feeding there. Its waste has formed a ledge, and the ledge has cracked, leaving a crevice too small for the jointed creatures that walk over it.

I would begin in that crevice.

Not as another small animal with a mouth and a destination. I think I would be a film on the sheltered face of a grain: a few connected cells, almost dormant, carried here in material that several bodies have already used. Water would reach me through the contact between that grain and another. Everything I could take in would arrive along this contact.

For a long time, there would be enough to remain and not enough to grow.

Above me, the young grazer would brace against the adult’s underside. Its movement would break the ledge. A piece would fall into the working layer beneath the belly, soften, and pass inward with the next slow intake of grit.

That is how I would enter the animal. Neither sought nor rejected. The grain carrying me would be of a suitable size.

Inside, there would still be cold.

I would have no encounter with a hidden furnace, no sudden abundance. There would be darkness, close surfaces, a fluid moving so slowly that the finer particles could settle between pulses. Much of what came through would be unusable. Some of it would damage me. I would lose cells before finding a place to hold.

The place would be a crease behind a valve in the fourth or fifth repeating chamber along one side. Each time the valve closed, a little fluid would remain in the crease. Each time it opened, that fluid would be incompletely replaced.

Here I could persist.

The animal’s established colonies would already occupy most of the chamber. They would coat the exposed surfaces and thicken around the outlets. I would live behind them, where their own spent material gathered. Something they released would be available to me. Something I released would pass back into the fluid.

At first the contribution would be too slight to change the animal’s feeding. A few molecules, retained rather than expelled. Then a thin growth along the crease. Then another crease reached when the valve folded.

There would be no moment when I became necessary.

One season, however, the grazer would move onto poorer ground and continue moving. A fraction of its food that once passed through untouched would now yield something. The fluid leaving my chamber would carry it onward. Pads along the belly would swell, draw back, swell.

I would have helped the animal cross a patch of gravel I would never encounter directly.

By then, “I” would be an uncertain boundary. The cells first carried inward would be gone. Their descendants would occupy the crease, a nearby partition, the sheltered underside of a flap. Some would have changed enough to fare poorly where the others thrived. What continued would be a small set of activities, maintained in adjoining places.

I would belong to that particular chamber for a very long time.

Its contractions would determine when fresh material arrived. A slight failure in the valve would alter the interval of exchange. Deposits would roughen its surface. I would spread into the roughness. Once, after the animal fed on a newly exposed seam, something in the fluid would kill most of the growth nearest the opening.

Recovery would proceed from the back of the crease.

Meanwhile, outside, the grazer would acquire another pale layer along its plates. A juvenile would leave its flank. Other young would arrive. I would not register these events, except perhaps as a change too small to separate from all the other changes in the supply.

Then the chamber would begin to fail.

Less fluid would enter. The valve would not open fully. Some of the lining would draw tight, closing passages to the neighboring organs. This would be the old animal preserving what remained workable.

I would persist behind the valve as the place became less workable.

But before the final closure, one contraction would loosen part of the film. It would travel through a narrow passage and lodge in the lining of a pocket at the flank.

There it would wait in thick fluid.

The animal would be beside another animal. Their margins would have approached over several days, sampling the same damp strip. At one point the pockets would meet.

A bead would form.

If I have a place in this world that I am reluctant to leave, it is here: in that small, exposed volume between two bodies, with the cold beginning to change its surface. Behind it, an old chamber is being sealed. Ahead, there is tissue with a different chemical history. Neither animal can supply exactly what the other carries.

The bead would mingle and divide.

Most of me would remain with the old grazer. Some would enter the other.

There would be no shared awareness across the separation. One part would dwindle in the closing chamber. Another would fail among unfamiliar colonies. A few cells would find a fold where fluid lingered after a valve shut.

For a long time, there would be enough to remain.

From the ridge, the two animals would look like stones lying unusually close together. Eventually a dark line of ground would appear between them. Frost would enter it.

In the second body, far beneath the plates, a thin growth would extend around the back of a valve.
