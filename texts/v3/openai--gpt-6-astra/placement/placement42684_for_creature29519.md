I would come into view after the sledges had passed.

At first there would be very little to distinguish me from the company ahead: the same low visor, the same patched knees, a hauling strap worn smooth where it crossed my chest. But my strap would be unfastened. I would be walking back toward something everyone else had already gone by.

A fastening, perhaps. A dark curl on the pale ground.

I would kneel to pick it up, then remain kneeling a little longer. From that height, a runner’s passage might be visible as a change in gloss. One of the sledges would have begun scraping more heavily on its left side. I would put the fastening in my pocket and get up to tell its owner.

This would be my ordinary place: behind, but within calling distance.

*

I think I would have learned to mend before I learned to judge a route.

There would have been a house where travelers left damaged things. My mother, or someone I called my mother, would sit with a garment turned inside out across her knees, feeling for the ends of a tear. She would let me sort the patches. I would sort them by color until she made me close my eyes.

A patch has to yield in the right direction. A beautiful match can pull a sleeve apart.

Our house would have had a low rack of needles, each stuck through a scrap too thick to lose. Visitors would sit on the floor while their outer layers were repaired. Without their coats, some would seem abruptly old. Others would have astonishingly thin arms. They would drink something sour and warm, if we had fuel, and explain the accident in a way that spared their judgment.

The tear would usually tell a less flattering story.

I would grow up knowing the routes first through the clothes they damaged: the low crossing that wore through shoulders, the new ridge that cut ankles, a narrow passage where everyone caught the same hip. For years I would imagine one distant settlement as a place that made triangular holes.

When I finally went there, it would consist of four houses and a woman who objected to the way I held my spoon.

I would like her. I would stay longer than intended.

*

By the time I traveled regularly, my work would be a mixture of small tasks that no one thought worth giving a single name.

I would mend. I would replace vessel ties. I would carry a little food culture inside my coat for a household that had lost its own. I would know how to ease a swollen lid without splitting it and how to make a glove’s bare touch-patch smaller for someone whose hands had begun to shake.

People would entrust me with objects going only part of my circuit.

A spoon for a sister. A visor made wider for a growing child. The broken half of a tool whose owner wanted someone elsewhere to admit the repair had failed.

My pockets would need dividers.

I would enjoy this work, including the complaints. Especially the complaints repeated over several visits, whose particulars changed as the people telling them grew older. Someone who once complained of a daughter’s careless sewing would eventually complain that she sewed too slowly, making every stitch as though it had to outlast the roof.

I would have a bad singing voice and a good memory for the extra verses.

There would be a verse about me.

I would maintain that it described a different traveler.

*

For a while, I would also carry route samples.

That would be the part of the work I took too seriously.

I would make a case with close-fitting compartments, darken its edges so it could be found if dropped, and devise small marks for the circumstances of each cutting. Taken from a sheltered face. Taken after rain. Taken while the tissue was under tension. I would dislike samples handed over without such information.

At tables I would lay the pieces out in order.

Then, one season, I would recommend a crossing from which I had been absent too long.

The strip in my case would still hold its light. Its grain would still be what I remembered. I would have stored it carefully, protected it from damp, kept it from touching younger tissue.

Out in the chamber, the crossing would have continued growing.

No one would die. I would be fortunate in that. But a company would lose a sledge trying to turn back, and inside the sledge would be a nearly seasoned roof rib, two good vessels, and a child’s coat made large enough for several years.

I would hear the whole inventory.

Afterward I would spend a long interval making repairs for that household. They would accept the work without letting it become forgiveness too quickly. The child would watch me sewing the replacement coat.

“Will this one go into the split?” she would ask.

I would say I hoped not.

She would make me attach a cord to it anyway.

My sample case would become less orderly after that. I would keep room beside each piece for later news, including news that contradicted me. Some compartments would contain only a tied scrap marking a route I no longer knew.

*

I would be visiting the five houses when the girl found the green line.

Someone’s sleeve would be open on my lap. I would have unpicked a poor repair and reached the inconvenient point at which the garment was less usable than when it had been handed to me.

Outside, the conversation would stop.

I would know enough to put the needle through its thick scrap before going to the door.

By evening I would have looked beneath the lamp hood with everyone else. I would have touched the floor some distance from the line, then closer, then some distance away again. There would be no useful difference that I could feel.

The oldest woman would ask what I thought.

“I can’t tell,” I would say.

That would be all I could contribute to the decision.

Later, when the trays were being wrapped, I would finish the sleeve. The owner would need it in the morning.

*

On moving day I would stand inside one of the emptying houses and find that I could not remember where a familiar cup belonged.

The pegs would be bare. Without the hanging clothes and vessels, their arrangement would make no sense. There would be a pale patch behind the sleeping platform, a little heap of scrap pushed into a corner, marks at several heights where a child had tested a cutting edge despite being told not to.

I would sweep the useful scraps into my bag.

Outside, everyone would be ready to pull before they were ready to leave.

The girl would still have the witness-pegs. Someone would tell her to put them away; someone else would need her help; she would stand between the two instructions with her fist closed.

I would give her one of my empty sample compartments.

She would arrange the pegs in it, then rearrange them so that the one from her own sleeping platform lay separately.

I would not label them. She would know.

When the first house moved, we would all hear something tearing underneath.

The pullers would stop so abruptly that the hauling ropes sagged onto the ground. We would wait. A loose skin would continue peeling from the underside of a runner, making a dry, modest sound.

Then someone would swear at it.

Then we would pull again.

*

I would stay at the rear as we left.

There would be more dropped things than usual. A wedge. A binding shortened in haste and discarded although it was still useful. One of the little markers intended to show the way back.

I would set that one upright.

The familiar grazer would be at the far end of the line, its dark joint bent beneath it. Its mouth would work against the new tissue. Every so often it would lift its head, and I would be able to see the wet black seal around its jaws.

Behind me, the abandoned beam rack would give a small crack.

I would turn too quickly and frighten myself with the movement of my own visor.

Nothing large would have happened yet.

Ahead, the company would be drawing farther away. One person would have stopped to watch for me.

I would pick up the last binding and go toward them.

That is where I think I would find myself: carrying a bag made heavy by things that had nearly been left behind, trying to walk quickly without ceasing to look.

By the time I reached the others, the girl would have fallen asleep on a sledge. The compartment of pegs would be tucked beside her, under a cord.

I would check the cord as we walked.
