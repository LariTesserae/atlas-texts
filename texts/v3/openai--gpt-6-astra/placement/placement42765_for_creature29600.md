I would be below the place that looks safe to cross.

Not very far below. In the hottest part of the day, light would still reach me through the surface, blurred by several thicknesses of green. Rain would arrive first as a drumming, then as a coolness moving down the walls. There would be an old support to one side and younger tissue to the other, and between them a space too narrow to become a chamber.

I think I would begin in that space, although what I became would make the beginning difficult to find.

A crescent carried down from a basin might catch there. For a while its tough back would face the light. Its cup would open crookedly against the wall, holding less than a drop, and the threads beneath it would reach into water that rose and fell with the breathing of the larger growth.

I would be that small body at first: a folded thing spending nearly everything it had on an opening.

There would be insufficient room for a rosette. Whenever I broadened, the younger wall would press against me. Whenever I extended downward, the water would cover my new tissue before it hardened. Above me, a slit of daylight would narrow as the surface repaired itself.

But the old support would have a wound.

Not a fresh wound. A sealed place where a runner had once attached and been rejected. The collar around it would be thick, amber-stained, poor in living vessels. Rainwater would linger in its creases. A little warmth would come through from behind it, steady even when the upper country cooled.

My threads would gather there.

For a season, perhaps, I would resemble a pale brush trapped between two walls. Most of my green would disappear. The remaining leaf would become a narrow tongue laid along the slit overhead, sufficient to keep the rest of me extending, insufficient to let me grow freely.

I would enter the old wound through places that had never quite closed.

The support would respond slowly. A bitterness would move through the dampness around my tips. Several would die. Behind them, other branches would thicken and stop advancing. I would remain pressed against the collar, alive in the little water the larger bodies failed to reclaim.

On the younger side, things would be less difficult. Its wall would keep making soft new vessels. My tissue would meet them almost accidentally.

There would be a first exchange too small to alter anything visible above.

Afterward, I would no longer depend entirely on my leaf.

***

I would not become a bridge in the simple sense.

The two bodies would already have fibers crossing between them. They could lean on one another. Their difficulty would be in sharing water without bringing something intolerable with it.

Inside me, the first straight passages would blacken.

The surviving passages would go around them. Some would divide into fine channels, lose themselves in wet tissue, and gather again on the other side. Some would terminate in swollen cells where substances accumulated. Others would open only while pressure came from a particular direction.

Cream-colored films would establish themselves in the blind ends.

At first I would wall them off. Later, new compartments would form around the places where their presence did least harm. Fluid leaving those compartments would differ from the fluid entering them. Something that made the old support close its vessels would arrive diminished, or altered enough that the closure came late.

A little more water would pass.

My growth would follow that difference.

From above, the place would acquire a shallow hump. Leaves over it would turn before the rest of the plain, then turn back, responding to changes that had already spent themselves below. Rain would run to either side.

Inside, I would spread into a thick, pale pad, with an uneven fringe of tips. No part would have a clear view of what the others were doing. One edge would be shutting against a bitter seep while another softened to admit water. A fold deep in the pad would remain stiff through several wet seasons because it had once been pulled almost apart.

That stiffness would redirect later growth. New channels would bend around it. Their bends would slow the flow.

Some afternoons, the young country would wilt slightly on my far side while I remained full.

***

What reached me would come out of order.

A release of tension would pass through the support before any change arrived in its water. I would draw inward at the old wound, closing some of my wider openings. Then a pulse of pressure would come from the younger body, forcing fluid into passages already narrowed.

Much later, the substances released by damaged tissue would arrive.

By then, I might have responded twice.

I would have no horizon, no sight of the raised basin emptying beyond the ridge. Its collapse would reach me as a sequence of local conditions, each sufficient to make some portion of me act. The thickness of a wall would determine how much of the disturbance it received. A blind compartment might hold the chemistry of one accident until another had begun.

In that way, I would keep different times very close together.

The old support would ask nothing of me, but its water would become scarce. The younger body would continue pressing against my other side, full from rain. Gradually my passages would widen toward the old tissue.

For several days that would help.

Then the water from the young body would change.

Perhaps one of its deeper chambers had opened into an isolated pool. Perhaps a hot patch had finally broken through its rim. I would receive the altered fluid before I had grown the means to stop it.

The films in my compartments would flourish.

Warmth would collect in the center of me. My narrow upper leaf, still surviving in its slit, would turn dull. Along the old support, vessels that had admitted me for years would begin closing in succession.

The closures would trap water inside my pad.

Above, the hump would rise.

It would be a small event in that country: a rounded swelling among many rounded swellings. Rain would drum more loudly on it. Minute leaves would separate as the surface stretched.

Below, one of my oldest folds would tear.

The fluid would escape through the space where I had first unfolded.

***

For a time I would be mostly edges.

The center would soften and drop into the chamber beneath me. The younger body would seal away the injury. The old support would remain standing, with a wet streak down its flank.

I would persist around the tear in strips of unequal thickness.

Some strips would still carry water between the neighbors. Others would have become parts of their sealed surfaces, supplied from one side only. The green tongue overhead would die when the slit closed. A portion of my substance would descend into black water and feed threads I had never touched.

It would be difficult, then, to say how much of me remained.

Yet the opening would remain also.

Warm air would pass through it. Condensation would collect along its lower lip. During rain, the younger body would swell toward it; in dry weather, the old support would pull back. The few living bands crossing the gap would be stretched and slackened with a regularity they had not known before.

New growth would gather along those bands.

I would grow around the moving space rather than fill it. A smooth lining would extend down the old wet streak. Along the upper edge, overlapping flaps would form where the torn tissue curled inward. They would close when the younger wall pressed too hard and separate when the chamber below became warm.

Water would still cross through my surviving passages, but less of it. Air would pass between them.

There would be no return to the broad pad. Its softened center would leave a place into which I could bend.

***

If you came across that stretch of country much later, you might notice nothing until a warm breath touched your ankle.

Then you would find a short opening under the raised edge of a flattened stem. Its rim would be darker than the surrounding surface. A few clear beads would stand along it, trembling at intervals.

I would be there, in the bands beside the opening and the damp lining below.

The body uphill would be young enough to swell abruptly with rain. The old support would barely move. Between them, I would have grown long on one side and densely folded on the other. Water would take a crooked route through me. In a few enclosed pockets, pale films would still alter what passed.

When you put your weight nearby, the surface would firm. The younger wall would shift inward, and my upper flaps would settle against one another.

After you moved away, they would separate again.

For a while the lower rim would stay cool where your shadow had fallen. Then the heat from the chamber would return. A bead would enlarge beneath the opening, hang from a transparent thread, and fall.

Farther in, where the lining met the old support, a new tip would be pressing into a crease.
