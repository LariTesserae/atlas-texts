I would first mistake the oldest ground for safety.

I would go there because a dropped shard lands where it seems likely to land, because water stays inside its rim, because it is possible to leave something in a hollow and find the hollow again. Beneath the split end of the wooden paddle, I would discover a strip of shade just wide enough to occupy.

For a while I would take the shape of that strip.

But dependable ground is not necessarily good ground for a soft body. Dust would gather in my creases. The damp beneath the paddle would diminish. Eventually I would have to loosen myself from the wood and go looking for a place where something still leaked.

I think I would live near the boundary of that district, under a badly attached length of trough.

Not at the largest break. Somewhere less conspicuous: a joint that opens when the basin above it fills, then closes before the escaping water can empty it. The ground there would remain damp through most of the long still intervals. An old wedge beneath the trough would keep the roof from settling completely.

I would know the underside of that wedge better than anything visible from above.

My body would be broad at one end and divided into several folds at the other. Spread out, I might cover the wooden paddle. Gathered beneath the trough, I would be no larger than a heap of wet hide. I would have a place along one side that never quite flattened, even with all the hard pieces moved away from it.

An old injury, probably. Something once closed around me.

Inside, I would carry five useful fragments and one that mostly got in the way.

The useful ones would include a blunt triangle of fired clay, two flakes that could lie closely together, and a thick piece with one smoothly rounded edge. The fifth would be so small that I sometimes lost track of it until I put my weight down badly.

The troublesome piece would be part of a handle.

Not a whole loop. Just the bent portion, broken at both ends, with a little glaze remaining where it had once joined its vessel. It would make an awkward lump when I tried to pass through narrow spaces. I would have to turn it lengthwise, ease one end through, then draw the other after it.

I would not know what it had belonged to.

Sometimes, resting, I would arrange my body around its curve and leave the space beneath it empty.

*

There would be work close to home.

The trough’s faulty joint would admit grit as well as water. Too much grit would prevent it from closing. Too little would let the two sides meet so tightly that the seep beneath them stopped. I would spend considerable time drawing grains out of the gap and pushing a few back in.

From above, this would look like a repair undertaken without much conviction.

Other creatures would pass through my shelter. A broad one with a dark, polished underside would come whenever the upper basin began to develop a milk-colored skin. Two smaller ones would arrive together and leave separately. Sometimes one would stay where I needed to work, and I would press against it until it moved.

I would recognize them through the ground: the distribution of their weight, the pauses between their contacts.

The broad one would never settle all at once. First an edge, then a long interval, then the rest of the body.

When it failed to return, I would continue leaving room for that interval.

There would be no particular day on which I stopped.

*

What I did beyond the trough would begin almost accidentally.

A young creature would approach a fresh seam while I was resting across it. The far side would be holding steady beneath me. I would lift a fold, making space, and it would cross over my underside rather than touch the seam itself.

Afterward, I would remain there longer than necessary.

There are small satisfactions available to such a body. Water entering a dry crease. A hard fragment settling exactly where the next movement requires it. The weight of another creature passing across a portion of you that has been properly supported.

I would begin testing nearby crossings.

Not making paths. I could not keep a path. A good stretch of ground might be folded under before I reached its end. But at certain joints I could find an arrangement in which my fragments did not shift unexpectedly, my moisture did not pull toward the far bank, and the reaching part of me remained the same length when I brought it back.

There I would wait.

Sometimes another would cross. Sometimes nothing would come, and the heat would drive me home.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

A seam could change beneath the second creature after holding for the first. I would feel the crossing body tighten against me, and gather my fragments beneath the pull, and discover that the support I had trusted was no longer directly underneath.

Then there would be two of us to get out.

Once, I think, I would return to the trough with a fold stretched so thin that water shone through it. I would try to tuck it away. It would keep opening.

The broad creature would be there that time.

It would place itself against my damaged side with an inconvenient firmness. Each time I tried to resume work, it would move with me. Eventually I would settle. Wet clay would be worked between us.

For a long interval I would have nothing to do but attempt, occasionally, to release the shape of the accident.

The leaking joint would go untended.

It would survive.

*

I would not always carry the handle.

During a storm, on a sheet of ground too new to have become reliable, I would have to pass my hard pieces forward. One after another, they would leave through a loosened fold and enter the body ahead of me.

The handle would catch.

I would tighten around it. The creature ahead would pull. Behind me, the ground would begin to separate into shallow layers, each sliding a little farther than the one above.

For a moment I would hold the handle and the other body with nearly equal force.

Then I would let the handle go.

Without the fragments I would be unexpectedly difficult to move. Reaching would spread me rather than carry me forward. The others would have to draw me across in sections, keeping my edges gathered so that I did not lose too much water.

On firm ground, the pieces would come back.

The blunt triangle. The paired flakes. The rounded edge. A small fragment that might or might not be mine.

Not the handle.

I would search the bodies nearest me by pressing lightly against them. One would open a fold containing a curved shard, but its curve would be wrong. Another would still be supporting someone farther back.

The sheet we had crossed would lift at its distant edge.

There would be no returning to look.

*

Later, under my trough—or under another trough, with a different wedge holding up its roof—I would find that I still made room for the missing piece.

Water would collect in the space.

At first it would spill whenever I moved. I would gather my body around it, unnecessarily, preserving the old curve. Then, over many crossings of the small chamber beneath the bank, I would learn to carry it.

A few drops only. More than I needed to keep that part of myself damp.

One day I would come upon a little creature on dry ground, folded tightly around its first fragment. It would be lifting one edge and setting it down again without advancing.

I would settle beside it.

I would not know whether it was injured or merely learning. I would bring the hollow in my side close to the clay and loosen it until water touched the ground between us.

The little body would open toward the damp.

I would have to wait while it drank. Above us, a tool would knock against something beyond sight. The sound would pass, and for a while no pressure would follow.

There would be time to lower the hollow a little farther.
