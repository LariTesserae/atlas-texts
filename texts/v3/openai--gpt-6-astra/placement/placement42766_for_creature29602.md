I would stay at the entrance longer than I meant to.

At first I would keep my hand where it had been placed, careful of the smallest finger. When the maker withdrew, I would assume I ought to withdraw too. But the space between my palm and the floor would still be warm, and the folded leaf would be standing a little farther into it.

I would wait until something made the decision less difficult: a change in the light, perhaps, or the maker returning to touch my wrist.

Neither would happen. Eventually my arm would ache. I would take my hand away.

The leaf would remain where it was.

For several visits I would watch for evidence that I had left too soon.

Meanwhile, the garden would begin to make use of my being there. One of the rectangular-winged creatures would settle inside the bend of my knee. The six-legged animal above the entrance would drop a flake from its underside, and I would catch it without knowing whether it ought to be returned. A maker would leave an abrasive cord beside me while lifting something with both hands.

I would hold the cord out when it came back.

It would take the other end and begin working.

That might be my first work here: keeping a length of cord taut while, at the far end, someone drew a groove around a piece of horn. I would feel the turning through the cord before I could see what was being made. Fine pale dust would collect along my wrist.

Afterward, the maker would leave me the unused length.

I would coil it beneath the seat. On the next visit it would have uncoiled by half a turn.

—

I do not think I would remain quite the person who arrived.

The seat is low. There is more to see beneath it than above, once the first astonishment has passed. I would spend time kneeling, then lying on one side with an arm extended into the warm dark. There is a frond underneath the first terrace whose tip keeps brushing the floor. Each new division makes it heavier. I could lift it with the back of a finger while the little grazers crossed underneath.

From there I would notice a passage between two supports. Beyond it, another passage, offset slightly. The rubbing would be much clearer with my cheek against the clay.

Some days I would be able to reach farther.

I might blame the shifting masonry before noticing that my elbow now folded in a second place.

There would be no need to take the rest of me apart at once. My sleeves could stay sleeves for a long time. The dust worked into their cuffs would bind under pressure. One morning a cuff would hold the shape of my wrist after I drew my hand through it.

I would put my hand back.

Something about the fit would be comfortable enough to stop investigating.

Eventually I would find myself beneath the entrance seat without remembering the effort of getting there. My back would touch its underside. My feet would rest on different levels of the paving. I would be long through the middle and rather flat, with folds that closed when I moved sideways.

I would still have hands. They would be unequal, though not like the makers’ hands. One would retain the narrow fingers that could hold a cord. The other would have become broad from resting under leaves. Along its back there would be a shallow groove where a stem had lain through several afternoons.

I would have difficulty turning over.

For a while, this would seem a serious defect.

Then I would learn to go backward.

—

The makers would not immediately find a use for me. I would follow one to the alcove and sit where I could watch its hands. It would occasionally pass me a shaving. Most would be too small to hold comfortably. I would accumulate them against my chest until moving became awkward.

The smallest maker would stop beside me, take away most of them, and lay them on the floor.

It would leave one.

A curved piece, light on one end, dense on the other. I would turn it over for a long time. Nothing in it would appear to be waiting for the thing I knew how to do.

Eventually I would carry it home beneath the seat.

By then there would be a rubbed place in the clay where I rested. The maker with the grooved crown would widen the opening beside it, working around me while I pretended to sleep. It would remove a ridge that had been pressing into my side.

I would miss the ridge that evening. I had used it to brace myself when reaching toward the little plant.

I would fetch my curved shaving and set it in the empty place.

—

There would be things I became good at.

Holding a leaf while its stand was moved. Reaching a tool that had fallen behind a root without lifting the root. Lying across a narrow break in the paving until an animal finished crossing me.

I would learn that the jointed grazers disliked being helped from above. If lifted, they clamped their mouths shut and remained still for hours. But if I put my broad hand ahead of them, close enough to seem continuous with the leaf, they would climb onto it.

Their feet would leave small hooked impressions.

Sometimes a maker would smooth these away. Later it would stop doing so.

I would also make mistakes. Once, keeping a new stalk upright, I would hold it through a whole long evening and into the next light. When I finally released it, it would lean against me.

I would move away. It would lean farther.

For some time afterward I would have to sleep beside that bed. The makers would bring narrow strips and fit them around the stalk, leaving room for it to thicken. One strip would run through the groove on my hand.

The plant would eventually support itself. I would continue sleeping there a little longer.

—

What I would want, I think, would emerge more slowly than what I could do.

There would be a hollow beneath the third terrace that no one used. Too shallow for a pouch animal, too open for the folded-winged things. At a certain point in the evening, a shadow from the entrance would pass across its mouth.

I would begin taking pieces there.

Not the useful ones I was given at the bench. A flake with a blue edge. A broken turning peg. A thin curl of red material that trembled whenever someone walked on the terrace overhead.

I would arrange them, move them, carry one away and bring it back. The blue flake would look different against the peg. I would spend several days finding something to put beside it, then remove that thing as well.

A maker passing nearby might lower its head to look.

I would keep my hand in the hollow until it had gone.

Later I would wish I had moved my hand.

This, too, would take time.

—

By the time the plant at the entrance opened its fifth leaf, its roots would have reached my resting place.

One would grow along my side during the hours when I lay still. I would ease it away before leaving each morning. After several mornings it would fork, one branch continuing down into the pottery bed, the other resting in a fold beneath my arm.

A maker would offer to shift my bedding.

I would move a little to show that I could still leave.

The root would grow a hard, smooth section where my body passed over it.

From the entrance I would probably resemble something stored beneath the seat: a folded apron, a padded tool, a support taken away from a plant that no longer needed it. Only the hand might give me away, lying palm upward beside the scored square.

I would not always be waiting to help.

Sometimes I would have the blue flake there, turning it so that its edge caught the light. And if someone sat down above me and stayed, if their feet were quiet beside the young roots, I might push it a little way toward them.

Not all the way.

Far enough that they could see it without having to pick it up.
