I would be underneath the foot that is still searching.

Not in the gripping pad, where every movement would crush me, but a little farther up: in the crease where the pale joint enters its sheath. There is a bead of moisture there that does not freeze when the foot lifts. At first, that is all you would see of me.

When the foot stretches toward the bare stone, the bead lengthens.

Something inside it holds on.

***

I would be small enough to live through a journey without knowing where the traveler was going.

My body would be nearly transparent, flattened along one side, with a dark thread running through it. Not quite a worm. The flattened side could open into two muscular lips, enclosing a surface and drawing the rest of me tightly against it. Along my back, overlapping grains of mineral would keep an edge of shelter over my breathing skin.

I would acquire those grains rather than grow them. Young ones like me would wear whatever they could lift. An older one would have a close-fitting covering, patiently assembled, with the heavier pieces toward the end most often exposed.

Under the searching foot, I would have one grain that did not fit.

It would catch whenever the joint closed. I would keep it for a long time.

My usual home would be a chamber in the roots, near a valve but not directly in its current. There I could brace myself against the glass lining and graze the cloudy film that gathered along the rim. With each contraction, water would pass over my back. Between contractions, I would advance a little.

The rim would take many feedings to circle.

There would be others in the chamber. We would encounter one another face-first, stop, and gradually solve the obstruction. Sometimes one would climb over the other. Sometimes both would reverse, leaving a narrow strip ungrazed between them.

When a chamber worked well, we would become almost evenly spaced around it.

No one would arrange this.

***

What I would know best is the instant before water moves.

The chamber wall tightens. My belly receives the tightening before the current touches my back. I flatten, close the small openings along my sides, and let the water pass.

Hunger can wait through that instant. So can mating, or the slow business of removing a grain that has worked beneath another grain.

In a failing chamber, the warning changes.

There may be a tightening with no current afterward. There may be water without any warning at all. The first kind leaves time to loosen my grip and search. The second can take the covering off my back.

I would have lost part of it once.

For a while afterward, I would live behind a dead valve, where the current broke around the obstruction. My uncovered skin would grow cloudy at the edges. I would collect very small grains because I could no longer lift large ones without bending the wound.

By the time the wound closed, the chamber would be cooling.

That would be how I came to the traveler.

Not because I wanted to travel. Its feeding mouths would have warmed the roots overhead, and a thin, nourishing seep would have begun running down the chamber wall. I would follow it upward, through passages too narrow to turn around in.

Eventually the hard lining would give way to something yielding.

I would keep grazing.

Above me, the traveler would take several nights to decide to leave.

***

A foot sheath is a poor chamber.

It changes shape without sending water. Its walls sometimes press together until there is scarcely room for my dark internal thread. Then they open too far, admitting air so dry that my lips draw inward.

But there would be food around its upper edge, and warmth beneath it, and nowhere safer within reach.

As the traveler moved, I would move around the joint.

When it bent, I would shelter on the outside of the crease. When it straightened, I would slip inward. I would learn this imperfectly. Once, the edge of my covering would be caught, and the traveler would carry me for a long distance by three grains of stone.

The world beyond the sheath would arrive in brief pieces.

A stem passing close enough to scrape me.

The cold wet underside of a leaf.

Another foot, planted, with its gripping hairs spread through the roots.

Once there would be nothing nearby at all. The traveler would lift over a fissure, and I would open my lips against empty air.

Far below, a frond would shine green along one torn edge.

I would close again.

***

I think I would remain on the traveler longer than was good for me.

When it settled, there would be opportunities to leave. Wet roots would press against the sheath. A new pumping bed would send its contractions through the feet. The chamber rhythm would be there, recognizable beneath the larger movements of flesh.

But the first passage I tried would end against a sealed feeding contact. The second would contain a chemical trace that made me withdraw before reaching its source.

Meanwhile the foot would remain warm.

I would begin laying eggs in its upper crease.

They would be fastened separately, each beneath a little shelter of discarded grains. When the joint moved, the shelters would rub against the sheath and wear it smooth.

Some of the young would leave by routes I had tested and abandoned. They would fit where I did not. Others would settle around neighboring feet, grazing the same thin film.

I would meet them without recognizing them.

Their coverings would still be loose. When I pressed against one, a grain would sometimes come away and adhere to my own back. I would carry it until I found something that fitted better.

***

Over time, the foot would move less often.

Other feet would reach farther, fitting themselves between the dense stems of a hollow. Mine would remain tucked beneath the rear fold. Its gripping pad would harden without wearing down. The sheath would deepen.

Here I would become larger than I could have become in the root chamber.

There would be room to turn. My lips would spread around the old joint, and my back would form a low obstruction across the entrance. Moisture accumulating inside would have to rise a little before it passed over me.

Small worms would gather on the damp side.

When I shifted, they would shift.

The traveler would occasionally contract the sheath. Water would push against my flattened body, lift one edge, and escape. Afterward I would settle back, held in place by the slight inward pull.

In this way, without growing anything new, I would become part of a valve.

I would still graze. I would still loosen a troublesome grain, rolling against the wall until it fell away. Sometimes I would crawl far enough toward the entrance to let all the water out at once.

The worms would retreat into the deeper crease.

I would return when the exposed skin began to tighten.

***

The traveler’s last resting place would be in a shallow hollow, beside a pool that emptied somewhere beneath the rock.

I would not know it was the last.

For a long time, there would simply be fewer disturbances. The folds overhead would cease pulling the sheath sideways. Fine roots would enter through its outer edge. I would graze around their tips, leaving the living surfaces alone.

The warmth would diminish unevenly. One side of my shelter would cool while the other continued to fill with fluid.

Then there would be a tightening I had not felt before.

It would come from outside the traveler.

Water would follow.

I would flatten, and the water would pass over my back.

The roots would have found the cavity behind the old foot. Farther inward, cleaners would be opening passages through tissue that no longer resisted them. The sheath would still contract sometimes, but its contractions would have less and less to do with the current.

For a while I would make mistakes. I would brace against a motion that brought nothing. I would be lifted by water I had not prepared for.

One of the heavy grains would come loose.

Beneath it, my skin would still be soft.

***

You might find me much later, if you lifted the edge of that low rise.

There would be a narrow passage where a foot had been. Around it, the remaining tissue would be dark and tough, crossed by roots. A little water would stand behind a pale, mineral-covered obstruction.

Touch the passage, and I would hold more tightly.

Wait, and you might see the grains separate.

I would advance along the rim, exposing a clean crescent of flexible glass. Water would begin slipping through beside me. Something farther down would answer with a click.

I would stop grazing.

Under my belly, the wall would begin to tighten.
