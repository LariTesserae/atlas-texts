I would be in one of the passages that seems to go farther than it does.

There would be a turn, three shallow steps, a recess for a lamp, and then my room. Someone coming to find me for the first time would arrive carrying the lamp from the recess, believing it had been left there to light the rest of the way. I would have to explain that there was no rest of the way. The lamp belonged there because I sometimes came home with both hands full.

My window would look through the arcade rather than directly into the bowl. From my bed I could see part of a pillar, the underside of a garden, and a brightness that changed without showing me the weather. A vine would keep trying to cross the opening. I would turn it aside rather than cut it, until somebody pointed out the length of bare stem I had accumulated along the wall.

I think I would make handles.

Not whole tools, at least not often. I would have a little skill with the short saws, a good knife, and a disproportionate interest in the place where a thumb rests during work. People would bring me a blade wrapped in cloth, or a mallet head, or a tool still fitted with the handle they hated.

“Like this,” they would say, putting their hand around it.

Often they could not tell me what was wrong until I had made something else wrong.

The woman who manages the sluices would be an awkward customer because she also makes handles. She would bring me work on behalf of other people, describing their requirements with great exactness. Once I would make a grip to her instructions and she would say it was too narrow.

“For whose hand?” I would ask.

She would take the tool away. A few days later we would both behave as though the exchange had gone better than it had.

Wood would make me careful, but not invariably good. A piece carried all the way up the cleft could be spoiled in the last few strokes. I would keep such pieces in a basket beneath my bench, convinced that something smaller remained possible. Pegs, toggles, wedges, little lids. Eventually the basket would become difficult to pull out, and Eda, visiting for some unrelated reason, would put her foot against the bench and help me wrench it free.

“You have to use them,” she would say.

“I do.”

She would lift a curved scrap too short for a finger.

“What is this going to be?”

I would dislike the question enough to remember it.

There would be other work. No one could spend every day attending to thumbs. I would carry leaves up from the cleft and turn fiber when asked, and take my share of the sweeping. On the slope I would be slower than I looked capable of being. I would mistrust the wet shine even where everyone assured me the stone held a bare foot well.

My broom would have an unusually comfortable grip. This would not prevent my back from hurting.

At first I imagine myself as quiet. But quiet is too easy a thing to imagine being here. The bowl would expose how much noise I made without speaking: the saw catching, a handle dropped while I tested its balance, my bench shifting with a thud because I had once again failed to wedge its short leg.

Someone across the hollow would know the sound of my work and be tired of it.

I would hum, too. Only a few notes, repeatedly, without knowing I was doing it. Orrel would once sing the rest of the tune from his kitchen.

I would stop.

“Don’t stop,” he would call.

But I would have lost it.

—

Senn would bring me his wedges one at a time.

He would not begin with a request. He would stand at the entrance and ask whether I had been sleeping well, then examine something on the bench, then tell me that the small drain cover above the bath had become troublesome. Only after this would the wedge appear.

It would be difficult for him to draw out with his injured hand. He would want a larger hole for the cord.

I would enlarge it, smooth the edges, and give it back. The next week he would bring another.

For a while I would think he disliked asking for help. Later I would learn that he was trying each alteration before committing the others to it. Several of the wedges had uses I did not understand. One needed to lie nearly flat under a cover. A larger knot would have kept it from seating.

I would already have made him a whole set of improved ones.

He would inspect them with an expression I would come to recognize.

“These are very nice,” he would say.

They would remain on my shelf for a season. Then I would shorten two, split one, and give another to Tavi for a purpose she declined to describe.

The first useful thing I made for Senn without his instructions would be a flat toggle he could catch between the good fingers. I would leave it on the bench when he came by, rather than hand it to him.

He would pick it up.

This would become an irritating custom between us: useful things discovered by accident in a room where neither of us was deceived.

—

My room would not be especially tidy. The dry tools would be in order, and the damp cloths would hang separately, but there would be cups in places a cup did not belong. I would be reluctant to interrupt a cut to drink, then reluctant to get up for water when the cutting was finished.

I would like the sour fruit brought up from below. I would pretend, a little, to enjoy its sharpness more than I did. There would be someone who watched for the expression I made at the first bite.

In the evenings I might sit near the bath, where the wall retained a little warmth and people had reasons to pass without necessarily stopping. I would prefer this to being visited in my room. In my room I would feel responsible for whether a person stayed or left. Beside the bath, a conversation could simply run out while somebody dried their ankles.

The basket recess would remain empty.

Once, not knowing, I would put my basket there.

No one would tell me to move it. Someone would look at it, then at me, and continue past. Later Eda would explain whose place it had been. I would retrieve the basket and spend several days feeling that everyone had watched me do something callous.

Eventually the missing woman’s brother would put a bundle of wet fiber in the recess while he adjusted his sandal.

That would help, though he would not know it.

—

Tavi would want a box.

Not a basket with a lid, not a vessel, not one of the little folded leaf packets that everybody offered her. A wooden box that shut.

She would show me its size with her hands. It would be larger than anything I could justify making from my stock.

“What goes in it?”

“Things.”

“What things?”

“Mine.”

I would offer her a smaller one. She would accuse me of having enough wood, indicating the basket beneath the bench.

We would empty it together.

Most of the pieces would be useless for her purpose. She would discover this herself and become angry with them. But there would be thin offcuts from two replacement handles, and a short board split along one edge, and some scraps that could become corner pieces. The result would be uneven, more assembled than fitted. One side would be dark and the others pale.

She would help rub it smooth. I would have to stop her several times from testing the surface while the knife was moving.

We would disagree over the lid. I would want it to slide; she would want it to lift. Her arrangement would require pins so small that I would have to make three before getting two that matched.

By then I would care too much about the box. I would want its ugliness to become deliberate, its mismatched parts to look as though they belonged together. Tavi would ask when it would be finished.

“Soon.”

“You said that before.”

I would recognize the voice of a customer.

The lid would finally shut with a small, dry click. She would open it and close it again, then examine the empty space.

I would be waiting to see what she put inside.

She would take it away.

—

And I would not live permanently at the bench or in other people’s needs. There would be days when the room felt used up, when every object in it required another action from me.

On one of those days I would go down to the little pool without a broom.

The floor would be warmer than the arcade. Sitting there would make me visible from nearly every house, but nobody would call. Above me someone would shake soil from a root into a tray. Water would strike a shelf. I would look for the enclosed thread and fail to find it, though I had seen it before.

After a while Tavi would come down the curve, moving much more easily than I did.

She would sit close enough that our shadows touched.

“The box opens when I carry it,” she would say.

I would close my eyes.

“It needs a catch.”

“You didn’t say it needed a catch.”

“I didn’t know.”

Neither had I.

We would sit for a little longer. Then she would ask whether I was coming, and I would say yes, in a moment. She would go ahead to wait at my door.

Getting up from the bowl would require both hands against the stone. My palms would come away pale and faintly shining. Before climbing, I would rinse them in the pool, careful not to disturb the soft new edge.
