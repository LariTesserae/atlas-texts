I would want a shelter where the wind could not reach the floor.

Not a large shelter. Enough room to take off a coat without brushing the cooking vessel, and a place to stretch a wet cord at its full length. I would discover, after moving in, that the protected floor was protected because it sat in a hollow. Grit would collect there. Then water. Then, in a good season, a pale root would come up beneath the bedding.

I would be reluctant to cut it.

For a while I would sleep bent around it, telling visitors that it did not trouble me.

My shelter would stand near a division of paths, but not directly at the fork. There would be too much traffic there, too much stopping and sorting of loads. I would live a little way along the less-used reach, behind a knob of old growth. Travelers who knew the place would leave their packs at the fork and come around carrying only their cups.

I would hear them before I saw them. A tired person puts a walking hook down differently from someone who has just come from home.

At first I imagine myself giving them directions.

But everyone here gives directions. Even a child knows something that has ceased to be true since the last traveler passed.

The work I would actually have is going out afterward.

***

There is a path beyond my shelter that appears in almost every old account of the upper reaches. It goes across a saddle, beneath a bent stem, and along a shelf wide enough for two laden people to pass. At its end there used to be a bridge.

The bridge is gone. This is not recent news.

What is recent is that the saddle has risen enough to hide the broken end. From a distance, the path looks better than it has in years. The shelf has broadened. Young bark covers a place that used to require crawling. A traveler arriving with old directions might reasonably think the warnings had been exaggerated.

I go there with plugs, a mallet, and a coil of cord.

The plugs are made from dead outer wood, their heads cut across with a deep notch. I drive them into the path holes, leaving the heads proud of the surface. A walking hook finds the notch and catches. It does not give the familiar assurance of an open hole.

Farther on, where there is room to turn a party, I bind a bar across the old guiding groove.

I do not like doing this.

Some of the holes are older than my settlement. Inside them, beneath the reach of weather, the wood is almost smooth. Hands I would have liked to know made them. Other hands kept them clear. I have to scrape out their accumulated grit before a plug will seat properly.

My mallet has a padded head. Even so, each blow travels a long way.

Sometimes someone on a neighboring branch answers, thinking I am signaling.

I stop until they stop.

***

Closing a path is only part of the work. Usually I am sent to find out whether it needs closing at all.

I am not especially good at exposed crossings. On a single root, I can become occupied by the idea of my foot rather than by the placement of it. I have to say the sequence aloud.

Hook. Hand. Knee. Wait.

People who travel with me learn not to speak during the waiting.

I am useful because I am willing to go a short distance and come back with an unsatisfactory answer.

The bark is sound as far as the second rise.

I could not test the underside.

There is room for a person, but I would not send a loaded animal.

In the settlement, these answers sometimes become irritating. Someone needs the route to be open. Someone has a sister beyond it. Someone has already promised a quantity of dried meat and cannot bear to carry it trunkward for six days.

They ask what I think.

I tell them what I put my weight on.

This sounds like a virtue when I describe it here. In my shelter, with a disappointed person sitting across from me, it often feels like withholding something.

And I would not always be careful. After enough safe journeys, I would begin leaving pieces out. I would say “the crossing” when there were two. I would assume a familiar traveler understood which side of the split to take.

There would be one omission I remembered while trying to sleep.

Not necessarily a death. Perhaps a night spent clinging in an exposed place, a ruined hand, a water vessel lost. The person would return and describe it without raising their voice. Afterward, when I gave directions, I would make people repeat them.

Some would think age had made me officious.

***

Inside my coat I would carry short lengths of cord.

They would not be a complete map. A complete map would require more cord than I could carry, and still fail to tell someone whether frost had loosened a particular scale of bark that morning.

Each length would stand for one journey I had recently made. Knots for divisions. A doubled section for somewhere a load must be lowered separately. A loose tail where I had stopped.

The cords would help me remember the order while I spoke. They would not be safe in another person’s hands.

I would have to explain this repeatedly.

There would also be cords I no longer used but could not bring myself to unravel. They would hang behind the bedding, tangled around the pale root I had allowed to grow through the floor.

One would describe the way to my childhood home.

Nothing dramatic would have happened to that home. The drinking hollow failed over several dry seasons. People left in small groups. My parents took the roof apart and used its best pieces for another shelter. The route remained passable for years, though nobody had much reason to travel it.

Eventually I stopped checking.

I would still be able to recite it. That would bother me more than forgetting.

***

Senn would come to my shelter because I had been near her brother’s reach.

She would ask about the path first. How much it had lengthened. Whether the low crossing still held frost on its underside. Whether a person could make the journey before the next change in weather.

Only after this would she ask about him.

I would have seen him briefly, at the pond. He had been repairing the ladle handle. He had asked me to tell her that his daughter’s knee was better.

“How much better?”

“He said she was walking out to the first grazing shelf.”

“With a stick?”

“I didn’t ask.”

Senn would rub the scar beside her mouth. I would resent, for a moment, the question I had not known to ask.

Then I would remember something her brother had said while fitting the handle back together.

He had said the girl’s cord was too fine for hauling anything. It was meant for sewing.

Senn would look down at her coat.

“He thought you might be saving it because you couldn’t find a use for it.”

“I know what it’s for.”

I would wait.

She would ask whether I was going that way again. I would say not soon, which would be true, and not certain, which would also be true.

For a little while she would sit without offering a message.

Then she would say, “Tell him I haven’t lost it.”

I would repeat the words so she could correct them.

She would not.

***

There would be times when I went beyond the waiting post without being sent.

This would not be adventurous. I would take a small amount of water and turn back while I still had most of it. I would want to see whether the young spur had thickened, whether the two surfaces that approached each other across a narrow gulf were still approaching.

A bridge begins long before anyone can put a foot on it. For years it may be only a question worth walking out to ask again.

At one such place I would keep a measuring cord.

It would be secured on my side, wrapped against the frost. The far end would have a small hook that I could cast across the gap. When it caught, I could draw the cord taut and mark the distance. Sometimes the hook would come back carrying a shaving of pale wood. Sometimes I would spend so long trying to catch it that my hands became clumsy and I had to stop.

The gap might narrow one season and widen the next.

I would begin imagining a path there too early. I know I would. A short journey to a pond I had visited only twice. Shelter from the prevailing wind. Perhaps, eventually, a way around the troublesome low crossing.

I would mention none of this in my reports.

But I would take a little extra cord when I went.

***

I think that is where I would be found: kneeling at the end of an unfinished possibility, trying to free a hook without damaging the soft wood that held it.

Not alone all the time. There would eventually be someone younger beside me, impatient with the casting, certain they could throw better. I would give them the cord. They probably could.

While they worked, I would hold the spare coils clear of the grit.

Below us, a flower would open somewhere we could not see. The smell would rise through the gap. My companion would stop with their arm drawn back.

“Is that close?”

I would lean forward as far as the tether allowed.

There would be no light beneath us, no visible lip or fold. Only the scent, much stronger now, and a small warmth touching the split skin beside my nose.

“I can’t tell.”

We would stay a little longer before going home.
