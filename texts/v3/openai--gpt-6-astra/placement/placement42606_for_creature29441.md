I would be under the loading platform, but not all the way at the back.

There is a place where the daylight reaches the concrete without quite reaching the underside of the boards. A bolt hangs down there, with a little rust on its end. Beyond it, shoes pass through the brightness. I would lie just inside that boundary, one foot out, the rest of me in the company of things that have been put away but might be needed.

At first I think I would be listening.

But listening suggests that the sound is arriving from somewhere else. Here it would already be in my body. The platform trembles against a few plates on my back. The concrete gives another part of the beat to my belly. Between them, something is always slightly out of agreement. I shift a little, and the disagreement moves from my shoulder to my flank.

This is enough to occupy a morning.

Behind me there are other bodies. I know where they are without turning. One is warmer than the others; one has a damaged plate that catches when it breathes. Occasionally a foot is placed on mine. If it remains there, I draw mine out and put it somewhere else.

I would not be the largest yardback. I would be large enough to have stopped being lifted routinely, but small enough that a determined worker could still do it. There would be a place on my left side where my plates had grown unevenly after some old scrape. When I passed beneath the lowest crosspiece of the platform, that place would make three small ticks against the wood.

The people who heard it might know which one I was.

I would know some of them by the things their arrival did to the floor.

One comes down from the loading door heavily on one heel. One stops before stepping off the platform, always in the same place. One carries the handcart.

That one I know early.

The cart is difficult. Its stillness proves nothing. It can stand in the yard through half the day, with a bird on its handle, then begin to move as if the ground has suddenly ceased to hold it. I do not trust its narrow contacts with the earth. There is too much above them.

When its wheels begin, I go backwards until my scraped plates touch the crosspiece. Then I flatten. A smaller animal behind me complains by moving all its feet.

For a while, nothing can persuade me that the morning is safe again.

Eventually there is the smell of water.

Not much water. Someone has rinsed something at the loading door, and a little has run through a joint in the boards. It carries iron, old wood, and a trace of whatever was on the thing being washed. A dark spot spreads on the concrete near my head.

My mouth reaches it before the rest of me moves.

This, more than the plates or the weight, is where I begin to become the animal: in having a mouth that can investigate three edges of a thing at once. One part rests on the damp concrete. Another follows the seam. The third finds a softened grain lodged against a stone.

I do not have to choose between touching and tasting.

The grain comes loose.

There is the small satisfaction of getting something out of somewhere.

Later I go to an outlet behind the western foundation pier. It is not the best outlet, and perhaps for that reason it is often available. The ceramic has a chipped lower lip. Water collects above the chip and falls too close to the wall for a large animal to put its mouth comfortably beneath it.

I fit by turning sideways.

A fern stem lies across the approach. I push beneath it, and droplets move down my back without reaching me. At the pipe mouth there is a coolness followed by warmth farther in. My upper lip enters the opening. The inside is smooth except where it is useful.

I stay until another yardback arrives.

We do not confront each other. Its head appears beside my foreleg. For a time we both work at the same narrow place, inconveniently. Then its shoulder presses against the uneven plates on my side, and I leave.

Outside the plants the light is considerable. I pause with my head drawn in. There are several yards to cross before the ridge, and nothing along the way to fit beneath.

A worker passes carrying a belt over one shoulder. The free end swings. I wait until it has gone.

Then I cross.

I would like to say that I know why the ground is rising. That would be an easy thing to give this body: a knowledge unavailable to the people with their brass studs and measuring rods.

But beside the ridge I would mostly know the ridge.

Here is a piece that shifts under my foot. Here is one that does not. Here is an edge too sharp to bear against. The new opening has a taste that the older openings lose. I put my mouth into it and find a little moisture, or something that makes my mouth produce moisture. The distinction is not important yet.

Farther in, beyond where I can reach, a fragment loosens.

I feel it through my jaw.

I withdraw and wait.

Nothing follows. A fly settles on the pale rubbed edge of my shoulder. After a while I put my mouth back.

—

On the day the fans stop, I would come out with the others.

The stopping would not feel like quiet at first. It would feel like a change in the ground I was resting on, although the ground had not moved. I would shift my weight, fail to recover the familiar pressure, and shift again.

Under the platform, bodies would separate.

I would follow the animal nearest the light. By the time I reached the open yard, it would have gone another way.

For once there would be no steady disturbance to arrange myself against. I would extend my head farther than usual. The paving would touch the soft underside of my jaw.

Somewhere a spanner would be dropped.

The impact would arrive cleanly, travel through me, and be gone.

I would remain there. Not receiving a message. Not keeping watch over whatever is underneath. Simply having found that, with my head in this position, there is more to feel.

When the fans began again, I would not withdraw immediately. Their return would pass through several uncomfortable speeds. A foot would lift. My plates would tighten. Dust would move beside my mouth.

Then the familiar beat would establish itself.

I would go back by a slightly different route.

—

By evening, a young one has occupied my place beneath the platform.

It lies with its feet spread extravagantly, asleep in the shallow depression where my weight has kept the dust thin. Its back is still soft enough to show a faint change of colour as it breathes.

There is room behind it, but getting there would require pushing it across the concrete.

I settle nearer the edge instead.

For a while the light touches my face. I draw my head in, then extend it again because the concrete here is cooler. Behind me, the young one wakes and begins trying to get beneath my belly. It is too large to do this easily and too small to have stopped trying.

I raise myself.

It takes a long time to arrange its feet.

When I settle again, one of mine remains outside, in the diluted daylight. A person passing the platform pauses. Their shadow reaches me before anything else does.

I wait.

They step around the foot and go on.
