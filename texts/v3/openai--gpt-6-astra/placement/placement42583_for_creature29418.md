At first I would be there to measure something.

I would arrive with a folding rule, a notebook, and shoes that were suitable for walking but not for standing in canal water. Someone outside the district would have asked for a drawing of the drains. There would be an old plan to work from, folded until a small white hole had opened at the meeting of its creases. On it, the pump house would be a rectangle. Several inhabited rooms would be blank space.

I would expect to finish in a week.

The woman would let me put my papers on the unused end of her workbench, but not my bag. The bag would go on a hook. She would tell me this before telling me her name.

For the first two days I would draw what I could see. On the third, I would discover that a trough passing under the winding shop did not emerge where the plan said it did. The apparent outlet belonged to a roof drain. The real trough continued beneath a kitchen, turned at a wall that no longer existed, and entered the canal through an opening concealed by someone’s landing steps.

“Then how do you clear it?” I would ask.

“You go through there.”

She would point at the kitchen.

This would be my first difficulty: a drawing could show the route of the water without showing the route of the person who had to follow it. I would begin a second sheet.

***

I think I would end up in the little space beside the winding shop where the replacement motor used to stand.

Not a proper room at first. A roof, three walls, a sliding grille that caught on the floor. There would be a clean rectangle on the brickwork where a cabinet had been removed and four bolts projecting from the concrete. I would strike my ankle against one of them often enough to wrap it in a rag, and then, much later, borrow a saw and cut it off.

There would be room for a narrow table and, if the chair were pushed in, a cot.

I would have taken lodgings elsewhere while making the survey. Then the work would run over, and the person who had commissioned it would stop answering promptly, and the machinist would say I might as well use the empty space until things were settled.

I would dislike how easily my possessions fitted there.

For a while I would keep my shoes pointed toward the door.

***

I would be someone who was good at finding the particular thing in a quantity of almost identical things.

A washer with the right inner diameter. A telephone number written beside the wrong date. The difference between the motor ordered and the motor delivered. I would have no special ear for machinery. The pump keeper could tell me to listen, and I would listen earnestly to the wrong part of the noise.

“No,” she would say. “Under that.”

I would hear it then, perhaps. Or think I did.

But I could copy a worn label before the last of its lettering disappeared. I could keep a list of the parts that had actually fitted, rather than the ones the suppliers insisted should fit. I could make a drawing large enough for the machinist to read without holding it beneath his fluorescent tube.

This would become my work by accumulation. Not the care of the pump; she would still do that. The errands and comparisons around its care. The things requiring a patient conversation with someone who had a catalogue open in another part of the city.

I would be less patient with the people near me. This seems important. I would be capable of spending forty minutes establishing the dimensions of a seal and then answering a child sharply because she had moved my ruler.

Afterward I would take the ruler outside and ask what she had needed it for.

The answer would be something I could not have guessed.

***

My place would acquire a shelf above the table. Then a second shelf, higher up, after someone pointed out a stain I had mistaken for old paint.

I would store parts in jars until the pump keeper objected.

“If you drop that, where will the glass go?”

After that I would use shallow tins. The labels would face outward. This would please me more than I would admit.

There would also be a drawer that would not fit the system: a red button, a smooth piece of tile, foreign coins, a tiny screwdriver no one trusted, a photograph found behind the replacement motor. In the photograph two men would stand beside a newly painted gate. Neither would be looking at the camera. I would ask who they were and receive three incompatible answers.

I would keep the photograph anyway.

At night I would read accounts of places with winter. I would be interested in the practical inconveniences: pipes that burst, steps that needed salt, the quantity of clothing a person had to put on just to buy bread. People would assume I wanted to go there. Sometimes I would. Sometimes the pleasure would be in sitting beneath a slow fan, with sweat drying on my chest, and reading that a river had become solid enough to carry a cart.

I would not tell the woman this. She would be quite capable of buying me a bus ticket as the first stage of solving what she took to be my problem.

***

We would become familiar without becoming entirely informed about one another.

She would know that I disliked being watched while adding figures. I would know that, when a repair had gone badly, she preferred an ordinary question to an expression of sympathy.

“Do you want the window left open?”

That sort of thing.

I would learn not to hand her a tool by presenting its working end. She would learn that if she said a measurement aloud while I was looking for a pencil, I would lose it.

There would be disagreements. I would want the clearances written down. She would say they were written down. I would hold up three pages with three different numbers.

“They’re for different places.”

“Which places?”

She would take the book, irritated, and then discover that she could not remember which of two repairs the middle number belonged to.

For a day she would avoid my table. The next morning she would bring a piece of chalk and we would number the flanges.

Neither of us would mention the argument.

***

Eventually she would go to the coast again.

Not because I had become able to replace her. The machinist would take the morning round, and someone from outside would be paid to remain within reach. She would leave instructions that repeated some things and omitted others. At the bottom of one page she would write the telephone number of the restaurant near her hotel, though she would also tell us not to call it.

My task would be the evening lamp, the intake, and the readings. Nothing beyond that.

On the first evening I would switch the lamp on too early. It would look weak in the remaining daylight, an unnecessary yellow patch on the railing. I would consider switching it off again, then leave it.

The children would come past and ask when she was coming back.

“Thursday.”

“This Thursday?”

“Yes.”

They would stand beside me while I worked the forked pole. I would catch some weed, lose it, catch it again. Behind us, someone would be scraping a pan.

Then there would be a sound I did not recognise.

I would stop moving the pole.

For several seconds everything would continue: the belt, the motor, a lid being dropped in a kitchen, water touching the underside of the landing. I would try to separate what I had heard from what I had begun to imagine.

The older child would say, “That’s the loose window.”

I would look up. He would be right.

Later I would write the readings in the book. There would be a damp patch on the table where my forearm had rested, and I would keep the paper away from it. Before going to eat, I would walk round to the window and fasten the hook properly.

Her postcard would arrive the day after she returned. I would find it pushed through my grille: a white beach, an unreasonable blue sea. On the back she would have written that the food was expensive and the room was good.

I would put it on the shelf above the parts, leaning against a tin, where I could see it from the chair.
