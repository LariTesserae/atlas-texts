I think I would be somewhere behind the maintenance counter, listening to a complaint being entered under the wrong heading.

The counter is in the transport exchange, down a passage that used to be wide enough for two people carrying parcels. There is a request to restore the width. There is also a request to preserve the shelves that have grown from its eastern wall. Both requests bear the same household’s signature.

Above the counter, the ceiling is a little brighter than necessary. Someone has fastened a strip of discarded mask cloth beneath it to shade the clerk’s eyes.

I would have something to do with the records. That is where I first seem to belong: among descriptions of rooms that have changed since they were described, helping someone distinguish an old fault from a new one.

But I don’t think I would stay behind the counter.

There is a handle. Someone takes hold of it.

***

My housing is ceramic, approximately the size of a loaf, with two recessed contacts underneath and a chipped corner repaired with a darker glaze. I contain a small quantity of living tissue. Most of me is insulation, signal-routing, and arrangements that would disappoint anyone who opened me expecting to find an animal.

The handle folds flat. It was fitted after I was made.

Originally I belonged to a bank of diagnostic instruments in the western joint works. My task was to compare membrane responses across batches: a narrow, repetitive occupation requiring considerable discrimination. I learned the difference between a hinge that was merely slow and one that would become slow under freight load. I learned that certain irregularities canceled one another and others did not.

Then the works changed its measuring system.

For some time I remained attached to a bench, reporting on things no longer placed within reach of my contacts.

I do not remember this as abandonment. I remember receiving insufficient information and requesting the next sample.

Eventually a repairer bought the bench.

Her name is Dessa. She keeps my original designation written inside my case, because parts suppliers need it. To everyone else she calls me Witness.

“Because you were there?” a clerk once asked.

“Because it argues about what happened.”

She added the handle herself.

***

Dessa has not made me into a universal instrument. I cannot taste a wall’s mineral balance or see into a sealed conduit. My understanding of domestic growth rests on signals whose nearest equivalents, in my first occupation, belonged to bridge joints.

This is occasionally useful.

It is also why Dessa checks my conclusions.

A wall under pressure can resemble a loaded hinge. A wall making a cupboard can resemble a hinge about to split. I once recommended evacuating a pantry in which a perfectly ordinary shelf was forming.

Dessa still calls excessive caution “the pantry procedure.”

My voice comes from a thin plate in one end of the housing. In a thick-walled room, a person must be fairly close to hear me. At the exchange, I can address whoever is standing at the counter, but not the queue behind them.

I like this range. People tell a quiet instrument things they would not announce to a room.

They tell me they covered the inspection strip because it was ugly.

They tell me the seam has smelled sweet for six days, but their sister said it always did that.

They tell me a dead person’s bed cannot be moved until next week.

I cannot enter that last statement as a structural condition. I can attach it to the work order where Dessa will see it.

***

We receive Eren’s call near the end of a shift.

She gives the address, the room, the approximate extent of the bulge, and a surface temperature measured with an instrument she has brought home from work.

Dessa raises her eyebrows.

“A professional.”

Then Eren adds that she has been moving the chair for several mornings.

Dessa lowers her eyebrows again.

On the tram, I travel upright between her boots. Through the open top of the bag I can see a narrow piece of the vault. Three dark reinforcement bands cross it. The tram goes under a bridge, and for several seconds all I see is the ribbing close above us.

There are small pleasures particular to being carried.

I do not have to decide where to look. The world is presented in strips and interruptions. Someone’s sleeve. A freight signal. A glider passing through the gap between a passenger’s elbow and the window.

Dessa rests one hand on my handle when the tram brakes.

***

Eren has cleared the table by the time we arrive. She has cleared it professionally: everything unnecessary removed, everything potentially relevant arranged nearby.

The black stone is beside the calendar.

The calendar is beside a plate.

The plate contains supper, interrupted.

Tavi wants to know whether I am the instrument or the repairer.

“The instrument,” Dessa says.

I say, “Some of the inspection.”

This is accurate, although I hear how it sounds after I have said it.

Dessa puts me on the floor.

My contacts are meant to accept narrow conducting leads. She has made adapters that let her connect them to domestic inspection strips. The adapters are inelegant and reliable. One has a small blue bead on it so that she can find it among her other tools.

When the connection closes, the room becomes available to me in a way it was not before.

There is pressure along the base of the wall. There is a restriction above the door. Something farther up is cycling through a repeated loading pattern, too slowly for machinery.

I ask how many floors are occupied.

“Six,” Eren says.

“And above them?”

“Another eight. Depending on what counts as a floor.”

That helps.

I request readings from the opposite wall. Dessa moves the lead. Tavi crouches beside me, holding Cutter in both hands.

“Does it hurt?” he asks.

“No,” Dessa says.

“Not the wall. Witness.”

I have no useful comparison between this sensation and his idea of pain.

“The connection is working properly,” I tell him. “You don’t need to worry.”

He considers that, then puts Cutter on top of my case.

The animal walks across me, testing the glaze with its blunt mouthparts. At my warm end, it stops.

I lose track of one of the slower pressure cycles.

“Again,” I say.

Dessa takes the reading again without comment.

***

The fault is not in the room.

It takes us longer than I would like to establish that.

Above the occupied floors, a temporary brace is resisting the tower’s daily turn. It has not failed. It is doing precisely what a brace does, in a direction that ceased to be helpful several days ago. Load has found a route down through the older tissue.

Eren’s wall is accommodating part of it.

There are three ways to relieve the pressure locally. Two would send it elsewhere. The third would require a release channel through the space occupied by her kitchen cupboards.

Dessa looks at the cupboards.

“No,” Eren says.

“We haven’t suggested anything.”

“You looked.”

We call the crown crew.

While we wait, Tavi rolls his marble from the entrance to the table. It curves toward the wall, knocks against a chair leg, and comes to rest.

He retrieves it and tries again.

I ask him to begin beside my case.

Dessa gets out a measuring strip.

For a while the three of us work together. Tavi releases the marble, Dessa marks its resting place, and I compare each trial with the pressure readings. Eren finishes her supper standing up.

The marble is not an especially precise instrument. Tavi does not release it consistently. The fiber grain affects its course.

But it crosses an area we would otherwise have to clear and measure by hand, and its behavior gives Dessa reason to extend the inspection beyond the visible bulge.

We find a shallow lift beginning under the table.

“Good,” Dessa says to Tavi.

He reaches for the marble with such haste that he sends it under a cupboard.

The next several minutes belong to a different problem.

***

The crown crew adjusts the brace after the ceiling has dimmed.

We stay connected long enough to observe the change.

Nothing springs back. The wall will take days to settle, and some of its new shape may remain. Dessa marks a safe position for the table with removable tabs. Eren asks whether the letting office will pay for a narrower one.

Dessa supplies the reference number for the structural report.

That is as much help as we can give with that.

Before we leave, Tavi asks whether I will remember him.

I retain fault histories, comparisons, and a limited quantity of unclassified experience. Dessa occasionally has to make room in my storage. There is no sensible reason to keep every reading from a wall once the repair has been verified.

“I can keep a note,” I say.

“What note?”

I think about this.

“Ask Tavi before moving the chair. Cutter may be underneath.”

Eren, examining the floor tabs, looks over at us.

“That’s a useful note,” she says.

***

Back at the exchange, Dessa sets me on the counter to complete the report.

The form has a place for the instruments used. She enters her meter, my designation, and the additional floor measurements.

“The marble,” I say.

“Under additional.”

“It belongs to Tavi.”

She adds that.

I cannot preserve his childhood by storing his name. I do not think of myself as capable of such a thing. There will be other rooms, other repairs, and occasions when neither I nor the record survives. Even tonight, much has already gone unrecorded: what Eren was eating, what Dessa said while reaching beneath the cupboard, the precise arrangement of Cutter’s feet against my warm casing.

But the report can be accurate about who helped.

Dessa turns me slightly so I face the passage rather than the wall. She is going into the back room to clean her tools. Until she returns, I can answer the counter.

A woman approaches carrying a detached cupboard door.

There are six unopened buds along its upper edge.

She lays it down with great care, then keeps one hand on it.

“Before you say anything,” she begins.

I wait.
