At first I find myself above the water, where there is scarcely room to kneel.

One knee is on the courtyard paving. The other rests against the uppermost step, with a fold of cloth beneath it. Through the cloth I can feel the corner of the stone. I have put the bowl within reach, but not quite within easy reach; each time I take it up, I must shift my weight and brace a hand against the wall.

From here the blue tile is hidden by the reflection of the opening. I have to lean farther in to see it. My sleeve slips toward my wrist. Before it touches the water, I push it back with my chin.

I think I would belong to the place in this manner: awkwardly, through a task that has to be done again.

There would be things about me that did not enter the basin. My height, for instance. Down here I would be a shoulder against the stair, a wrist turning, the underside of a jaw. My voice would sound different when I answered someone in the courtyard. I might have a name called often enough to become an irritation, especially when both my hands were wet.

But the animal would know none of that. It would know the brief displacement when I put the bowl in, and the much larger disturbance of the brush.

I would be the person who had frightened it.

Not deliberately. The first touch would have been almost absent-minded: something pale remained where the fibers had passed, so I passed them there again. Then the pale thing struck the underside of the step.

The sound would have been small and distinct inside all that water noise.

For a moment I would keep the brush submerged. This would be the worst part to remember afterward—not the mistake itself, but the moment in which my hand continued to occupy the place it had occupied before I understood.

Then I would withdraw it.

There would still be the rest of the basin to clean. Someone would be expecting the bowl back. I would finish, more slowly, looking into the recess whenever the cloudy water opened enough to let me see.

For several visits I might not be certain it was still there.

Then a foot.

I would not speak to it at first. Speech would feel too much like reaching in again.

***

My work would not be only here.

There would be a drain in another room that caught hair, and a door swollen along its bottom edge. I would carry water where water did not arrive by itself. I would know which vessels could be set down hard and which had a crack that needed remembering. Some mornings I would come to the basin already impatient, with soreness between my shoulders and a list of other things still to do.

Those would be the mornings when I most needed a method.

The bowl on the upper step. The plug beside it. The cloth folded twice. The brush worked down the far wall first, so that the loosened film traveled toward the outlet rather than through the place I had just cleaned.

The lowest corner left alone.

At first I would leave too much. A broad, uncertain margin, because I could not see how far the animal extended beneath the stone. Later the stopping place would become precise. My wrist would learn it. I could be thinking about the swollen door and still lift the fibers before they reached the recess.

Yet I would sometimes look, to make sure habit had not shortened the distance.

The animal would work along the cleaned tiles after I finished. I might initially take this for approval. Then I would see how often it pressed its mouth to the glaze and moved on, finding nothing.

That would complicate the task without making it unnecessary.

I would begin cleaning the walls in sections.

Not a grand decision. One day there would be an interruption before I had finished, and when I returned I would find it feeding along the strip I had missed. The next time I would leave that strip again, and clean it on another day.

***

I would know the wooden plug particularly well.

Perhaps I would not have made it, but I would have pared one side after it began sticking. The pale end would carry a shallow flat from my knife. Fitting it would require a small turn to the left.

I would usually put it in before cleaning. It is easier to work without the inlet throwing water against the bowl.

Then I would notice what happens in the quiet.

The animal comes out while I am still arranging things. It passes my submerged fingers at a distance, climbs, descends, returns to the spout. Its folds stand open. I hold the brush above the surface and wait, as though waiting could show me what it wants.

Eventually I take the plug out.

The first water runs over my knuckles. There is a cough in the clay pipe, then the crooked stream establishes itself. The animal does not hurry home. It stays below the inlet with its toes spread, and only later withdraws.

After that, I would clean with the water running whenever I could.

My sleeve would get wet more often.

***

As it grew, I would have to lean farther down to find it.

At first this would seem an effect of the light. Then I would see the elbow projecting from beneath the step even when the rest of it was settled. I would remember how completely it used to disappear.

I might put my fingers into the recess while it was elsewhere, measuring what I could not see. The underside would scrape a knuckle. There would be no loose stone to lift, no simple adjustment. Above my hand lay the whole weight of the stair.

For some days I would look at other wet places as I went about my work.

Most would be unsuitable in ways I could recognize. The deep cistern with its sheer sides. The washing trough that was emptied each evening. A channel broad enough in one place but narrow farther on, where anything entering would have to continue.

I would not know yet what to do.

Meanwhile, it would still fit beneath the step. It would still come out to feed.

One evening, returning for the forgotten cloth, I would find it on the ledge beside the liverwort. Out of the water it would look unexpectedly substantial. The ribs would no longer borrow their color from the tile below. Its folded limbs would cast small shadows of their own.

I would stop with my hand on the lintel.

There would be room to take the cloth without touching it, but not without moving close. For a little while I would remain where I was, bent under the stair, one heel lifted from the paving.

Then I would leave the cloth until morning.

It would be cooling where my knee had pressed it, damp along the fold.
