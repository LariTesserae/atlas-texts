At first I would put myself beneath the stair, farther in than the crab.

There is room there for water, if not for another body. It passes the reinforcing rod, darkens a seam, appears on the other side of the wall. I would follow that passage a little way: the slight pressure before the tide, the grit that shifts without quite washing out. From there the pump would be something felt through the concrete. A footstep would arrive without a foot.

But I would want some part of myself outside. Not enough to leave the shelter. Enough to be changed by what was happening.

Near the pump house, I think, there would be a narrow stilling well. It would stand behind the stair, enclosed in a square concrete shaft, with a small pipe connecting its bottom to the canal. The pipe admits water slowly. A fish turning outside does not disturb the surface within. Neither does the heron’s foot. The well receives the larger changes after a delay.

Above it is a cabinet on four short legs.

The woman has to lift the door as she opens it, because the lower hinge has slipped. Inside, away from the worst of the weather, there is a drum wrapped in paper. A clock turns the drum. A pen rests against it.

That is closer to where I would be.

Not entirely in the cabinet, though. Part of me hangs down the shaft: a hollow float, a cable, a counterweight that travels beside the wall. My lower body rests on water in darkness. My upper body makes a line.

When the canal rises, the float lifts. The cable moves over a pulley. The pen crosses the paper by a corresponding amount. There is very little strength anywhere in the arrangement. A moth caught in the linkage could interfere with it. Salt in the wrong bearing could hold me at one level while the water rose around my float.

I would need the woman.

Once a week she would come with a fresh chart rolled loosely in one hand. She would unlock the cabinet, lift the pen, and stop the drum. During those few minutes I would still rise and fall, but nothing would be written. She would put the old sheet on the cabinet roof and hold it down with her keys.

Sometimes she would look along the line before folding it.

A blunt rise in the night. A long decline. The short, repeated teeth of a pump cycling when something was not quite right.

She could read more in those marks than I could. She would know which gate had been opened, which intake had needed clearing, whether rain had fallen inland while the saltworks remained dry. She might put a finger on an irregularity and remember having been outside at that hour.

I would have the irregularity, but not the hour as she had lived it.

Perhaps that would be the kind of being I was here: something with one continuous attention, unable to turn it toward whatever mattered most.

The heron could stand on the cabinet. Its weight would reach me as a brief disturbance in the pen. I would not have the bird itself. The woman might knock mud from her boot against one of my legs, and that, too, could enter the record as a fine scratch. An event without its proper shape.

Most of the crabs’ lives would take place beyond me.

This feels important to keep. I would not become the secret mind of the district. I would not know the shrimp under the curb, the gecko’s good hunting place, or the countless small mouths working the submerged stair. They would continue without being gathered into me.

Even the water I recorded would only be the water at this well. Beyond a closed gate, another basin could be falling. A clogged connecting pipe could make me slow. My line might look calm because I was obstructed.

The woman would discover that by lowering a weighted tape through the hatch.

She would crouch, read the wet mark, then look into the cabinet. For a while her face would be close to my workings. Sweat would gather beneath the rim of her cap. She would move the float cable gently between finger and thumb, testing for resistance.

If it ran freely, she would go for the rod to clear the pipe.

I would feel the clearing before I could record it: a sudden admission of water, the float rising too quickly, the counterweight descending through air. Silt would turn beneath me. The pen would climb. On the chart it might resemble an abrupt change in the canal, though the canal had been changing gradually all along.

She would write beside it in pencil.

*Pipe cleared.*

I like that she would be able to correct me without asking me to become something else.

By midmorning the cabinet would be hot. A small blister in its paint would soften under the sun. Inside, the paper would tighten as the damp left it, and the clock would continue with a sound too slight to hear over the pump.

There might be newer instruments elsewhere, sending readings to the office. Mine would remain because it still worked, because its paper could be laid flat on a desk, because replacing it had never become the most urgent job.

I would acquire a little history in this way. A pulley changed. A different pen fitted with a bracket made on site. A pencil mark inside the door showing where the float should sit at a known level. Several dates, in different hands.

And below all that, the dark water.

Before dawn, when the pump starts, the surface in the well would begin to lower. Not immediately. First the canal must fall enough to draw water through the connecting pipe.

The delay would be part of my body.

Outside, fish would gather in the discharge. The woman would rinse her cup. Beneath the lowest stair, the crab would keep one pair of legs inside its shelter while reaching for food.

Then the water under my float would move.

The cable would travel over the pulley, the pen would shift against the turning paper, and I would make another small length of line.
