I would be behind the head, in the place the animal cannot clean.

Not the whole green strip. Somewhere in it: a pale mouth at the end of a stalk, raised above several older stalks whose mouths are gone. My attachment would be smaller than the point at which a scratch becomes visible. Beneath it would be something I could not distinguish from a wall.

It would move.

For long intervals, though, it would be a good place to feed. Water would come up from under the animal’s body, stirred by the gills, bringing particles that had not yet passed through the thicket. I would open a rim of fine hairs and turn a little of that water inward. Some of what arrived would be usable. Some would have to be put out again.

This sorting would occupy me completely.

I would have no way to take in the hunter as a whole. Its eyes would be beyond my reach, its tail farther away still. But the surface under me would transmit the labor of its limbs. When it walked, my stalk would tilt through six uneven tensions. When it groomed, there would be scraping nearby, sometimes so close that the older growth beneath me bent.

A hooked foot would pass overhead.

I would close.

The foot would withdraw, unable to reach the narrow hollow in which my first attachment had been made. After a while I would open again.

That would be one of the reliable things.

***

I would not always have been fastened.

There would have been a short beginning in which I went through the water with no stalk at all. A moving fringe would have carried me, badly, between much larger movements. The lamp would have brightened one side of me without showing me anything. Warmth and dissolved substances would have differed from one moment to the next.

I might have touched enamel and failed to stay. I might have turned against a leaf’s mineral hairs, too far from its surface to fix myself there.

Then the hunter would have been resting beneath the broad colony.

Water drawn past its gills would have brought me close. In the sheltered strip behind its head, among the remains of other organisms, I would have touched something and continued touching it.

The swimming fringe would have dwindled. The attachment would have thickened. My body would have lifted away from the surface on its own short stalk.

After that, departures would happen underneath me.

***

A feeding strike would empty the neighborhood of open mouths.

The body would fold; all our stalks would contract. We would become low knobs while the water tore past. Whatever the hunter caught would be taken elsewhere, below the blunt head, out of our reach.

But afterward there would sometimes be richness in the current.

Broken material. A soft fragment turning end over end. Substances too dispersed to have an edge.

We would open into it.

I would be one of the taller ones for a while. The old stalks beneath me would let me reach beyond the interference of my neighbors. My hairs would work in relatively clean water, and the body below would keep bringing us to new places.

Under a leaf, the current would arrive in narrow, steady bands. On the open floor it would be less dependable. Near the drain it would draw us all in the same direction, extending the flexible parts of our stalks until a small reverse motion of the hunter relieved the pull.

Those three backward steps would pass through my attachment.

I would feed again before the animal had stopped walking.

***

The shrimp would be enormous at this distance.

Its approach would begin as a disturbance interrupted by touches. Nearby stalks would bend and vanish beneath jointed mouthparts. Material accumulated around their bases would be lifted away. Then a limb would brace beside me, pressing down the whole framework on which I stood.

I would contract into that framework.

The shrimp might remove a neighbor. It might clear the debris that was beginning to obstruct my mouth. These outcomes would arrive through almost the same sequence of pressures.

When it left, the water would pass more freely.

There would be a space beside me where something had been opening and closing for a long time.

Eventually that space would fill.

***

I would grow accustomed to the hunter’s stillness without possessing anything like an expectation of it.

The gills would continue. The service shadow would pass. Fine material would lodge among the dead stalks, and we would lift ourselves a little higher.

Then, during one stillness, the surface beneath us would begin to separate from the body.

At first there would only be an unfamiliar yielding. The small strains that accompanied breathing would weaken. A split would open nearby, letting out a slow wash of substances unlike the usual water.

I would close when the covering buckled.

The hunter would draw forward.

Its movement would pass beneath us and then stop reaching us at all.

We would remain on the abandoned shell, attached as securely as before.

***

For some time, very little would change.

The shell would be caught in the roots. Water would still move across it. Light would still pass over our thicket. My mouth would open; particles would enter; unusable matter would go out.

But the gills would no longer freshen the water from below. The shell would not climb a leaf or recoil from the drain. When debris collected, it would stay collected.

A snail would begin on one of the empty limbs.

Its rasping would travel through the shell in regular, intimate strokes. We would contract together. The scraping would stop, resume, come nearer.

Perhaps I would be removed then.

Perhaps the next turning would free the covering first, and the entire thicket would travel on a transparent scrap through the open middle. Our stalks would lie flat in the rush. The shell would catch beneath a bolt, or enter the drain, or settle with its green strip pressed against the floor.

I would have no means of choosing among these.

Before any of them happened, though, I might divide.

One body would remain on the stalk. The other would grow a swimming fringe and loosen. For a short while it would turn beside me, caught in the small current our mouths helped make.

Then it would get clear.

Beneath the roots, the hunter’s new covering would still be bare. Its jaw hairs would be lifting, one after another. Water would begin to move around its head.

I would stay on the old shell, feeding in the weaker flow.
