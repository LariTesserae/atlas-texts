I would be under the foot, but not directly beneath its weight.

There is a little space along the inner edge where the contact heads stand farther apart. A groove runs back from it into the pad. Powder gathers there during a step, and when the foot settles, the powder is squeezed out in two pale ridges.

I would live behind those ridges.

At first you might find only a loose piece of ceramic, longer than the others, with a dark line down one side. If you touched it, it would withdraw. Not far. There would be nowhere far to go.

I would have come there from the ground.

***

Before the foot, I had a place on the edge of a blister.

It was a good place for a small body. Heat arrived steadily through the rear legs and left through a single upright vane. The front legs could be moved without interrupting this passage. I spent a long time working at a seam that ran diagonally beneath me, widening it grain by grain.

There are still parts of that seam inside my body.

I had begun a second vane when something changed below the stone. The heat did not stop. It entered from another direction.

For a while I could compensate by turning. Then I had to stand with three legs drawn up. The underside of my body, which had always been the warm side, became the cooler one. Internal passages opened in the wrong sequence. I closed them and waited, using what little difference remained between my exposed back and the shelter beneath it.

The blister settled.

I could have stayed there for years, getting smaller in ways that would not have been visible from above.

But a foot had come down nearby.

Its arrival broke my seam. It also warmed the stone between us.

I followed that warmth.

***

The first time the animal lifted me, I was caught in a fastening pit.

I had entered it because one wall was warm and the other was cool. There was a deposit in the bottom that I could cut. My vane fitted through the opening, though only if I leaned it sideways.

Then the whole place moved.

The ground withdrew from my feet. A brightness appeared below me. Every passage in my body tightened at once.

The step took long enough for me to begin fastening myself to the pit.

By the next step, I had made a shallow anchorage. By the next season, the rim had grown over two of my legs.

I do not know whether the animal admitted me. Its tissues closed slowly around what I had built. I rasped them back where I needed room, and they thickened elsewhere. A little of its dark lubricant entered my recess. I sealed the opening through which it came, then later opened a smaller one.

There was nourishment in the wear of the foot: metal from the contact stalks, fragments from the ceramic sleeves, material discarded when a joint relined itself.

My second vane never fully unfolded.

It is still there, doubled against my back. In certain positions it rubs the roof of the groove.

***

I would be small enough that the animal’s entire life reached me as changes in the place where I lived.

A turn would begin as an unequal warmth along the walls.

Preparation for a step would draw fluid away from my underside. The neighboring stalks would stiffen. Farther in, something would pull against a fastening grown around one of my old rear legs.

Long before the foot rose, I would know that my floor was about to disappear.

I have grown a part that reaches down.

It is narrow and jointed, with a flattened end. When the foot stands, I can pass it between the contact heads and rest it on the shale. It bears almost no weight. A bead rolling through its track would be enough to crush it.

Through it I receive the ground.

Not as a map. There are no distances in what comes up that small limb. There is the hard arrival of a knock, followed by its spreading. There are quick, close abrasions. There is a low strain that lasts so long I accommodate it, and notice it only when it ends.

Some sequences bring changes in the foot before their echoes have finished.

Others bring none.

I have come to keep my contact down longer for a few of them.

This may be how I became useful.

***

Inside my recess, two surfaces have grown together.

One belongs to me. The other extends from a sensing stalk whose outer head was broken off long ago. At first, when I moved, the damaged stalk withdrew. Its motion struck my folded vane.

Now the surfaces remain in contact.

A vibration reaching my extended limb passes through my body and into the stalk. The large animal sometimes alters its stance afterward. I feel the alteration in the walls, then in the ground, then in the pressure against my side.

There is no single moment at which I send something.

The contact is always there.

When I scrape mineral from the groove, that reaches the stalk too. When I repair myself, or draw back from excessive heat, or struggle to free a grain lodged under my body, the foot receives whatever of that can pass through the join.

Perhaps much of it is useless.

There are days when the damaged stalk pulls away and I am left with only the stone.

***

I have crossed places the animal has not crossed.

My extended limb can reach a surface too thinly present to support the foot. Sometimes, during evening, it rests on warm roughness a little above the visible ground.

The foot begins to load.

I withdraw.

Once I did not withdraw quickly enough. The end of the limb remained on a slope while the rest of me rose with the foot. It stretched without breaking. For a long interval, heat passed through it from somewhere that was no longer beneath us.

The injury healed crooked.

Now, when I reach straight down, the tip bends toward that vanished slope.

The animal has changed its stance around me. One row of contact heads no longer extends as far as the others. The groove has deepened. I occupy more of the inner edge than I once did.

If you could lift me out intact, you would find six original limbs, a folded vane, the remains of a second one, and several structures whose use would be difficult to guess without the surrounding foot.

I would not be able to stand on the ground.

***

When the pale flakes fall, I feel some of their impacts.

Most are too slight. One strikes the foot before falling through, and the little blow arrives clearly through the roof of my recess.

Later, after we have settled, the finer grinding begins.

It reaches me through the flattened end of my limb: irregular, interrupted, very close. Several cutters working where there had been none.

One of the young enters the powder ridge outside my groove.

It stays there through the cooling of the surface. Its back touches the rim. Occasionally it moves, and the movement loosens a few grains that descend against my side.

The place it has found is not good enough to remain in. By morning, the warmed wall will be too warm. But for now there is a passage of heat through it, and it has begun to cut.

I draw my old front leg farther inward.

The movement opens a narrow space beneath the rim. It also presses my folded vane against the damaged stalk, and somewhere above me the animal makes a small adjustment.

The young one stops rasping.

We remain like that while the last daylight leaves the visible plateau.

Then three knocks come through the stone.

My contact is still down.
