I am drawn first to the solitary workshop in the paved square.

There would be good light there, and room to lay out something larger than a person could hold: a roof frame, perhaps, or the full-sized pattern for a stair. I can imagine a long board with holes bored through it, pegs marking distances, a cord stretched between two points that cannot be seen from the same window. A place for making the scattered parts of the city answer to one another.

But I have put myself too neatly at the center of a useful arrangement. I have given myself the good light and a task whose value is easy to explain.

The workshop can belong to someone else.

There is something more difficult for me in the house above the market: the higher stool, the stockinged foot, the mouth holding its objection. I keep returning to the person whose boot is being repaired.

I think I would be that person.

My foot is very large. More particularly, it is large in ways a boot does not readily accommodate. There is width where the maker expected length, and a prominence below the ankle that makes a sound piece of leather behave like an accusation. The other foot is less troublesome, though the two boots must look sufficiently alike that I do not have to discuss them with strangers.

My objection concerns the opened seam.

I believe she has opened it farther than necessary. There is a difference between making room and making something loose. I am explaining this difference to a woman whose finger is on the exact ridge that hurts me.

She knows where it hurts. She is giving little weight to my account of the leather.

From outside, this may look like a familiar little comedy: the knowledgeable craftsperson and the customer who cannot leave her alone. From my stool it is less tidy. A loose boot on a descending stair is a real difficulty. So is a repair that holds the foot securely by injuring it. We agree about the requirements. I dislike how little that agreement settles.

One of my hands rests on my knee. The other holds a short piece of smooth wood, darkened along an edge by use. I have brought it to show her something. She has not taken it.

That piece of wood gives me more of myself.

I work with patterns, though not in the solitary workshop. My place is at the end of a storehouse bay, partitioned from the rest by a hanging of coarse cloth. There is a low table, a narrow shelf, and an inconvenient stone projection that supports one end of anything long enough to reach it.

People bring me the shapes of things that have to fit other things.

Sometimes they bring the things themselves. A lid with a damaged edge. A hinge whose holes must be transferred to a new frame. Two short lengths of channel that meet badly at a corner. Sometimes they bring a board with marks on it and the firm conviction that the marks are sufficient.

I make thin wooden patterns, notched strips, folded pieces of hide. I can preserve an awkward angle without knowing what it is called. I can take the shape of a socket into a room where the socket is not.

I am less good at explaining why a person’s measurements cannot all be right.

There are several ways of making that explanation disagreeable, and I know most of them. My work is careful. The cloth across the entrance to my bay has a tear beside its fastening that I have secured with an ugly cluster of knots. These facts do not trouble me equally.

Against the wall of the woman’s room is a narrow bundle of my patterns, bound with a strap. They are here because I dislike leaving them in the storehouse when other people are shifting grain. One has a little ear projecting from its side, easy to break and difficult to replace accurately. I have turned the bundle so that the ear faces the wall.

The woman has noticed this. She has also noticed that I set it where her sweeping brush belongs.

She has not made everything she notices into a matter for discussion.

Under the window, light reaches the side of my bare stocking. There is a darn over the smallest toe, paler than the surrounding wool. My heel rests on the stool’s crossbar, and the pressure is comfortable. The crossbar was not placed there for my heel. Its usefulness is exact nevertheless.

I would have a name here, one she uses without ceremony. There would be people who like me and people who prefer to deal with whoever works in the next bay. A child might find my offcuts valuable. I might be ungenerous about one particular kind of offcut because its wood is hard to obtain, and generous about another without recognizing that the child can tell the difference.

I would not know the city as your description knows it.

From this room I cannot see the grazer with its foot above the slab, or the blade at the root, or the person looking toward another doorway. I know the basin from certain places, interrupted by roofs and walls. The market’s low tone comes through the floor. A pale trunk is visible beyond the opposite eave, but not the ground it grows from.

Much of what holds me here would be unavailable to someone looking in.

Why I dislike having my ankle touched. Which member of my household has tried to make me discard the boot. Whether the small polished stick in my hand is a useful gauge or merely the object I chose because an objection feels better with evidence attached.

I do not want to settle all of that. Some of it belongs behind the face.

And I would not be waiting for her needle to move.

My hand holds the gauge. Her hand occupies the boot. The seam is open, the felt is firm in the toe, and the trouble has been located more precisely than I care to concede. There is no approaching instant in which I become gracious about this.

Still, I have brought her the boot. I have taken it off in her room. My other boot stands beside her stool, where she can compare the two.

The higher seat leaves my knees at an easy angle. Through its hollow, one of my stockinged feet is visible from the doorway. It is an undignified view of a person with a skilled occupation and several strongly held opinions.

There is room for it.
