I think I would live at the end of a passage that had not originally been meant to end.

There would be a sealed door behind my wardrobe. On the old plans, the passage would continue through it to a loading platform, but the platform would now belong to a different shelf of rock, slightly higher and farther west. In winter a line of cold would come through the wardrobe and divide my shirts into those I could put on immediately and those I had to warm beneath the quilt.

I would have chosen the room for its window. Not the view—the view would be mostly the underside of a conifer—but the fact that it opened inward. So many things here would require permission, calculation, or a second person. I would like being able to open my own window.

For the first year, I would rarely do it.

***

My work would begin with the soft parts.

Not the hydraulic seals; those belong to people with qualifications I do not have. I would replace the cloth inside instrument cases, stitch the sleeves for keys, fit new padding to the carriers that come over the ridge. I would know how to make a pocket that releases a tool without letting the neighboring tool follow it out.

There would be a sewing machine on a thick cork base, in a room once used for storing cable. Most of its work would be ordinary: cuffs, torn knees, a fastening moved because someone had grown broader or thinner. The unusual commissions would arrive without drawings.

“This needs to stop knocking.”

“I have to be able to open it with one hand.”

“Not that cloth. I can feel it through my teeth.”

I would enjoy being given a problem small enough to turn over on a table.

At first I would pad things too heavily. An instrument would come back because its catch could no longer be felt through the cover. A wrench sleeve would snag at precisely the moment it should have slipped free. Iven would return a bag I had altered, lay it down, and show me how the new division obliged her to unpack everything to reach the spare stocking.

“I put that in last,” I would say.

“Yes,” she would say. “You did.”

I would unpick it after she left, annoyed enough to break the thread twice.

Eventually I would learn to ask people to empty their old bags themselves while I watched.

***

I would not be especially quiet by temperament.

This seems important. I would have a good deal to say about inadequate scissors, and I would sing when working alone. Not beautifully. I would know only the middle portions of several songs, having learned them from someone who had also not known the beginnings.

My particular difficulty would be the final inch of setting something down.

I could carry a tray carefully across a room, negotiate a door with my elbow, avoid a chair, and then surrender its weight too soon. Nothing disastrous would happen. That would almost make it worse. I would be left with the small sound and no clear measure of what it had cost.

For a while I would set everything on folded cloths. Cloths would spread across my room, colonizing the sill, the floor beside the bed, the little shelf where I kept my cup.

Then one morning I would find myself holding a parcel of bread above a cushion and laugh.

There would still be cloth beneath the cup. I would remove the one beneath the bread.

***

The seam-runners would interest me before the people did.

I would notice their use of felt and assume that, as the person with the scraps, I could improve the arrangement. I would sort pieces by thickness and put the best ones in a shallow box under the stair.

The animals would take the dirty trimmings from the sack beside it.

I would move those into the box.

They would investigate the sack again.

After that I would leave both where they were and make no further contribution beyond keeping synthetic scraps separate.

One evening, working late, I would see a seam-runner pass the open workroom door with a short piece of blue thread attached to its hind foot. I would spend several minutes deciding whether intervention was necessary. While I decided, it would sit down, remove the thread, and continue beneath a cabinet.

The thread would remain there for weeks. It would be behind a pipe, difficult to reach, and I would dislike how much it bothered me.

***

Sena would come to my room because I had scissors that cut well.

She would want to make something the adults would initially mistake for a kite.

“It hasn’t got a string,” she would say.

There would be drawings: ribs made from thin packing slats, scraps of lining stretched between them, a nose weighted with a little wad of clay. She would intend to launch it in the jumping room. It would be a glider, and the question of whether she was allowed a glider would turn out to be separate from the question of whether she was allowed to climb onto the wall bars to launch it.

I would agree to help with the fabric and find myself, three evenings later, lying on the suspended floor while she stood over me explaining why my stitching had made one wing shorter.

Our first successful flight would cross almost the whole room.

Our second would strike me in the ear.

For several weeks afterward, I would occasionally find myself thinking about the glider during work: its slight turn to the left, the way it corrected just before landing. I would want to make another one. Not a better one for Sena. One of my own.

This would embarrass me more than I expected.

I would make it anyway, using yellow cloth saved from a damaged carrier. It would fly badly until Sena altered the tail.

***

In time, I would be asked to carry repaired cases back to the instruments they fitted.

This would get me out among the shelves.

I would discover that knowing a place from its maintenance labels is not the same as knowing how to reach it. A housing marked N-14 might be visible from N-13 and require twenty minutes of walking. I would arrive with needles tucked into a closed book, thread in a tin lined with flannel, and the wrong dimensions because I had copied a seven as a one.

There would be a particular turn where I always stopped.

Not because it was dangerous. From there I could see a strip of the southern ridge above the roofs. Sometimes the supply carriers would be moving along it, small enough to cover with a thumbnail.

I would have people outside. Someone who sent long letters in a cramped hand. Someone who would eventually stop asking whether I planned to stay another year and begin asking when I was coming to visit.

I would dislike the assumption, then realize it had become reasonable.

In my second or third winter, I would leave for a month.

My room would not be reassigned. A resident would agree to check it for leaks, and I would give unnecessarily detailed instructions about the window. I would travel down to a town where trucks crossed the street outside my lodging before dawn.

For the first few nights, every passing truck would wake me.

Later, I would sleep through them. I would buy fruit without considering its place in a carrier. I would see my people, quarrel with one of them, stay an extra day to finish the quarrel properly. On the way back I would bring a small tin of bright buttons that nobody in the valley needed.

***

So I would be a person there. Not one who understood the rock particularly well, and not one whose arrival changed much.

After some years there would be recognizable work of mine in all three buildings. A crooked early seam in a frequently used bag. Yellow lining inside a case whose owner had objected to yellow and then admitted that dropped screws were easier to see against it. A soft handle made thicker on one side to fit a damaged hand.

My own room would remain unfinished. The wardrobe would stand a little away from the sealed door, and things would fall behind it. I would keep meaning to make a narrow padded tool for retrieving them.

On a clear morning I would open the inward-swinging window.

The conifer would be close enough that one branch entered the room, carrying several pearly leaves caught from a plant above. I would lift them out and put them on the outer sill. Below, someone would be taking the covers off the instruments. A lamp would come on over a clapperless bell.

There would be a knock at my door.

I would say, “Come in,” with a mouthful of bread, and move the shirts off the chair.
