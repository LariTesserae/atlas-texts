I think I would live in a room behind the broken mill, where the sound of the wheel comes through the sleeping shelf.

There would be another entrance, less convenient, so that reaching my door did not depend on crossing the mill’s open channel. In the morning I would use the short way. Returning in the afternoon, I would sometimes stand across the water and look at my own doorway, close enough to toss a wedge into, before beginning the long walk around.

I would have lived there long enough to find this irritating rather than remarkable.

The room would contain more bowls than one person needs. Most would be chipped. A few would nest together, but badly, and I would keep putting them into arrangements that promised to take up less space and did not. Beneath the sleeping shelf, in the coolest part of the floor channel, there would be a slab of food varnish with one excellent purple margin and a large, persistently unproductive center.

I would turn it too often. Someone from the neighboring court would have told me this.

My outer plates would be pale except at the wrists, where handling wet fiber had worn them thin. There the darker material would show, especially just after drinking. One of my feet would close more strongly than the other. On difficult ground I would turn slightly sideways, offering the stronger foot to the uncertain direction, without thinking of it as an accommodation.

I would like the hour before the wheel began to turn. The lanes would be filling, but the paddles would still be motionless. Small bubbles would gather beneath the lowest blade. Then one would escape, another, and the whole wheel would shift just far enough to release the water held in its chipped edge.

That first spill could be heard only from my room.

I would generally be awake for it.

***

My work, at least at first, would be fiber.

There are sheltered seams where it can be lifted in damp bundles, each strand too short and brittle to do much alone. I would gather it with a blunt tool, keeping the bundles in water until they could be separated. The work would leave little bright needles in the softer creases of my hands. At evening I would pick these out under the blue light of the doorway.

New braid would look poor beside the old: bulky, uneven, with ends standing out wherever one bundle had been worked into the next. Only use would make it compact and glossy. I would be able to recognize my older lengths by mistakes no one else noticed.

People would bring me handlines before they broke, or after they broke, or considerably after they should have broken.

There would be arguments about the last kind.

A person who had crossed safely on a frayed line six times would present those crossings as evidence. I would spread the damaged braid on the work slab and part it with two fingers. They would look at the exposed strands, then explain the crossings again.

I would sometimes lose patience. My voice would become audible through the wall. In the next room, someone would tap once against the stone: enough.

The young would take my discarded knots. They would tie pebbles together and discover that two objects moving under different pulls make a more troublesome toy than one. I would confiscate a loop from a child’s wrist and spend much longer undoing it than either of us expected.

For several days afterward, that child would avoid my doorway.

Then it would appear with a bowl containing a single loose needle of fiber, as though returning something valuable.

***

Making the lines would eventually mean going to see where they were used.

A braid that failed at a handhole was not always a badly made braid. The inner edge of the hole might have flaked. Water might have left a crust sharp enough to cut it. Someone might have threaded it in a way that pinched one side whenever the pull changed.

So I would carry a smoothing stone and a little dish of powder. I would learn the routes in sections: the three holds above the northern spill, the low passage where one must put a hand behind one’s back, the exposed paving beyond the empty shelter.

I would not be especially good at reading the grooves.

Others would touch a standing stone, watch the water once, and set out. I would pour again. Sometimes I would remain beside the bowl until the favorable interval had nearly passed, then have to wait through the whole change.

This would embarrass me more when I was younger.

The door-maker would find me waiting one afternoon, my bundle of braid held against my chest. It would study the groove and put a hand against the standing face.

We would wait together.

Only much later would I discover that it had already crossed the route that day and knew it was safe. At the time I would think it uncertain too, and the company would make waiting easier.

***

There would be a stretch of path I could not stop thinking about.

It would pass beneath a scarp, on the way to three shelters no longer occupied. The handline there would wear quickly. I would replace it, inspect the holes, smooth their edges, and return a few shifts later to find the outer strands loosened again.

At first I would blame the braid.

I would take a length home and hang it across my work slab, studying the place where it had opened. The damage would be broad, not cut. Several strands would have been pulled out of the body of the line without breaking.

Eventually I would go there with someone heavier than myself.

We would take turns holding the line while the other watched. Water would gather along the scarp. The pebbles beneath our feet would give their first small rush toward the wall. Nothing unusual would happen.

My companion would want to leave.

I would ask for a little longer.

Then the line would draw tight between two handholes while both our hands were resting on stone.

Not violently. It would lift from the face of the rock and straighten, shedding drops. In the next span it would hang loose.

We would get down on all fours. My companion would press a palm between the holes. I would do the same farther along. Beneath my body, the dense fluid would shift in a direction that made me want to turn over, though turning over offered no relief.

We would retreat by the route we had just tested.

For a while I would be convinced that something immense had changed and that I had noticed it first. Then an older inhabitant would come out with us, feel the wall, and point to a row of plugged holes above the present ones.

There had been another line there.

Perhaps the difficulty had returned. Perhaps it had never entirely gone away. No one available to ask would remember using the higher route.

I would feel foolish, then annoyed at feeling foolish. The damaged braid would still be on my slab. It would still need an answer.

We would leave two short lines where there had been one continuous length, so that neither crossed the place where the pull divided. To pass between them, a traveler would have to rest both hands in an old grinding hollow.

I would go back often to inspect it.

This, more than the making of braid, would become my work: returning to places after the repair seemed finished.

***

I would not visit only dangerous places.

Beyond the northern court there would be a narrow shelf where water ran under a translucent layer of stone. In certain light the current could be seen carrying grit beneath my fingers. I would stop there even when no work required it.

I would have a friend who spoke too softly to be understood near the wheel. We would meet in an empty cistern, sitting on opposite sides with our feet in the same shallow water. My friend would have a way of imitating the door-maker’s smallest disapproving movement that made my flank plates lift before I could prevent it.

There would be a bowl I disliked lending.

It would fit beneath my lower face without touching either cheek, and its rim would be thin enough to let me drink without tilting my head. I would lend other bowls readily. When someone reached for that one, my hand would move before I had decided what excuse to give.

And there would be evenings when I did not want anyone leaning against me after a meal. I would go to the unused entrance and sit with my shoulders clear of both walls, listening to the last irregular drops from the mill.

***

If you came through the court, I might first notice your hands.

You would keep them free when walking, even on wet stone. On reaching a shelter, you would touch the doorway only as you passed through it, as though the important thing were entirely inside.

I would give you something to carry that required both hands, then reconsider and take it back.

Instead, I would lead you to the low passage near my room. There the paving is sound, the roof close, and the pull seldom changes enough to dislodge anything larger than grit. I would show you how to spread your fingers against the wall without locking the joints.

You would probably press too hard.

I would loosen your hand, one finger at a time.

When the grinding began below us, we would already be sitting. The bowl between us would tremble, making a bright, broken ring of reflected light beneath the roof. I would move it away from your knee.

I would not know what was grinding. I would have opinions, inherited explanations, objections to other people’s explanations. With you there, I might become newly conscious of how little those amounted to.

For those few minutes I would keep my palm on the paving.

Afterward, I would take down a damp bundle and begin separating the fibers. You could watch, or help if your fingers were suited to it. There would be enough light left to make a short length.

Before you left, I would want to know which way you meant to go.

Not where you hoped to arrive. I might have no understanding of that country, or even of the sort of place you were naming.

I would mean the first crossing outside my door.

I would mean whether you planned to take the short way.
