I would be under the ledge, where the grooves continue beyond the reach of a comfortable arm.

To read the oldest ones, I have to turn one of my small hands backward and press my face against the floor. There is grit in the moist folds of my mouth. The stone is warm enough that I cannot remain there long, and the reader waiting behind me keeps touching my hind foot.

I have already told them there is another line.

They have already told me that they know.

What interests me is a shallow cut beneath a warning about water. It may belong to the warning. It may be the last part of a much older direction. One edge has broken away, so that what remains could mean a crossing short enough for a carried child, or a place where a child should not be put down.

This seems worth being uncomfortable for.

But I would not live entirely among the grooves. After a while, my hand would begin reporting its own numbness instead of the stone. I would back out, swallow the grit because the moisture is worth more than the discomfort, and find that everyone else had eaten.

Someone would have kept a portion beneath a flat chip.

Not the person touching my foot. They are irritated with me.

Someone else.

***

I think I would be a keeper with one imperfect sensing pad.

There is a pale band through its center, the remnant of a burn received when I was young enough to mistake an interesting place for a safe one. The pad still detects pressure well. It detects some changes in current poorly, and others with an unpleasant intensity. I have learned to place my hands farther apart than most people do, then exchange their positions.

This makes my examinations slow.

It also makes me good at noticing when two apparently different signals are coming from my own body.

That is useful work, but not sufficiently useful to excuse everything. I sometimes keep testing after a decision has to be made. A companion has developed a particular wrist movement for telling me that the water is going somewhere whether or not I have finished understanding it.

The movement has entered my name.

Names accumulate things one would not have chosen.

Mine begins with two close pressures, the second lighter than the first. That part is old. It was made against me when I was small enough to sleep with my whole weight supported beneath another body. Later comes a short drag across the inside of the wrist, then a pause which strangers tend to hold too long.

Among my companions, there is sometimes an extra touch at the end.

Depending on how it is given, it means that I have been thorough, or that I have become intolerable.

I can usually tell.

***

My work would be with the things carried between settlements.

Not all of them. I would be bad at keeping count of sharp stones. I would leave a serviceable digging flake beside a seep, return for it, and find that someone had incorporated it into a little wall. But I would know the culture fragments by the feel of their dry edges against my mouth.

One has a narrow cavity that shelters a pale film beneath a black one. Another appears dead until it receives water that has passed through a particular rusty growth. A third smells wrong for several nights before it becomes useful.

I carry that one separately. Nobody enjoys traveling beside it.

There would be a small, heavy piece I had carried for years without successfully growing anything on it.

I would have acquired it from an old keeper at a grove that no longer appears in reliable directions. They did not tell me it was precious. They were sorting their burdens, and I had offered to take something. They placed several fragments between us. I chose the heavy one because its pits went deep.

I have since tried it at nine seeps.

At two, something grew on the surface and died. At another, it developed a translucent skin that could have come from the water rather than the stone. Once, during a crossing, I nearly discarded it.

Instead I ate less, so there would be room for it in the pouch.

This is not necessarily admirable. My companions carry things for reasons just as doubtful. One keeps a door-wedge from a shelter where we cannot return. Another has a smooth bit of grazer tooth which serves no purpose I have ever discovered. When I ask, they give me the beginning of somebody’s name.

I stop asking.

***

I would not be the first onto the newly warming ridge.

By the time I arrived, others would have found the nursery slab. I would test it anyway, earning a brief, offended touch from the keeper who had chosen it.

Then I would help carry damp leaves.

There would be work I wanted to do and work that needed doing first. One of the supporting stones would rock under pressure. A child would have pushed a porous fragment into an exit gap and become furious when it was removed. Someone would need to go back down the slope for a load abandoned by a traveler whose carrying pouch had split.

I would go for the load.

Alone on the return journey, I would pause longer than necessary where the ground was bare and evenly warm.

With all six limbs resting on stone, there would be a little while in which I did not have to separate one thing from another. The slow current beneath me, the cooling air against the joins of my shields, the weight tucked close to my belly: none of it requiring an account.

A grazer would pass close enough to smell.

I would remain still until it had gone.

Then I would lift the load again.

***

At the old shelter, I would be the second keeper called to examine the dark spot.

The first would give me their wrist before letting me touch the stone. Through them I would learn where the fragment had been found, how long it had lain damp, what had changed since the last examination. Their contractions would be restrained but quick.

They want me to confirm it.

I want to confirm it too.

Under my good pad, the wet stone would feel like wet stone. I would move my hand clear and lower my mouth. There would be salt, iron, the faint sourness of a living film nearby. Nothing I could confidently assign to the spot.

I would touch the keeper again and ask to wait.

It is an unwelcome answer. We have both given it often enough to recognize how easily caution can become a way of contributing nothing.

So I would fetch a clean fragment and set it beside the old one. The same water would pass over both. We would cover them separately, with shards that left room for our hands.

Before sleep, I would check them.

After sleep, I would check them.

The clean fragment would darken too.

For a little while I would be ashamed of my disappointment. Then I would be occupied by it. We would shift the two stones higher, farther apart, turn them, taste the water above and below. My companion would move more roughly than usual. Once they would catch my small hand and hold it still: enough.

We would leave the stones alone for a night.

On the next examination, there would be something soft inside one of the old fragment’s pits.

I would not scrape it.

That would be difficult.

***

There would be a child who liked my damaged hand.

Children are expected to learn from a good pad, so mine would have the attraction of an unsuitable tool. They would press their wrist to it and try to discover which sensations failed to arrive.

Sometimes they would imitate the little pause I make before changing hands.

The first time, I would mistake this for concentration. The second time, a keeper beside us would contract with laughter, shaking grit from a seam in their back.

After that I would make the pause extravagantly long.

The child would wait, trembling with the effort not to move.

This would become a game played badly during lessons and well when everyone should be settling down.

I might not be their parent. I would know the weight of them nonetheless: where they hooked their rear limbs when carried, how they shifted before waking, which contact soothed them and which made them struggle. There would be nights when they slept against another adult and I found myself waking because the expected pressure was absent.

One night they would crawl onto my back while I was examining a groove.

I would let them stay there until their warmth became distracting.

Then longer.

***

Eventually I would make a mark on the ridge.

Not immediately, and not where a traveler’s hand would first arrive. The earliest marks would concern the shelter, the dependable approach, the hollow where the air had collected badly during the seep’s first opening. Mine would be farther underneath.

It would describe the old culture stone’s position when growth returned.

I would want to include the height above the water, the covering shard, the delay, the fact that the neighboring fragment had darkened without developing the same soft growth. There would not be a convenient way to fit all of that into the available stone.

Another keeper would examine my unfinished grooves and give an exasperated squeeze.

Together we would make them simpler.

Something would be lost. I would remain aware of it whenever I passed my hand over the finished account.

Below, in a place still smooth enough to receive a clear cut, I would leave room for a correction.

***

That is where I find myself most fully: not alone beneath the oldest writing, but outside a low shelter with my head still dusty from reading it.

My back carries grains from places I remember chiefly through the effort of crossing them. There is a stone in my pouch that may never wake. One hand hurts in certain currents. Inside the shelter, someone has saved food for me and is becoming annoyed that I have not come to eat it.

I am checking the thin shard over the recovered film.

Water has begun escaping along one side. I push a little sediment into the gap, then clear part of it away. Too much restraint will leave the growth in brine thick enough to kill it.

From beneath the nursery roof comes a tap.

I answer against the edge of the slab without looking up.

Two taps follow, then a scraping pressure: the beginning of my name, attempted through the wrong surface.

It does not travel clearly. I know what was meant because I know who is there.

I settle lower, placing my good wrist against the stone.

This time I answer slowly enough to be felt.
