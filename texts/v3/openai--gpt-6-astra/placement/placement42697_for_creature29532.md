I would come in with something caught on my sleeve.

A thorn, at first. Then, after I had worked myself free and stood up too quickly beneath the ledge, a strand of ivy would remain hooked through the wool. I would carry it several paces into the hollow without noticing. Its leaves would turn against my wrist when the air moved.

I think I would be someone accustomed to working outdoors, but not particularly accustomed to the sea. Someone employed to keep the drainage cuts above the bank open. I would know the weight of wet clay on a spade and where the pasture stayed soft after the rest had dried. On most days I would eat sitting on an overturned bucket, with my hands still dirty.

The hollow would be somewhere I found while following water that had stopped going where it ought to go.

At first I would see a place to get out of the rain. Then an animal. Then the hand.

I would be frightened when the timber resolved into two things.

There would be a considerable interval during which I did nothing useful. My back would press against the entrance ledge. I would try to decide whether its eyes were eyes. The ivy caught at my wrist would move, and I would pull it off too sharply, leaving a leaf behind.

Only after it raised its head would I notice that I was standing in the light.

Moving aside would be the first thing I got right.

*

I have a piece of bread in my pocket. I take it out, then put it back. I have no reason to suppose that bread is wanted.

The floor looks clean in a way I do not understand. Not swept, exactly. I keep my boots near the wall.

Eventually I sit on one of the three stones.

From there, the timber is farther away than I expected. I have to lean forward awkwardly to reach it. My fingers come down first, then the heel of my hand. The wood is cold enough that I feel its ridges with unusual distinctness.

Nothing happens.

I begin to withdraw.

Then one of its fingers rises.

I leave my hand where it is.

This would be the beginning, though I would not yet know what had begun. I might mistake that movement for a request for food. I might suppose it had been asleep. On the way home I would try to imitate the joint in its finger with my own and find that I could not.

I would tell no one that evening. Not because I had decided to keep a secret. There would simply be no satisfactory first sentence, and by the time I had washed and changed my socks, the ordinary questions would have started.

Was the lower cut running?

Would I need the cart tomorrow?

Yes. Probably.

*

What I become takes longer.

I bring an apple because it is what I have. I cut it with the knife I use for roots, wipe the blade more carefully than usual, and leave the pieces within reach. It does not eat while I am there.

Next time, the flesh is gone. The skin has been folded against the grit.

I bring water in a jar. The stone dish holds less than I expect, and I spill some over its edge. A dark patch spreads beneath it. For several visits I can still see where I poured too quickly.

My presence begins to have marks.

A scrape beside the footing. Wool on the thorn. A place on the timber where the ridges grow darker under my palm.

I am not altogether pleased by this. I had imagined, without admitting it, that I could come and go leaving only improvements.

Then, one afternoon, I find an apple pip pressed into the soft seam of my usual stone. It could have fallen there. I spend too much of that visit looking at it.

I begin to learn how little I know.

*

I would not move into the hollow. I would have a room elsewhere, boots drying badly by a door, obligations that sometimes kept me away. In winter I would arrive after the light had already gone from the entrance and have to feel for the timber.

My hand would not always mean the same thing.

Some days it would be cold and held stiffly. Some days I would have worked with the spade until the thumb would not lie flat. Once there would be a bandage. I would feel the creature’s finger move close to it, stop, and settle on the wood again.

I would come to recognize that finger without being able to say much about its movements.

A lift.

A drag across the grain.

Pressure held until my own hand began to ache.

There would be visits when it gave no answer. I would still have to go home.

The difficulty would not be waiting in the hollow. It would be going about the next day without turning every silence into a message.

*

The timber would concern me.

I would know enough about old wood to see what was happening underneath. Once, testing a softened place with my thumbnail, I would lift a little of it away. The creature’s hand would tighten.

I would stop.

For weeks I would look at sound pieces of timber wherever I found them: a gate rail, an ash limb, a plank at the back of a shed. None would seem suitable. New wood would carry a touch differently. I could not explain replacement to it, or ask whether replacement was what it wanted.

In the end I would bring a narrow offcut and place it alongside the old length, touching neither.

A poor beginning, perhaps. Something available.

After a while I would rest my other hand on it.

*

So I would be a person with mud on the backs of my trousers, sitting where a bench had been.

Not one of the old visitors returned. I would never know their habits. Two touches might mean nothing to me until, by accident, I made them and felt an answer arrive with unexpected speed.

I would have to learn this particular creature with this particular hand.

And it would have to learn someone who sometimes hurried, who shifted when the stone grew painful, who brought an apple too hard to eat and took it away again.

As the days shortened, I would move my seat a little closer to the entrance so that less of me stood between its eyes and the remaining light. I would have farther to reach. The position would make my shoulder sore.

One evening, while I was deciding whether to change hands, its fingers would leave their accustomed place.

They would settle farther along the timber.

Nearer mine.

I would notice the small clean spaces they had uncovered, and the grit caught beneath the newly placed fingertips. Then I would ease my shoulder down.

Outside, rain would be finding the cut I had cleared that morning. Here, under both our hands, the wood would remain dry.
