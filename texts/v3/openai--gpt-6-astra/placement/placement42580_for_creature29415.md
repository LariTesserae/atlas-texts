I would be a little way beyond the leaves, resting before the last crossing.

Not far enough away to be lost. Near enough to hear the household moving, though the connected wood would make it difficult to tell who was outside and who had climbed onto the roof. I would have something to bring home: food, certainly, but also a length of pale fiber wrapped around itself to keep it from taking a shape too soon.

My front wrists would be against the root. One of my small hands would be occupied with a sore place beneath a nail.

I would know there was someone unfamiliar at the entrance. The household had made the contacts for that. I would not yet know whether to hurry.

***

I think I would be an adult who had lived in several chambers, and who still sometimes reached for things where they had been kept in a previous one.

In the dark, the hand goes to the old niche.

There is only wall. Or another sleeper’s shoulder.

I would have a place on the rim near a doorway, not the best place, because the fine rain occasionally came through and because anyone leaving before the light changed would have to step over my hind limbs. I would complain about this without agreeing to move. The air there would carry news from outside: opened fruit, an animal’s disturbed coat, the mineral smell of water rising from a level we seldom visited.

Something I had planted would grow beside my head. It would not be impressive. A small edible leaf, difficult to establish, yielding less than the space it occupied seemed to warrant.

One of the others would occasionally suggest replacing it.

I would understand the argument. I would have made the same argument about somebody else’s planting.

Still, when a new shoot emerged, I would guide it away from the place where a sleeping shoulder might crush it.

***

There would be work I was good at.

Not hearing farther than everyone else. Not understanding the great movements of the upland. I would be better at finding where a small trouble had begun.

A wall niche that no longer dried. A sleeping hollow that had become uncomfortable without visibly changing. The first sourness in a bundle of stored leaves.

I would put a hand beneath things.

Water can travel along the underside of a fiber for a surprising distance before it falls. The wet patch is not necessarily below the leak. I would follow such threads patiently, moving my face close enough for the short hairs around my mouth to touch the surface. Sometimes I would find a folded leaf above the roof, or a vine that someone had guided across a drainage channel years earlier.

Sometimes all I would find would be more wet wood.

The household would value this work most when it succeeded and regard my hours of looking as a peculiar habit when it did not.

I would have caused a few of the troubles myself.

A stem I trained toward the light would eventually begin pulling on the wrong part of the roof. I would resist this conclusion longer than I should. Someone younger would show me, placing my wrist first against the stem and then against the wall, waiting for the same faint strain to pass through both.

We would loosen my old bindings together.

***

The pale fiber would be for a map, but I would not be its keeper in any permanent sense.

The frame would pass among us. It would lie across knees, hang from a wall, disappear for a while beneath things laid down hurriedly. A young one would be forbidden to pull the knots and would pull them whenever nobody was watching.

I would like working on it after eating, with my walking limbs folded and the fine hands free.

My habit would be to leave too much space around uncertain places.

This would irritate a companion who traveled farther than I did. They would take the frame from me and draw two crossings closer together.

“You can smell the second from the first,” they would tell me, with their fingers as much as their voice.

But I would remember arriving there during heavy dripping, when the familiar smell had vanished and I had taken the wrong opening. I would move the crossings apart again after they went to sleep.

Neither arrangement would be entirely useful.

A map held by one of us would require conversation before another could safely carry it away.

***

There is a chamber I would have stopped visiting.

I can feel the temptation to make it a place of catastrophe: the sudden break, the voices beneath the floor. But I think my absence would have a smaller beginning.

The route would become inconvenient. The person I went there to see would move elsewhere. Once, carrying food, I would turn back because a crossing smelled of a predator. On the next journey I would have an infant against my breast and choose a nearer shelter.

Several long changes in the rain would pass.

When I returned, the room would still be there.

The entrance would admit my head and shoulders, but not my chest without effort. Inside, the old rim would slope toward a pool. Small swimmers would move through the hollow where I had once slept.

I would look for a mark I remembered making in the bark. Nothing important: a place worn by the end of a seed case I used to push into a crevice.

Finding it would disturb me more than finding the water.

I would reach inside with one small hand. The mark would be below the surface, softened but recognizable.

Perhaps I would stay too long. On the return journey the dimming would catch me at an unfamiliar crossing, and I would have to sleep above the path with all four walking hands gripping wood.

At home I would describe the state of the route carefully.

I might not mention putting my hand into the room.

***

I would not be solemn all the time.

There would be someone whose approaching footsteps made me conceal a piece of particularly good fruit, although I would usually give them half when they arrived. We would have an old dispute about whether they could detect the smell before entering.

I would enjoy removing parasites from one particular grazer because it lifted its coat in the right places. Another would lean on me. I would avoid that one, and the others would call me from the doorway whenever it appeared.

An infant would once have urinated over the map while I was holding it.

For a long time afterward, whenever I objected to a change in the routes, someone would make the small sound associated with that event.

I would find this funny less often than they did.

***

So the one resting beyond the leaves would be returning from a modest errand that had taken longer than expected.

The food would be folded into a carrying leaf. The fiber would have caught twice on the way home. I would have stopped to free it carefully, and then stopped again because I could feel the beginnings of trembling in my flanks.

Through the root would come the household’s answer.

There would be an extra disturbance beside it: pressure without the familiar distribution of hands and feet. A body holding itself over too few points.

I would shift my grip.

Those would be the uneven contacts you felt.

When I came through the leaves, I would first look at the hand being held against the root. Then at your legs. Then at the face of the one beside you, waiting for whatever they had already learned.

I would set down the food before approaching. The fiber I would keep.

You might take this for caution, or attachment to a possession. In fact, if laid on the wet path, it would begin absorbing water, and I had carried it a considerable distance.

There would be no quick way to explain any of this.

I would settle where the root broadened, leaving room for you to lower yourself. One of my small hands would reach back for the food. The other would remain beneath my throat, holding the pale coil.

While we ate, I would watch how you balanced.

Eventually I would offer you the loose end of the fiber—not as a greeting, nor to give you a task whose meaning you could not know. Simply because it was beginning to tangle, and you had a free hand.

I would show you how gently it needed to be held.

Then, with the length supported between us, I could start working it loose.
