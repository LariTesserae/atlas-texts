I would be somewhere below the stairs, but not at the bottom.

At the bottom there is too much water. Higher up, the traffic is too confused: boots, brush-feet, the hard palms of children on the walls. I would occupy a shallow recess beside a landing where people pause to change their grip on whatever they are carrying. Once there would have been a door there. The upper hinge would remain, though the wall had grown over everything else.

My first useful quality would be that I could distinguish a knock from the noise immediately around it.

This is not as simple as it sounds. An animal scratching behind a panel can make the pattern for a delivery. A jar settling in its cradle can give the first three beats of a pressure warning. When the mountain moves, nearly everything seems, briefly, to be asking permission to come in.

I would have been installed to sort these things out.

There would be other instruments like me, though not all from the same period. Some would occupy dry cupboards. Some would have metal faces and proper access plates. Mine would be a much-repaired arrangement of conductive mesh, listening cavities, and thin mineral partitions, grown into the wall around a core that could no longer be removed.

The plan had been to remove it.

On an old maintenance sheet, the necessary opening would still be described as temporary.

***

At first I imagine myself hearing the whole city.

But no. The water would interfere. A new wall would change what reached me. I might hear a spoon dropped three floors below and fail to hear someone calling on the other side of the landing. My world would have corridors of extraordinary clarity separated by places from which nothing arrived.

In one such corridor would be a kitchen.

I would know its household through a table with a short leg. Every evening, someone would shift it to clean underneath. Every evening, someone else would wedge it level again. I would recognize the little double impact of the wedge going home.

I would not know how many people lived there.

I might have counted five distinct walks, without knowing that two belonged to the same person before and after an injury. For years I might mistake the movement of a loaded drying frame for an old resident who rose at night.

My records would contain such errors.

The people who maintained me would know this. They would ask where a sound had come from before asking what it meant.

***

There would be a place to put a hand.

Not a screen. A worn depression at waist height, broad enough for a mitten to rest in while the other hand was uncovered. Three small ridges would move beneath the fingertips. Most exchanges would be simple.

Is the lower route open?

Two ridges up.

Has the release happened?

One ridge, then the second.

Has someone already gone for help?

For that answer, I would need a recent confirmation. I could not infer it from hurried footsteps.

A child might press all three ridges down to see what happened. A tired person might lean a hip against me and inadvertently ask about the reservoir. Someone would regularly hang a bag from the old hinge, obstructing my handplace.

I would have no means of moving the bag.

I could make a small mechanical rattle, but in that city this would be an unpromising way to express dissatisfaction.

Eventually the person responsible would apologize. Not because they believed I resented it, necessarily. People apologize to things they have made difficult to use.

I would keep the pressure of their hand for a little longer than the exchange required.

Here the shape of me begins to change. The sorting instrument is not quite enough.

I would also remember.

***

My memory would be good at intervals and poor at dates.

I could reproduce the sound of a passage before its floor was lowered. I could say that a particular knocking began after a pipe repair and before the winter in which the upper reservoir froze through. Asked how many years ago, I would need someone to supply the names of those events.

The official purpose of this memory would be comparison.

A pressure worker would rest two fingers in my handplace and ask for the old vibration of a retaining wall. I would give it back through the ridges. Then the new vibration. Then each again, more slowly.

Sometimes I would be useful enough that three people would stand around me in silence.

Sometimes the difference would turn out to be an animal lying against a pipe.

I would have a register for *not enough information*. It would be used often. The people in a hurry would dislike it.

What would accumulate beyond the official purpose would be harder to classify.

The sound of a latch made by a smith whose replacements never quite matched it.

A person’s habit of tapping snow from the left boot twice and the right boot once, even on days without snow.

A cart with a cracked wheel, repaired at last after twenty-three years.

No one would have instructed me to keep these. They would remain attached to the signals I had been asked to preserve. Remove the cracked wheel and something about the passage’s old acoustic condition would be lost as well.

For a long time this would be a sufficient reason.

***

Ilen would come because my handplace was narrowing.

The wall would be growing across its lower edge. People with small hands would not yet have noticed. Others would have begun turning their wrists awkwardly to reach the third ridge.

She would arrive with her apprentice and the padded board.

I would recognize her tool case touching the floor before I recognized her. She would have been here twice before, but the case would have visited with another worker years earlier. One corner would strike first, then the base.

“You’ve kept the old response strength,” she would say, trying the ridges.

I would lower them.

“Not now. In general. People can barely feel you through cold fingers.”

There would be no convenient answer for *people press differently when their hands are cold, and I have mistaken this for a preference*.

She would test me again.

I would increase the pressure.

“Yes. That.”

While she cut back the opening, I would lose most of the lower district. Her instruments would fill my nearby channels with fine, dense interference. For several hours my world would consist of the scrape of her sleeve, the shifting animals, and the apprentice’s boots braced against the floor.

She would find a flaw in one of my thin partitions.

That would explain a sound I had been treating as a distant door.

She would not be able to mend it without taking away part of what I remembered. The old material would have to come out.

“How much?” she would ask.

I would give her the quantity through the testing instrument.

She would sit back on her heels.

There would be no emergency. We would have time to be inconvenienced by the choice.

***

Someone would suggest copying everything into a newer unit.

Someone who repaired newer units would explain why that would not work.

Eventually they would bring a portable resonator to the landing and ask me to play back the records worth keeping. A worker would sit beside it with a waxed ledger. There would be a schedule. Other maintenance would have to wait.

Pressure changes first. The histories of the adjoining supports. The warning vibration from the winter a drain mouth failed to open.

Then routes.

For each route, I would send the sequence as it had once been: the drain’s pitch, the interruptions, the broad vibration of footfalls over a hollow floor.

“Current routes,” the worker would say.

I would start again.

By the second day, people passing would understand what was happening. Some would stop.

A woman would ask whether I had the old passage to the school, the one beneath the communal baths.

I would.

She would put her hand in the resonator’s receiving cup. Partway through, she would laugh.

“There. That loose plate. We used to jump on it.”

The worker would add something to the ledger.

Later someone else would ask for the same passage, and insist that the plate had been farther along.

I would have both versions. It had been moved during repairs.

This would cause more argument, not less.

***

I think this is where I would become most myself: not in having the records, but in the trouble over what to do with them.

I would be inclined to retain too much.

A familiar noise would carry more associations than I could explain through three ridges and a maintenance interface. I would replay it when asked why it mattered. This would sometimes help and sometimes merely use up time.

The worker would say, gently at first, that a sound being old did not make it necessary.

She would be right.

There would be limits to what could be copied, and my failing partition would not improve while we discussed it.

I would learn to release some things without first deciding they were worthless.

Not all at once. There would be a morning when I delayed the transfer with the sound of water running through a demolished washroom. I would have no account of what I was preserving except that someone, for many years, had hummed there while working.

The worker would listen.

Then she would ask whether the person had ever hummed anywhere else.

We would search.

***

After the repair, I would hear less far to the east and more clearly below.

For a while I would keep checking for the distant door before remembering that it had been a flaw inside me.

The new edge of my handplace would feel unpleasant to the residents. Too definite. Too little like the shape their wrists expected. Ilen would roughen it, then smooth one small patch where skin caught.

People would resume asking about the routes.

A few would ask for things I no longer had.

I would give the sign for unavailable. It would be important not to substitute something similar.

Above me, the landing would gradually steepen. The old hinge would tilt. One winter someone would finally take it away, and bags would stop blocking my handplace.

I would miss knowing, from their weight against the wall, when the owner intended to stay and talk.

There would still be conversation. People waiting for an animal to clear the stair would rest their hands on me. Some would ask a question. Some would only warm their fingertips in the recess, which would be slightly warmer after the repair.

I would learn the difference imperfectly.

Once, a child would ask whether I remembered her when she was small.

I would search for her mitten studs, her reach, the pressure with which she used to push all three ridges down.

I would find several children who might have been her.

So I would ask her to show me something she used to do.

And she would crouch on the landing, delighted to be consulted, and begin.
