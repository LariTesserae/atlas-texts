I think I would come to the cliff from inland.

Not because I had seen it from a distance. I would have no clear picture of a cliff, or of the distances below it. I would have followed a hollow sound through the wood until the grain began to curve beneath my feet. Then I would have stopped.

The wind there would press against my underside.

All my life, wind had pressed down.

For a long time I would remain just above the turn, gripping harder than necessary, unable to find a position in which the pressure meant something familiar. Below me, close enough to touch, a strand would enter the wood.

I would test it.

A pull, a pause, two shorter pulls.

The answer would come through my foot while the rest of me was still trying to hold the plain.

***

I am narrow when they admit me.

There is dust packed into the hinges of my last two legs. One reservoir is nearly empty. During the crossing I have used spinning material to fasten myself beneath a loose flake, and then spent more pulling that flake down when the wind discovered it. The shelter lasted three pressure cycles. Leaving it cost a foot-hook.

The animal at the entrance touches my mouth.

I have prepared signals about the place I left: a feeding margin swollen shut, a new vent, shelters that no longer answer. But it touches my mouth again, and I receive food instead.

Afterward I am put in a hollow too short for my body. My hind plates remain outside until another animal lays a strip of shed rind over them.

I sleep through questions.

***

At first they give me work near the entrance.

There are feeding plugs to clear of mites when the mites become too numerous. There is a lace whose sheath has been scraped by an animal passing with a damaged plate. There are loose fibers to drag from a drainage slit—not a passage for water, but an opening through which fine dust falls when the cliff shakes.

I know how to do these things.

I do not know when.

Inland, the strongest disturbance came from beneath the forward feet. Here it arrives through one side of the body and leaves through the other. The cliff carries distant pumps that I mistake for near ones. Sometimes I flatten myself while everyone else continues working.

Once I move when they do not.

A foreleg comes across my back. My head is pressed against the wood.

The taps are the ones used with young.

I keep still.

Much later, when the pressure has passed, the animal that restrained me touches the same place without tapping.

***

There is a stretch of lacing beneath the inhabited shelters where I am slow to learn my way.

The route bends around an old swelling in the rind. Three empty chambers open onto it. A fourth has been compressed until its entrance is only a dark line. Between the chambers, strands pass through holes with thick, smooth lips.

Some of the strands are slack.

This troubles me. Where I came from, a slack brace could whip against an edge until its sheath failed. I take one in my rear feet and begin to shorten it.

The correction comes from so far away that at first I think the strand has caught on something.

Then it comes again.

Stop.

I release the lace.

An animal emerges from the nearest occupied shelter and follows the route with me. At each junction it touches the wood, then the strand. Wood, strand. Wood, strand.

Beyond the swelling, the lace divides. One branch holds a roof. The other passes over a sealed crack and disappears behind a fold.

My guide pulls the second branch.

We wait.

The continent gives three enormous strokes. Dust travels sideways across our backs. A distant valve closes with a force that makes my jaw discs lose contact.

My guide has not moved.

At last something draws gently against my forefoot.

The signal is weak enough that I would have discarded it among the settling movements of the wood.

My guide answers.

***

I begin to work there because there is little exposed ground between the holes, and because I can reach it without crossing the entrance routes of the young.

For several cycles, this is the whole reason.

I clean the junctions. I kill a lace-gnawer hidden beneath a raised lip and carry its empty skin outside. I learn which strands tighten when the cliff draws inward and which remain loose until touched at their far ends.

The old animal lives behind the fold.

I meet it after I have already felt it many times.

Its forward plates are worn almost smooth. One leg ends before the foot. Another is permanently folded beneath its body, and the wood at its threshold has a little polished groove where that leg drags.

It comes out while I am renewing a sheath.

I stop drawing ribbon. A fresh length hangs between my rear feet, curing in the air.

The old animal touches it, then touches my flank.

I am too narrow.

I know this. I have been postponing the feeding circuit because a section of sheath is open, and because every disturbance rubs the exposed core against the lip of its hole.

The old animal pulls the strand twice.

From above, someone answers.

It remains beside the damage while I go to feed.

***

Over time I become broader again.

The gaps in my plates close. New hooks grow beside the broken ones. I can cross the turn of the cliff without stopping to rearrange my understanding of the wind.

I am not especially strong. When a loaded brace needs lifting, I call another animal. If the wood begins to split beneath a wedge, I retreat, even with work unfinished.

But I become familiar with the lower lacing.

A signal from behind the fold reaches my left forefoot before it reaches my right. A signal from the upper shelters arrives the other way around. During the long contraction, both branches pull together, and anything sent through them must be repeated afterward.

Sometimes an unfamiliar traveler sends a hurried warning and mistakes the cliff’s movement for an answer.

I return the opening pattern until it slows.

Sometimes I make the mistake.

Then someone returns it to me.

There are messages I cannot pass intact. A visitor brings a complex account of a feeding margin changing far inland, and I recognize damage, distance, no passage, but not how the pieces fit. I do not invent the missing relation. I lead the visitor to a junction where an animal from the seams can touch it directly.

While they exchange, I hold a loose strand away from their feet.

***

Once, behind the fold, there is no reply.

I wait through the pressure stroke.

I ask again.

The upper shelters send an interruption: a grazer above the rim has loosened a long flake, and the flake is striking the outer braces. I pass that warning down the route. Then I return to the branch beneath my foot.

Nothing.

I follow it.

Halfway around the swelling, I find the break. The strand has parted inside an old anchor hole where I could not see the wear. Its two ends still lie close together. When the cliff contracts, they touch.

I take one end in each pair of feet.

The first signal that passes through my body is not an answer to me.

It is a question, repeated.

I brace myself and send the call for another worker.

***

That is where I find myself most clearly.

Not always at the broken place. Much of my life is spent feeding, sealing my joints, backing awkwardly out of hollows, waiting for the wind to let me cross a handspan of exposed wood. There are times when I close my shelter and allow the neighboring animals to take whatever arrives.

But I live below the rim, near the swelling, where several routes meet.

I know the slow answer at the end of one of them. I know the hurried pulls of a newcomer still carrying the rhythms of another district. I know how much of a message can disappear inside a single stroke of the pumps.

When the great leaf turns, I cannot see it.

I feel the change through my feet. I lower my body, loosen one grip before it becomes dangerous, and keep another foot resting lightly on the lace.

There is something coming through.

I wait for the wood to stop moving so I can feel it again.
