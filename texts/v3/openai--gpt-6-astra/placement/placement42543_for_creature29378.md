I would be walking home with an empty parcel frame strapped to my back.

The frame would make me look, from a distance, as though I were carrying a window. Two upright pieces of ash, several crossbars, a little shelf that could be folded out to support a sack. At the points where the straps crossed my shoulders, my coat would be permanently flat.

I would have stopped beside a patch of lichen to take a stone out of my boot.

Behind me, the path would continue toward three houses, only two of them occupied. Ahead, it would turn around a ridge before reaching the city. From where I sat, I could see the upper floors of a residential block but not the tram shelter at its foot. A woman on a balcony would be shaking something out. At this distance I could not tell whether it was a cloth or a small piece of bedding.

I would watch her longer than necessary.

Then I would put my boot back on. There would still be another hour of walking.

***

My work would be for the postal service, though I would not be a regular carrier.

The regular carriers have rounds. I would have a list of places that must continue to be reachable.

A house can receive no letters for months and still need a path. People forget this in the scheduling office. They see a blank column, remove a journey, and discover in spring that the last quarter mile has become difficult to agree upon.

I would be sent along the low-volume routes with whatever there was to carry. A packet of needles. A replacement pressure gauge. Notices about the conditions under which the service might be withdrawn.

On empty days I would carry a brass token from the office to a box at the far end of the route. The box would stamp the token. This was supposed to establish that I had been there.

The boxes would require more maintenance than the paths.

I would know how to strike one with the heel of my hand without spilling its ink.

***

I think I would be younger than Mera, but not young enough for people to be interested in what I intended to become.

There would be something wrong with the hearing in my left ear. Wind on that side would sound like running water. I would turn my head when spoken to, and people would sometimes lower their voices, believing I had leaned closer for a confidence.

I would learn a number of things that way which I did not want to know.

I would not be especially quiet. On the long walks I would talk to myself, generally about money. I would rehearse conversations with the scheduling clerk in which I was devastatingly clear. At the office I would accept the amended allowance and ask whether anyone had found my gloves.

My hands would be good at knots and poor at buttons.

There would be a particular small button at my throat that I left undone all winter.

***

My room would be above a shop selling lamp fittings.

In the evening, light from the display below would come up through the floorboards in short, hard lines. I would put a rug over the brightest part, then move it away because the room was less disagreeable with some light in it.

I would have plants, but not flourishing ones.

Three pots on a shelf, each containing a different attempt at a lowland bean. I would have brought the seeds up myself on one of the rare journeys below the clouds. The seller had said they were easy.

One would produce leaves. One would produce a long, pale stem that kept finding gaps behind the shelf. The third would do nothing for so long that I would begin using its pot to hold coins.

When it finally sprouted, I would have to move the coins.

I would be pleased enough to tell someone at work, and irritated when they asked whether the beans were edible. There would be two leaves. I would not yet be thinking of meals.

***

I would know Mera by her bag before I knew her name.

Once a month I would bring envelopes to a woman on the seventh floor of her block who repaired field instruments. Postal barometers, route clocks, the little stamping mechanisms from the boxes. Her address would remain on the fourth floor in the directory. This would have nothing to do with the rising stone. She would simply have moved upstairs without informing the office.

Mera would tell me this on my first visit.

On the second, she would tell me again.

By the third I would remember.

Sometimes we would meet on the stairs. Neither of us would ask the other to hurry. I would rest the bottom of my frame on a step while she let her knee settle.

One day she would ask why I was carrying a kitchen chair.

“Delivery.”

“I can see that.”

The chair would belong to a man beyond the northern shelf. His daughter had repaired its split seat and sent it back through the post because neither of them owned a vehicle. I would have tied it to the frame with its legs sticking above my head.

Mera would examine the repair.

“Keep that out of the rain.”

I would say that the rain had not consulted me about its plans.

She would wait for me to finish enjoying this, then tell me there was an old sack in the entrance cupboard.

***

The unoccupied house would be on my Thursday route.

It would have a yellow tank, a shed with no latch, and a garden laid out in narrow beds between warm pipes. The pipes would be cold by then. Most of the plants would have gone silver.

A man named Iven had lived there. He would still be alive, living with his brother on the southern shelf and sending complaints about the disposal of his belongings.

I would have helped carry some of them out.

He would have taken the stove, six boxes of tools, and a basin with a crack so fine that I could not find it even when he pointed. He would have left a bed too large for the temporary freight cradle.

For the first few Thursdays, I would look in through the window to see the bed.

Then I would stop looking.

My token box would stand on a post beside his gate. The path would be maintained for one year after departure, with an extension available on payment. Iven would apply twice for the extension to be waived.

Both applications would travel in my bag.

***

It would be easy to imagine that I loved the walks.

Sometimes I would.

There would be mornings when the cloud wall opened far below and exposed a succession of dark ridges, each one carrying more trees than I had seen in the whole previous year. I would sit down with my breakfast in my hand and forget to eat it.

But there would also be the distance back.

There would be wet straps and a sore patch above one heel. There would be a parcel that clicked against the frame for eleven miles because I had packed it badly and could not be bothered to stop.

In winter I would want a counter job.

I would fill out the application, leave it on my table, and put a cup on it.

***

The last Thursday at Iven’s house would not feel like the last until I took the box off its post.

There would be four bolts. Three would turn. The fourth would require oil and most of my lunch break.

Under the mounting plate, insects would have made a row of little sealed chambers. I would keep the plate facing upward while I worked, although I had no idea whether this mattered.

The instructions would say to recover the post as well.

I would try it once, feel how firmly the fresh stone had closed around its base, and write *embedded* on the form.

Then I would sit on the doorstep.

Without the box, the gate would look slightly too large for its purpose. I would have the unreasonable feeling that I had taken something from the house which belonged to it.

Inside, the bed would still be there.

I would eat the rest of my bread, brush the crumbs off my coat, and begin the walk back.

***

The following Thursday I would be assigned to the sorting room.

By noon my feet would hurt from standing still. Someone would have put a radio beside the heating pipe, where it received one station clearly and another underneath it. Every time the announcer paused, a woman would begin to sing.

I would sort pump seals, printed notices, a parcel wrapped in green cloth.

There would be nothing for the northern route.

At the end of the shift I would ask whether I could have the old token box. The supervisor would say no. It was serviceable equipment.

“The spring sticks.”

“Then put it with repairs.”

I would put it with repairs.

On my way home I would buy a small bag of soil from the reservoir gardeners. It would cost more than I thought soil ought to cost. I would carry it under my arm, without the frame, and keep changing sides.

Upstairs, the bean would have put out another leaf.

I would clear the table before opening the bag. There would be grit in the room for days afterward, however carefully I worked.
