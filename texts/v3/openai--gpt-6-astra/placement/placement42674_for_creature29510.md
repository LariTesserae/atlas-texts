I would be near the willow margin, but not beneath the deepest shade. There would be a grass blade over me, bent and held down by its own rooted tip, and a vole passage close enough that the roof moved when the vole went through.

At first I imagine myself as one of the cups. It is an easy place to put a self: a rim, an inside, a little opening that can close.

Mine would have a nick in it. A beetle had taken something there, and the new growth had left the two sides unequal. When I unfolded, one side would touch the grass blade before the other. Water would collect at that contact. I would work along it with my fringe, bringing in whatever had softened overnight.

There would be a particular piece of leaf I could not swallow and would not release. Too large to draw inward, it would soften unevenly. For several mornings I would turn it, lose purchase, catch it again. Other food would touch me and escape while I was occupied.

I would be capable of wasting time in this small way.

But the cup is not quite where I find myself.

Behind it, under the bent grass, a cord passes through a fork in a heather stem. The stem has thickened since the cord grew there. In dry weather there is room for both. After rain, their swollen surfaces press together, and a pull that once traveled freely now stops at the fork.

I think I would be there: in the part that has to grow around the obstruction.

There is food coming from the nicked cup, enough to keep the constricted length alive. On the other side there are two pale folds beneath a leaf, and sometimes nourishment comes from them instead. I would not experience this as an exchange between identifiable neighbors. There would be fullness at one end, then less strain along a length of skin. Something could be spent on growth.

The new growth would begin on the wrong side.

A blunt extension would follow the heather downward, into a space that becomes too wet. It would swell there and persist. Meanwhile another edge, hardly more than a thickening, would press against the underside of the grass blade. That would be the way through.

For a time I would be both attempts.

One would have bristles and no useful destination. The other would advance so slowly that a drop of water could remain beside it through most of its progress, shrinking each afternoon and enlarging before dawn.

Above me, the vole would pass.

The first few times, I would draw tight against the grass. This would make the roof less yielding. The vole would push harder, and one of its claws would scrape the growing end.

Later, I would loosen.

I cannot give that change a moment of understanding. Some growth would survive better than other growth. A contraction would become less likely after the familiar pressure reached me. Eventually the vole would pass over a roof that sagged beneath its belly and rose behind it, and my soft end would remain intact.

I would be making a way through something that was also a way through me.

The mill would be present as a repeated movement at the heather fork. Much of my skin would cease to respond to it. At one small point, though, the cord would keep wearing thin. Repair would accumulate there, a thickened patch turned toward the wood.

If the mill stopped, I would at first continue tightening into the expected movement.

The patch would press against nothing. The new connection would pull sideways. One of the pale folds under the leaf would close, while the nicked cup continued worrying its morsel.

This is where the boundaries become difficult. I could call myself the whole arrangement, but there would be no sensation available to me of a whole arrangement. Even these few inches would contain events that failed to reach one another.

I would be the pull arriving.

I would also be tissue it did not reach, still feeding.

By the time the new cord had gone around the heather fork, the old cord might have died within it. Nothing dramatic would mark the change. A narrowing, a darkening; less need to maintain that piece. The stem would hold it in place. A beetle sheltering there might eat some of it without disturbing either living end.

My successful repair would leave a small piece of myself behind.

Then a blackbird would turn the leaf.

The two pale folds would go with it. Perhaps the bird would take one and leave the other exposed. The uncovered skin would contract against the sudden dry air. A tension would reach the junction under the grass and fade before it reached the nicked cup.

For the rest of that morning, the cup would feed as usual.

I would want to let it.

There is a temptation, imagining such a creature, to give its dispersed life a wisdom: to make every injury instruct the whole, every surviving part preserve the lost. But here one end can be torn open while another holds a drop of water. The drop is not consolation. It is simply still there.

Toward afternoon, the surviving fold would begin to dry. The cord leading to it would shorten, drawing its edge a little way beneath the displaced leaf. Whether that was enough would depend on where the leaf had fallen.

I would live in that difference.

And after rain, if enough remained, I would grow again. A new cup might form beside the junction rather than out near the bird’s opening. Its fringe would unfold into the vole passage, where there was room, and close when the roof shifted.

For several days it would close too late.

Then, perhaps, earlier.

You could crouch above the spot and find nothing that distinguished it from the rest of the clearing. The grass would have a faint pale mark underneath. The heather fork would contain a dry thread. Something newly folded would be keeping a crumb of peat from falling into the passage.

That is where I would be when you arrived: occupied with the crumb, still joined to the nicked cup, growing into the space available.
