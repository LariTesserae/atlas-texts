I would be inside the ridge, a little farther in than the swimmer’s feelers can reach.

There is room there for a body to turn over, but only if it folds its rear feet first. Water enters through three openings and leaves through one. Across the floor lies a root that has never hardened. The current keeps it moving slightly, and the movement keeps one side of it rubbed bare.

At first that is all I would know of the place: a pale length moving across my mouth. Something to grip when the water strengthens.

My other feet would still be holding the skin of the egg.

***

I would grow into one of the builders.

Not one stationed at an outer seam, where passing bodies interrupt the light. My chamber would be well inside the living floor. Whatever brightness reached it would have passed through so many narrow openings that my eyes could distinguish only an exposed mouth from a closed one.

Most of the time I would work with my face against something.

A young root has a yielding skin and a firm center. Held too sharply, it creases; held for long enough in a gentler curve, it begins to keep the curve. I would brace four feet against the wall and draw it toward me with my jaws. The remaining feet would search for a fastening place.

The secretion would leave my mouth soft. Water would take away its taste before it became strong enough to bear a pull.

There would be an interval of holding.

Much of my life would happen in that interval. My jaws occupied, my feet spread, food going past where I could not reach it. Another builder might climb across my back. I would feel its weight move from one side to the other and tighten my grip until it had gone.

Then I would release the root.

Sometimes it would stay.

***

The swimmer would first arrive as a change in the floor.

I would not know that an animal had settled outside. A pressure that had always come from ahead would slacken. Loose hairs along my belly would rise.

Then the water would reverse.

I would let go of whatever I was carrying and press into the angle beneath the floor root. My feet would close around it. Beside me, another body would do the same, its jaws briefly catching my hindmost limb before finding wood.

For a few moments the chamber would empty around us.

The soft accumulation behind my back would lift away. Small bodies would strike the walls. A root end I had been trying to fasten would pull free, swing toward the exit, and remain there, trembling.

When the ordinary current returned, the room would be larger.

There would be food caught on my feet.

I would eat before beginning the repair.

***

Eventually I would move outward.

Not because I had become curious about the openings. A clutch of eggs would occupy the recess where I had rested, and the easiest grip beside them would already be taken. Farther along, water would carry the taste of an exposed root.

I would follow that.

Here the passage would be broad enough that I could lose contact with both sides. I would travel along its lower wall, stopping at each crossing stem. There would be places worn almost tasteless by the swimmer’s mouth, and softer places behind them where fresh growth thickened in the shelter.

At the entrance I would encounter a feeler.

It would enter slowly, flattening against the ceiling. Its tip would divide, and one branch would come down over my back.

I would remain still.

The branch would rest there long enough for my feet to begin loosening. Then it would move on, following the seam above me. Its skin would carry tastes from water I had never occupied: old leaf, disturbed silt, the rich residue of another channel.

I would put my mouth against it.

It would withdraw.

For some time afterward I would work at that spot, although nothing there needed fastening.

***

The root bundle would thicken a little at a time.

There would be no visit on which I could have found the whole change. I would encounter a loose fiber, a rubbed patch, an opening admitting too much water around the edge of a nest. Each would require a small adjustment.

A stem drawn inward would shelter the eggs behind it. Another, secured across that stem, would keep the first from flexing loose.

The swimmer would scrape them.

Its lower jaw would enter the passage and move against the floor. I would retreat only a short distance. As soon as the jaw lifted, I would return to the torn surface, where the taste of damaged tissue was strongest.

Sometimes I would still be working when it scraped again.

I would lose two feet this way. They would return smaller, pale at the joints, slow to close.

After that I would retreat farther.

The fastening would take longer, but I would finish it.

***

I would lay my own eggs behind the bundle.

There would be fewer than could fit there. I would have spent too much of the feeding interval holding roots, and several of the eggs would remain small and soft inside me until my body took them back.

Those I laid would receive a steady current.

I would clean their skins with my mouth. Other builders would cross the pocket without stopping. Once, a narrow juvenile swimmer would push its head between the stems, and I would take hold of a branching feeler and pull until the head withdrew.

One egg would open while I was away.

When I returned, its skin would be collapsed against the wall. I would loosen it, fold it into my mouth, and eat it. The young would already be somewhere deeper in the bundle.

***

The large swimmer’s visits would become less frequent.

I would notice this through the roots. Fastenings would last long enough to grow over. The outer ends of stems would remain tender instead of being scraped back to firm wood.

A new sill would form beyond the old one.

I would move my resting place twice.

Then water would begin arriving more strongly through a side opening, bringing a fine burden of loosened material. Somewhere beyond the ridge, another channel had been emptied.

I would turn toward the food.

For a while I would stay where the two currents met, picking fragments from the water. Behind me, the egg pocket would receive less of the flow. One of its inner surfaces would acquire a thick, soft film.

Eventually I would crawl back and graze it clear.

But the water would still be poor there.

I would climb through the bundle, find a slender root crossing the side opening, and begin to pull it down. It would resist. I would shift my feet, including the two smaller ones, and take a better hold.

The root would bend across the current.

A little more water would enter the egg pocket.

I would hold it there while the fastening set.
