I would be under the folded rim of the collecting basket.

It hangs beside the repair cradle, secured by a short chain. The basket has holes large enough to admit a fingertip, and a lid that has to be pressed down before it will slide. Mara puts the cuttings there while she works. Later, they are taken away from the intakes and emptied.

Under the rim, however, there is a space the width of a blunt needle. The water passes through it gently. Nothing used to clean the basket quite reaches the back.

I would begin there, although some of me would already be eleven years old.

***

At first I have too much edge.

The cut has opened several passages. Water enters where there should be a wall; the soft bodies moving inside me collect against a constriction, then reverse. A cloudy substance escapes. I lose enough of it that the remaining tissue draws tight across the fibers.

The basket moves.

Everything loose in me moves with it. What is fastened pulls against what is not. There is no useful direction until the basket stops and the water begins passing through its holes again.

A length of my edge touches metal.

It stays there.

That is the first thing I do that will last.

The rest takes longer. A sleeve closes. Another sleeve, too damaged to close, loses its tension and hangs through one of the holes. Small mouths arrive on the far side of it. Their pulling reaches me as a sequence of little stretches, each followed by less weight.

Eventually that part is gone.

Within the folded rim, I open three feeding cups. One faces the metal and receives almost nothing. One faces the narrow current. The third cannot quite unfold.

For a long time, this is sufficient.

***

The mountain’s noise is present in every attachment.

One fastening pulls against another, thousands of times between the arrival of one edible particle and the next. My fibers take some of the strain. Fluid takes the rest. Where the strain is too great, the tissue thickens or gives way.

There are places on the basket where I cannot persist.

I discover them by growing into them.

A bridge across one corner lasts until it develops an opaque line through its middle. The line widens. The bridge becomes two short sleeves, each with a closed end. Elsewhere, a much thinner membrane remains intact. Food gathers along its lower edge.

The basket teaches me its shape in this fashion.

It also changes shape.

A large pressure comes down on the lid. The rim bends by less than the thickness of my body. Several passages flatten. Their contents move elsewhere, except for what is caught between two closures.

After the pressure lifts, most of the passages fill again.

Over many such events, I become thick on one side of the fold and sparse on the other. A person examining me might suppose that I prefer the sheltered side.

The other side has killed me repeatedly.

***

More cuttings arrive.

They fall against the basket floor in loose, contracting knots. Some still carry open fringes. Some have already closed everything they can close. Fish enter through the holes, investigate, leave.

When the basket is emptied, most of the cuttings go with the water.

One remains caught beneath the lid.

It is yellow along one edge. Two of its passages are wider than any of mine. It lies against me for several days, feeding from the same slight current.

We press each other flat where we touch.

Then the surfaces between us begin to thin.

There is no moment at which the whole of either body agrees to anything. At one contact, the walls remain firm. At another, a pinhole opens and fluid passes through. Both sides contract. The opening seals.

It opens again later.

Afterward there is a route through me that was not grown by me.

Food entering beneath the rim travels into the yellow tissue. A small opaque body arrives from it, lodges for a while beside one of my cups, and continues on. The old distinction is still visible from outside. Clear tissue here; yellow there. Inside, it has become inconvenient.

Other cuttings do not join.

One whitens wherever it touches me, and I whiten in return. Between us a narrow strip of damaged tissue persists until the next emptying takes it away.

I am not everything put into the basket.

***

Mara eventually notices that the lid is becoming difficult.

She takes the basket off its chain and braces it against the cradle. Her glove crosses several feeding edges. The alarm begins there and travels under the rim.

Before it reaches the far end, she has opened the lid.

Cold water moves through passages that have been sheltered for months. Loose particles lift. A fish that has grown too broad to leave through the side holes departs through the top.

She turns the basket over.

From where she holds it, I look like a pale gasket that has slipped out of place. She works a flat tool beneath the thickest fold and lifts.

Much of me comes away.

For a little while the lifted portion remains connected by three narrow sleeves. Then two. Then none.

She leaves the difficult part beneath the rolled metal. Removing it would require another tool.

The lid works again.

I have fewer feeding surfaces now, but less tissue to supply. The cloudy losses stop. Food accumulates at a junction whose former outlet is gone, and takes a slower route around the corner.

The difficult part is enough.

***

I would live through many journeys without knowing that a journey was taking place.

The basket would be carried along the cradle, lowered beside a drain, secured in a transport rack. Each place would arrive as a different distribution of pressure.

For hours, all my attachments might tremble together. Then the trembling would separate into conflicting pulls. Water would strike the feeding cups from the wrong side. I would close them and wait.

Sometimes there would be warmth.

It would enter through the metal first. Activity would quicken in the sheltered tissue while the outer sleeves remained cold. Material would arrive at the junctions faster than it could leave. I would swell beneath the rim.

Then the warmth would go.

No part of me could remember a station as Mara remembers one. But a sleeve made thick during a warm interval would remain thick in the cold. A connection opened during transport would still offer a route after the basket was hung up again.

What happened would keep altering what could happen next.

***

At another station, the basket is set down on stone.

It is supposed to remain there only until a blocked waste port has been cleared. A tether holds it against the slope. The bottom rests unevenly, with one corner suspended over a fracture.

Below that corner the water carries more food.

I grow toward it through a hole.

The first extension is poor: a loose, thin sleeve with a single feeding edge. It reaches past the metal and swings. The movement strains its base. For a while I withdraw material from it, and it nearly closes.

But particles keep arriving.

The sleeve thickens. Its end divides. One branch folds back against the basket. The other touches stone.

There is a bacterial film on the stone, slippery and easily torn. My first attachment takes the film with it when the basket shifts.

The next reaches into a small fracture.

Here the surface holds.

By the time the waste port is clear, I have several connections outside the basket. They are short and difficult to see. My clearer tissue lies against pale rock, beneath an object that nobody has reason to inspect from below.

Someone lifts the basket.

The stone connections take its weight for less than a second.

***

This separation is different.

The cut made by Mara’s tool left short, clean openings. The lifting stretches me first. Fluid is driven along the sleeves. Fibers pull free of the softer tissue and remain extended after the passages around them have torn.

Some of me rises with the basket.

Some stays in the fracture.

For several minutes, the part on the stone loses fluid into the water. Its feeding edges close one after another. There is too little body behind them now. A cloudy clot forms in a narrow place and stops a loss.

The basket is secured somewhere above.

I cannot feel it.

The water moving over the stone is less reliable than the water beneath the rim. It comes from two directions, according to disturbances farther up the slope. One of my remaining cups is badly placed for both. Another receives enough to keep opening.

There is no metal here. The stone moves with the mountain, but it does not flex under a hand.

I begin to change accordingly.

***

That is where I would be found: below the place where a basket was set down.

For some time I would retain its curve.

A raised fold would follow the shape of a rim no longer present. Several closed sleeves would point toward holes that had been carried away. Water would pass through the empty spaces between them.

Gradually, those arrangements would become expensive to maintain.

The raised fold would thin. The upright sleeves would settle against the rock. Along the fracture, where food passes close enough to catch, a row of cups would grow.

One extension would advance into the darker space beneath a fallen chip of stone. There would be little food inside, but its walls would offer firm attachments. I would spend material reaching them, slowly, while the exposed cups supplied what they could.

At last the extension would touch the underside of the chip.

It would flatten there.

Farther back, the sleeve would still be loose. Over the next several days, it would draw taut.
