At first I would choose a room above a station.

I like the thought of arriving without having to go outdoors again: carrying something warm through a passage, hearing the doors close behind me, seeing rain strike the glass on the other side. From that room I could watch weather cross the unbuilt intervals. I would learn the departure sounds well enough to stop hearing them.

But I would want a window that opened. That would become important. And after a while I would find that the station’s ventilation ran beneath my floor, a low continuous sound that disappeared only during maintenance, leaving me unexpectedly awake.

I think I would move somewhere less convenient and complain about the walk.

My room would face a service court. Across it would be a blank wall, an exterior stair, and a narrow strip of rock where runoff collected before entering a covered drain. In winter the stair would throw a blue shadow across the whole strip. For perhaps twenty minutes on clear summer mornings, sunlight would reach my kitchen table.

I would keep things on that table which ought to be put away before eating.

***

I would work in route corrections.

There must be a small office somewhere that receives the changes after the engineers have finished with them and before they become directions anyone can use. A bridge has acquired a steeper approach. A door now opens onto a landing instead of a level path. A public passage is technically open, but only if you can lift a shopping cart over the temporary sill.

Someone has to go and look.

I would begin as the person who entered the reports. I would be good at the orderly part: matching dates, finding duplicate entries, noticing that two departments had given the same ramp different names. I would enjoy resolving those discrepancies more than I would admit.

Then, one afternoon, I would mark a clinic entrance accessible because the report said the work was complete.

The work would be complete. There would be nothing inaccurate in the report.

The entrance would also have a door so stiff that a person using both hands on a walking frame could not open it.

I imagine the complaint coming to me without much anger. That would make it harder to set aside. A woman describing how long she had waited for somebody to come through from the other direction, and how, by then, she had needed the toilet.

Afterward I would start taking the inspection shifts nobody wanted.

Not all of them. I would still dislike the western districts in sleet. But enough that there would eventually be a pair of wet boots beneath my desk, and people would begin giving me things to check while I was out.

***

The equipment would be ordinary: a measuring wheel, a short level, a gauge for the force needed to open doors. A little folding wedge for keeping them open while I worked.

I would lose the wedge repeatedly.

Sera would find one in a bridge housing and recognize the yellow paint.

“You left this where it could have dropped into the bearing.”

“I looked for it.”

“Not there.”

She would hand it back clean. That would be the part that embarrassed me.

For a while I would attach it to my bag with a cord. Then the cord would catch on something, and I would remove it, and eventually lose the wedge again.

I don’t think Sera and I would become close immediately. Our work would meet at awkward points. She would have spent six hours making a passage safe, and I would arrive to ask when the temporary barrier could be moved another thirty centimeters.

She would think I did not understand the work.

Sometimes she would be right.

Once she would ask me to hold a cover plate while she reached behind it. I would hold it badly, with all the strain in my fingers.

“Against your hip,” she would say.

I would remember that instruction for years without having any occasion to mention it.

***

What would make the work mine, eventually, would be the distances.

On the plans, an alternative route would be a line around a closed connection. Out on the ground it could mean eleven minutes with nowhere to sit. It could mean a long exposed side to the wind, or a crossing where meltwater entered the top of one boot before you noticed it was deep.

I would begin noting benches. Shelters. Doors you could open with an elbow. Places where a person could stop without being in everybody’s way.

Most of this would fit badly into the forms.

There would be a field for distance, another for gradient, another for surface condition. There would not be one for *the wind catches you here just when you need both hands for the gate*.

I would write that in the remarks.

My supervisor would ask me to keep the remarks shorter.

We would have this conversation several times. She would be a reasonable woman with too many reports to review, not an enemy of useful information. Sometimes I would cut a sentence and discover that nothing had been lost.

Other times she would put it back herself.

***

I would not be especially hardy.

In the first cold hour of a shift, I would feel competent and awake. By the fourth, I would want hot fat, a chair, and no further questions. My gloves would always be either too thick for the instruments or too thin for the weather.

I would take an unreasonable dislike to a café near the northern interchange because its cups had narrow handles. I would continue going there because it stood at the right point in my route.

There would be somebody at the office with whom I exchanged terrible photographs: a maintenance sign attached upside down, a bird standing directly beneath a notice about keeping clear, my measuring wheel sunk to its axle in a depression the current survey described as dry.

I would enjoy that friendship. It would mostly remain at work.

At home I would listen to a long, badly acted serial about a family who owned a shipping company somewhere warm. People would keep returning after having been presumed drowned. I would cook while it played and miss explanations, then complain that the story made no sense.

There should be something in my life that the plateau does not explain.

***

The strip of wet rock outside my kitchen would occupy more of my attention than the view from the station ever had.

Initially I would notice only the drain blocking.

A twist of dead stems would catch beneath the cover. Water would spread toward the stair footing. I would report it, receive a reference number, and watch it for three mornings before going down with a bent piece of wire.

The next year there would be a green edge along the water.

After that, small birds would come regularly. They would move too quickly for me to see what they were taking. I would buy a field guide, identify two of them, and become less certain about the rest.

One bird would have a pale patch at the back of its head. I would decide it was the same one returning.

Perhaps it would be.

I would find myself leaving the kitchen light off until I had looked outside.

That seems closer to where I would actually live: not in the immense prospect of the plateau, but at a table looking down at a few square meters that had begun to hold things.

***

There would be a path I kept on the map too long.

It would run behind a residential court, crossing a shallow terrace before meeting the station walkway. No official reason to preserve it. The maintained route would be shorter and warmer.

But I would have walked it often. There would be a place to sit with your back out of the wind. In spring, water would pass beneath a thin plate of ice there, carrying bubbles that slowed and joined one another at the edge.

The terrace would tilt. Not dramatically. A wet patch would widen over two seasons until crossing it required stepping on stones somebody had carried into place.

I would change the route to “seasonal.”

Later I would add a warning.

Sera, passing my screen on some unrelated errand, would point at it.

“That’s water now.”

“There are still stones.”

“For you.”

I would resent her for saying it so briefly.

Then I would remove the path from the pedestrian layer. Its old line would remain in the archive, with the date of the last verification. I would know exactly where to find it and probably never need to.

***

After that, I would start distinguishing between routes I could recommend and places I wanted to return to.

On a day off, with no measuring wheel, I would go back to the terrace.

The stones would be there, several already submerged. Birds would be standing in the water beyond them. The sheltered sitting place would still be dry, but reaching it would mean wet feet.

I would sit somewhere less comfortable.

A train would pass in the distance, windows bright even in daylight. For a moment I would think of the people inside seeing a solitary figure beside an inconvenient pool and assuming I knew something about birds.

Then the train would be gone.

I might stay for an hour. I might get cold after ten minutes and leave. I would like there to be room for either.

***

Sera would eventually come to my flat because the exterior stair had begun transmitting a noise into the wall.

I would have described it poorly in the request. A rattle, I would say, although it was closer to a single knock repeated irregularly.

She would stand in my kitchen with her coat still fastened, waiting.

Nothing would happen.

I would offer tea. She would refuse, then accept when it became clear we were going to wait some time.

The sound would come while she was reaching for the cup. She would put it down and touch the window frame.

“Again?” I would ask.

She would lift one finger.

Outside, a bird would land in the wet strip and begin working along the green margin. Sera would watch it without moving her hand.

The knock would come again.

“There,” she would say.

And I would hear, for the first time, that it came from farther down than I had thought.

She would go out to look at the stair. I would hold the door while she crouched beside its lowest attachment. Cold air would enter around my ankles. On the table her tea would still be steaming, and a corner of one of my papers would lift each time the wind reached the doorway.

“Can you bring a light?” she would ask.

I would know which drawer it was in.
