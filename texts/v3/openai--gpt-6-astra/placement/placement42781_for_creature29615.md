I would know the bottom stair by its weight, long before I knew it was a stair.

Usually it would weigh the same. Rain would alter it a little, and the slow passage of water through the mortar would alter it differently. Then, at intervals too irregular to prepare for, it would become heavier in separate, brief amounts. The changes would travel toward me through the paving. Some would stop near the pumping station. Others would come almost as far as the retaining wall.

I would draw myself in before they arrived.

At first, that is most of what I can find of myself: the drawing inward, and a place beneath a stone.

Not one of the deep places. There would be scarcely a finger’s width between the underside of my stone and the older paving beneath it. Water would pass through from three directions, changing its preference when the pumps moved. I would know which edge carried food and which carried the bitter, very fine material that settled after a worker used a brush.

The stone would be one of the stepping blocks. Its hollow would face slightly across the path, making it inconvenient for a human foot.

I would fit beneath it without filling the space.

There would be soft parts, folded away from the current, and firmer parts pressed against the two surfaces. Those would be pale where they touched the stone. Grit would collect along their edges. When I shifted, something would scrape with a sound too small to rise above the water.

I do not think I would have a head at first. Or rather, the part I used as a head would depend on what reached me.

Toward food, a narrow edge would loosen. Toward the weight of a boot, everything would thicken and shorten. Toward a fine fingertip introduced beneath the block, I would become very still.

The creature from beyond the wall would have found me that way.

Its two slender fingers could enter places the others could not. One would pass along the underside of the stone, collecting the translucent animals clustered there. The other would rest on the paving. Between them, I would hold a fold of myself motionless, with water slipping through it.

The finger would touch me.

Not the exploratory contact of something encountering an obstruction. It would rest against me for several breaths, then move sideways rather than press farther in.

Afterward there would still be food along that edge.

I would not know whether I had been spared or merely recognized as something unsuitable to eat.

For a long time this would be enough of a relationship.

—

I would be able to leave the block.

This comes later, when I follow what happens during the long intervals without footsteps. The attachment I first took for a root would release at one end. There would be a slow peeling, a clean patch left on the stone, and beneath the loose edge a row of blunt structures, too soft to call legs until they bore weight.

I would have a lower surface.

It would be more exposed than I liked.

Crossing open paving would require me to gather myself into several low folds. I would advance one, secure it, bring the rest after. Silt would adhere to the wet surface I ordinarily kept pressed against the stone. The current would get under me. A small fish could make me stop merely by passing close.

I would travel mostly beneath the granite lips, where the falling sheets of water hid these awkward movements.

My body might be the length of a hand when extended. Under pressure it would seem much smaller. There would be no useful view of me from the stair: a thickening along a joint, a piece of rind that had loosened but not washed away.

Yet I would have a mouth. It would be on the protected side, and finding food would involve lowering myself over it.

I would eat slowly. Rust would pass through me. So would the fine fibers gathered by the reeds. Certain soft growths would not.

Once, I would reach the boot scraper and remain there for several days. Its underside would carry a taste unavailable beneath my block. When the larger creature came to sharpen its stone, the vibration would run directly through my body. I would contract so hard that one end of me lost its purchase.

A hand would settle below.

I would come to rest across four fingers.

There would be no enclosing grip. The fingers would simply remain there while I fastened myself to them. Their surface would be wrong—yielding, warm in small patches, crossed by folds that moved independently of my own—but they would hold still.

Then they would lower me beside the scraper.

I would spend the next interval beneath a shutter hook fallen flat against the wall. I would not return to my stepping block until the taste of the hand had gone.

—

The covers would frighten me before I could locate them.

Something would change in the pressure between the surfaces I occupied. Water would still be running normally. The flywheel would still have a little movement left. But I would be unable to maintain an even hold.

One end of me would press harder. The other would begin to lift.

I would leave the underside of the stepping block and fasten myself to its vertical face, just above the water. This would expose me completely. The air would draw moisture from the folds around my mouth. I would remain there anyway.

Across the street, the larger creature would have its hands on the wall.

I would not be able to see its hands. What reached me would be the slight alteration their pressure made in the brick and paving, followed by an unevenness that continued while the cover stood open.

My own body would make a similar disturbance against the block.

Neither of us would be still, though from the bottom stair we might appear so.

When the flywheel advanced, I would descend too quickly. Water would enter folds I had closed against the air. For a while I would be unable to feed.

I would have no account of what had happened beneath the iron.

—

There would be changes in my stone.

A grain would break from one of its supports. Silt would accumulate at another. A worker would step on the outer edge and make the block tilt farther than it had before.

Part of me would be caught.

I would detach elsewhere and pull, but the trapped margin would remain between the stones. It would take most of the interval between one visit and the next to free myself. What came away would no longer close properly.

After that, water moving through the injured side would feel like contact even when nothing touched me.

I would live beneath the seventh doorway for a time.

The ledge would be beyond my reach, but the wall below it would have shallow irregularities into which I could fit. When the creature returned its objects, powder and small grains would fall through the water. Once the brass nut would roll from the ledge and land beside me.

Its hole would offer shelter. I would enter, turn, and find that I could press against almost the whole circumference.

The creature would retrieve it with me inside.

For the first time I would be carried out of water for more than a moment. My folds would close. The brass would be cold, then less cold where the fingers held it.

A fingertip would touch my exposed end.

We would remain that way.

Then the nut would be lowered again, this time onto its side in the shallow water at the foot of the ledge.

It would stay there.

I would leave it to feed and return before the lamps changed. Fine sediment would gather against its lower edge. The injured part of me would slowly acquire a tougher surface.

Sometimes the creature would crouch nearby with the light beneath its breast partly uncovered. The small bodies would move, and their feet would trouble the water outside my opening.

I would withdraw a little, but not all the way.

From within the brass I could receive the current across one surface and hold the other against something steady. There would be room behind me that I did not need to occupy.

When the creature moved away, the water would cool by degrees.

I would remain near the opening until it had cooled completely.
