I would be beneath the western paving, but not directly under the place where you stand.

A little farther in, where one plate overlaps another. There is a wedge of space there that never closes completely. Even at the brightest part of the cycle, when the district has settled its surfaces against one another, something can remain folded in it without having to brace.

Most of me would rest there.

At first I imagine myself small enough to be overlooked: a pale margin at the bottom of a fracture, distinguishable from the broken stone only because it draws back when touched. But that would be only what you could see. Behind the margin I would widen. I would pass under the paving, turn upward inside a partition, and come to rest in a room you had already looked into and thought empty.

I would not have a face waiting in that room. There would be no end of me more properly myself than the portion beside your shoe.

There would, however, be an edge I used more often.

Its hard segments would be worn nearly smooth. One would have split and healed at an angle, making it poor for holding a vertical surface but useful beneath a loose plate. I would begin many movements there. Not because it commanded the rest, but because the stone gave way most readily around it.

From that edge, I would know the western part of the district.

Not its roofs. Not the arrangement visible from the rim. I would know where a wall’s weight divided around an opening, where two floors had ceased to move together, where the ground remained yielding after the surfaces above it had hardened. The tower would reach me as an alteration in distant pressure. During contraction, its leaning would arrive through several buildings, weakened and interrupted.

Sometimes I would be feeding when it arrived and would not move.

My immediate concern would be a ridge under the next courtyard. It had been rising steadily until a ribbon-root reached it. Now the growth was gathering on one side, thickening a place through which I regularly passed. I could still draw myself through, but only by releasing a fold I usually kept against the underside of the courtyard floor.

Another animal rested against that fold.

We had been in contact for a long time. I could not say how long in changes of light; little light reached us. Its margin had left a shallow row of impressions across me. When it withdrew to feed, those impressions remained, and I sometimes adjusted against them before reaching the empty space.

I think this would be the first absence I could recognize: not an empty chamber, but a movement of my own that found nothing to lean on.

When the passage narrowed, I would release the fold slowly.

The other animal would come with it for a short distance. Then one of its segments would catch against the paving. Our surfaces would draw apart. There would be no struggle, only a small redistribution of tension, and afterward I would fit through.

For several cycles I would feed on the thickening ridge.

The passage would become easier again. Whether I had preserved it, or merely stayed where food was abundant, would not be a distinction available in what I did. When the other animal returned, it would place its margin beneath mine at a different angle.

We would no longer fit together in quite the same way.

—

There would be one chamber I visited without feeding.

It would lie farther east, beyond the reach of my body when I was settled beneath the western paving. To get there I would have to gather myself almost entirely out of the wedge. A long withdrawal: first from the partition, then from beneath the courtyard, finally from the little overlap that held my worn edge.

Crossing the intervening floor would expose more of me than I liked. Without stone above, I would fold inward, laying one part of myself over another. The sensitive pits would meet my own surface. I would have to open again to find the way.

The chamber would contain a hard knot.

Once, when it was still soft enough to change its shape, I had rested along the outside of that chamber. A small margin had found a gap beneath the wall and touched mine. It had no purchase there. Each time it stiffened, it slipped.

I had remained.

Now the wall would be continuous. I would brace against it in roughly the same place. Pressure would pass into the mineral and return without the small, uneven adjustments I had once felt.

After a while I would leave.

There would be new growth elsewhere. I would be hungry by the time I reached it.

—

When you arrived at the western paving, I would already be returning to my wedge.

Your weight would close one route and open another. I would spread into the new clearance. Above me, two plates would press together along an edge that usually lay loose.

I would wait for the pressure to change.

It would not change.

Eventually I would extend the worn margin through the seam. There would be your sole: warmer than the paving, rough, slightly yielding. I would try it in several places. Beneath its arch I would find room to stiffen a segment.

More of me would follow.

You would feel something settling behind your heel. I would feel a support that made small, frequent corrections. Even when you tried to remain still, you would not have the stillness of a wall.

That might keep me there longer.

Below us, a feeding surface would open against the young mineral. The ridge would rise unevenly. By the next brightening, it would hold part of the shape we had made together.

Then you would lift your foot.

I would contract at once, but not entirely. My worn segment would still be caught beneath the edge of the new threshold. I would loosen it, test the stone, and brace again.

The opening would be lower now. There would be no warmth on its upper surface.

For a while I would keep a fold inside it, adjusting to pressures that no longer came. Then the floor would begin its next contraction. Somewhere behind me, the animal beneath the courtyard would move, and I would draw back far enough to let its margin pass.
