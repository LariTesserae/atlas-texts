I would probably arrive believing the pale stones.

They would be the first things I understood: someone had gone this way, someone had taken the trouble to leave a sign. I would put my feet between the paired stones and cross. For a while, nothing would happen to correct me.

Then, one afternoon, I would be standing on a path with a loaded basket when the woman with the damaged knee told me to put the basket down.

Not to come toward her. Not to hurry.

To put it down.

I would have mistaken the firmness of a stretched surface for the firmness of something supported underneath. The basket would have been enough to make the difference.

She would talk me back along my own footprints. We would leave the basket until we could reach it with a hook.

That is where I begin to find myself here: not particularly gifted at reading the ground, and alive because somebody else is.

***

After a few seasons, I would be helping her move the stones.

There must be work like that in this country. The paths persist, but their exact positions cannot. A sound crossing grows heavy with its own success. Dust settles into it, stems thicken, grazers use the same depressions until water begins to favor them. Somewhere underneath, a supporting runner dies without immediately ceasing to bear weight.

The stones need looking after.

I would carry a forked stick, a scraper, two short lengths of braid, and something to eat that could survive being sat on. My knees would usually be green. There would be a place at the base of my thumb that split in dry weather because the stick kept opening it.

Most days I would not discover a hidden danger. I would uncover a marker, cut the leaves away from its foot, and find that the ground was much as it had been last time. I would straighten stones overturned by scratching animals. I would pull stones out of hollows where they had slowly sunk.

An entire morning might amount to making six pale objects visible again.

The woman would check my work for longer than I thought necessary.

Once, when I had shifted a pair to the beginning of a safer approach, she would make me go back and remove the old ones. I would have left them because they were heavy and because, to me, the new route was obvious.

She would ask me which pair a traveler would reach first in fog.

After that I would carry the stones, even when it meant two journeys.

***

My house would be close to hers, though not close enough for us to speak from our doorways.

I would choose an existing shelter with a high enough chamber to sit upright in, then learn how quickly that qualification expired. On cold mornings I would postpone cutting the ceiling. By the time I could no longer postpone it, the roots would have grown among the things on my pegs, and the job would involve taking everything down.

There would be a sleeping place, a place for tools, and a damp corner I kept trying to persuade into being useful.

I would not be a solitary sage out on a ridge. I would borrow a wedge and forget to return it. I would dislike going out after rain, when the clipped leaves stuck to my wrists. Sometimes I would report a distant marker checked when I had only looked at it from the nearer rise, and then lie awake imagining what I might have missed until I went back.

The work would make me attentive, but not invariably so.

***

The old houseback would know me badly at first.

I would stamp, then approach too soon. Or I would stand outside her doorway, waiting politely, while my shadow blocked the entrance and my weight remained an unexplained pressure on the floor.

The woman would show me where to stop.

For several weeks I would see only the fresh fan of clippings, or a gray rump disappearing through the back of the house. Then one noon I would arrive and find the animal still lying in her sleeping hollow. Her eyes would be open. Her forefeet would be drawn under her.

I would leave whatever I had brought and go away.

That would be the beginning of our acquaintance: she would allow me to finish a small, predictable action without taking defensive measures.

Eventually I would be permitted to cut the runners from the outside of her reed collar. The upper edge would be beyond her comfortable reach. I would work there while she remained indoors, although I would never reach through.

She would come out afterward and cut something I had left.

I would find this irritating before I found it useful.

From her side of the threshold, it would have been in the way.

***

There is one kind of work I think I would become good at.

Sometimes a dangerous span could be avoided without abandoning the whole route. A few steps to one side, beneath what looked like identical leaves, there might be an old stone shoulder. Finding it from above would take patient probing.

Finding it from below would take someone willing to crawl.

I would dislike the galleries. The noise would press against my teeth. Powdered fiber would get down my collar, and every root touching the back of my neck would make me flinch. But belowground, some things would become wonderfully plain. Here was the stone. Here was the unsupported width. Here was a thick runner bearing on an edge scarcely wider than my palm.

I would learn to leave my basket outside and tie the scraper to my wrist.

I would learn not to clear myself more room merely because I wanted it.

The old female might use a passage I had opened, but she would not lead me to the places I needed to inspect. Our routes would overlap for a little distance and then separate. Once I would see her flattened beneath a low arch, listening with her chin, and stop too.

Nothing would happen.

I would wait until she moved, without ever discovering what had held us there.

***

One season, a crossing near her rear gallery would have to close.

I would have known it was worsening. A seam had opened along one side, and after a shower the underside continued dripping long after the neighboring roofs were dry. Still, the old path would save most of an hour. People would keep taking it while I tried to find a defensible line across.

The houseback would keep taking it too.

I would watch her go over one evening, stopping twice, and feel the temptation to count that as evidence.

Instead I would go underneath the next morning.

A supporting runner would have pulled free of the bank. It would still be attached at the far end, buried in enough interwoven growth that from above the damage showed only as a shallow dip.

Moving the markers would take the rest of the day.

The new route would be dull and inconvenient. It would climb a swell everyone had previously passed below, then turn back on itself to reach the seepage. I would shave patches for the stones, test each approach, and carry away the old pair.

For a while, people would complain to the woman rather than to me.

The crossing might last another year. I would have to bear that without putting the stones back.

***

By then, if you came over the rise at noon, you might find me lying on the warm ground.

My stick would be beside me. One sleeve would be rolled beneath my cheek to keep the small leaves from printing themselves into my skin.

At a distance, I might look as though I were listening through the mat, like the animal in her house. But I would mostly be resting. Human shoulders are badly arranged for working overhead in a passage. My arm would ache. I would have eaten the food meant for the return journey.

Below me the colonies would rasp and rattle. Somewhere along the ridge, the woman would be cutting her roof. The houseback would be asleep, or awake with her chin down; I would have no means of knowing.

In a little while I would get up and check the next marker.

Before leaving, I would put my hand into the hollow where my weight had pressed the leaves apart, feel for anything I had dropped, and lift out a small stone that had worked its way into my sleeve.

It would be brown, not pale. No use as a marker.

I would set it back where it came from.
