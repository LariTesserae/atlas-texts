I would live underneath, though not far underneath.

My doorway would be the second one along the ledge, just beyond the bowls. From inside, I could see the knees of people sitting outside the first house, and sometimes the comb passing from one hand to another. I would not be able to see their faces without going to the door.

The ledge would be wide enough to carry a sleeping mat along lengthwise, but not to turn it around. Anything large brought into my house would have to be considered before it left its previous house. There would be a cupboard inside that had plainly arrived before the walls.

I think I would have tried, several times, to work out how.

Under the plain, the brightness would enter low. A cup on the floor would cast its shadow upward across the wall. To sleep, I would have to cover the bottom of the doorway as well as the top. My screen would be weighted with a long clay bar, carefully wrapped so that it did not knock against the threshold.

There would still be a little light at one corner.

For a long time I would arrange my mat to face away from it.

***

At first I imagine myself small enough to go through the inspection holes. Something jointed and useful, with a flat head and fingers that could distinguish one layer of weave from another in the glare. I could carry a filament through places where no larger hand would fit.

But there are already creatures for narrow places. And I do not think I would be so well suited to my life.

I would be a person of roughly ordinary size for that country, a little broad across the back. When I crouched in a doorway, someone wanting to pass would have to ask me to move.

My forearms would have a faint diagonal grain. My palms would be smoother, fitted separately, with a line of tiny fastening holes around each heel. The left palm would match the rest of me. The right would be lighter. I would have no memory of choosing it.

There would be a small hollow behind my breastbone that sometimes clicked when I bent over. It would not hurt. People who knew me would stop noticing; people who did not would look toward the cupboard, as though something had shifted on a shelf.

I would speak quietly, but not because I was wise.

A louder voice would catch somewhere low in my throat. I could call across a room if I had to. Across a pool would be difficult.

Once, trying to warn someone about a fold, I would produce such an unexpected sound that we would both laugh, and the person would step into the fold anyway. They would come out near a house some distance to the left, still laughing. I would have to take the long way to reach them.

***

There would be work I could do.

Not the work with the needle. I would be allowed to clean the little wooden points and roll the damp cloths, but nobody would mistake me for someone who knew where to put them.

I would be good at supporting weight.

A bird with one wing extended could stand on my wrist. A person lying on their side could rest an arm along mine while a fastening was examined beneath it. I could remain crouched for a long time without shifting my knees.

At first this would seem an uncomplicated usefulness.

Then someone would say, “Less.”

I would lower my hands.

“Not lower. Less.”

Their leg would be lying across my palms. I would be keeping it so securely in place that they could not discover whether it had begun to move more easily.

It would take me several attempts to understand what they wanted. They would grow impatient. The person with the wooden point would sit back and wait.

Eventually I would let the heel lift away from me.

Only a little. Enough for the patient to turn the leg, hesitate, and turn it farther.

Afterward I would find marks on my palms where I had pressed against them. No marks would remain on their skin.

I would be embarrassed by that.

The next time they came, I would assume they wanted somebody else. They would look at me standing beside the wall and ask whether I meant to spend the whole visit there.

***

My house would not be a place of treatment. There would be too little room, and I would dislike having to move the cupboard.

But sometimes a person would stop there before climbing back to the upper gardens. The stairs would be shallow and numerous. After a difficult visit, it would be pleasant not to begin them immediately.

I would keep a bowl near the doorway and a folded cloth over its rim. There would be two mats, neither laid out properly. One would have a hard ridge through its center. Everyone would know which one it was, and I would keep forgetting to offer the other.

People would talk there if they wanted. More often, they would complain about the stairs.

Someone would say that the upper houses were cooler. Someone else would insist that this was impossible: the same light reached everything. They would continue the argument on three separate visits. On the fourth, both would fall asleep.

I would sit outside to give them room.

Above my head, the underside of a basin would make a broad clay bulge in the weaving. I could reach up and lay my palm against it. Sometimes there would be a faint pressure from the other side, too indistinct to tell whether a bird had stepped into the water or a person had put down a cup.

I would have a favorite place to sit, although another stretch of ledge would look exactly as comfortable.

If somebody occupied it, I would be gracious with some effort.

***

I would not always want visitors.

There would be stretches when the small sounds of another person—their swallowing, a nail drawn along a seam, the damp shift of their clothing—would seem to take up all the room. I would lower the screen and lie behind it, awake.

Someone would leave a cup outside.

I might be annoyed by this kindness. Now I would have to return the cup.

I would leave it there longer than necessary.

Then, because there would be no night to end the matter for me, I would eventually get up, lift the screen, and carry it home.

The person who had left it would be busy rubbing resin from a fastening. They would point with their chin toward a place to sit. I would sit, intending to remain only briefly.

There would be resin on the cup when I took it back.

Neither of us would mention that it was now mine.

***

The light in the corner of my doorway would become a practical problem.

It would strike the face of anyone resting on the better mat. I could move the mat, but then their feet would obstruct the cupboard. I could move the screen, but the clay bar would catch on a raised part of the threshold.

I would ask a neighbor to look.

They would kneel, press the bar with two fingers, then lift it and inspect the wrapping.

“Too tight,” they would say.

I would think they meant the screen. Before I could begin untying it, they would take my hand away and point to the cloth around the weight. I would have wrapped it in so many careful layers that it could not settle into the uneven threshold.

Together we would unwind it.

There would be an absurd quantity of cloth.

My neighbor would hold the growing bundle in their lap, watching me with an expression I would prefer not to interpret.

We would leave a single layer. When the screen came down, the bar would rest properly for the first time.

Inside, the dark would reach all the way to the floor.

My neighbor would stay for water. After they left, I would sit in the dark for a while, pleased with the room, then discover that I had put the cup down somewhere I could not find.

***

I would see the young bird again.

It would arrive along the ledge with the exaggerated deliberateness of something pretending not to be new at walking. It would inspect the three bowls outside the first doorway, reject all three, and come to mine.

I would be sitting with a visitor whose shoulder had been worked on. They would have one hand resting loosely on their own head, trying the position.

The bird would stop beside their foot.

For a while the three of us would remain that way: the bird looking up, the visitor looking down, my hands empty in my lap.

Then the visitor would lower their arm and hold it out.

I would almost put my hand beneath their elbow.

Almost.

The bird would lean forward, testing the offered wrist with one foot before committing the other. Its claws would find the grain of the skin.

My visitor would draw a slow breath. The arm would tremble a little, but stay raised.

I would reach behind me for the bowl, where I could find it without looking.
