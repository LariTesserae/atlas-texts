At first I imagine a room facing east, with a workbench directly under the window. But that is where I would put the workbench, not where I would find it. The window has been narrowed by a later wall. The light falls into a corner occupied by a tank too heavy to move, and whoever worked here before me arranged everything to be reached with the left hand.

I would spend my first winter reaching for things in the wrong places.

The room would be near the point where the workshops begin to sink into the ground. Not the last inhabited building. I would want to be able to go farther out, but I would not want everyone who went farther out to pass my door. There would be a footpath behind the house, partly concealed beneath the branches of a tree, which the people who lived nearby preferred to the street.

My roof would look like unoccupied ground from that path. This would cause difficulties.

Someone would have planted tubers in it.

***

I think my work would involve the signal lamps.

Not deciding what the signals meant, or keeping some complete account of what passed between the windows. Mostly I would repair shutters, clean contacts, replace the little springs that let a lamp return to darkness when a hand released its switch.

There would be several incompatible kinds. The oldest lamps would be the easiest to repair, except when they weren’t. A housing used for eighty years might open obligingly to reveal a mechanism assembled around a part nobody had made for sixty.

People would bring lamps wrapped in clothing and begin explaining the fault before setting them down.

“It answers slowly.”

“It answers when I haven’t touched it.”

“It works here. It knows perfectly well where it is.”

I would have a lamp of my own that did the last thing. I would keep it under the bench and produce it when someone seemed embarrassed.

I would not always be patient. People who said a mechanism needed only a moment’s attention would find that I had strong opinions about moments. I would charge too little for difficult repairs and then be unreasonable over the price of a replacement knob.

There would be a drawer of knobs I disliked selling.

***

As for what I would be: a person, I think, but not one particularly well suited to the town’s finest sense.

With my hand against a wall I could feel the larger pumps. I could tell a freight tram from a loom if the tram was close enough. The smaller distinctions would escape me.

I would have tried to learn. There would have been afternoons standing beside someone in a passage, our palms against the same damp stone.

“There,” they would say.

“Yes,” I would say, too soon.

Eventually I would stop doing that.

On the bench I would keep a shallow dish with a little water in it. A scrap of bright wire fixed above it would cast a crooked reflection. When the bench trembled, the reflection would break in ways I was beginning to recognize.

This would not make me secretly better at feeling the town than the people who could do it with their hands. A delivery cart could confuse the dish. So could my knee against the bench. Sometimes I would spend several minutes attending to a disturbance caused by an insect struggling at the rim.

But it would let me notice certain things while I worked.

I would become fond of looking up and finding the reflection steady.

***

Senn’s grandmother would dislike my first repair to her window switch.

I would have made it easier to press. That would seem an obvious improvement, given her hands.

She would send for me two evenings later.

“Now I have to keep hold of it,” she would say.

I would explain that it required less pressure.

She would look at me until I tried operating it with the sides of my fingers.

The old switch had offered something to lean against. I would put its stiff spring back and make a longer lever, bent so she could use her wrist. She would test it without thanking me, then ask why I had left such a sharp corner underneath.

After that I would bring unfinished things to her occasionally.

She would object to being used as a testing apparatus. She would also object if she discovered I had finished something without showing her.

Through her I would meet the new nursery operator, whom I would initially consider careless. Later I would spend an hour in the pump room and discover that the troublesome correction had to be made from a narrow platform, with one hand holding a valve that tried to close itself.

I would still think the operator careless about some things. But I would stop repeating what I had said before seeing the platform.

***

The roof garden would belong to Olin, or he would speak of it with sufficient confidence that I would assume it did.

When water first came through the ceiling, I would go looking for him with a basin containing plaster, soil, and three small roots.

He would examine the roots.

“Those aren’t mine.”

“The water is in my room.”

“Yes,” he would say. “I can see that.”

We would eventually lift a strip of planting and find an old gutter filled with copper moss. Clearing it would solve most of the trouble. The rest would require moving one of his beds, and this would take considerably longer to agree upon.

In exchange for leaving the deepest section planted, I would receive vegetables at irregular intervals. I would not know whether this was rent or an attempt to make me stop mentioning rent.

There would be something satisfying about hearing him work overhead while I sat at the bench. A scrape, a pause, the soft double knock of a tool laid down. In cold weather the room would smell faintly of wet roots.

I would begin calling it our roof only when he was not present.

***

I do not think I would investigate the returning water at first.

I would be occupied with keeping it out of the lamp housings stacked beneath the bench.

The house would stand across an old groove, one too shallow to have influenced the street but still deep enough to remember its direction. During an ordinary rain it would stay dry. During a return, water would enter beneath the rear wall and cross the floor toward the door.

Someone before me would have cut a drain at right angles to it.

The water would pass over the drain.

My first attempt to divert it would leave a black wet line up the legs of a cabinet and achieve nothing else. Afterward I would build shelves.

Then I would make a little bridge between the bench and the stove.

There would be a particular indignity in having to cross a stream to reach one’s kettle. I would complain about this often enough that visitors would begin asking whether the kettle was on this bank or the other.

One night a boat would come through.

It would emerge beneath the rear wall, turn once beside a table leg, and lodge against my shoe. It would have a roof made from a thin, bowed piece of wood, carefully fitted above a space scarcely large enough to admit a fingertip.

I would lift it out, look beneath, and find Senn’s mark.

For a while I would hold it near the lamp. The roof would make it difficult to see whether anything was inside.

I would resist taking it off.

Instead I would clear the loose wire from the floor and open the street door. The boat would hesitate at the sill, where an old nailhead caught its underside. I would ease it past with a screwdriver and watch it go between the paving stones.

In the morning I would find two grains of white sand in the hollow of my shoe.

One would disappear while I tried to transfer it to a folded paper. I would keep the other in a little screw-top container formerly used for lamp contacts, and forget to label it.

***

That is where I find myself becoming definite: not at the precipice, though I would go there; not in charge of the lamps, though several would be entrusted to me.

In a room with a badly placed window, learning the reach of another person’s hand.

I would have friends who came in without calling and customers whose footsteps I recognized with dismay. Some evenings I would close early and take the concealed path beyond the gardens. Other evenings I would work past the first collector drops without noticing them.

And once, late, the nursery operator would arrive carrying the window lamp I had lent them while theirs was being repaired.

They would set it on my bench.

“It’s doing it again.”

I would press the switch. The shutter would open cleanly, close, open again.

We would stand looking at it.

Outside, Olin would drag something heavy across the roof. A little soil would fall beside the tank.

“Leave it here tonight,” I would say. “Take mine.”

Then I would have to find mine, and remember what was wrong with it.
