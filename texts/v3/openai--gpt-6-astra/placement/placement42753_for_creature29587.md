At first I look for a place in the shelter.

There isn’t one. The space that seems available beside Meret’s bedding is where his knee must go when it will not bend. The gap behind the cooking stone holds fuel. Even the stretch of wall with nothing against it needs to remain empty, because water comes down there in weather that has not yet arrived.

I could make myself very small, but that is not the same as fitting.

So I think I would come up the path rather than already belong at its end. I would arrive carrying something that must be kept dry, with one hand inside my coat to make sure it was still there. I would have been told the hollow was just beyond the next cleft. There would have been several clefts.

By the time Essa saw me, I would have stopped believing in the directions but would still be following them.

What I carry is paper.

Not fine paper. Offcuts from the people who keep accounts below: narrow strips, spoiled sheets, margins removed from documents whose centers were worth saving. Some have writing on one side. Essa dislikes these because damp can bring the ink through. I have been told this already and have brought a few anyway, having misunderstood the difference between a preference and a requirement.

Also thread, two needles inside a reed, and a small parcel from her daughter.

“Did she send the wool?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did you ask?”

“No.”

Essa takes the parcel. This is the beginning of my usefulness, and a fair measure of it.

I would be someone who can write a clear, small hand. Down below, that is the first thing people mention when recommending me for work. Up here it would be several days before anyone found a use for it.

I know the names under which the seeds are sold. Those names turn out to be only partially connected to the plants.

One belongs to three different things. Another is the name of the man who used to buy them. A third means, approximately, the yellow sort, though there are yellow flowers everywhere and this particular plant is collected after its flowers have gone.

I have brought a little book.

Meret asks whether I intend to put the mountain in it.

“Only the things I need to remember.”

“You’ll want another.”

Essa tells him to give me something to eat.

***

I would not be an apprentice exactly. They have not asked for one, and I cannot afford a summer of learning without wages. I work for a seed seller who has grown tired of receiving packets labeled in ways only Essa understands. The seller has sent me to collect an order and settle several questions.

Why are there fewer seeds this year?

Can roots be supplied instead?

Can the blue-flowered kind be gathered earlier?

Essa answers the last question first.

“No.”

I wait, with the book open.

“No,” she says again, more helpfully.

The first journey ought to take three days, including the return. Weather makes it six.

That is how I acquire a place—not in the shelter, but in the cleft through which the path enters. A stone has lodged between its walls above head height. Beneath it there is room to lie down if I draw up my legs. Wind comes through at my ankles. They lend me a hide that smells strongly enough of its former owner to remain noticeable through several nights of smoke.

Meret helps me build a low lip of stones at the entrance.

“Leave that gap,” he says.

“For air?”

“For water.”

I had chosen it because the floor was dry.

***

For a while I think I would become their recorder.

I make columns. I distinguish the plants. I learn to ask which slope a packet came from, and whether the ground was wet there, and when the snow left it.

This seems like work I could do well.

Then Essa looks over a page and puts her dirty finger beside an entry.

“That wasn’t this one.”

“You said the western patch.”

“The other western patch.”

There is enough mountain west of us to occupy the rest of my book.

We go out together. She shows me the place she meant, which is distinguished from the first by a rock that resembles no particular thing. I draw it badly. Beneath the drawing I write: *split underneath; red grit; water audible but not visible.*

She studies this.

“You could put ‘Meret’s trousers.’”

“Why?”

“He tore them there.”

That name has apparently been in use for eleven years.

I keep the drawing.

But I begin to understand that I have not been sent to straighten out a muddle. I have walked into a system that works, carrying instructions for a different one.

There is still work for me. Someone below must be told that the packet called Meret’s trousers contains the pale, late-flowering variety and not the common early one. Someone must explain why an order cannot be doubled simply because the price has been raised.

I can do that. Whether the seller will like having me do it is another question.

***

I would have an irritating habit.

Probably I repeat things aloud while counting. Not loudly enough to be useful to anyone else. Just a murmur that continues until Essa looks up.

And I would be too ready to lift things to see what is beneath them.

A drying tray. A flat stone. A cloth covering a compartment of the basket.

Once, on an almost windless morning, I lift the wrong cloth.

The seeds do not scatter spectacularly. There is only a small sideways movement, a dark thinning across pale rock. By the time I put the cloth down, part of the harvest is in the runnel.

For a moment I say nothing. The loss looks so much smaller than the work that made it.

Then I call Essa.

She gets down on her knees. We use damp fingertips. Meret brings the bowl and blocks a shallow groove with the side of his hand.

Some seeds we recover. Some are grit. Some cling to torn petals and go over the edge.

Afterward I ask how much I owe.

Essa tells me.

It is more than I expected. She does not reduce it because I look stricken.

For the rest of that visit, I sort under cover. When I go down, I carry a lighter order and a note explaining why. I have to write that note twice. The first version makes the wind seem responsible.

***

I think I would return.

Not immediately with some grand feeling about having found my place. I would return because there was another order, because part of my payment was still owed, because Essa’s daughter had wool ready this time.

And because below, in the shop, I had opened a packet and recognized the smell of the cleft where I slept.

On the next journey I bring paper with no writing on either side. Fewer sheets, better chosen. I carry a needle broad enough for the cord in the bowl’s rim, after measuring the old one against my smallest fingernail.

I also bring news of the copper pot.

The trader still has it. The lid fits badly.

“Badly how?” Essa asks.

I try to show her with my hands.

She makes me start again.

Eventually I draw the pot in my book, larger than any of the plants. We discuss the rolled edge, the handle attachments, the dent beneath the rim. Meret says a dent will affect the taste.

Essa and I both look at him.

It is the first time we have done anything together without arranging it.

***

My place would remain the cleft.

Even after there is space in the shelter—on an evening when Meret goes down early, perhaps—I find I would rather sleep in the place I have learned. I know where to put my hip. I know which part of the hide must be folded twice. I have wedged a little shelf into a crack, then moved it when the first long rain showed me why the crack was empty.

There is a spider beneath it. I damage her web every time I take down my cup until I shift the cup a hand’s breadth to the left.

I do not name her. I have enough difficulty with the plants.

By late summer, Essa lets me take one of the shallow baskets to a slope above the path. Nothing difficult grows there, she says. Then she gives me so many instructions that I have to set the basket down.

I come back with less than she would have gathered.

She examines it while I wait.

“These are ready,” she says.

That is all. We spread them out.

I would like that: a small task whose success can be inspected, followed by supper. I would also like, sometimes quite badly, to be below, where a wall stays visible all afternoon and there is room to straighten my legs in bed. On those evenings I would be poor company. Meret might try a joke twice before abandoning me to my book.

I would not stay through the winter. I would not take over the hollow or learn everything the two of them know. Some years another person might carry the paper, and I would have to ask them how the runnel was flowing, whether the bowl had split, whether Essa had bought the pot.

But for part of each summer there would be three cups to secure.

Mine would be the one with a thread around its handle, because I still set it down badly when I am tired. Essa would call after me as I left it on the rock.

Eventually, once or twice, I would turn back before she called.
