I would first look beneath the plate.

There is room there for a body to draw its legs inward, for something approaching to be tested before it is endured. The mite has a place to retreat to. Even with the light reaching underneath, there is a difference between the exposed shore and the low ceiling over its back. I would be slow to leave that difference.

But the water is already entering. Shelter here does not mean being left alone. It means that what arrives has lost some of its force.

I think I would be farther beneath the lip than the mite can go, in the joint where the underside of the plate almost touches a fallen chip. Not in the narrowest part. There the water remains, but scarcely changes. A little nearer the opening, a warm thread passes intermittently, bringing material from the wall.

At first, I might be no more than something caught.

A loosened piece of the bright film, folded against a crystal. Part of it still works. Charge passes through an assembly whose next connection is missing. Useful material arrives and departs because there is nothing ready to receive it. For a while, the fragment does less than it did before it was torn away.

There would be no moment at which I could say I had arrived. The current would press me into an irregularity. Another fragment would lodge nearby. Between us, a little water would persist after the exposed face had dried.

That is where I would begin to have a location.

Not the hollow. Not even the chip. A seam along its lower edge, near enough to the passing water to receive something, recessed enough to keep it.

The first thing I retained might become part of a repair. The repair might make it possible to retain something else.

I would be the fifth patch, though there would be no fifthness in me. The other patches would arrive as altered water, as loose components, as substances already taken up or released. Their brightness would fall across my surface without telling me how far away they were.

What worked on the rear wall would not all work here.

Some arrangements would persist uselessly for a time. The heat would be insufficient, or would arrive too briefly. A passage would open in anticipation of nothing; its exposed surfaces would begin to come apart. Elsewhere, something less exacting would continue. The little I could keep would be distributed unevenly.

My body would develop a wet side and a side that repeatedly had to be abandoned.

I would not willingly give up the exposed face. When water spread across it, I would spread too. There is a shallow scar there with space for a much larger holding. For several minutes each afternoon, perhaps, it would be suitable. I would enter it, establish connections, move calcium into a better fit.

Then the water would withdraw.

The edge would fail first. Some pieces would be recovered. Others would remain attached beyond the reach of the retreating film, still fitted to a surface on which they could no longer function.

The next wetting would bring me across them.

So there would already be remains of me on this new stone. Not an old coating, but a few interrupted attempts, distinguishable from the bare mineral only by what could happen when water returned.

Near nightfall, the chip would cool faster than the wall. This would change almost everything. Water would linger, while the energy available to use it diminished. I would be widest when I had least strength to maintain that width.

A faint light might appear along the protected seam.

It would not illuminate the mite. It might not be separable from the reflected brightness at all. Yet in one small part of the patch, the reactions would be completing. Material that had arrived loose would now be held in a working surface.

Above me, the animal would shift.

One foot would touch the chip, close enough to disturb the meniscus. The water would pull sideways. For an instant a strip of my edge would be exposed, and then covered again. A few components would leave with the motion. The animal would clean its leg.

I would continue with less.

Later, a ceiling drop would strike the plate. Most of it would run toward the basin, but some would enter the joint and press through my seam. The flow would be cooler than what I had been receiving. A passage would close. Another part of me would swell and loosen.

The chip itself would move slightly.

Until then, one corner would have rested against the plate. Now a grain beneath it would turn, opening a gap. Water would begin to pass where it had previously stayed.

For a while, I would lose material faster than I could catch it.

Then, along the newly wetted underside, there would be a rough place.

I would reach it with what was still attached.
