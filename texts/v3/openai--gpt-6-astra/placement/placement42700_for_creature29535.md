I would be behind the meal pots, with one foot against the wall and the other pressed under a shelf.

There is more room here than the keeper thinks. A seam runs up the back of the stone, too shallow for their fingers but deep enough for mine. It contains an old cord, a flattened husk, and a layer of meal that has escaped from the pots over many feedings. I have been working the cord loose. When I pull, something farther up the wall makes a small answering movement.

I do not yet know that this might be a reason to stop.

From here, the shelter is mostly ankles. The keeper passes on the edges of their feet, carrying their weight differently according to what they hold. The meal pot brings both feet flat. The hooked pole makes one foot turn outward. When they return without a tool, they sometimes remain in the doorway long enough for me to come out.

I usually do not. I put two fingers around the edge of the lowest pot.

They put a pinch of food within reach.

This arrangement has developed between us. Neither of us is entirely satisfied with it.

***

I would be the small one, though I would not know myself by smallness. The basket fits me badly now; that is a property of the basket. The gap behind the pots fits well. The keeper’s hand is a difficult object, warm and full of joints, which can lift me even when I am holding something that ought to prevent it.

I know them first through the floor.

There is a scrape before the weight comes down, then a second, shorter scrape. One side lands more carefully. I can feel their return before I can hear it, with my jaw against the bench or my feet spread inside the basket.

Other things announce themselves through stone. The seed eaters strike. The roots make an almost continuous pressure that becomes noticeable only when it stops. Far below, there are movements I feel through my whole underside. At those times the keeper becomes quick.

I am learning that some kinds of warmth stay where they are, and some are about to arrive.

The warmth in the bench is dependable. I can lie across it with my throat open and let a mouthful of meal work its way inward. The warmth that comes through a fissure tickles the folds under my limbs. I like it more.

The keeper repeatedly removes me from it.

For a long time, this is one of the less comprehensible things about them.

***

After the flowering, their elbow tastes bitter.

I discover this while trying to remove the new wax. Its surface has a little raised edge, exactly the sort of edge my fingers have become good at catching. The keeper moves my hand away. I put it back. They move it again, then fold a strip of leaf skin over the repair and bind it down.

The binding conceals the edge but introduces a knot.

Eventually they give me the wooden ring.

Their other hand remains underneath me. Even after I stop vibrating, it does not go away. I hold the ring with both hands and push one foot between their fingers. The folds along their throat move unevenly. Every so often a few grains escape behind the jaw.

Outside, something breaks. The sound reaches us partly through the bench.

The keeper shifts forward.

My foot closes.

They settle back.

I do not know what has fallen, or what it might be crushing. I know that the large warm surface against my back has stopped moving toward the door. I work a finger into the ring’s central hole. There is a grain lodged there that I cannot get out.

***

For several days I am attached to things.

The cord runs from my garment to a leg of the bench. Later it runs to the keeper. It catches under pots, passes around a tool handle, and once pulls an entire folded garment onto my head. I learn to follow it backward when something prevents me from going forward.

The keeper learns to look behind them before turning.

We make little progress across the shelter.

Outside, I am carried beneath the overlapping skins of their garment, against the unscarred side. The garden reaches me in pieces: a golden ceiling; a gripping foot larger than my body; animals running upside down; the blunt end of a branch approaching and being pushed aside. Dust gathers in the crease above my eyes.

Sometimes the keeper’s body tightens before the leaves move.

Sometimes I tighten too, because they have.

Much later I will distinguish that from knowing for myself.

For now I put my face against the opening in the garment. My filaments catch a thin downward movement of air. I pull back. A fan shuts where my face has been.

The keeper stops working.

Two fingers touch the top of my head, parting the filaments without bending them. Then the hand goes on to the cord it was tying.

I put my face out again, but not as far.

***

I would not become useful all at once.

My first attempts at tending would look like damage. There is a cord around a young creeper, and I have spent much of my life learning what to do with cords. I loosen it. The creeper moves. Encouraged, I loosen another.

The keeper finds me with a length of successful work gathered between my feet.

They sit down so abruptly that I retreat under a leaf.

For a while they do nothing. Then they retrieve one end of the cord and draw the creeper back. They must pull harder than before. A translucent plant beneath it has collapsed along one side, and pale grains are collecting on the stone.

I reach for those.

The keeper covers them with a hand.

We remain like that, my fingers on the back of their hand, the meal underneath going nowhere it can be used.

When they repair the second tie, they place my foot against the creeper. I feel its steady turning pressure. They let it push me a little way across the rock before drawing it back.

The next time I find a cord outdoors, I hold it before I pull.

This is not yet restraint. I want to feel what is attached.

But it gives the keeper time.

***

There would be things I kept from them.

A striped caller’s empty throat casing, which breaks into several disappointing pieces beneath the bench. A seed I carry for days before eating it. The old cord from behind the pots, eventually freed along with a fall of flakes and a noise that brings the keeper inside at speed.

And one thing I do not bring back.

Near the northern thicket, under the root that has lifted clear of its old groove, there is a space with fibers caught deep in its walls. I can get into it headfirst. Turning around is difficult.

Inside, the wind is reduced to touches at my heels.

I find myself pressing outward with my feet and the backs of my hands. My throat swells in the confined warmth. There is a movement it wants to make, an old whole-body effort for which I no longer have quite the right shape.

Nothing opens.

I lie there until the pressure in my throat subsides. Then I scrape backward, bringing fibers out on my garment.

The keeper is nearby, trimming a dead sheath from a root. They see the fibers. Their hand pauses, the cutting flake held away from the wood.

I go past them toward the shelter.

They finish the cut.

***

In time, I would have a place at the edge of their work: beneath the branch they are lowering, above the wedge they cannot reach without changing their grip.

I would learn to keep one hand free. Not always. There would still be dark wood worth picking up, cases worth opening, something moving inside a split that I would want to feel move again.

The basket would stay under the bench after I stopped sleeping in it. I would continue putting things there. The keeper would continue returning things to it that they did not want underfoot.

One evening I would be holding a creeper away from a wind lane while they secured its new tie. A current would pass low across the stone. Before the broad leaves turned, I would shift my weight and open my inward toes against the root.

The keeper would glance at my feet.

Then they would pass me the loose end of the cord.

I would take it with the proper pair of fingers. They would test my hold before reaching for the wedge, and I would feel, through the cord, the brief extra weight of their hand.
