I would arrive with the right address and the wrong entrance.

The entrance would exist. That would make it worse. Through its wired glass I could see a clean corridor, a bench, a row of coat hooks: all the ordinary assurances that I had found the place. My key would turn halfway.

Someone carrying folded aprons would eventually explain that this door had been taken out of use when the corridor became part of a food-packing enclosure. She would give me directions from the other side of the glass. I would hear most of them.

For the first few months, I would carry a folded route home in my pocket. At the bottom, in my own handwriting, it would say: **If you reach the aprons, you have gone too far down.**

***

My room would be near a change in the district’s numbering.

Not directly above Irena, but close enough that we might share a lift for part of a journey without knowing we were neighbors. A narrow room divided from a larger one, with a bathroom fitted into the recess where a machine control cabinet had stood. There would be unused screw holes in the bathroom wall, filled carefully but not painted.

The window would face the underside of a bridge.

I would have been shown the room at eleven in the morning, when light reflected from the bridge’s wet flank onto the ceiling. Later I would discover that this happened only when the bridge was empty. Loaded carts would interrupt it, passing across the room as broad, slow dimmings.

I would buy a lamp before buying a proper bed.

There would be a ledge outside the window, too narrow for a planter and reached by almost no rain. I would try growing something there anyway. Not a fern; everyone would recommend a fern. I would want thyme, and would spend a year moving a pot of increasingly bare stems between the window and the kitchen light.

On visits, people would politely crush a leaf between their fingers to see if it still smelled.

***

The work that brought me there would sound temporary.

A contractor would be updating access drawings. Most of the work would already be finished, according to the contractor. They would need one more person to resolve a few discrepancies before handover.

I would sit in a former dispatch office beside a lift that no longer carried passengers. My desk would have a shallow drawer too wide for my knees, once used for consignment sheets. When I pulled it open, the monitor would tremble.

There would be drawings of every level.

That would be the first difficulty.

The second would be that the drawings answered different questions. A fire drawing would show a route through a door that could be opened only from the other side. A lease plan would give a wall ownership but no thickness. A machinery plan would leave out a stair because the stair was not machinery.

At first I would make lists of inconsistencies. Then I would be sent to look at them.

I would become a familiar person to find standing in a passage, holding a tape measure with no obvious place to attach its other end.

“Are you here about the smell?”

“No.”

“Could you put it down anyway?”

I would put it down.

By the end of the first year, most of the work would still be finished.

***

I would not be especially good at understanding machinery.

This seems important. I could learn symbols and follow pipes a long way without knowing whether a sound inside one was normal. I would look too long at the object that appeared to be causing the problem. Irena would have to say “Upstream” to me more than once, and once she would say it when I was only trying to find somewhere to put my bag.

But I would be good at returning.

If someone said a passage became impassable after the afternoon deliveries, I would come back in the afternoon. If two workers disagreed about a stair, I would walk it with each of them.

One would be carrying nothing. The other would be pulling a cleaning cart.

The disagreement would survive, but it would become possible to describe.

My reports would acquire photographs of handles, measurements taken with doors fully open, little observations that annoyed the people responsible for keeping them brief.

*Accessible* would be returned to me with a request for clarification.

I would begin to dislike that word.

***

The first serious mistake I made would involve a door that opened perfectly well.

It would lead between a residential corridor and a sheltered route toward the passenger lift. The route would be shorter than the one on the existing notice. I would check the width, the lighting, the incline. I would walk it twice.

At the evening change of shift, the door would lock automatically.

A woman returning from treatment at a clinic would reach it with two shopping bags and have to go back uphill. She would not be injured. In the complaint, someone would underline this.

I would meet her later to correct the directions. She would be wearing a soft cap and would remember exactly where she had stopped to rest.

I would want to tell her that I had tested the door.

She would be showing me where the handrail ended. There would be no useful reason to interrupt.

Afterward I would start writing the hour beside every visit in my notebook. For a while I would also check things at unreasonable times, staying late without being asked, as though sufficient inconvenience to myself could revise hers.

Eventually I would stop doing that. The hours would remain.

***

Irena would first call me in because I had drawn a line through her kitchen.

Not through the room on the plan. Through the view.

A proposed inspection route would run along the repaired alder trough. In my photograph, the glazing behind it would reflect foliage, and I would have mistaken the window for part of the testing hall.

She would let me stand in her kitchen and see the route from there.

Outside, someone’s boots would pass at counter height.

“Yes,” I would say.

“Yes,” she would say.

We would have tea, though not immediately. First she would make me explain who could authorize the change. When I said that I only checked the drawings, she would ask why I had brought a pen.

Her granddaughter would be there, sitting at the table with one foot hooked around a chair leg. She would ask to see my plans.

She would find them disappointing.

“Where’s underneath?”

I would turn to the next sheet.

“No. Underneath that bit.”

What she meant would be a sheltered wedge beneath a stair, open at one end and too low for an adult to stand in. On the drawing it would be included in the stair’s footprint.

She would ask me to draw it.

I would make a small dotted outline.

She would watch my hand.

“It goes further back.”

***

By then I would own a table too large for my room.

I would have bought it from a man three levels away, after measuring the space where it was to stand but not the turn outside my door. Its legs would come off. Two would go back on properly.

For several weeks I would eat above a folded piece of cardboard while insisting I intended to repair the joint.

This would be the table on which I sorted my notebooks, repotted the unsuccessful thyme, and opened letters I had carried home unopened for days. I would leave its surface mostly clear before visitors came, stacking everything on the bed.

There would be a period when I considered leaving.

The job would have become permanent without becoming secure. My room would receive no direct sun even in summer. I would be tired of dressing to take the rubbish out, tired of damp cuffs, tired of explaining to people elsewhere that I did not live in a greenhouse.

I would look at rooms beyond the saddle. One would have an apple tree visible from the bath.

Then my own building would change its entry system, and I would spend three evenings helping a neighbor whose hands shook register a new key. This would not persuade me to stay. I would resent those evenings.

But afterward, when I came home late, she would sometimes have taken my washing off the shared drying rack before the room cooled.

I would keep looking at the advertisements. Less often.

***

I think I would become a person people stopped in doorways.

Not an authority. Not someone who knew the whole district. Someone believed to have written down the name of the person who might know.

This would suit me on some days and make me take a longer way home on others.

I would learn to say, “I can find out,” without always offering to.

There would still be places I had never entered. Whole inhabited levels would remain behind doors whose lettering I knew only from the outside. Sometimes, waiting for a freight crossing, I would see somebody on an unfamiliar balcony shake crumbs from a cloth and feel briefly surprised that there were so many kitchens.

***

One evening I would be sent to check a temporary waiting area beside a moving bridge.

The marked rectangle would be clear, the lamp visible, the rail sound. Everything on the form would be satisfactory.

I would stay for a complete cycle.

When the bridge moved, cold water retained beneath its deck would spill across the rectangle.

Not much. Enough to soak the shoulders of anyone waiting there.

I would step away too late.

Later, in the dispatch office, I would move the rectangle a meter to the left on the drawing. Then I would go back with chalk to check that the new position left room for a cart.

By then the lamps would be on behind the greenhouse screens. The opposite slope would have disappeared. Someone coming home would pause beside me and ask whether the bridge was broken.

“No. I’m finding somewhere to wait.”

They would stand where I pointed.

We would wait through the next cycle together.
