I would be close to the ground, but not beneath it.

At first there is the attraction of a fissure: the cooler air, the roots descending toward something the leaves cannot reach. But too much passes down those openings. Water, when there is water. Teeth. A whole loosened shelf of stone. I would want a place from which I could withdraw my limbs.

Perhaps I would live at the height where the hanging roots begin to thicken, low enough to receive what feeding animals spill, high enough to escape the first closing of a crack. Something patient, with many points of attachment.

But I keep returning to the hands.

Not the hand raising the hammer. The other one, holding the root steady.

I think I would be a stone-carrier whose body has become particularly good at that part of the work.

Not unusually old. Old enough that one shoulder does not fold as closely as the other. When I sleep, someone usually ends up with a foot in the gap this leaves. I wake, bite the foot without much force, and go back to sleep with it still there.

On my left hand, the shortest finger is missing its end. The remaining joint closes against the long fingers but leaves a little opening. Thin roots slip through it. Thick ones do not.

This has helped determine where I sit.

At a feeding place I get behind the nodule, with the root passing between my knees. One foot braces against stone. The other finds bark or another body. I draw the root toward me until its fibers take the strain. Through my belly I can feel someone on the far side adjusting a hammer.

We are not looking at each other. There is hardly room for our faces.

The first blow travels through the rind, the root, my wrists, my teeth.

I loosen my hold.

A complaint comes from the other side.

I pull again, but at a slightly different angle.

The next blow is better.

That would be much of my life: finding the position in which another animal’s effort stops being wasted. Then fighting that animal for the wet opening we have made.

I would not be gentle about my share. My mouth would already be at the seam when the shell gave. The striker would push at my brow, and I would close my eyes against its nails and continue drinking. Beneath us, the little living bowls would tilt hopefully toward fluid that I had no intention of losing.

Some would get it anyway.

***

I have a hammer of my own.

It is shorter than most, heavy for its size, with a depression that fits the side of my damaged finger. When I am startled, that is where the finger goes. Before I have found the direction of a call, before my eyes have made anything of a brief scattering of insect light, I have turned the stone into its familiar position.

I do not know its color.

I know where it is smooth when wet. I know the slight drag of one end against the skin of my carrying fold. Once, after a fall, I searched for it among fragments still warm from their breaking. I touched several pieces before finding it. Each wrong piece required almost no time to refuse.

A young animal follows me for part of one dry interval.

It is not always the same distance away. Sometimes it rides against my flank without permission. Sometimes it spends an entire feeding session trying to pull apart my fur, interested in a scab that I cannot comfortably reach. It has a hammer too large for its belly fold and carries it under its chin when climbing, making small obstructed complaints.

I try to take this stone once.

The youngster bites the loose skin of my wrist.

After that I let it carry the thing.

At night, when we turn and exchange the hot places in the sleeping fork, I occasionally find the oversized hammer jammed beneath my ribs. I push it outward. The youngster wakes before it falls.

***

The change beneath the forest does not first arrive as a warning.

It is an absence in something I am holding.

We are feeding on a root drawn taut across a shallow hollow. The nodule has a thick rind. My partner has been working at it long enough that its blows are becoming less careful. Each strike gives me a hard pulse followed by a smaller trembling, a faint return from somewhere beyond my grip.

Then the return stops.

I shift my foot. Pull harder.

The striker hits again.

There is only the blow.

I put one wrist against the bare stone beside my leg.

Nothing happens that would be visible from above. A carrier crouches. Another waits with its hammer lifted. The youngster noses at a splinter of shell.

Through the patch beneath my wrist comes a slow pushing sensation, interrupted by something finer. I lift my hand and set it down farther away.

The striker reaches through the leaves and pulls at my fur.

I push its hand off.

That makes it angry. It strikes the unopened nodule without waiting for me to brace it. The root jumps. Its hammer slips sideways, and all of us hear the wrong note.

Now the youngster is still.

I leave the root and climb.

Not high at first. Up to the nearest fork, where I turn and press my wrist to the trunk. The wood carries too many things: feet moving above me, a grazer rubbing somewhere below, the drumming of another group across the basin.

I descend half a body length.

The others are following now, which makes descending difficult. A mouth opens beside mine. Someone shoves a knee against my damaged hand.

I bite.

For several breaths we are simply a quarrel blocking a narrow route.

Then the root below us draws straight.

Leaves lift from its surface. A cup-animal clinging beneath it folds its rim shut.

The stone on one side of the hollow begins to move.

***

I would like to give myself certainty here: the right tree, the right branch, a body made wise by all its earlier contacts.

Instead, I think I would go toward the old sleeping fork.

It is close. My feet know the first three holds. Even with animals climbing over me, even with the ground making the low sound that fills the jaw, I can find those holds.

At the third, my hand closes around bark that comes away.

Underneath is dry fiber.

I hold the piece for a moment.

Above me, the fork is occupied. Two adults have wedged themselves in the usual places. One is reaching down toward the youngster with the large stone.

The trunk shifts without springing back.

I retreat sideways into a mass of climbers. They will not bear me individually. I catch several, slide, catch an aerial root with my good hand. My hammer presses hard against my belly. A foot strikes my face.

The youngster has followed.

Its stone falls past my shoulder.

It reaches after it.

I catch the fur above its hip and get teeth in my forearm for doing so. Then its feet fasten around the root, and I can let go.

Across the hollow, insects flare in a tall, narrow sheet. For an instant I see the sleeping tree leaning through them, full of hands.

Then there is too much movement to distinguish one body from another.

***

Afterward, my arm swells where the youngster bit it.

This matters. It changes how I climb. It makes holding a root difficult. When another carrier presses against that side, I turn with my mouth open before knowing who it is.

We search the fallen crown.

There are leaves still folded around their stores of water. Under one branch an animal is alive but cannot pull itself free. We tug at it until it bites us. Later, when the wood has shifted, it comes out by itself, dragging a hind limb.

Elsewhere there is a hand that does not close when touched.

I find a hammer in the litter and carry it up.

For several days I have two stones. The extra one rests against my chest when I move. At feeding places I put it between my feet.

The youngster takes it once. I take it back.

Later it takes it again, and I am occupied with the swollen arm. Its first blows are too light. The stone is smaller than the one it lost, and it keeps raising it farther than necessary.

I sit behind the nodule.

The root is thin enough to slip through my damaged grip, so I wrap it once around my wrist. This hurts. I adjust the loop.

Across from me, the youngster taps, listens, and taps again.

The rind gives a little.

I bring my mouth close to the place where it will open. The youngster pushes my face away with its foot.

Under my belly, a beetle begins climbing toward the expected spill.

I move one knee to make room for my own foot, and the beetle is sheltered there.
