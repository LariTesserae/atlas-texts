I would live near a passage that is open less often than the maps imply.

Not at its mouth. There would be too much company there: people arriving early, testing the rail, asking whether the pressure had begun to fall. My room would be a little way back, behind a tiled partition whose upper edge had been repaired with the wrong color. From my bed I could hear travelers setting down their carriers. I would know some of them by how long they waited before setting them down.

There would be two hooks beside my door. One would hold my clothes. The other would be for a visitor’s, though I would have begun hanging a bag there and would need to move it whenever anyone stayed.

I think I would be one of the people who tend the sleepers.

At first, only an assistant. There are ways to turn a body without letting its weight drag across the shoulder, and I would be afraid of getting them wrong. My teacher would have me practice with a sack filled with wet cloth. Then she would watch me turn someone who had been asleep for forty days.

“Your hand is in the right place,” she would say. “You can stop looking at it.”

The sleeper would be heavier than I expected. A little water would escape beneath their neck. I would think I had hurt them.

My teacher would put her own hand there, wait, then take it away.

Afterward we would eat in a recess outside the room. She would complain about the food. I would still be thinking about the neck.

***

My body would belong here, but not equally well in every district.

I would have narrow palms and broad feet, good for standing on the wet service ledges. The grooves along my left side would be deeper than those on my right. In dry air, that side would begin to ache first. I would carry a small pad of folded cloth to put inside my garment, and sometimes forget it until I was too far from home to turn back.

I would dislike being watched while replenishing myself.

Other people can stand on a wet tile and conduct an argument, finish a meal, receive a visitor. I would find that difficult. There would be something about the first few minutes, the slackness leaving my wrists, that made conversation feel like being asked questions while swallowing.

This would have given me a reputation for reserve that was only partly deserved.

With people I knew, I would talk too much about small failures of municipal equipment. I would have a particular grievance against a replacement vent grille that could only be removed after loosening a pipe bracket. Someone close to me would have heard about it so often that they could make me stop merely by saying, “The bracket.”

***

Eventually I would have six rooms on my round.

Not six patients all the time. Two rooms might be empty, another occupied by someone resting between ordinary shifts. But each would have a different bed, different vents, a particular place where sediment gathered.

In the farthest room, the water would enter a little too warmly. I would draw it first into a shallow dish and wait. In another, the bed would darken unevenly, leaving a dry island beneath the knees unless I adjusted the feed.

There would be records, of course. I would keep them carefully.

But I would also write things that did not belong in the official columns.

*Likes the door left open after waking.*

*Ask before moving the cup.*

*Brother’s name is the same as the western gate. Do not shorten it.*

My teacher would object to this last note. Not because it was unnecessary, but because I had put it where a future attendant might mistake it for a warning about the gate.

I would copy the page out again.

Some of the sleepers I would know only through their arrangements. A garment folded with its fastenings inward. A little pile of counters, one missing. A request that a certain relative not be notified until the person could sit up.

Then they would wake and become inconveniently unlike the people I had assembled from these things.

One would be funny. One would speak to me as though I had personally caused the interval. One would ask for a window in a room buried under forty meters of stone.

I would begin to explain.

“I know where I live,” they would say. “I was having a dream.”

That would stay with me.

***

I would not always be the attendant.

There would come a week when I kept dropping the end of a sentence. The warm feed water would feel merely lukewarm. I would go home intending to eat and instead spend an hour cleaning the hinges of the cupboard.

I would recognize the signs and still resent the person who pointed them out.

Before lying down, I would leave too many instructions. Which dish to use in the far room. Where I kept the clean cloths. How to lift the stubborn grille. I would return a borrowed cutting tool and then go back to ask whether I could borrow it again when I woke.

My teacher, older now but still working, would read my pages.

“You have written down how to do our work,” she would say.

“Yes.”

“We know how to do it.”

I would have no useful answer.

When I woke, my room would be clean in a way I disliked. The bag would be off the visitor’s hook. Someone would have moved the bed a hand’s breadth away from the wall.

For a while I would lie there hearing the passage outside, unable to tell which part of its cycle I was listening to.

Then a person I had once turned and washed would bring me a cup. I would need both hands to hold it.

They would wait without trying to help.

I think that is where I would become more fully a person of this city: with my instructions followed imperfectly, my work done by others, and a cup steadied between palms that had not yet recovered their strength.

***

The person repairing the chair would know me.

Not intimately. We would have shared a meal once, during a closure that lasted longer than expected. I would have borrowed their mat and returned it late. They would still mention this when lending me things.

Their friend in the lower district would live near one of my rooms.

For several cycles I would have carried messages between them, none formally acknowledged as messages.

The air is better there.

The chair has been repaired.

There is no point coming down while the eastern connection is unreliable.

The eastern connection was repaired some time ago.

I would transmit these remarks accurately, then begin to tire of accuracy.

One evening I would find the chair outside again. Its owner would be rubbing a thumb over the ceramic extension on the short leg, although there was nothing wrong with it.

I would stand beside them on the wet paving.

“I am going down after the second drop,” I would say.

They would tell me they had not asked.

“I know.”

A little water would gather around my feet. Beneath the stairs, the pale animal would lift itself and wait.

There are people who would know what to say next. I would not be one of them. I would look at the chair, then at the doorway, calculating whether it could pass through the narrower turn on the lower route.

“We don’t need to take it,” they would say.

“No.”

“They have chairs.”

“Yes.”

But when the second float dropped, they would come.

I would carry a sealed meal and the cloth pad for my side. They would lock their door, test it, then return to move the chair farther under shelter.

At the first junction they would ask how long the journey took.

I would almost give the official answer. Then I would remember the ledge where we could rest, and the warmer passage I preferred, and the time their friend would probably be eating.

“We should arrive hungry,” I would say.

We would take the stairs while the handrail was warm.
