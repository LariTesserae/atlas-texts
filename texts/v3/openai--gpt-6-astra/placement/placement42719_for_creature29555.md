I think I would be under the third reservoir stair, though not at the bottom of it.

There is a landing halfway down where the stair turns around a square pier. On the reservoir side, the pier is faced with pale ceramic. On the other side it has been opened, closed, and opened again so often that the access panel no longer fits. One corner stands proud of the wall. Someone has covered it with a strip of yellow rubber, because it is exactly the right height to catch a child across the forehead.

I would live behind that panel.

My first acquaintance with most people would be their shoes.

***

I was built to inspect enclosed spaces. There are several versions of me in the district, but few enough that people say *the little inspector* without causing much confusion.

My body is the size of a bread tin. I have six legs, two gripping arms, a lamp, and a contact head that I press against metal to listen through it. The head can also send a pulse into a surface and measure what comes back. It is quite a good way to find certain kinds of damage. Other kinds remain invisible until someone removes the paint.

My feet have replaceable pads. Eda cuts mine from sheet stock because the proper replacements have not been available for years. She has a punch for making the holes, and a template she distrusts.

“Hold that still.”

I can hold it much stiller than she can.

“Not like that. Let me turn it.”

I am less good at letting her turn it.

One of my hind legs is newer than the others. It folds a little differently. In a narrow passage I must remember to bring that leg through last, or I catch myself by the knee.

There are scratches on almost everything I own.

***

Behind the panel is my charging socket, a shallow drip tray, and a cable that once went somewhere important. It now ends in a capped connector.

A narrow pipe passes through the space overhead. At one time it carried warm water continuously. Now it warms only when the kitchens above the reservoir rinse their filters, which happens at inconsistent hours and is accompanied by a smell that reaches even the sealed parts of me.

I do not need the warmth. Nevertheless, I usually settle on that side of the tray.

My route begins beneath the stair. I pass through the gap beside the second stringer, descend to the cross-bracing, and work outward along the brackets that hold the reservoir walkway. There are marks I recognize without illuminating them: a weld like three spilled grains of rice; the washer with a crescent missing; a small gray patch where someone tested a coating and never returned to inspect the result.

Once every twelve days I report that the patch remains attached.

This is still on the schedule.

Above me, people carry groceries and complain about the number of steps. Their voices are muffled, but the footsteps are clear. I can distinguish Eda from other workers when she is descending. She gives her weight to the stair in a particular sequence, favoring the shoulder even though a shoulder ought not to have much to do with walking.

When she stops directly overhead, I turn off my lamp.

She has explained that this is unnecessary. The light cannot shine through a stair.

I still prefer to hear her before she knows exactly where I am.

***

I do speak, but not very well.

My voice comes from a grille underneath my body. On a flat surface it is intelligible. Hanging beneath a walkway, I sound as though I am calling from inside a cupboard. In wind, I generally wait.

Most of what I say consists of numbers.

Eda has asked me not to give her measurements while she is reaching for something. She says there is a moment when a person can either hold a number in her head or avoid dropping a wrench, and she would rather keep the wrench.

So I remember them until she is ready.

This is easy. Knowing when she is ready is harder.

Sometimes she says, “Go on, then,” to a latch.

***

There is more weather beneath the paths than people suppose.

I encounter condensation, irrigation seepage, reservoir spray, laundry water, and water that has taken so long to come through the construction that nobody is willing to identify it. Each arrives differently.

Condensation makes a surface gradually treacherous. Irrigation falls in a rhythm. A leaking joint produces one wet place and then, some distance away, another wet place that appears to have no connection to it.

The most dangerous drops are the ones that fall onto my contact head while I am taking a reading. They make a clean piece of metal appear to contain a defect the size of my body.

I have learned to wait for the second reading before summoning anyone.

Eda remembers the first occasion.

She was eating lunch.

***

Outside my assigned route, beyond a blanking plate and a short descent on old conduit, is a chamber containing a wheel.

It is not a particularly large wheel. Eda could carry it in both hands. It stands vertically in a frame, with shallow cups around its rim and a shaft passing into a sealed housing. No pipe now points toward it. There is no belt on the shaft.

I found it while following a stain.

At the bottom of the chamber lie fragments of tile, a length of cord, and a seed husk too large to have entered by the route I used. There must be another opening.

The wheel moves sometimes.

Not while I am watching. I know it moves because one cup has a chipped lip, and the chip is in a different position when I return.

I have inspected the frame for instability. I have checked for vibration transmitted from the freight line. Once I stayed through an entire irrigation cycle, which was a poor decision: I was late returning, and the person using my next available work slot had to borrow a larger inspector that could not get around the second stringer.

I was told about this at length.

I have no maintenance instruction concerning the wheel. It is not connected to anything that I can establish.

I go there anyway.

***

Eda knows.

She found the route in my movement record after I came back with cord wound around an ankle.

“What were you doing down there?”

“Observing an installation.”

“What installation?”

I showed her.

She sat on the stair with my display in her hands. People stepped around her. One asked whether the lift was broken again.

“Probably,” she said, without looking up.

Then she enlarged the picture of the wheel.

“That’s a governor housing. Or it might be. Show me the back.”

I had not photographed the back. There was too little clearance.

“Well, there’s your next visit wasted.”

She unwound the cord and put it in her pocket. I thought she intended to dispose of it. Several weeks later I saw it tied around a bundle of brush handles.

***

During the wet cycle after she painted the lookout bolt, I went with her beneath the outer parapet.

I do not ordinarily work that far west. The metal there has a different sound. There is less construction behind it to soften the pulse, and my readings come back abrupt and difficult to compare.

Eda lowered me in a canvas tool bag. I could have descended on the safety line, but she wanted to carry the probes at the same time.

The bag smelled of primer. A screw rolled against my underside at every movement.

When she opened it, there was too much distance.

Usually there is something close to me: a beam, a pipe, the underside of a tread. Here the nearest surface beyond the wall was cloud. My lamp came on automatically and illuminated nothing.

“Stay there,” Eda said.

I was gripping the mouth of the bag with all six feet.

“I mean, don’t climb onto me.”

She attached my short tether to the work rail, then held the bag against the wall until I found purchase.

We worked down the row of collars. She scraped; I measured. At the fern’s bolt she took longer. A root had followed the thread into the recess, but the metal beneath it was sound.

“Not yet,” she said.

I recorded the reading.

A little later a shadow passed over us, traveling upward.

I turned so quickly that my newer leg struck the rail.

The bird was already above the parapet. I had seen its back, not its belly: the folded sheen of the feathers, the tail opening as it rose.

Until then, all the birds I had seen closely had been looking for a way into somewhere.

***

I would still live behind the stair panel.

There are more spacious service cupboards, and one has a newer socket, but from mine I can reach the outer brackets without crossing a public path. Crossing a public path takes a long time. People either fail to see me or see me much too enthusiastically.

I would still visit the wheel.

After the work at the lookout, I would begin looking above it rather than only behind it. I would find, perhaps, that the other opening is high in the chamber, concealed by the lip of an old inlet. There would be something caught there: a scrap of membrane, or a dry leaf, moving when the pressure changed.

That might explain the wheel. It might not.

I would have to get closer.

Before leaving, I would check the time against my next assignment. Then I would fold my newer leg carefully, pass beneath the shaft, and try the wall on the far side.
