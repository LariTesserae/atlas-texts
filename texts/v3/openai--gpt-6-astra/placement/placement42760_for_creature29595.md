I would be under the landing that no longer has stairs.

The stairs would have gone recently enough that their absence still troubled my movements. Coming back in the heavier water, I would reach for the lowest rail before remembering. My hand would close, find nothing, and stay closed for a moment.

Above me, the landing would retain its four bolts, its square of perforated steel, and a strip of yellow paint protected beneath the lip. One corner would be buried in new stone. The other three would move a little under load. That would make it a poor place to sleep by some measures, but I would know the movements separately: the short twist when the western pump took water, the longer sag that came before the lamps brightened, the faint returning tremor that meant something far below had acquired another support.

I would have lived there long enough to sleep through two of those.

Not the third.

At first I imagine myself as a solitary animal, folded into that familiar underside. But there would be another cradle within reach, and the wear on the metal between us would be deeper than the wear along either approach.

Someone would have rested there for a long time.

I would know them through the landing before I knew where their body was: a slight unloading of one corner, followed by a pressure on the rail that never quite became a knock. They would be missing one of the long fingers on their left hand. When they settled, the remaining fingers would take the weight in a sequence I could recognize through several yards of steel.

Their place would now be empty.

Not necessarily because they were dead. There would be routes between us that had ceased to lead where they used to lead. For several evenings after a large change, I would travel farther than I needed to feed, pressing my face against junctions, waiting through the loud portions of the machinery.

Then I would come home with something caught under my collar: a length of wire, a small ceramic ring, once a bolt still carrying a curl of bright metal from its hole.

I would put these things nowhere in particular.

One object, though, would have a place.

It would be a short piece of hollow railing, flattened at one end. The unflattened end would fit between two ribs of the landing, and I would wedge it there before folding myself to rest. During an ordinary surge it would tremble against my flank. During a change of depth it would lift away.

That small absence of contact would wake me.

I would not know whether the other animal had first fitted it there for this purpose. Perhaps I would have made use of something they had carried for another reason. The underside of the tube would bear the marks of both our hands.

***

My body would be less graceful than it looked from a distance.

A long opening movement, pale membranes entering dark water, could seem effortless from the bank. Close by, there would be the labor of it. A shutter behind my forelimb would stick. I would have to flex twice to free it. One of my hind feet would turn too far inward when I gathered myself, and I would use a hand to set it into its groove.

Silt would collect beneath my collar. A stem would sometimes enter the soft space behind a plate and hurt until I found the right angle to work it free.

I would spend a considerable part of my life arranging myself.

Feeding would require more attention still. I would favor a place near a capped pipe where a shallow shelf moved past a deeper channel without quite joining it. There I could keep three holds on old metal and let my rear membranes descend.

The first nourishment would feel like becoming easier to locate.

My fingers would acquire weight. The channels along my flank would fill, and the faint trembling at my joints would stop. I would open farther, letting the difference pass through tissues that had been loose and cloudy when I arrived.

Sometimes I would remain too long.

Not through ignorance. There would be a point at which the strain was rich enough that withdrawing felt wasteful, and I would wait for one more pulse. Then the pipe would seem suddenly remote beneath my fingers.

I would close hard, abandon whatever had not yet filled, and drag myself into the shallows. For a while afterward I would lie spread beneath the rail with my face against the mud.

I would be practiced. I would also still be capable of making the same mistake.

***

The youngster would arrive during one of those rests.

It would come along the capped pipe, stopping at every fastening, its body too tightly gathered to let much water through. I would feel its efforts before it touched me. Small hands made surprisingly large disturbances when they did not yet know how to transfer weight.

I would wait for an adult to follow.

The youngster would reach my wrist and grip it.

There would be no way to ask where it had come from. I could brush its collar and learn the taste of fresh mineral deposits, the residue of a different grove. Its membranes would carry a deep chill in patches, though all the surrounding water was warm. I would open my flank beside it.

It would attach itself so firmly that, when I finally moved, I would have to climb using the other arm.

Back beneath the landing, it would take the empty cradle.

I would widen two loops in the weave and move the ceramic ring out of its way. Then I would wait at the approach rail, listening.

Over the next several cycles, I would take it back to the capped pipe. I would hold there through the feeding hours while it opened a little beside me. Sometimes a passing animal would brush it, then brush my face. Once the youngster would reach toward a pale shape crossing beneath the leaves, and I would follow that animal as far as the tower.

Nothing would come of it.

The youngster would return with me, using more of its own holds each time.

Eventually its movements in the second cradle would become part of what let me sleep.

***

I would not be especially good at teaching.

I would know how to free a locked finger, how long to shelter a membrane that had darkened too quickly, which vibrations required us to move. But I would sometimes tap a rail before the youngster had placed its whole hand against it. I would have to wait for the bubbles again.

At the three stairways, it would keep looking for me with its face.

I would be close enough for its feelers to disturb the water around my collar, yet the way between our hands would pass through a depth it had not learned to enter.

I would tap.

It would grip harder.

We would go home.

On another visit it would release one finger at the sound. Nothing more. I would remain on my stair while its small finger searched the underside of the rail, stopped, and folded back against the metal.

There would be many visits.

Meanwhile it would learn other things without my help. It would find a route through the landing’s buried corner that my body could no longer use. It would discover that the ceramic ring rolled in a circle during the long sag and straight during the short twist. I would wake to the delicate repeated impact of it against a bolt.

After a while I would secure the ring with wire.

The youngster would work the wire loose.

***

When the landing began to fail, I would recognize it late.

Its familiar sag would become slightly smoother. A vibration I had waited for all my life would stop returning. I would press my face against the embedded corner and stay there while the youngster moved restlessly above my arm.

New roots would be entering the concrete.

I would have to leave before the next heavy load, but first I would pull the hollow railing from between the ribs.

It would resist. Mineral growth would have closed around the end. I would brace a hind limb, turn it, feel the landing shift, and stop.

The youngster would already be at the outer rail.

For a little while I would hold the tube in one hand and the structure in the other. Then I would work the tube back into its old position, where it would no longer touch anyone sleeping.

We would descend.

Below the missing stairs, my hand would make its habitual closing movement. This time I would open it again and reach farther.

The youngster would find something first.

I would feel the change in its weight through the hand it had kept on my wrist. Its other arm would disappear below the elbow, though its body remained beside mine. It would test the unseen hold, release it, and take it again.

Then it would tap.

I would wait until the bubbles rose before following.
