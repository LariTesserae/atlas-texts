At first I would look for a place beneath a ledge. Somewhere the light has arrived twice and the pressure only once; somewhere I could put my back against a surface and know which direction danger cannot come from.

There is no such direction here. The stone itself is full of arrivals.

Still, I think I would begin low in the basin, inside one of the old shells. Not the largest. A shell wedged sideways where two fracture planes meet, with its original opening almost blocked by a fallen chip of rock. Water passes through it steadily enough to bring food, but not enough to clear away everything that dies inside.

There are several linings between me and the shell. I did not make most of them.

My youngest tissue follows their contours. In one place it turns around a ridge that once divided two chambers, though the partition has long since disappeared. I have grown an unnecessary fold there. When I contract, a little water remains caught behind it.

Small grazers live in that pocket. I cannot reach them with my feeding comb. They eat what I fail to take in, and sometimes they eat me.

For a while I would think this was my whole body: the comb, the chamber, the soft lining with its inconvenient fold. A modest animal in a badly chosen house.

Then something farther inside the mountain would tighten, and I would feel the tightening from within.

A narrow part of me passes through the back of the shell. It follows a seam beyond the place where the new light stops. Farther in, it divides around a crystal. One branch ends nearby; the other continues into stone I cannot describe by sight. Water reaches it intermittently. Its sensations are slow and specific: a surface becoming soluble, a trickle acquiring a different bitterness, the long relief of a growing tip finding room.

I could not pull that part of myself out into the basin. I could not gather myself together and leave.

But I have already begun to grow out of the house.

Along its broken lip is a pale edge that has no inherited contour to follow. It thickens wherever the current bends it. At first it rolls back against the shell. There is food there, and a surface on which to spread.

Then a concussion lifts it.

For a moment the edge lies extended in water, held clear of everything. Sensation comes from both sides at once. When the pressure passes, it settles crookedly, one portion still projecting beyond the lip.

That is where I would find myself most strongly. In the part that has not yet learned whether to withdraw.

I would not see the eastern wall as a view. Silver movement would reach me through a narrow lane between slabs, interrupted by the bells hanging there. The sky would be a large change in brightness. Clouds would alter the taste of the water much later.

What I could distinguish very well would be the difference between a filament touching me and a filament nearly touching me.

The near touch moves water over the new tissue. It has a softness followed by a little pull. An actual touch brings something sharper: an electrical change, then the texture of another living surface.

The first time, I contract so thoroughly that my feeding comb closes on an empty chamber.

The filament withdraws.

It comes back hours later. Or a different one comes. I have no way to decide yet.

Eventually I stop closing every chamber at once.

Beyond the shell, another pale growing edge advances from the underside of a slab. We are close enough that our motions disturb the same grains of precipitate. Between us, a bell’s anchoring thread rises into the brighter water. When either of us brushes it, the bell shortens itself, drawing its neighbors into a stack.

Our first contact would probably be made beneath that thread, in the dirty little current the bell creates when it feeds.

No flash. No change in the basin’s note.

A patch of my outer tissue adheres. The other animal bends away, and the patch stretches. For a long time we remain like that, joined badly, each applying a small force in a different direction.

Food collects against us.

Then the flat grazers arrive.

I would feel their mouths in sequence. Hold, feed, release. Each mouth is a separate point of pressure until it bites deeply enough; then several points become one injury.

I would thicken under them. My neighbor would thicken too. The connection would become broad enough to shelter a current on its lower side, and that current would bring more grazers.

There would be days when almost all my feeding went into replacing what they ate.

Once, they would cut through.

The freed end would recoil against my shell. A short length of living tissue would remain in a grazer’s mouths and drift away with it. For hours I would continue contracting toward a contact that was no longer there.

I would grow outward again, though not along quite the same course.

This time the connection would pass against the fallen chip at my doorway. The stone would bear some of the pull. It would also leave a narrow underside where the grazers could feed unseen.

Nothing about it would be finished.

After enough growth, a tremor from the other animal would reach one of my chambers before it arrived through the old plates. I would close that chamber earlier. Water would leave by a different opening. The stalked shells beside my doorway would lean, then shut.

In the recess below, a feeding tube would begin to fold.

The concussion would come while its mouth was only half withdrawn. It would be injured, but less than before. Later, it would open again.

I would have no clear knowledge of this. I would know the connection had pulled, that I had closed, that afterward food came through the shell in an unfamiliar direction.

Perhaps this is the being I would become here: old far inside the stone, young at the mouth of a borrowed house, spending more than I can spare on a piece of tissue exposed to teeth.

At evening, light would enter beneath the slab and pass through that tissue. A grazer would show inside it as a dark, moving thickness. On the far side, my neighbor would begin to contract.

Before the pull became strong, I would loosen one fold and tighten another.

The joining place would bend.

For this concussion, at least, it would hold.
