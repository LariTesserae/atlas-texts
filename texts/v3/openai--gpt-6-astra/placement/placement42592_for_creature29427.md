At first I would put myself under the roof.

There is room enough to sit, and the grooves offer something to do with a fingertip. I would touch each one, then return to the seventh, trying to determine whether the tool had slipped or the hand had weakened. I would keep my shoulders away from the wall until the cold drove me against it.

But that position is already occupied by an absence. Even the animal approaches it along a route established before I arrived. To sit there would be to interrupt something without knowing what.

I think I would find myself lower down, where the water leaves the last basin.

From above, the crack looks like the basin’s failure. Follow it beneath the floor and it becomes a covered passage, protected from the noon glare. It widens beyond the first obstruction. There is a pocket there, and in the pocket a flat stone rests at an angle, with a small quantity of liquid beneath it.

For a while I would take that stone to be myself.

It has a yellow upper face and a blue underside. It broke from somewhere sheltered long after the exposed walls had faded. One corner has been worn smooth by movements too small to register against the mountain: a turning, a settling, another turning the following afternoon. In winter it rests on the same three grains for months.

Something closes around it when the cold comes.

From the stone’s position, being carried and being kept would be indistinguishable. The pressure would arrive on every side. Sometimes it would lift; sometimes the whole surrounding softness would shrink until a corner touched the floor. Then the small hard sound would go out into the notch.

Yet I would also be in the softness that hears the contact through its own flesh.

I would have to loosen there. Water would be trapped under the raised corner. It would need a way inward before the margin froze.

This would occupy me completely.

***

My attention would not cover my body evenly.

At the advancing edge, the rock would be available in great detail. A grain would present several surfaces. A shallow imperfection could hold enough water to change the direction of an afternoon. I would spread across it, find the continuation too dry, thicken, wait.

Farther back, there would be little detail. A pressure. A slow arrival of fluid. The distinction between a passage still open and a passage becoming difficult.

The oldest parts would be hardest to feel.

One lies behind a slab that has settled twice since I entered it. The first settling widened the seam at one end and closed it at the other. The second left almost no room at all. The flesh there is thin and pale, with none of the cloudy knots that gather at a fresh break.

I keep water moving toward it.

I do not know it as old. Nothing in that darkness indicates how much time has passed. But whenever I extend toward the basin, a tension develops along the route behind me, and my edge turns aside before it has reached all the wet stone it might have occupied.

Some afternoons this costs almost nothing.

On others, a bead lies just beyond me until the sun takes it.

***

There would be no moment at which I discovered the person had gone.

The wall would cool. The water would cease to arrive in the same quantities. An exposed strip would dry before I had withdrawn from it, injuring a margin. The following afternoon I would approach beneath the strip instead.

There would be adjustments.

Salt would remain for a while in hollows that ordinary seepage did not reach. The shelter would still receive its brief warmth. I would continue to descend, though less far, and my folds would become thicker under the roof.

Whatever the person had meant by pouring water would pass entirely outside me. What reached me was water, slightly warmer than the stone, carrying substances I could use. It entered one part of my body and allowed another part to move.

I would retain the route.

I would retain the burned place too, in the width of the detour around it. There would be no picture of flame anywhere in me. My flesh would simply arrive at the edge of the old injury and take a direction that had not killed it.

Under the roof, these two movements would lie close together: the approach and the avoidance. To reach the place that had sustained me, I would go around the place that had hurt me.

Over time the route would become easier to follow than to alter.

***

Tonight I would begin in several places.

A searching rim would still be pressed against a blue fracture near the western wall. Beneath the upper basin, a thick fold would be draining into its winter channels. The fringe in the shelter would have reached the lowest groove.

The cold would arrive unevenly.

I would withdraw from the fracture first. In the shelter I would remain extended a little too long, because warmth persisted in the stone above me. By the time that warmth failed, a white edge would already have formed across the groove.

I would pull free, leaving some of myself there.

Then the stone under the roof would turn.

Its corner would strike the wall. The movement would compress a fold, sending a small quantity of water along a route that was nearly closed. Farther down, beneath the basin, the pulse would reach a junction.

Here is where I think I would become most distinctly myself: in that junction, for as long as it was in difficulty.

One branch leads toward the sheltered folds. The other passes behind the settled slab. The water entering them cannot remain where it is. Ice is already making the junction narrower.

I would draw taut.

For an instant, the old branch would receive more pressure than it had received all day. Something deep in it would yield—not the rock, and not a rupture. Two surfaces of living flesh, held against each other, would separate enough for liquid to pass.

There would be no answering movement I could recognize.

Still, the pressure would ease.

Under the roof, the fold would finish closing around its misplaced stone. The searching rim at the blue fracture would become a thin case of ice. Across the notch, my exposed parts would cease to resemble one another.

I would remain in the passage.

By morning, most of what happened there would have left no visible mark. A little ice in a groove. One chip resting differently. The last basin apparently empty.

When light reached its floor, a patch above the crack would shine. Its lower edge would gather slowly, making itself thicker.

Before it could move uphill, water would have to come back from somewhere behind the wall.

I would wait for it.
