I think I would first be mistaken for the part of a doorway that had filled in.

There would be a lintel above me, low enough that sediment had bridged the space between its underside and my covering. A few stems would grow there. Behind me, the chamber would still be accessible through a narrow gap, but the gap would lead into cold water, and nothing inside would need tending.

The warmth would be under the threshold.

Not much. A seam too small to supply a garden of broad leaves. Enough for a brown mat on one side of the stone, and for something living inside that mat to continue its slow work.

My body would have settled across the seam unevenly. The parts nearest it would be thick and responsive. Farther back, beneath the old doorway, I would have to wait between deciding to move and knowing whether I had moved.

I would not have arrived intending to stay.

***

There had been three of us in the western garden.

I remember the others by the places where their coverings touched mine. One had a coarse margin that caught on loose fibers. The other folded an edge beneath my body when the colder water came, so that for a while I rested on living tissue instead of stone.

When warmth was plentiful, we lay apart.

Later, the distance between us disappeared. There was still enough food in the old mat, but feeding took longer. Sometimes one of us occupied the channel without noticing that the flow had ceased to reach the others. A hand would touch its mantle. It would shift. We learned to make these adjustments without fully waking.

I left during a strengthening of the seep.

This was necessary: I could not have left during its failure.

The others cleared my underside and helped loosen the felt that joined us. Some of that felt remained on each of them. One held a strip while I drew away, releasing it gradually so it would not tear across a pocket of younger growth.

There was no disagreement about which direction I should take. Warmth had once been found beyond the low ridge. The path still existed.

At its first sounding stone, I gave the sequence that required no work.

I felt an answer through my rear pads before I had lifted them from the ground.

***

The threshold was not my destination.

It lay below the path, where a fan of shale descended between two planted terraces. I crossed the fan, rested, crossed another stretch, and found that resting no longer restored the movement I had spent.

For a while I continued to reach forward.

My hands could still take hold. They could draw the front of my body toward them, but the rest followed badly, stretching the folds beneath my mantle. I stopped with one hand extended into a shallow depression.

The depression held slightly different water.

I did not recognize it as warmth at first. It was only a difference that persisted when the current changed.

I lowered more of myself into it.

Below the gravel was the top of a buried step.

***

The seam under the doorway would be easy to ruin.

I learned this after removing a stone that appeared to obstruct it. Warm water rose briefly around my hand, stronger than anything I had felt since leaving the western garden. Then it thinned.

I searched uphill. I searched beneath the lintel. At last, with most of my body pressed flat against the ground, I found the warmth emerging beyond reach, through a crack among loose slabs.

I put the stone back.

It did not fit as it had before. Grit had fallen into its bed. I cleaned the bed with the short branches of one hand, holding the stone with the other. Twice I let it settle. Twice I lifted it again.

The third time, a thread of warmth returned beneath me.

For a long interval I did nothing with it except remain alive.

***

I would become broad on the warm side and narrow on the cold side.

The doorway would shape me. One extension would usually remain folded against a jamb; another would grow accustomed to reaching around the buried step. I would know the small flaws in the lintel through the roots descending from them. When a root thickened, I would feel the change before I could reach high enough to examine it.

Eventually I could move again.

By then, moving away would require preparation.

There was a patch of mat to leave behind, and a channel whose upper edge collapsed unless I packed it with coarse chips. There were places on my underside that had begun to receive food without my arranging them to do so. Detaching those places was different from lifting a pad.

I practiced by clearing one edge.

Cold water entered. I held myself free of the floor until the exposed growth contracted, then settled back.

Later I cleared two edges.

I still meant to reach the next garden. There were times when I raised my mantle toward its distant light and remained that way until the light had gone.

***

The sounding stone was above me.

I could feel travelers use it, though none came close enough to disturb the water at the threshold. Their knocks entered through the uphill jamb. Some were distinct. Others reached me as a pressure I almost recognized.

Once I received the signal for a stopped channel.

I had no useful answer.

Once came the sequence with no task attached.

I pressed my hand against the jamb.

There was no mechanism there. Stone did not answer pressure as flesh did. I pushed harder, then remained with my hand spread against it long after the last vibration had passed.

Afterward I began clearing the steps.

Only their downhill ends. There was no reason to expose the whole stair, and moving sediment over my body cost less than carrying it away. I pushed it behind me into the chamber. The dark gap beneath the roof became smaller.

Beyond the third step I found a loose slab.

It took many journeys between the slab and the warm seam to set two chips beneath it.

My first knock was poor. One chip sank into the old sediment. The slab rocked instead of striking.

I returned to the doorway.

The next time, I brought a wider support.

***

An answer did not arrive immediately.

I sent the sequence and waited with my underside laid across the step. Then I returned to the seep and fed.

When I tried again, a current was developing above the terraces. The rock carried its roughness everywhere. I could not distinguish anything smaller within it.

I learned to leave the sounding slab tilted when I withdrew. Silt collected beneath it otherwise.

I learned that a knock entered my body differently depending on which pads I released.

There were things I could do here that I had not been able to do in the western garden.

This did not make the threshold sufficient. Its warmth remained narrow. Some portions of my covering never recovered their former thickness. But I began to clear a second depression beside the seam, working where I had previously stored the gravel.

I diverted a little water into it.

Too much at first. My own mat paled along its farthest edge. I narrowed the opening and waited for the color to return.

***

The first visitor reached me from uphill.

I felt its approach through a succession of small displacements: grit pushed onto the upper step, a fold dragged across the slab, the faint lifting of sediment as it stopped.

It was carrying almost none of its outer growth.

For a while it remained above the doorway with its hands on the step below. I put one of mine within reach.

The visitor touched it, withdrew, and touched the stone beside it. Then it began to descend.

I had cleared too little space.

Its mantle caught beneath the lintel. I moved gravel from under its rear extensions while it gathered itself. When it could advance no farther, I loosened my own cold edge and drew it beneath me.

That made room.

The visitor settled into the depression. Its feeding grooves stayed closed for a long time. I directed the warmer thread against them with the side of my hand.

Later, one groove opened.

***

I would still sometimes be mistaken for the doorway.

But there would be a strip of bare stone beside me now, too narrow to call a path. The mat would be thicker at its lower end. Above it, a flat slab would stand slightly raised on two supports.

When the visitor was ready to leave, some of its covering would remain tangled with mine.

I would help it separate.

And after it had gone uphill, I would stay extended across the threshold, feeling where its weight had compressed the bed. One small hollow would be warmer than it had been before. I would clear the grit from it with a branching hand, working slowly enough not to lift the new growth.

Then I would go up the three steps and lower the sounding stone.
