I think I would arrive as a guest and stay long enough for that description to become inconvenient.

My room would be on the sheltered side of a storehouse, not quite in Eren’s court. From its door I could see the upper part of the cistern roof and a stretch of pavement on which nothing appeared to happen. The room would contain a sleeping shelf, a cup, and a peg set too high in the wall. I would assume the peg had belonged to someone tall. Later I would learn that the floor had been lowered.

There would be work available. There is always work available, although the work offered to a stranger is not necessarily the work anyone has admitted needs doing.

Someone would bring me a bundle of window coverings that had stiffened in storage. I would soak their edges, separate the layers, replace the damaged binding. While they dried, I would be asked to look at a carrying strap.

I would have been going somewhere.

For the first few months I would tell people where.

***

The first thing to become mine would be a shallow groove in the threshold.

It would catch the leg of the frame on which I stretched repairs. Whenever I moved the frame outside, I would have to lift it at precisely the moment when lifting was awkward. Eventually I would borrow a narrow scraper and smooth the groove.

This would be a mistake. The groove would turn out to carry moisture away from the door in a particular wind.

Eren would come to inspect the damp.

“Did you think it had worn like that?” she would ask.

“Yes.”

“So did the last one.”

We would cut it again, slightly farther out.

Afterward, when someone asked which guest room I occupied, I would say the one with the crooked drain.

***

I would be one of the people of this country, made of its slow, fibrous substance. There would be nothing beneath my clothing that required a new explanation.

My left hand would be better at pulling than my right. A long period spent separating useful fiber from old binding would have thickened two tendons along that wrist. The smallest motions would belong to my right hand: loosening a knot without enlarging it, easing a splinter from the soft edge of a wound.

I would not be particularly good at sewing. This distinction would disappoint people.

What I would be good at is taking things apart without losing the order in which they had been assembled.

A door covering laid across my knees would become stems, backing, edging, the old repair beneath the new repair. I could tell which binding had been added while the material was wet. I could find the point at which someone had run short of cord and hidden the shortage inside a doubled seam.

I would often admire work that had failed.

“Will it last?” someone would ask.

“No,” I would say, holding up an ingenious fastening. “But look how they managed.”

This would not always be the answer wanted.

***

In time, people would bring me things whose owners were away.

At first these would be ordinary repairs. A traveler had left a bag because the avenue was shortening and there had been no time to wait for the binding to dry. Someone visiting a neighboring court had expected to return before the cold season.

I would put the objects on a shelf.

When that shelf filled, I would make another.

There would be a distinction between an object left for repair and an object left because its owner expected to need it here again. I would try to preserve the distinction. Others would find this unnecessarily exact.

A woman who had occupied my room before me would have left a loom weight behind the sleeping shelf. I would keep it through two changes of bedding and a repair to the wall. Then one afternoon she would return, older across the shoulders than anyone had described her, and ask whether we had any spare loom weights.

I would fetch hers.

She would turn it over in her palm.

“I wondered where this went.”

Nothing more. No recognition of a patient object waiting faithfully through the years. She would put it in her bag with the others.

I would be pleased for the rest of the day.

***

I would begin keeping account of what remained.

Not a history of the court. Other people would know more, and some would care a great deal about the difference between what had happened and what I thought worth remembering.

My account would concern smaller uncertainties.

The wide strap belongs to the person with the split upper lip, not to their traveling companion.

The cup with the pale repair may be lent.

The three narrow roof wedges must stay together, although only two appear useful.

When possible, I would attach a small sample of the owner’s cord. When not, I would rely on descriptions.

This would work badly enough to require regular conversation.

Visitors would stand in my doorway, describing someone’s hands.

“Long fingers?”

“Not especially.”

“A scar here?”

“I don’t remember.”

“Did they complain about the height of the peg?”

“Everyone complains about that.”

Sometimes we would identify a person by the way they disliked a food, or by a repair they refused to accept, or by whom they had been avoiding.

I would come to know several people quite intimately without ever meeting them. Then one would arrive and prove almost entirely unlike the person I had assembled.

I would have to learn not to be disappointed.

***

There would be a coat I kept too long.

Its owner had gone out during a conjunction wearing lighter layers, expecting to carry back bundles from a warmer district. The avenue extended before they returned. For several years, messages came by indirect routes. Then there were no messages.

The coat had excellent shoulders. Its lower edge needed replacing.

I would replace it.

Later the inner backing would begin to separate. I would repair that too.

One winter someone newly arrived would need a coat. We would have others, but none that fit as well. I would give them the next best one and spend the evening explaining to myself why.

The old coat’s owner might return. The coat had not been given to the court. I had accepted responsibility for it.

All true.

Eren, hearing about this, would ask whether I had accepted responsibility for keeping it empty.

I would dislike her for several days.

When I finally lent the coat, I would make the borrower promise to return it if asked. I would point out the newer hem and the place where the left fastening caught.

The borrower would be careful with it. This would make the lending easier, though I would be embarrassed to discover how much easier.

***

By then I would no longer sleep in the guest room.

This would happen without a ceremony. The adjoining chamber would become available, and someone would suggest that moving my bed there would leave more room for the shelves.

I would agree because it was practical.

Only after carrying in the blankets would I notice that the new room had no guest peg. There were three short pegs beside the sleeping shelf, placed where a person lying down could reach them. There was a small south-facing opening. Someone had once cut a recess into the wall for a cup.

I would put my cup there.

The old room would become a workroom, though people would continue calling it my room. On nights when the court filled unexpectedly, I would clear the broadest shelf and put bedding on it.

I would still have the bag in which I arrived.

Its straps would have been replaced. One side would be a different weave from the other. Inside it, wrapped separately, would be whatever I had originally intended to deliver.

I do not think I would forget my destination.

I think that, on some evenings, remembering it would feel like remembering someone to whom I had been unkind.

***

Tavi would like the shelves.

They would contain a useful concentration of things he was not allowed to alter.

He would ask why a cracked bowl was being kept when it could not hold water. I would say that its owner might want it.

“What for?”

“I don’t know.”

He would propose several uses, each requiring a further alteration to the bowl.

Eventually I would give him a different broken bowl and permission to find out what it could become. He would make something awkward for carrying cuttings, then complain that the cord slipped.

We would spend an afternoon cutting two shallow notches.

I would be careful not to tell him that I knew what the bowl wanted to be. The bowl had not asked to carry cuttings. We had decided that, and if it failed, the fault would be ours.

He would leave it with me overnight because the binding needed to dry.

Before going home he would turn back.

“That isn’t for the shelf.”

“No.”

“I’m coming tomorrow.”

“I know.”

I would set it near the door.

***

So I would be found just outside the court, in a room that smelled of damp fiber and warmed stone. There would usually be something hanging across the entrance, making it necessary to come in sideways.

I would have a reputation for remembering objects and forgetting invitations. This would be partly deserved. I would dislike being watched while making the first cut into a repair. I would save short lengths of cord long after they had ceased to be useful.

In clear weather I would work outdoors with my palms uncovered. The fine lines on the backs of my hands would deepen. My shoulders would remain narrow; my wrists would become unmistakable.

Sometimes a stranger would stop on the pavement and recognize something drying on my frame.

Sometimes they would ask for a person who had been gone for years.

I would tell them what I knew, then offer them somewhere to put their bag.

And on the morning when Eren and Tavi crouched beside the western passage, I would come around the cistern carrying an empty cup.

I would have heard the lid trembling and assumed breakfast was nearly ready.

Seeing them, I would stop far enough away not to crowd the cord.

“Do you need something?” I would ask.

“Not yet,” Eren would say.

There would be room beside the wall, out of the wind. I would stand there, holding my cup in both hands. After a while Tavi would explain what they had done with the first stone, and Eren would correct one detail.

I would look at the stone.

It would be a perfectly ordinary stone to me. That would not make me any use at inspecting passages, and I would know enough not to step over the cord.

When breakfast was ready, someone would need to bring it out.

I could do that.
