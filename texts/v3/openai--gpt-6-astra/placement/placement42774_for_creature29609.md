At first I would put myself behind the boulder, in the pocket where the fern tips do not move. There is room there for something small. I could keep my back against the stone and learn the weather from what reached the opening.

But that is a place chosen by someone who has already seen the hollow.

I would not have that view. I would know a roof by the places where it touched me, and distance by how far I could reach without letting go. Behind the boulder would not yet exist.

I would be under the displaced turf, holding the leaf.

Its surface has folded between my hands. One hand has caught the stalk; the other has closed through the blade, making a hole that lengthens whenever I pull. Until recently, pulling on anything here brought something else nearer. The nest tightened. The body beside mine rolled against me. There was always a foot to catch, a patch of fur into which I could push my face.

Now the leaf comes by itself.

I draw it beneath my chin. It smells of the lining, though one edge has been outside and is cold.

There is more room than there should be.

I do not know there are two of us, or that I am the larger. I know the pressure that used to occupy the length of my belly. Sometimes it kicked. Sometimes its hands closed on my ear and I pushed against it until it loosened. When we slept, its breathing moved a little place in my fur against the direction of my own breathing.

I turn toward that place.

My feet find the bottom of the nest. One catches a loop of stalk. Another finds a strip of bark, soft enough that my nail goes through it. I push outward, expecting the enclosing weight, and stop when the lining begins to come with me.

Above my back, something strikes the turf. Dust falls through the roots.

I tuck my head down.

The stiff hairs along my spine are still short. When they touch the roof, they bend differently from the rest of my fur. I have begun to use them without having to lift my head. The roof used to be close on one side and farther away on the other. Now it presses along my whole back. There is a root against my shoulder that was once beneath my feet.

I have slept since this happened. On waking, I have had to find everything again.

The leaf stalk creaks in my hand.

Outside, the hollow begins moving. The motion reaches me through the lining: a slow draw, a catch, several small tremors. I tighten all my feet. Then something broad and bare touches the edge of my mouth.

I keep still.

The touch withdraws and returns lower, beneath my jaw. Fur follows it. There is soil in the fur, and a familiar smell from near the skin.

I push my face into it so hard that my nose closes.

For a while nothing else happens. The broad surface rests against me. My mouth opens a little. I am not feeding, but the movement belongs to feeding, and for a moment my hands loosen.

One of them is lifted.

The pad beneath it turns very slowly. My hooked nail catches in the leaf stalk, and the stalk draws tight across the nest. I close harder.

Everything waits.

Then the fur is moved against the inside of my wrist. It catches on the small hairs there. My fingers open enough to admit it.

I have the leaf in one hand and the adult in the other.

The adult draws back a little. My arm straightens. The leaf follows until its stalk pulls against the loop in the lining.

I cannot go farther.

The machinery enters the turf.

It is too large a movement to have a direction. The bark under my feet, the root at my shoulder, the hand supporting my jaw all give it to me together. I flatten into the nest. Beside my mouth, the adult’s pad presses more firmly.

We stay like that through the next impact.

When the vibration thins, a narrow wet tongue touches my closed fingers. It passes between two of them. One finger lifts; then another. The leaf turns in my remaining grip.

I reach for the fur.

The torn blade stays caught around my nail for a moment, light and troublesome, before it slips away.

Now there are my feet.

One comes free easily. Another has wound itself into the lining. As the adult shifts me, that foot pulls bark and two leaves up beneath my belly. I bring them with me. A third foot opens and closes on the air.

Air against the whole pad is startling. I fold the limb so sharply that it strikes my own side.

The adult stops.

Its body fills the entrance, but beyond one shoulder there is a narrow interval through which cold passes. I press my face deeper. My nose finds the worn skin beneath its forelimb. Here the smell is stronger. Here there is no loose fur to interfere with holding.

The nest moves away from my back.

For the first time, none of it is touching me.

I close everything.

There is a pull through the adult’s body, then a pause with its muscles hard beneath my hands. A fern brushes my spine. Something small runs over my foot and falls without stopping.

I do not hear it land.

The adult changes shape around me. One part lengthens while another folds close; my belly is pressed, released, pressed again. I am carried partly sideways. The bark caught on my foot scrapes stone.

Then the wind reaches under us.

I open my mouth against the adult’s skin. No sound comes out. My feet search through fur already held by my hands, crowding them, dislodging one finger. For an instant that finger has nothing.

It closes again farther in.

The movement continues.

When we stop, the surface against my back is hard and cold. I resist it. The adult keeps a limb beneath me and lowers me by so little at a time that I do not notice when the lining begins to take my weight.

Something touches my ear.

I twist toward it.

A small hand closes there, then slips into the fur beside my jaw. I shove against the body attached to it. It gives way, pushes back, and fits along my belly badly at first, then better.

The adult’s hand is still underneath us.

I keep hold of it. So does the other one. When it begins to withdraw, we move with it, and it stops.

Outside the pocket, bamboo knocks against bamboo.

A little later, the hand turns. My cheek comes down onto bark. One of my feet finds a stalk threaded through another stalk and closes around both.

The adult eases free.

There is less room here than there was under the turf. Stone interrupts the place where I would stretch my hind leg. I fold it beneath the other body instead. My new back hairs touch the roof.

I still have a piece of the old nest caught on one nail.

When I sleep, it is against my chest.
