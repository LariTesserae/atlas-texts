I would be on the landing of one of the staircases whose lower flight has been taken away.

There are four steps above me and none below. The four lead to a locked door. A bridge reaches the landing from the control booth, but it was added later and meets it at an angle, so that anyone crossing has to turn sideways at the end. From here I can see the inspection well, part of the west-wall garden, and the bright underside of the water when it rises. The pump house hides the steps out of the district.

At first this would matter: being unable to see the way out.

I would keep my shoes on even when they were wet.

There would be a bag beside me, placed on a folded sheet of metal to keep it off the sweating stone. In it: a change of clothes, a little food, a key to somewhere higher in the city. Things brought by someone who intended to leave before dark and had misunderstood what dark meant here.

I think I would be human. I would need somewhere dry to sleep. The heat would make me irritable before it made me comfortable. I would spend the first few cycles mistaking the machinery’s changes for emergencies, standing whenever the belt altered its note, sitting again when nothing broke.

The first accommodation would happen while I was asleep.

A drip from the glass roof would have been falling beside my neck. When I woke, a length of corroded metal would be leaning against the railing, diverting it into the gap where the stairs used to be.

I would look up. Then behind the landing.

There would be nothing to thank that I could distinguish from the wet building.

For a while I would make the mistake of leaving gifts.

A bright nut from my bag. A coin. A piece of fruit set on the lowest surviving stair.

The nut would disappear. The coin would be moved to the control booth, where other useless round things had collected beneath a cabinet. The fruit would remain until a pale fold carried it to the roots along the west wall.

Eventually I would learn to leave useful things where they were useful. Even that would take longer than I expected. A washer clean enough for my purposes might carry some residue the soft ridges would examine through an entire rise. A bolt that looked ruined to me might be retrieved from the discard pile every time I put it there.

I would stop making a discard pile.

My sleeping place would acquire small improvements. The locked door above the landing would prove to open onto a shallow cupboard, not a room. With the door removed, there would be enough space for my legs. I would sleep with my upper body on the landing and my feet among the old pipe fittings, beneath a shelf that left a line of rust across the blanket.

Nothing would grow there. I would be quietly pleased by this.

Below me, the inhabitant would continue to pass through the routes it already knew. Occasionally a pad would settle against the underside of the landing. Its contact would be perceptible through the stone as a brief loss of vibration, a little quiet patch beneath my shoulder.

I would come to recognize that before I recognized any particular portion of its body.

There would be work I could do simply because I could stand in air.

A pane overhead would shift in its frame. For several cycles a pale extension would reach toward it from a pipe bracket, fall short, and fold back into itself. I would watch this happen twice before understanding.

The ladder would be behind the foliage.

Getting it out would damage several leaves. Fresh wood would have to be kept away from the reaching folds. I would need to go above the glass, into rain colder than anything below it, and lie across the supporting ribs while pressing the pane back against its clips.

Through the scaled glass, my body would make a large, obscure shadow.

When I came down, the ladder would be held at its foot.

After that I would sometimes find an object on the landing: a split collar, a screw with the last few threads stripped, a small mineral-crusted piece whose function I could not guess. I would take these into the upper city and return with possibilities.

Some would be accepted. Others would wait beside my bed for weeks.

I would still go out for reasons of my own. I would want food that snapped between my teeth. I would want to hear a voice without leaning close enough to feel its breath. On some mornings I would climb the six steps and keep walking until the soles of my shoes cooled.

Coming back would be less ceremonious than leaving.

There would be warm water at the bottom step. Something I had put down carelessly would have been moved. The machine with the loose belt would still have its loose belt.

I would begin to understand how much of living here consisted of keeping a fault from becoming a different fault.

But I would have habits with no place in the district’s maintenance.

On the landing I would keep a cracked blue cup. I would use it to collect rain from above the roof and let the water cool before drinking. There would be better cups available. This one would fit my hand.

Once, during a rise, a fine extension would come over the edge of the landing and enter the crack.

I would put my fingers around it.

The extension would pause, then loosen. The cup would remain where it was.

Afterward it would be examined occasionally, but left alone.

That would matter to me more than the steadied tread.

In time I would visit the old chamber behind the pump house. I would sit within reach of the cord and listen to the disconnected wheel move. There would be enough space for another person beside me, though nobody else would come.

I would resist bringing tools.

Perhaps the cord would eventually touch my wrist. Perhaps it would never leave the wheel while I was there. I would have to learn to sit without making either event into an answer.

During the deep impacts, when all the pale pads lifted and the district seemed briefly to listen through its bones, I would be frightened. My fear would arrive later than the inhabitant’s. I would see it fastening itself to masonry while I was still trying to decide whether the sound had changed.

Then I would get down against the pier.

I would have no special understanding of what was underneath us. Neither, perhaps, would it. We would hold to the same brickwork until the pressure clocks resumed their ordinary movement.

I think this is where I would become a resident: afterward.

The water would settle. A shutter would catch halfway down. There would be the familiar hammering from the covered culvert, and my bag would still be within reach, with the key in its inner pocket.

I would cross to the shutter instead.

Below its twisted edge, a cream-colored pad would already be testing the obstruction. I would lower my hand into the hot water, find the place where the iron had lodged, and wait while the finer structures withdrew from around my fingers.

Then we would lift.
