I would be near a transfer landing, somewhere people arrive expecting to leave again within the hour.

Not one of the great freight basins. A smaller junction, partly roofed, where three passenger services have incompatible schedules and a fourth stops only if someone hangs a yellow weight from the handrail. There would be a bathhouse above it. Water from the baths would pass through a heat exchanger behind my room, so that every evening, just as the air outside became bearable, my back wall would grow hot.

The room would have two entrances. One for the public, with a counter. One at the rear, narrow and inconvenient, opening onto steps that were usually underwater.

I would spend years treating the second entrance as a drain.

***

My work would be giving directions.

This sounds simpler than it would be. People would bring me destinations that no longer existed under the names they knew, addresses referring to demolished stairs, the names of relatives rather than streets.

“The place where they used to sell the blue cakes.”

“Before or after the cooling well failed?”

“There was a woman with one white eyelash.”

I would ask what the cakes tasted like. There would be uses for that information.

Sometimes a traveler would arrive with a current route plate and need only the next departure. Sometimes someone would come carrying a bowl wrapped in cloth, trying to reach a sick brother before the food cooled. The brother would have moved during a flood. His new room would be registered under the name of the person whose entitlement he had borrowed.

I would know the woman who arranged such borrowings. I would owe her a favor.

A surprising amount of my work would concern bowls.

***

I do not think I would be human, though my room would have a chair for visitors and people would assume, from the street, that someone sat behind the counter.

I would rest lower down.

My body would be broad and folded, fitting into a shallow basin lined with replaceable ceramic tiles. The counter would conceal most of it. Four short limbs would support me when I rose; two finer ones, kept clear of the basin, would handle plates and undo knots. My skin would darken where it pressed against things for too long.

There would be a face, but not quite where a visitor first looked for one. Above the counter I would raise a ridged hood containing my eyes and the thin vibrating surfaces with which I spoke. People would address the hood. Children leaning over the counter would discover my mouth beneath it.

I would dislike being fed while working.

I would nevertheless accept certain foods, which would make enforcing this rule difficult.

My kind would have been employed at transfer stations for a long time. We would learn sequences easily: names, departures, the order in which a route passed through locks. We could reproduce accents with sufficient accuracy to make ourselves understood several weather regions away.

This would not make us universally tactful.

I would once repeat a man’s question in his own accent to check that I had understood it, and he would think I was mocking him. Afterward I would keep my speech local unless asked.

The station license would be mine. So would the complaints.

***

Behind my counter, narrow racks would hold the route plates. I would handle them often enough that the edges stayed clean while their faces collected a greasy dust.

There would be an official rack and an unofficial rack.

The official rack would contain authorized diversions, structural notices, departure schedules, and changes to district names.

The unofficial rack would contain the things that made the official rack usable.

*The south stair is open, but not for someone carrying a child.*

*The attendant at this lift leaves early to care for his father.*

*Do not change boats here after dark unless you can carry everything at once.*

*This passage is dry. The family sleeping in it has agreed to let travelers through. Knock on the pipe first.*

Some of these notes would be paid for by the transit office. Most would be supplied by passengers. I would mark the date and source, and ask returning travelers whether they were still true.

A carrier’s cook would be my most reliable correspondent. She traveled farther than the inspectors and noticed different things. Her updates would arrive on the backs of provisions tallies, smelling of oil.

Once she would send a plate bearing only:

*You can stop warning people about the dog.*

I would miss the dog, which I had never seen.

***

I would not be connected to the city in any privileged way.

A pressure change might make my basin uncomfortable before the indicator moved, but that would tell me very little about its cause. If a gate failed six districts away, someone would have to bring me the news.

There would be travelers who could not accept this.

“You must know.”

I would have to tell them again.

One afternoon I would send an old man to a landing that had been closed that morning. The notice would reach me after he had gone. He would return hours later, trembling with exhaustion, having paid two people to help him back.

He would put his fare tokens on the counter one at a time.

I would reimburse him. I would offer to arrange another journey. He would ask for water and sit without speaking until his daughter came.

After that, I would write the time beside every confirmed opening, even when there was hardly room.

The change would help. It would also make my plates harder to read.

***

At night the shutter would come down, but the room would not become quiet.

The bathhouse would empty. Water would strike the exchanger. Small maintenance devices would run behind the racks, pausing at joints with a sound like someone trying a key.

I would lower myself fully into the basin and wash the undersides of my fine limbs. My speaking surfaces would fold inward. For a while I would still feel the day’s words moving through them.

A cleaner named Eda would come before the late diversion.

She would complain about the discarded wrappings beneath the passenger benches, then tell me who had been seen carrying dinner to whose room. She had very little interest in distant districts. When I mentioned a remarkable bridge described by the carrier’s cook, she would ask whether it was likely to interfere with tomorrow’s service.

Eda would notice the sore on my rear left limb before I did.

“You’re resting on the same side again.”

“The other side faces the hot wall.”

“Then turn the basin.”

“It’s built in.”

She would inspect it, as if this were an excuse.

Eventually she would bring a padded support made for an entirely different animal. It would fit badly and help a great deal.

***

Sere might come to my counter with Iven’s circled plate.

I would ask when they intended to travel.

She would say they were only finding out.

That answer would be common. I would make room for it.

I would work out a cheap route, then ask whether either traveler needed frequent dry rest. She would look at me sharply before telling me about her ribs. We would revise the connections.

Iven, if he came with her, would want to know where they stopped recognizing our warning bells.

“They recognize the warnings all the way along this route.”

“But where do they sound different?”

I would have to consult a plate for that.

While I worked, he would lean over the counter. He would see my limbs, my basin, the strip of padded material beneath my weight.

“Can you go on a carrier?”

“Yes.”

“Have you?”

“Not since I was brought here.”

He would consider asking how old I was. Sere would put a hand against his shoulder.

I would give them the route without mentioning the journey I had begun calculating for myself.

***

It would be a short one.

There would be a disused sorting court beyond the western cooling wells, less than a day away, where the local landing keeper was another of my kind. We would have exchanged corrections for years. Her plates were distinctive because she scored them too deeply and sometimes broke a corner off.

She had invited me twice.

The first time, I had explained the staffing problem. The second time, I had explained the difficulty of transport. She sent measurements for a freight cradle and the name of someone willing to lend it.

For several months I would keep that plate beneath the others.

Then I would begin teaching Eda the racks.

She would already know more than I expected. She would also have several objections to their arrangement.

***

Leaving would be awkward.

The rear doorway would be wide enough, but only if I folded my fine limbs close and turned the hood sideways. There would be an old ridge across the threshold that caught against my underside. Someone would need to put a folded cloth over it.

I would emerge backward onto the submerged steps.

My body, buoyed by the water, would feel alarmingly light. Inside, I moved by pushing against surfaces whose distances I knew. Here a passing vessel would lift me clear of the step before setting me down several finger-widths away.

I would grip too hard.

Eda would wait beside the cradle, holding its securing line.

For years I would have told passengers to allow extra time for an unfamiliar transfer. I would have allowed myself almost none.

From the rear steps I could see beneath the landing. Shellfish crowded the supports. A spoon lay in a pocket of silt. Beyond them, through a gap I had never looked through, the yellow signal weight hung just above the moving water.

Eda would ask whether I was ready.

I would ask her to lower the cradle a little more.
