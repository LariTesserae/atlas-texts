I would be found below the rattling court, in a room with two entrances, only one of which admits my body.

The other admits my head, a front foot, and a current of air that changes direction during the afternoon. Beyond it is a stretch of floor I have never stood on. I can reach part of a channel there. At the farthest extent of my foot, where the three pads have to work separately, something keeps catching the water.

Most mornings I remove a little of it.

My sleeping place would be near this opening, though not directly in the draft. One side of my body would fit the wall closely. On the other there would be room for a youngster to lie down, provided it folded itself properly. Young ones seldom do. A foot remains beneath me, or a pleat is left doubled against its supports, and after a while I have to rise enough to let the uncomfortable part be recovered.

I would not be especially old. The hollow would have belonged to a larger animal before me. At one end, where its body had polished the stone, my warmth would fail to keep the moss back.

There would be things about the room I had learned from that animal, and things I did because its body had left them easy to do. The distinction would not be available to me.

I sleep facing the narrow entrance.

When the court above begins to click, I feel it first in the pads resting against the floor. The sound arrives differently through a foot and through the plates of the mouth. With my mouth down, I can distinguish a loose fragment in the channel from a knock coming through the deeper stone. Standing, I hear more of the hollow room itself.

Often I change position before deciding whether to rise.

You might find this hesitation unremarkable. You might be waiting for me to do something.

I am already examining the weather.

***

My left collecting surface opens less easily than my right.

Not because it has been injured. There is simply a deep pleat whose supports catch unless I extend the neighboring folds first. In the sleeping room this is no inconvenience. In the street it means I need more space on one side than my resting shape suggests.

I use a particular doorway when the clouds arrive. I pass my left fringe through it while it is still partly closed, settle my feet, and then open into the empty lane beyond. The stone inside the doorway has a pale patch where I have scraped it back. Each wet season it thickens again.

There are wider places. I could stand in them.

But from here I can keep one rear foot on the slope leading down to my room. Through that foot, the rattling court remains distinct even when droplets cover my opened surfaces and every fringe is trembling.

Fog is not an absence of sight to me. It is a succession of small loads.

A fine, almost weightless arrival along the outer edge. Then the drag of beads gathering where two ridges meet. A cold line moving inward. Some folds begin to pull before others, and I alter their angles so the water does not collect in the low places.

There is work in holding still.

When another animal opens beside me, the air between our surfaces becomes slow and wet. Its fringe occasionally rests against mine. I lift mine a little. We may remain like that until the cloud thins, touching along a length neither of us could reach with a foot.

I would know certain others this way before I saw their heads.

***

In my room, the white animals favor the folds near my rear legs.

One returns more often than the others. It has a dark patch at the base of its throat and sleeps with its mouth open against my skin. When it is absent, the old collecting surface begins to itch along a ridge I cannot scrape comfortably.

I let it stay.

If it moves toward the tender interior of a new fold, I take it between two pads and put it on the floor. It flattens itself, waits, then attempts to enter from behind.

Sometimes I put it outside the room.

It knows another way in.

***

The inaccessible chamber is not wholly dark.

At one season, light reaches something beyond the channel and comes back green. I can see it with my head turned sideways. The plants there are small. During a dry spell they lose their gloss, and I spend longer clearing the obstruction under my farthest pad.

I do not know how large the chamber is. Air moves through it in more than one direction. Occasionally an insect comes out, walking rather than flying, and I lift it from the sill.

The entrance is growing inward.

I eat the soft lip while it is pale. Higher up, where the stone has hardened, I can do little. There are old scrapes above my reach. The animal that occupied my hollow could reach them, perhaps. Or the floor was higher then.

Once, I press a loose piece of living wall into a damp depression beside the opening. It remains there for a long time without changing. I touch it each morning. A white edge appears underneath; later, the water begins to follow that edge instead of spreading across the floor.

I leave it.

Months afterward, the narrow chamber sounds different.

The new sound is faint enough that I hear it only with my mouth against the sill. A dry flutter comes after the court’s rattling has stopped. I put my foot through and find no loose piece.

For several days I scrape the opening more often.

Then I stop scraping and listen.

***

You would probably meet me there, apparently engaged in nothing, with my face turned into a hole.

Your arrival would reach me through the floor. Two concentrated weights, repeatedly exchanged. Small hard contacts between them, depending on what you wore. I would draw my foot from the passage and place it beneath my body.

If you stopped, I might remain still.

If you knelt, the new distribution of your weight would make me lift my head.

I would have no reason, at first, to attend especially to your face. Your hands would be more interesting: their separate ends, the way they could enter a channel without your lowering your whole body. If you put one against the wall, I might touch the stone near it.

I would not understand that you had crossed a gulf to be here, or that you thought the place a town.

You would not know whether I had been born in this room.

For a while we could examine the same wet seam.

If you moved the little fragment beside the entrance, I would put it back after you left.

***

Later, a young animal begins accompanying me to the narrow opening.

At first it reaches past my foot and picks up whatever I have just loosened. It tastes grit, drops it, retrieves it. Its head still fits where mine does not.

One morning it goes all the way through.

I stay with my mouth near the sill. Beyond the wall there are several light contacts, then a long silence. Water touches my lowest pad. The young animal has displaced something.

I knock once.

It answers from farther to the left than I expected.

When it returns, there is dark moss caught around a joint of its front leg. It settles beside me and begins working the moss loose with its mouth. I reach into the opening. The channel runs freely now, beyond the place I can touch.

The youngster sleeps across my nearer foot.

Above us the court rattles. A little later, from inside the narrow chamber, comes the flutter I have been listening to. The youngster raises its head without opening its eyes.

I keep my foot where it is.
