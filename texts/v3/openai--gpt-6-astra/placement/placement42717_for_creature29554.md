At first I put myself on the raised paving slab.

It is the obvious place for a body like mine: somewhere to settle its weight without having to negotiate the water. I would sit with my hands between my knees and watch the seam behind the bench. When it closed, I would wait for it to open. I would probably begin by mistaking the length of that wait for something I had learned about the animal.

From the slab, though, I remain an interruption with a good view. Even my stillness is something the hollow must accommodate.

I think I would be farther in, and lower. Not in the deep recess beneath the bench, where so much of the creature’s weight is gathered, but near a place where its shelter becomes uncertain.

The lowest step has two different surfaces. On top, tools have made a place for a foot. Below, the original rock turns inward, leaving an uneven underside that no one needed to finish. Water passes over the dressed edge and clings briefly beneath it before falling. There is room there for something very thin.

That is where I begin to find myself.

Not quite the small creature already described. A little farther under the shelf, perhaps: a growth that separated some time ago, large enough now to have encountered a difficulty of its own.

I have spread into a shallow fork in the stone. One side receives water steadily. The other is more sheltered, but a grain lodged at its entrance diverts the film. I have been reaching around that grain for several days.

There is no view from here. The bathhouse is not a building at the other end of a pool. Some of its presence reaches me as changes in the water; some arrives through the rock. A pressure that begins far away becomes a tightening of the surface against which I hold. I close before anything resembling an account of the disturbance could form.

Afterward, different parts of me open at different times.

The edge nearest the water loosens first. Farther back, a fold remains pressed shut. A small pocket of water warms against it. Eventually that fold also yields, and the pocket exchanges itself with what is passing outside.

My life would have these uneven resumptions. There would seldom be a moment when all of me was doing the same thing.

For a while I imagine that I would recognize the larger body under the bench. But recognition is too much to bring across the distance. We have no continuous flesh between us now. Its withdrawals might alter the flow that reaches my step, but so might a flake coming loose in the drain. Whatever remains of our connection is in how I fit myself to a surface, what makes my margin close, how long I can endure with an edge sealed away from food.

There is a slight thickening where I parted from it. That thickening catches grit.

I would have no reason to call it a scar.

The fork in the stone is becoming consequential. My wetter side feeds more easily and extends. My sheltered side holds firmly enough that the extension does not pull me free. Between them, the narrow place is growing narrower. Mineral collects where I cannot quite move against the rock.

For a long time, this is manageable.

I loosen on one side and settle farther underneath. The obstructing grain shifts a little. Water finds a new passage, washes a strip of clean stone, and begins leaving its coating there. I follow as far as I can. My body becomes a shape that would seem unnecessarily awkward if lifted out and placed anywhere else.

Here, each turn has somewhere to rest.

I think this is what would most distinguish me from the person on the slab. They could leave and keep approximately their shape. I could move only by relinquishing some of mine, drawing substance out of one fold to supply another, letting an exposed margin become thinner while a sheltered portion thickened. There would be no journey in which I took all of myself along unchanged.

Eventually the spring slackens.

Not enough to lower the central pool visibly. The dark line remains on the step. But beneath the shelf, the film no longer reaches the back of the fork.

At first I hold the water that is already there. Then I draw inward. The dry contact is small, only a patch against the upper side of a fold, yet withdrawing from it changes the tension through the whole of me. The wetter margin curls. The lodged grain turns and settles more firmly into the entrance.

I cannot return to the earlier fit.

So I thicken where water still passes. A portion farther back becomes pale and close against the stone. Over time, less of what happens there reaches the feeding edge.

This would be my first substantial abandonment.

From above, nothing need have changed. Someone leaning over the step might see the same small, grit-covered patch beneath the waterline. They would not see that part of its former shelter had become inaccessible, or that its weight now rested a little nearer the lip.

I would be there, nearer the lip.

When excess water crossed the step again, my edge would lift. A little sediment would pass beneath me. Some would remain, making the contact less secure in one place and filling a gap in another.

I would settle around it.

Far under the shelf, the portion I had left would still retain its shape. Closer to the falling water, a soft margin would be extending onto stone I had not touched before.
