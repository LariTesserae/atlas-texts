I think I would live behind one of the doorways that no longer opens.

Not in a buried room. There would be another entrance, inconveniently placed, reached by going out along a wall and returning through a neighboring household’s shallow court. The sealed doorway would still be the part of the house toward which I rested. Its dampness would arrive in two bands, one on either side of the former opening, and in the interval between them I would keep a fold pressed to the stone.

Something on the other side would make that interval warmer at irregular times.

For a while, that would be enough to know about where I lived.

I would be small when gathered, though not young. My upper crest would settle crookedly because one of the inner folds had healed shorter than the others. At unfamiliar thresholds I would usually try that fold first. It would be a bad habit: the pale edge would meet the opening, recoil, and leave me convinced for some time that I could not pass.

Someone who knew me would wait through this without coming closer.

Eventually I would try with another part of my body.

My reflection would extend farthest in a direction I seldom traveled. Beneath three residential lanes, beneath a long blank frontage, it would enter a court where the plants grew too high to reach. Nothing important would have happened there. I would have gone there often when I was younger, when its distance was easier to hold, and the route would have thickened through use. Now, when rain moved through that district, a seam deep inside me would open.

I could be feeding at home and suddenly admit water I did not need.

This would annoy me.

I don’t think I would understand the city especially well. I would know a few rooms with great precision: where to brace before upward rain, which apparently broad opening required the body to become narrow in an unexpected direction, how long to wait beside a particular wall before the space beyond it became available. Elsewhere I would make the same mistakes as a visitor, only more slowly.

There would be one public hall I avoided because, after every gathering, the route home felt slightly wrong.

I would attend when someone came to fetch me.

***

The room behind the sealed doorway has a low place near its center. Water collects there in a depth scarcely greater than the thickness of my outer skin. I cannot rest in it. Something about its pressure keeps my seams swollen.

Young ones like it.

They come through the neighboring court, sometimes accompanied, sometimes stretching the patience of a household whose reflected extensions remain tangled beneath my wall. In the low place they find purchase almost anywhere. Their little bodies lift on ridges that have not quite formed before they are abandoned. They make the water lean. One discovers that closing sharply will send a tremor into the sealed doorway, and for several rains I have no peace.

I do not teach them.

At first I keep them away from the wall where I rest. Then one catches a thin fold in the place where the floor becomes stone too gradually. It cannot tell which part of the resistance belongs to its body. Each attempt to withdraw makes it spread farther.

I put an open fold beneath it.

It holds to me with unnecessary force. When it finally loosens, it leaves a cloudy thread caught along my pale seam. I spend a long time working that away.

Afterward, I remain nearer the low place.

There are usually two or three of them. I recognize them by how they arrive. One presses into the room before its reflection has cleared the court; another waits outside until the water within has responded to it. A third enters correctly and then cannot leave.

For this one I go ahead.

At each opening I establish a little weight, let the surface take the shape of my presence, and release it gradually. The young one follows into the place I have just occupied.

We get as far as the court.

Then it goes back inside.

I tighten so abruptly that the moisture gathers into a band across the wall.

The young one opens all its folds.

For a moment it is almost entirely sensitive surface, extravagantly exposed, receiving the room from every direction. Then it closes and enters the low place again.

I remain at the threshold until my irritation has passed through the water.

***

There is another resident whose room lies beyond my sealed doorway.

We are not together every day. Sometimes its moisture withdraws and does not return through several changes of rain. Sometimes it rests so near that I can distinguish the slow rearrangement of its inner folds through the stone.

We could meet by going around.

Usually we do not.

When the wall between us grows warm, I make a small taut surface and release it. The reply comes through the old opening more clearly than it would through ordinary stone. We have acquired an exchange that depends on this distortion. Elsewhere, face to face, we occasionally misunderstand each other.

Once I go around because no answer has come for a long time.

It is there, spread low beneath a mineral ledge, engaged in a dispute with its neighbor. Their extended folds nearly touch. Between them, a new plant has opened one small cup.

Neither acknowledges me.

I wait, then return home by the inconvenient route.

Later the wall warms.

I do not answer at once.

***

I would have a use in this neighborhood, though I doubt anyone would distinguish it from my habits.

When a passage changed, I would stay near it longer than most. Not because I was brave. A new opening would trouble me until I had found out where its resistance lay. I would approach it low, then higher, then from the wall. I would leave and return. Sometimes I would discover that the difficulty was not in the threshold at all, but in a room beyond it that had failed to make enough distance for an arriving body.

There would be no way to explain this quickly.

I would bring someone else and settle in the troublesome room while they tried to enter. By holding a few folds against its inner wall, I could sometimes keep it broad enough.

Sometimes I made it worse.

Once, after a gathering in the public hall, a new recess would develop beside my own entrance. It would be exactly the kind of alteration I had spent the gathering trying to prevent. For a long time afterward, every visitor would pause there, mistaking it for the way in.

I would wear its edge down by crossing it.

The wear would spread into the court. Eventually the recess would become a tolerable place to rest.

***

Then there is a visitor with feet.

I feel the impacts before the body comes near: brief, hard claims upon the paving, released too completely. At each step the space has to receive the visitor again.

The young ones close in the low place.

I move out into the court.

The visitor stops.

It carries its warmth high above the water. Most of its sensitive surface is held away from everything that might support it. Even standing still, it makes small corrections, one after another. I cannot tell whether it is injured.

I establish myself at the next doorway.

When it follows, the paving strikes beneath it.

I close, wait, and open again.

Our passage takes a long time. At one junction its direction changes and I have to draw a fold out of a narrowing gap before I can go after it. Once it comes too close. The air around its skin presses into my exposed rims, unfamiliar and full of differences I cannot sort. I withdraw so hard that part of my reflection catches beneath a stair.

The visitor waits.

That is the first useful thing it does.

I free myself. We continue.

The room I find is not my own. It is one I have tested since the last exchange of foundations, a shallow interior whose floor holds a little warmth without giving off much water. The opening admits the visitor. Its body folds strangely in the middle and lowers onto the raised stone.

For a while I remain outside, preparing for it to move again.

It does not.

Gradually I let the supports beneath me soften. My weight spreads against the opposite wall. Far away, rain enters the court with the high gardens, and an old seam opens inside me.

Under the lintel, water gathers.

I lift a narrow fold before the drop falls. The movement is familiar; in my own room the young ones are disturbed by that sudden impact.

The visitor shifts, then settles again.

I keep the fold raised. The drop spreads along its underside, warm and increasingly heavy. I could let it pass through me, but not without opening the surfaces nearest the visitor.

For some time I hold it there.

Then, a little at a time, I make room.
