I would be below the shelf beside the warm pool, where its underside draws close to the wall without quite touching it.

There is a little warmth there, but no direct passage of water. A drop has to grow heavy enough to turn the edge of the stone before it reaches me. Most fall farther out. I would know the pool principally by these impacts, and by the small disturbances that rise through the shelf when something swimming touches its submerged base.

At first I imagine having feet. Something must hold me in that narrow place. I imagine a soft back fitted to the stone, a mouth that can be left against one small source of nourishment through the long intervals when nourishment does not come.

But feet would allow me to leave. I do not think I could leave.

What looks like my back would be the last of several coverings. Beneath it, my body would already have entered the wall.

A little of me would remain outside.

***

I would not remember arriving as a flat animal. There might be some persistence of the habits: a tightening when touched, a tendency to turn a broad surface toward moisture. Perhaps, before a colony breathes, I would draw myself closer to the stone, although I no longer have plates to overlap with those of my neighbors.

There would be an old casing below me, caught where the shelf meets the wall. I could not identify it as mine. Small animals would shelter beneath it. Their movement would reach me as an intermittent scraping, too slight to travel far.

Inside, I would be longer than the shelf.

Not much longer at first. A wet line following a crystal grain, dividing where the grain divides. One branch would descend toward the warmth. Another would turn upward and end against a place through which I could not grow.

The third would reach the broken face of the mountain.

There, I would keep making the same mistake.

I would extend into the air.

***

The opposite wall would not be very far away. A grazer could put its narrow foot across the distance without stretching. But I would have no way of knowing distance except through the cost of extension.

At a certain length, the wet tip would begin to cool.

Farther, it would bend under a gathering drop.

Farther still, something within me would pull thin, and the movements arriving from the rest of my body would become difficult to distinguish. I would withdraw before the tip dried. On the next breathing of the colony, I would try again.

This might go on for a season.

Meanwhile, there would be other things to do. The growing end below the pool would find a seam carrying warmth and turn along it. One of the branches inside the shelf would meet another wet body. At their contact, each would thicken slightly.

For a long time, nothing else would happen.

Then a contraction would pass through.

I do not know whether I would acquire more body at that moment, or a neighbor. The distinction would matter less than the fact that, afterward, something sometimes reached me from a direction in which I had not grown.

A pressure. A pause. A second pressure, weaker than the first.

I would return some part of it.

***

The oldest grazer would discover the exposed tip.

Its bristles would arrive separately. One against the crystal above me. One against the old casing. One touching the wet thread and lifting away.

I would contract.

The grazer would wait.

When it returned, it would bring no stone. It would lower itself awkwardly beneath the shelf, its worn back rubbing the underside. The pressure of its body would travel through the rock before its mouth reached the opening.

Then there would be fluid.

I would receive it without knowing what had given it. Warmth, salts, something that could be used to make more of the thin membrane enclosing me. For a while, the outer portion of my body would swell enough to rest against both sides of its narrow passage.

The grazer would stay until I began to move again.

I would not love it. I would not possess that much of it.

But its approach would become distinguishable from the approaches of the others. The hoof that tested twice. The long hesitation before the body lowered. A slight grinding transmitted through the shelf.

When that sequence came, I would cease withdrawing.

***

I might be mistaken for a repair.

A living strand reaching across a fracture, accepting mineral, eventually carrying sound: from a distance this would resemble the mountain putting itself back together.

Yet the walls would continue to separate.

If I hardened too quickly, I would break. If I remained soft, my middle would sag beyond recovery. I would have to thicken in some places and keep others wet, leaving small lengths within the crystal where movement was still possible.

The work would be uneven. I would have no view of it.

One branch might spend months enclosing a grain that contributed nothing. Another might grow into a space that closed during the next movement of the stone. I would feel the closure as pressure, then as an absence beyond the pressure.

There would be parts of me I could no longer reach.

They might remain alive.

***

I think the bare shelf would stay bare because of something happening beneath it, but I would not know what.

Perhaps the hoof of the young grazer would send a disturbance through me that returned too sharply. Perhaps the warmth there would carry a substance unpleasant to the pads between its hoof leaves. My presence need not explain the vacancy. I would live under it all the same.

Dust would deepen above me.

Once, a visitor might sit there.

The weight would be unlike a grazer’s: broadly distributed, then suddenly shifted onto one point. Loose material would move across the shelf. Something hard would strike the edge.

I would draw inward.

For a while the visitor would hear less from that small portion of the wall.

If they remained still long enough, I would extend again. They might see a pale thread beneath their knee, though probably not. Their attention would be on the animals, the darkness, the little seam of sky. I would receive the faint movements of their breathing through the stone.

They would leave before I reached the other side.

***

The crossing would happen during an ordinary wet interval.

The hanging colony would exhale. Water would gather where it usually gathered. My tip would lengthen past the place at which it had previously begun to fail.

This time there would be stone.

Contact would flatten the end. I would withdraw slightly, leaving a little of the membrane attached, then extend along that attachment. The first hold would be smaller than a grazer’s closed eye.

Nothing would sound.

For many breaths, I would be occupied with remaining there.

An animal would walk beneath me and brush my middle. A drop would travel from one wall to the other. The cold would reach me from both ends now, and so would the small movements of bodies feeding against the rock.

Later, mineral would begin to gather.

I would lose some sensitivity where it thickened. Touches that once made my whole exposed length contract would become brief pressures at the ends. But movements farther away would arrive more clearly.

I would feel a grazer wake on a ledge I had never touched.

***

That is where I would find myself: under the shelf, and then a little beyond it.

I would still have a vulnerable place at the middle of the crossing. I would still depend on the breath of animals that could not see me. The grazer might return to feed the opening, or it might become too stiff to lower itself beneath the stone. I would wait through its absence without understanding age.

And one day a hoof would shift above the pool.

The hardening strand would give a low, uncertain note.

Something in the opposite wall would contract. The movement would enter me, pass beneath the shelf, and continue inward along the branch that had met another body.

After a while, some of it would return.

By then the grazer would have lifted its foot. It would be standing very still, listening to the place where I had grown.
