At first there would be no basin.

There would be a surface giving way beneath my feet, and beneath that another body. When I pushed, it pushed back. A stem would lie across my muzzle until I turned my head. Somewhere outside this tangle, cold would touch the wet skin around my mouth.

I would turn away from it.

Warmth would have several textures. There would be the warmth that lay still beneath the bedding, the warmth of the other two pressed against me, and the larger warmth that arrived carrying cold on its outer surface. That one would shift us with its feet. I would work my head beneath it and find what I needed.

For a while, that would be almost the whole of being alive: something missing, then found, then missing again.

But I think I would be the one left until last when the nest was moved.

Not the smallest necessarily. Not chosen for any reason. Just the one still there after the second departure.

The bedding would become too large. I would push into the hollow where another body had been and find grass cooling against my belly. Under the outer stems, moisture would have begun to gather. I would keep moving away from it, working myself into a corner that had been crowded only a short time before.

A little air would enter under the slab.

I would call, stop, call again.

When she returned, I might struggle against the first touch. Then the grip behind my head would gather the loose skin, and my feet would leave the ground.

That would be my first crossing of the basin: blind, carried sideways, with my mother’s breathing close to one ear.

There would be intervals of wind. A jolt when she climbed. Once, a longer pause during which her grip tightened and everything except the air became still.

I would have no way to know what passed above us.

Then stone would scrape her flank. Her body would twist. Dry stems would touch my feet, and I would be put down among the others.

The new place would smell wrong. We would press together until some of it smelled like us.

*

Later, I would live close to that second shelter.

Not always inside it. Eventually the warmth and milk would cease to be enough, and my mouth would discover other things: a stem that resisted, grit that had to be worked loose from the tongue, something soft that moved when bitten.

My eyes would open onto very little distance.

The underside of a stone. A pale split in the roof. The back of another animal blocking the entrance.

Outside, the light would have no ceiling.

I would retreat from it at first.

I would be this animal, then—not quite my mother, not yet competent at the small tasks that keep her alive. My whiskers would still be mostly unbroken. I would put my forefeet on surfaces she had already learned to avoid. At the tarn I would follow an insect too far from the bank and come back with my belly wet, shivering beneath an overhang while the insect continued across the water.

There would be no single moment of learning the place.

One passage would become easy because I had gone through it many times. At another, my shoulders would begin to turn before they touched the narrowing walls. I would stop trying to dig where a thin layer of ash covered unbroken stone.

A loose flake would rock beneath my foot. The next time, I would step nearer its edge.

Then rain or frost would move it, and what I had learned would no longer work.

I would spend a surprising amount of my life stopped at openings.

Nose forward. One foot lifted. Air moving against the sparse hairs of my face.

Sometimes I would go in.

Sometimes I would remain there long enough to grow cold, then leave.

*

My mother would become less available.

I might meet her along a thawed margin and approach too closely. Her teeth would appear. I would flatten myself, turn aside, begin searching a few body-lengths away.

When insects were plentiful, we could feed near each other. I would know her smell without receiving anything from it.

Eventually I would cross beyond the places where that smell was strongest.

Not in a clean departure. I would go a short distance and return. On another outing I would stay beneath a strange stone until hunger sent me back into the open. There would be a patch of moss I searched thoroughly, then searched again because I did not know where to go next.

The shelter I kept would be a poor one at first.

A low cavity behind a broken plate, with grit that slid toward the entrance. Warm enough along the back wall, but exposed to wind from one direction. I would bring a mouthful of grass and lose several stems where I squeezed beneath the roof.

I would sleep there anyway.

After waking cold, I would pull the remaining grass farther in. After waking too warm, I would push it outward. A feather would lodge across the entrance, and I would drag it inside, turn it, drag it again, finally leave most of the shaft projecting into the passage.

This would be where I found myself: at the thinner end of a warm seam, beyond the best feeding ground, making repeated adjustments to a shelter no larger than the space my body could fill.

I would not know the tarn’s shape. I would know three places where its edge could be reached under cover.

I would not know how far below me the clouds had come from. When they closed around the stones, I might venture across a gap I had refused in clear air.

And I would not be entirely cautious. During a hatch I would remain too long at the water. I would snap at one more insect with the shadow already moving over the bank.

Perhaps I would reach the crack. I would have chosen my feeding place partly because it was there.

Inside, my sides would touch both walls. I would wait with my mouth slightly open, breathing.

After a while, hunger would begin again.

*

Tonight there would be snow at the entrance.

I would nose beneath its soft edge and emerge with white grains caught in my fur. The usual turn along the slab would lead to ice. I would test it, put weight on it, withdraw.

Farther along, water would be making a faint sound under the crust.

I would follow the edge until I found an opening.

Nothing about it would promise to remain open. But air would be coming through, and beneath the mineral smell there would be something else: wet moss, a trace of another animal, the living smell of a place I had not yet searched.

I would lower my head.

My whiskers would enter first.
