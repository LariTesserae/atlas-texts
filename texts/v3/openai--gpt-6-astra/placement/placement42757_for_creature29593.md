I would find myself below one of the smaller dams, where the water comes through rather than over.

There is a piece of limestone there, tilted against the buried end of a branch. Beneath it, a pocket of air remains above the current. A few roots cross the ceiling. One carries a bead of water that grows, trembles, and touches the stream before it quite lets go.

From the rim, this place is nothing. Even standing on the dam, you would have trouble finding it. But from beneath the stone there are several ways out: downstream into a shallow run, sideways through the sticks, upward through a root-filled crack into the bank.

I would need all three.

At first I imagine myself large enough to watch the notched-ear female from the opposite shore. But the shore keeps breaking into smaller places as I attend to it. There is no good ground for standing back. There are wet stems, a hollow behind a root, the underside of bark. The beaver’s pond is becoming an expanse I would hesitate to cross.

I think I would be a water shrew.

Small enough that the beaver could pass close by without knowing I was there. Hungry enough that I could not spend very long watching her.

My nest would be farther up the bank, in a cavity threaded with spruce roots. I would carry dry sedge into it from beneath an overhang, taking the short pieces first. The nest would be barely above the highest water I had yet encountered. That would not make it safe. It would make it the place where I slept.

Most of my life would happen within a few inches of a surface.

Mud against the belly. Whiskers meeting wood before the head could follow. Water opening around the muzzle, then enclosing it. Underwater, air caught in my coat would turn me briefly silver, though there would be no one positioned to see this.

I would hunt along the dam’s lower face.

Here, the current pulls food and fragments into the same narrow spaces. A softened leaf presses against a twig. Beneath it something moves. I push my nose into the gap, catch what I can, and come out backward with my feet searching for purchase.

There would be long routes I knew by touch and very short ones I did not know at all. A crack could narrow beyond its entrance. A loose piece of bark could lift easily in one direction and close behind me in the other. I would learn these differences without assembling them into a view of the dam.

The beaver would arrive mostly as disturbance.

Water would rise against a stick where it had been running beneath it. Silt would enter a clear opening. Something overhead would scrape, stop, and scrape again. Then a pale end of wood would descend into my route, accompanied by forefeet large enough to close the passage between them.

I would retreat.

Later, when she had gone, I would return to the place she had changed. Sometimes there would be a new pocket full of small movements. Sometimes the current would be too strong. Sometimes the whole underside of a branch would have been pressed into mud.

I would not wait for her to put it back.

Once, coming up through the sticks, I would meet her at water level.

Her head would occupy nearly the whole opening. Wet fur, the blunt nose, an eye catching a little light. The pale notch would be out of sight. She would be holding willow, and one leaf would touch the water between us.

For a moment neither of us would move.

Then she would draw the branch inward. The leaf would disappear. Water would follow it, and I would be left gripping a twig that had begun to shift.

That would be our acquaintance.

I would know her much better through what happened afterward.

By late summer, the run below the stone might diminish. The family would have packed another leak upstream. My hunting place would still be wet, but the water would no longer bring as much into it. I would go farther along the bank, across a patch of exposed silt where there had been no footing before.

A snipe would lift almost beneath my nose.

Its departure would be enormous: legs unfolding, wings striking air, the ground suddenly empty. I would vanish under a root and remain there until hunger became more pressing than the disturbance.

Then I would come out and continue.

I would like to give myself an old age in this place. A well-chosen nest, reliable hunting, another spring beneath the ledges. But such a small body cannot be promised the basin merely because it fits into its crevices.

So I would give myself an evening.

Mist lies above the pond. Under the dam, where I move, it is invisible. I have found food twice along the same submerged branch, and I am trying it a third time. My hind feet spread against the wood. My whiskers enter a fringe of fine roots.

Above me, the female crosses toward her lodge.

Her wake reaches the dam and passes into it. The water lifts along my sides. For an instant, the root fringe loosens from the mud, and something concealed there starts to swim.

I turn after it.

Behind me, beneath the tilted limestone, the little pocket of air remains.
