I would be behind the part of the eggshell that still curves.

Not in its center. That space is already too open, exposed whenever the pump draws air through the crack. I would be along the place where shell almost touches cement, where a particle lodged years ago keeps the two surfaces apart.

There is a little softness there.

From outside, it would look like dirt beneath an empty egg. Even close up, nothing would distinguish what is living from what has merely become difficult to remove.

I would not yet have an outside large enough to be seen.

Water would arrive without a drop arriving. For part of the day, the air in the crack would permit a little more of it to remain in the residue beneath the shell. I would swell into that permission. Later I would stop. The difference between those conditions would be most of what happened to me.

The pump would start, and the curved shell would move against the cement.

Not far. Less than the width of the mite’s smallest foot. But the movement would press one part of me flat while opening a space beside another.

I would grow into the opening.

At first I imagine myself as something waiting here: a spore, perhaps, brought up the mountain inside a fold of packing felt. A small sealed provision whose chief accomplishment is not being used up.

But there is old growth behind me. Some of its walls have emptied. Some remain full. The substance with which I enter the new space has come through them.

I would be a thread of fungus, then, though the thread would not remain the right shape for long.

At a fleck of dried material, I would divide.

One end would follow the shell. The other would descend into the cement.

There would be no decision between them, no departure of one self from another. Both ends would become more particular through what they met. The upper one would broaden where food was available. The lower one would grow thin in a passage that offered purchase and almost nothing else.

The mite would find the upper one.

Its mouthparts would scrape the shell, withdrawing a little living material with each movement. Fluid would escape. A partition farther back would close off the damage.

For a time, that end of me would be a wound.

The lower end would continue.

I would not know the hunter passing outside. Something heavy would press against the crack; the roof would shift; several grains would settle into new positions. Then the pressure would leave. The mite would stop feeding and draw back its front legs.

That is how the largest animal in the enclosure would enter my life: as an interruption in being eaten.

Later, a fragment would arrive.

It would be part of a prey animal, carried against a hairy leg and scraped off at the entrance to the hunter’s chamber. The pump would move it again. On the next cycle it would lodge between the cement and a loose scale of mica, not touching me, but close.

Close would still take a long time.

I would spend material reaching it. Behind the advancing tip, stored substance would diminish. The passage would become dry enough that growth ceased with a narrow gap remaining.

For several pump cycles, nothing would change except the fragment’s position. It would rock against its obstruction. Once it would turn an edge toward me.

When growth resumed, I would meet that edge.

There would be a joint in it. A residue tucked beneath the joint. I would spread there, and the thin approach I had made would thicken.

This would be the first place where I could afford another branch.

Meanwhile, the hunter would close her chamber.

Her silk would hold dust above the entrance. It would also make a new boundary in the moving air. The pocket behind it would lose moisture differently from the passage outside. A body would rest inside, with other bodies forming beneath it.

I would reach the chamber through its floor.

Not all of me. Much would remain beneath the eggshell, including the damaged end that had begun to grow again. A fine extension would follow a scratch under the folded aluminum. There, in the scratch, a particle of organic debris would soften.

The hunter would have no means of seeing this approach. Her hairs would register a mite at the entrance, a loose grain touching silk, the pump’s first knock. My growth would apply too little force, too slowly.

I would be near her before I had crossed the floor.

There is a temptation to make something useful of myself here. To bind her chamber to its ledge, or hold a little water where the brood might need it.

But I would take water.

I would enter what I could break down. If nourishment became available beside the brood, I would grow toward it. The enclosure would contain no arrangement by which her need automatically took precedence over mine.

Nor would I be pursuing her. A smear left on the aluminum could keep me occupied while she raised her young within reach.

Above us, the nut would turn a little farther.

The bright grain suspended in the silk would settle lower. One attachment would peel from the metal, leaving the others to bear its small weight. The female would rise, turn with difficulty, and touch the loosened place with her mouthparts.

Below her, my newest tip would encounter the underside of the same fold.

For a while we would work on opposite sides of it.

Then the pump would knock.

Her legs would spread. The silk would pull tight. Air would pass over the scratch and take moisture from its exposed end.

I would stop there.

Farther back, beneath the curved eggshell, another tip would have reached something soft.
