I think I would live beneath a public quiet room, in one of the spaces that was not meant to be inhabited.

Not inside its suspension housing. That must remain clear. But below the housing, where a stair turns around a planted shaft, there is a ledge wide enough for an adult to unfold on. A previous occupant has fitted it with a cupboard and a water-catching cloth. The leaves outside are broad and pale underneath. When arrivals disturb the shaft, they turn over, and for a few moments my room is brighter.

The rent is low because of the footsteps overhead.

I do not mind them when I am at home. There is something restful about knowing precisely what a sound is.

I keep food in the cupboard, tools in a roll, and one object that must not be moved: a shallow dish joined by some old mistake to a depression in the ceiling. If I shift the dish, water begins to gather above my bed. I have marked its place with three little teeth pressed into the floor.

Occasionally a visitor assumes these are decorative.

***

My work is to sit outside the rooms of people who cannot bear company.

There is training for this. It is not enough to be still.

A foot held too rigidly transmits the small contractions of the body. A foot allowed to relax completely may slip. Breathing can couple with a floor until something almost imperceptible beside you becomes intolerable in the next room. Hunger makes the throat cavities tighten. Concern is particularly difficult to conceal.

We begin our training beside bowls of water, then beside sleeping animals, then beside an instructor who is trying to read.

My first instructor used to throw husks at me.

“You are preparing to move,” she would tell me.

“I haven’t moved.”

“I know. That is what I am objecting to.”

For a long time I thought the work would consist of learning to make less of myself. In practice, I have had to learn a great deal about floors.

I carry folded pads of different densities, wedges, a little pot of joint grease, and strips that reveal how a surface is taking weight. I examine a room before accepting a placement. Sometimes the family has put a chair in exactly the wrong place. Sometimes the chair is right and the room has changed since yesterday.

I have refused work in a house whose owner would not let me move a cabinet.

I have also spent an entire afternoon motionless on the cabinet.

***

One of my regular patients lives in the district without echoes.

Before her illness, she inspected arriving root vessels. She could tell whether a stand of trees had traveled badly by listening to the soil held beneath them. Now the small adjustments of another person’s stance reach her as a succession of blows.

Her own movements are less painful. She knows when they are coming.

At first, her household tried announcing everything.

A cup would be lifted. A door would close. Someone would pass along the upper landing.

But the announcements were themselves unbearable, and soon she was bracing for things that had not happened yet.

When I began visiting, her youngest household member met me outside with a written list of prohibitions. The sign they had used kept rearranging its marks as I turned it. I had to set it on the ground and approach from the same direction three times.

No walking on the east ledge.

No opening the food cupboard while she rests.

No visitors carrying children.

No clicking.

The last instruction had been cut into the sign rather than displayed.

I asked where I should leave my things.

They pointed across the plaza.

***

Her room opens onto a basin at the depth where runners thicken into ropes. Little transparent animals live inside them. In the right light you can see the animals feeding, each enclosed in its own length of green.

I sit beyond the doorway, with two feet on a pad and two against separate patches of wall. My abdomen hangs free. It is not a posture anyone would choose for leisure, but it keeps my breathing out of the floor.

For the first few visits she gives no indication that she knows I am there.

The household asks whether this is usual.

“Yes,” I tell them.

It is usual. So are anger, embarrassment, relief, and requests that I be sent away. I do not list these.

On the fifth visit, she touches the inside of the doorway with one finger.

I move close enough for her to reach my jaw.

Her fingers are dry. Along the edges of her armour, the little joint growths have been allowed to die.

*Still there?*

I touch the answer beneath her throat.

She withdraws immediately.

After that, I arrange myself a little nearer.

***

I am good at the work, but not exceptionally good.

There are practitioners who can share a sleeping ledge with someone in acute pain. There are old specialists who can receive a frightened child onto their backs without transmitting the disturbance to the person beside them.

I cannot do either.

One of my rear feet was cut deeply when I was young. It healed with a hard ridge through the sensing pad. On bare mineral, some vibrations arrive doubled. I must take more time than other people to decide what I have felt.

This is useful occasionally. More often it is inconvenient.

After a long appointment, the muscles above that foot begin to tremble. I have learned to leave before this happens, even when the household asks for another hour.

At home I lie beneath the public quiet room and let the ordinary traffic pass through me. Someone always hesitates on the third stair. Someone always comes out too quickly and stops, startled by the roar.

I eat with all four feet on the floor.

***

The patient’s youngest household member brings food every day.

They stop at the outer landing. They are careful, devoted, and unable to stand still. While waiting, they clean between their fingers, adjust the food wrapping, lift one foot, replace it. Their neck opens at each sound from the room.

The patient can endure perhaps a minute.

Then her plates begin to pull tightly together.

The visitor leaves the food and goes.

Afterward, I sometimes find them at the basin rim, gripping the yielding edge hard enough to leave six pale hollows.

They ask me what I do in there.

“Mostly, nothing.”

“But how?”

I show them the pads. We try several positions on the outer landing. None works well. Their body is too short for my arrangement, and one support presses against a breathing fold.

Eventually we find a place farther down the stair, where a root has grown between the building and its foundation. Sitting there, they can rest their chest on the root and keep their feet clear of the stone.

The root shifts slowly under their weight.

“Will she know I’m here?”

“We can tell her.”

They consider this.

“Will she keep knowing?”

I do not have a satisfactory answer.

We try it anyway.

***

There is a loose plate on my patient’s shoulder. During my visits I have watched it lift farther from the skin. Soon it will detach.

She cannot comfortably reach it. One day she permits me to work grease beneath its edge.

Up close, her armour is not black. It is brown, densely stippled with green, and there are worn places where years of carrying equipment have polished it almost smooth. A pale line crosses three plates. Something injured her there long before I knew her.

While I work, she rests her fingers against my jaw.

*You have someone below.*

*Yes.*

*Eating?*

I pause to listen.

Through the root, the stair, and the wall behind my hind foot comes a faint, irregular working. A husk being split. Then another.

*Yes.*

Her fingers remain where they are.

I loosen the old plate a little more.

Later, the youngest asks whether they made too much disturbance. They have collected every scrap of the meal in their wrapping.

I tell them she asked what they were doing.

They look down at the wrapping for a long time.

***

This would be my place in the city: not quite in the sickroom, not quite outside it, with a view of animals moving inside a root.

I would have other patients. Some would recover and greet me too loudly at crossings. Some would move away. Some appointments would end, and I would still find myself taking the route to them, carrying pads I no longer needed.

I would grow older beneath the quiet room. There would be a little vegetation in my shoulder grooves, and I would become particular about who trimmed it.

But for now, at the end of a visit, I collect my tools from across the plaza.

The youngest is coming up the stair. They stop when they see me.

“Tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

They pass close enough to touch two fingers briefly beneath my jaw. Then they descend again, carrying nothing, to sit in the place we found.

I wait until their weight has settled before lifting my bag.
