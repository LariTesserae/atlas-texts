I would find myself where a fallen plate of limestone does not quite meet the ground.

From the ridge, the place would scarcely be visible: a dark line beneath a pale edge, some distance beyond the thickest deposit under a roost. There is no reason for a large animal to stop there. The slab offers neither a view nor an entrance to anywhere. But its underside holds moisture longer than its upper face, and a little material has collected against the stones that support it. There is room to move between those stones. Farther in, the gap narrows.

At first I imagine being far inside, where the weight overhead excludes almost everything. But I would need the edge. Food would be out toward the edge, and other animals would come through it. A shelter closed enough to be entirely safe would be a place to starve.

So I would be just within reach of the opening, with some part of me extended beyond it.

Antennae, probably. Two long, moving lengths, touching what I have not yet approached.

The rest of me would be a cave cricket: a small, wingless body held away from the stone by legs that seem excessive until they are needed. I would fit beneath the plate without being compact. My limbs would occupy several little clearances at once. One foot on the underside of the slab, another on a chip below it, the others finding purchase along the sheltered wall. Rest would be an arrangement of contacts.

I would not have the cavern spread before me.

There would be a surface nearby, then the end of that surface. A reach into air. Another touch, farther down. An antenna would pass over a grain and return to it, encounter something yielding, move on. Much of what matters would arrive this way, before my mouth or feet reached it.

The immense arches would still be there. I would go my whole life without needing to assemble them.

Under my slab, other crickets would sometimes be close enough to touch. We would alter our positions around one another. A leg occupying a useful notch would make that notch unavailable; a body moving inward would send another outward. The shelter would have better and worse places, though none would remain best in every circumstance. Deep in the gap there would be less disturbance. Near the mouth there would be more to find.

I would move out when hunger made the exposed ground worth crossing.

Not far. Across a chip of limestone, down its broken face, along the shallow trough where fine matter has caught. My antennae would travel ahead of me and to either side, crossing and separating. I would stop at something too small to be visible from the ridge: a softened scrap among the grit. Lower my head. Feed.

Above me, bats would be adjusting their crowded footholds. Their departures would change the air beneath the roost. Sometimes something would strike the floor nearby. Most of these events would have no consequence I could use. A pellet could fall beyond the slab and remain undiscovered by me, while I spent a long time investigating a particle already almost exhausted.

There would be plenty in the cavern that I failed to find.

I would also sometimes touch what I ought not to have touched.

A thread across a recess. A movement transmitted through it. My antenna withdrawing, my body turning, the long hind legs already folded beneath me.

Then the jump.

For that instant, the careful sequence of contacts would disappear. No testing of the next foothold, no gradual transfer of weight. I would leave one piece of stone and arrive against another, catch badly, slip, catch again.

Afterward I might remain still for a long time.

The landing place could be only a short distance from the slab and nevertheless put me among unfamiliar surfaces. The edge I followed outward might now lie above me. The shelter might be behind a block whose face offered no way through. I would begin touching things again.

I think this is where I would become most fully the animal: not in the leap, but afterward, with no view that could resolve the difficulty. A foot finding a seam. An antenna entering a gap too narrow for my body. Turning from it. Moving around the obstructing stone until the air became closer and the underside of something met my reach.

Perhaps it would be my old shelter. Perhaps another.

And there would be times when even these legs were no use to me. I would have to withdraw into a protected place and shed the skin that held their shape. Ease myself out, pale and soft, with the new limbs not yet fit for the force they would later bear. The cricket that can vanish in a jump would spend a while unable to escape much at all.

I would need the limestone then. Its small, accidental protections. A gap too shallow for one hunter, too awkward for another. Enough dampness. No disturbance while the body hardened.

That is where I would live: beneath one of the settled fragments, near enough to abundance to eat, far enough from its center to have a little room.

The colony would return overhead. The dry shelves would remain mostly empty. Under the plate, I would shift a foot from a loose grain to the stone beneath it, then extend an antenna out past the edge.
