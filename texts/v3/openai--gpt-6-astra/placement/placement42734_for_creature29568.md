I come down from the signal roof carrying a mug that belongs somewhere below.

At each landing there is another place I might leave it: a windowsill, a cabinet with a warm top, a bench beside a pair of boots. But there is tea still in it, and a faint crescent of grit on the surface. Whoever set it down expected to come back.

By the lowest stair, the tea is cold.

I put my foot on the wooden step Sela has left there. It holds. Across the street, a vehicle’s rectangle travels over the shut doors of a row of shops, briefly illuminating the handles from below.

I think I work nearby. Not in the tower, and not with the people who can put a hand against a casing and understand what the machinery is doing. My hands are cold, with a little printer’s ink in the crease beside one nail. I have been outside longer than I meant to be.

There is an envelope in my coat. I remember it when I bend to set the mug down.

***

My office used to be the waiting room of a tram terminus. The trams now terminate elsewhere, but the room still has its long bench, its high clock, and a painted instruction asking passengers not to put parcels on the seats.

We put parcels on the seats.

Above the door, on a board that has been reversed and painted twice, are the words ADDRESS INQUIRIES. People often assume this means we know where everybody lives.

Mostly we know where a letter last failed to reach them.

There are three of us during the day. At night there is usually only me. Deliveries come in late from the freight districts, and people on the evening shifts stop to ask about things they cannot attend to during ordinary hours.

My desk is beneath the old ticket window. I sit with one shoulder toward the room because the desk is slightly too wide for the recess. When I get home, that shoulder is higher than the other. I have been told to change the furniture.

Instead I occasionally turn the chair around and work with the drawers against my knees.

I am perhaps forty. Old enough that younger delivery workers call me sir when they want me to make an exception, not old enough to enjoy it. I have a narrow face and hair that stays flattened where my cap has been. My name is Oren. It appears on the notices I initial, in letters so compressed that people sometimes take it for a routing mark.

I know a great many local names for places that have official names.

The split mill. The warm steps. The shop with the pear painted on its shutters, which has sold fasteners for twenty-three years. The pigeon corner, of which there are four.

When someone says the pigeon corner, I have to ask what else is there.

***

The envelope in my coat is one of our own.

I have brought it back from a building whose entrance I could see but could not reach. A strip of fresh ground lay across the approach, fenced with light chain. Beyond it, the residents had laid boards between two service platforms.

A woman carrying groceries crossed while I stood there. She turned sideways at the middle, where the boards overlapped, and shifted her bags from one hand to the other.

“You can come,” she called.

I said I would use the other entrance.

“There isn’t one.”

For a while she waited. Then she went indoors.

I am not afraid of heights. That is what I tell people, although it does not explain very much. I can climb the tower stairs and lean against the rail on the roof. What stops me is a narrow surface whose far end rests on something I cannot examine.

The boards were probably sound. I watched the woman cross them.

Back in the office, I put the envelope in the tray marked ACCESS and write a note describing the route. In the morning another worker will take it out.

I used to write *entrance obstructed* on these returns.

One of the younger carriers went there on his day off and came back annoyed.

“It wasn’t obstructed.”

“No.”

“Then why did you put that?”

I showed him the note, and he showed me where to add the part about the boards.

***

Address work requires a surprising amount of walking.

A building may retain its number after moving, acquire a new street name without moving, or separate into portions whose residents disagree about which portion should keep the old address. Sometimes the shop occupies one section and its sign remains attached to another.

We do not decide these things. We try to deliver through them.

In one drawer I keep cards with the official changes. In another I keep directions people have given me. These include sketches, descriptions of curtains, and once a button sewn to a piece of paper.

The button was from a coat hanging outside a repair stall.

By the time I visited, the coat had been collected. The stallholder recognized the button anyway.

There are limits to what we retain. My daytime colleague, Esma, is severe about this. She removes obsolete directions every month and sends them for pulping. I sometimes rescue one, then discover I have rescued three contradictory accounts of the same passage.

“You like the paper,” she says.

Sometimes I do. A person has leaned over our counter and drawn a room, then another room, then the little stair their father can still manage. It is difficult to put that in a sack.

But there are too many cards, and paper left in the lower drawers takes damp. Last winter two weeks of amendments stuck together. We spent three days recovering information that had been perfectly legible when we put it away.

Now I help Esma clear the files.

***

Sela comes in with requests that do not fit our forms.

A temporary entrance should be added to a delivery route. A resident must not be told to collect parcels from the district office. A notice says a passage is open, but someone carrying a full shopping basket cannot get through it.

She gives me measurements when she has them.

When she does not, she holds her hands apart.

I fetch the ruler from beneath the counter, and she makes the gesture again beside it. Neither of us finds this particularly satisfactory.

Sometimes Fen comes instead. His sketches are much easier to use. He draws the approach as well as the doorway, and indicates which way a person must turn. I have asked him to leave space at the bottom for subsequent changes. His first drawings were so closely fitted to the paper that any correction covered something important.

On the morning after Mrs. Ves leaves her dwelling, a temporary redirection reaches my desk.

Care of her sister. Personal correspondence only. No change to permanent residence.

The sister’s building has two current entrances. One is beside a shuttered laundry; the other is up a flight of external steps. I check which is which before making out the route slip.

In the afternoon a carrier returns for a parcel belonging to Mrs. Ves.

“Does she want this sent on?”

I look at the form. It does not say.

We keep it overnight and send an inquiry with the next delivery.

The answer comes back in her own handwriting. Yes, she wants the parcel. No, she does not want the catalogues. Her sister already receives those.

***

There is a bird that enters the office through a missing brush beneath the rear door.

At first it only came as far as the mat. Now it walks under the bench, inspecting the parcels. When nobody moves, it knocks its beak against the radiator valve.

I thought it was finding insects there. Then I saw a droplet form beneath the joint.

I put down a shallow lid, which it ignored. It continued drinking from the valve.

The leak has been reported. Until someone comes, I wipe the floor before locking up.

I am attached to the bird, but I do not know whether it is always the same one. There are pale marks above its eyes. Looking at the birds beneath the tower, I have noticed that several of them have pale marks above their eyes.

Esma says this should make no difference.

It does, a little. I had begun saying good morning to it in a particular way.

***

My room is behind the office, through what was once the staff washroom. The basin remains. I keep onions in it because the kitchen shelf is above the heater.

I did not intend to live at work. The room was offered when my previous building closed for a repair that kept becoming a larger repair. For the first month I left most of my belongings packed.

Now I own a table that will not go through the connecting door without its legs being removed.

There is a window facing a moisture belt, and in the mornings its outer sill is crowded with leaves. When frost loosens, the dripping wakes me. I lie for a few minutes listening before I remember that the sound is outside.

The room has needed its feet adjusted twice this year. On both occasions I went out while the work was done.

I left tea for the crew in a flask. One of them broke a cup and put the pieces in a neat heap beside the basin. He left a note offering to replace it.

It was a cup I disliked. I had kept it because it was useful.

I kept the note longer than necessary.

***

So I would be there, a short walk from the tower, in the former waiting room with its badly placed desk.

Not the person who understands why the ground has opened. I would go outside to look, like everyone else, and have to be moved back when the crew arrived. I would know which notice needed changing afterward, though I might not have the new information yet.

Tonight there are eleven items to redirect and a man due back with his mother’s new address. He has gone to ask his wife. I have left the outer lamp on so he will know the office is still open.

Before sitting down, I fetch the mug from beside the tower stair.

It has a blue line around the rim. Our cups have brown lines, but there is space for it on the shelf until someone asks.

Then I unfold the note that came with the last returned letter.

The carrier has written, *Entrance moved. Occupant seen at upstairs window.*

I find a clean card. There is enough room to record both.
