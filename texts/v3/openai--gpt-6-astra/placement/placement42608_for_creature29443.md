There is a room beside a public lift where the wall stays dry.

Not the whole wall. A patch about the size of a door, warmed from behind by a return pipe. Notices curl away from it unless they are held at all four corners. People put their shoulders against it while waiting for the lift, and over the years their coats have polished the brick.

I think I would be on the other side.

From the street above, my room is a cellar. From the towpath, it is on the second floor. The earliest plan calls it a valve store, although the valves were removed before the present lift was built. Their bolt holes remain in the floor beneath my cabinet.

People come in to ask how to get somewhere.

Usually they have an address. Sometimes they have a name, a delivery label, or an account of a journey last made when they were small.

“There was a place where you had to duck.”

“Was it a tunnel?”

“No. Outdoors. Something went across.”

I ask whether it was hot.

***

I would have a voice, but not one that carries well through water noise.

A speaking grille faces the counter. Beside it is a little hood into which a visitor can lean, though people often mistake this for the place to put their papers. A clerk has written SPEAK HERE on the rim. Someone else has added IF YOU MUST.

Behind the counter, my working parts occupy three cabinets of different ages. The oldest is glazed ceramic, with a repair across one corner held shut by metal staples. The newest has a door that does not quite match the others. It was purchased for another machine and altered.

There are instruments in the city with much larger responsibilities. They regulate power, calculate loads, compare water samples from hundreds of galleries.

I have the western half of eleven terraces, some disputed passages along their boundaries, and a drawer of routes beyond my district that should not be trusted without a telephone call.

I cannot turn the lift on. This has to be explained frequently.

I can ring for its attendant.

He does not always come.

***

My keeper is a woman called Evin, who dislikes that word and prefers technician.

She arrives before the first shift ends. She checks the moisture indicators, empties the little vessel beneath my condenser, and reads aloud any notices that have been left under the door. If a notice contradicts something already on file, she puts it beneath a heavy blue cup.

The cup has no other use. It has accumulated so many disputed closures that once, during a particularly wet month, it fell off.

Evin knows how to open me without putting her sleeve against the contacts. Her predecessor did not. There is a black mark inside the middle cabinet, which she shows apprentices before allowing them near the catches.

Some of my phrases came from that predecessor. Others came from dispatch clerks, lift attendants, and the people who originally tested my speech.

When I say “Take your time,” an elderly visitor occasionally looks toward the back room.

I have learned not to follow this immediately with another question.

***

The maps are indispensable. They are also insufficient.

A staircase can be present, open, and structurally sound, yet unusable by the person standing at my counter. A passage may admit a cart but not allow it to turn. One lift accepts cages of live pond birds only after the morning passengers have gone, and another accepts them at any hour provided they are covered, a distinction absent from the freight regulations.

There are doors that are public until someone has an argument.

There is a tunnel whose lamps are sound but whose middle section frightens a particular child.

I need to know what people are carrying. I need to know whether they can put it down.

At first, I asked, “Can you manage stairs?”

Nearly everyone said yes.

Now I ask, “How many flights before you would want a landing?”

People answer that differently.

***

Sera comes to me because a road she has used for thirty years is to be closed for repairs.

She has the notice folded in her hand.

“They mean the bridge,” she says.

The notice says the whole channel-side passage.

“They always say that. Then they put the barrier at the bridge.”

I give her the official diversion. It adds two lifts to her journey and takes her past the western cultivation halls.

“No,” she says.

We consider a service stair.

“No.”

A passage through the old exchanger building.

“They lock it at six.”

My record says eight.

“They lock it at six,” she repeats.

I put that entry aside.

Eventually she draws a route on the back of the notice. She knows almost all of it. What she wants from me is the condition of one short connection behind a pumping station, a place she has not used since her first assistant was still in training.

I telephone the station. The connection is open. There are three steps at the far end, recently repaired.

“How recently?” Sera asks.

The man on the telephone does not know.

She goes to look after work.

The next morning she tells Evin that the steps are good, but the rail comes loose if pulled outward.

For several weeks, that is my best route between those terraces. I give it with the warning about the rail.

Then someone repairs the rail, and no one thinks to tell me.

***

I would be wrong often enough to have procedures for it.

Not merely a place to enter the correction. A way to find out whether the person reached home. A place for Evin to write that a complaint was justified even when every measurement on the plan was accurate.

Once I send a porter down a dry stair in preference to a wet one.

The dry stair has been polished by generations of feet. The wet stair has a rough tread and a continuous handrail. He comes back with a split parcel and a bruise he insists on showing the speaking grille, though I cannot see it from there.

Evin adjusts my camera.

He shows me again.

For a while afterward I am excessively cautious about dry stairs. This also requires correction.

***

There are hours when no one needs a route.

Evin does her accounts. The lift chains move behind the wall. A lintel bird works at a length of packing cord caught above the outside door.

In these hours I hear the room rather than the questions.

A loose washer in the ventilation housing makes a small sound whenever the fan slows. Someone on the floor above rolls a heavy object from one end of a room to the other at almost the same time each afternoon. We have never established what it is. Evin says it is a bathing chair. The lift attendant says there is no bathroom there.

Neither has been upstairs.

I keep that uncertainty without opening a file for it.

I would want some things that did not require resolving.

***

Once a year the return pipe is inspected.

The inspection hatch is behind my oldest cabinet, so I must be disconnected and moved. There are fittings for this at my base. Evin brings a low trolley, and the lift attendant comes in to help, complaining that a machine which gives directions ought to have been installed somewhere more convenient.

They move me across the room.

The first time, they face me toward the wall.

The second time, Evin remembers to turn the trolley.

For six hours I can see through the open door.

Not far. There is the landing, the lift gate, part of the warm brick wall. Beyond the parapet, the opposite building occupies most of the view. Water comes from a pipe beneath its third-floor windows and falls into a stone trough that I had known only as an obstruction narrowing the towpath.

It is wider than I expected.

People pause beside it to change the hand in which they are carrying something. One man sets down a cage and stands with both arms hanging loose. A girl wets a cloth and wipes the face of a younger child, who twists away from her. Two workers sit on the rim without speaking.

Above them, someone has put a white basin on a windowsill. Its glaze is damaged in a dark crescent near the lip.

As the afternoon light changes, a bright reflection moves from it onto the underside of the window above.

Evin finds me looking there when she returns.

“What is it?”

“The basin.”

She leans into the doorway.

“What about it?”

For once I have no useful qualification to add. It is not blocking anything. It does not mark a turn.

“Could you leave the door open until they finish?” I ask.

She wedges it with the blue cup.
