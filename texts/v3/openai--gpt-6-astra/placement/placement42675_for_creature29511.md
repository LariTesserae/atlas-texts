I would be beneath the animal, where the carrying legs close around something they do not have to look at.

At first I would mistake their grip for the pressure of the root. There would be warmth on all sides, small adjustments, the long creaking of a body too large to know. Only when the animal lifted me would I discover that I was loose.

I think I would be the splinter.

Not its sharp end. The other one, broken in the lower opening, softened by drinking. The part that enters the damp before the mouth does.

There would be very little of me capable of feeling. Most of my length would remain dry and stiff, with the old parallel structure of the root still running through it. But at the broken end, water would separate the fibers. Each wetting would reach a little farther. Each drying would draw them together imperfectly.

My shape would remember this, though I would remember nothing else.

I would not know the animal by its outline. I would know the two places where it grips me, the slight twist before it changes direction, the pressure of its mouth that is different from the pressure of its feet. When it sleeps, those pressures become steady. The root continues its enormous work around us, but for a while no one asks me to bend.

Once, I was attached to that work.

There is no whole memory of it. Only an old pull along my length, a tendency to straighten toward something that is no longer there. Occasionally, when the animal sets me down, the living floor takes hold of a few fibers at my end. It does so without a jerk. A dampness, then a faint adhesion.

The animal comes back and lifts me.

Sometimes a little of me stays behind.

—

With the smaller animal aboard, the journey to the new water is different.

Its weight is almost nothing. Its movements are not. It grips too near one edge, then shifts abruptly, trying to find a surface broad enough for all its feet. The larger animal compensates beneath me. We turn a little toward the wall.

One of the younger animal’s forelegs lies across my broken end.

For the first time, something looks at me from there.

I do not shine as the floor shines before opening. Whatever light leaves me has already left the root. The little foot lifts and comes down again, farther along. It seems to be searching for the continuation of a surface that I cannot provide.

Then the larger animal stops.

Ahead, water fills a crease so new that its edges are still connected by threads. It is not one of the familiar pools. The animal tests the near side, then reaches toward the far side without transferring its weight.

I am lowered across the gap.

My dry end catches under a ridge. My worn end enters the water.

For a moment, nothing presses.

The young animal is still beneath me.

It has to come around.

There is a long confusion of small contacts: a foot beside the larger animal’s mouth, another hooked into a fraying strand, the delicate scraping of an abdomen against my underside. The larger animal holds still. Thunder travels through the wall. The water trembles without spilling.

Then the little body reaches my upper surface.

It advances along me with its forelegs raised.

I have never been walked upon this way. Always I have been carried, placed, pressed, pulled free. Now the pressure moves from one end of me toward the other, stopping often. Under each foot I bend too slightly for either animal to notice, but the bend runs through my whole length.

At the wet end, the younger one lowers its head.

Its mouth does not fit.

It tries against the side, where a bead has gathered between two fibers. The bead withdraws. It tries again.

The larger animal shifts its grip and presses me down.

Water rises through the broken wood, carrying with it something faintly bitter that was once part of me. This time the small mouth meets it.

The feet tighten.

—

I had thought being the splinter would mean being passed between the root and the animal, belonging fully to neither: wood that the floor wants back, a tool the animal will not surrender.

But there is no need to settle that here.

The younger animal is drinking. Its hindfeet rest on a dry part of me. Farther back, the larger animal holds on with four feet and keeps the remaining pair around my middle. Through those several points of contact, the wall’s movements reach us in different strengths.

A thread breaks overhead.

The young one flattens. Its mouth comes away wet.

We wait.

At my end, loosened by the water, one short fiber curls outward. The younger animal catches it with a foot. When it shifts its weight, the fiber bends but does not come free.

The larger animal begins to lift me from the crease.

Both of them are attached now.

I leave the water slowly, heavier at one end than when we arrived.
