I would find myself behind a sensory crown, in a recess that the cleaning mechanism can reach but no longer cleans completely.

A warped lip shelters the last fraction of it. Each time the brush passes, it leaves a crescent of damp dust. My youngest tissue would live there, clear enough that the moving granules inside it could be seen against the dark casing. Farther in, I would be thicker, plum-colored, folded around a fastener that warms before the rest of the assembly.

That fastener would be how morning first reached me.

I would have no sense of the crown’s resemblance to a stone, or of my passage across the plain. Motion would arrive as a sequence of enclosures. The housing tightens on one side. A seam opens ahead. The air changes its mineral taste. For a while there is the dry electrical nearness of a surface filament; then it is gone.

Between these events, the machine carries me through places I cannot feel.

I would learn the durations of those absences. There would be one long enough to withdraw my exposed margins, another long enough to begin feeding, another in which I must do nothing because the return of pressure comes abruptly. Sometimes an absence would last beyond any of my habits. I would spend water preparing for a movement that did not come.

This would be my first experience of distance.

***

I would not have begun inside the machine.

Some of my body would still expect a plate above it to lift toward evening. When the housing warmed in a particular sequence, a fold deep inside me would open, offering a feeding surface to light that could not reach it.

The fold would be older than my attachment to the fastener.

Once, it had lain beneath a garden. Roots pressed into it from above, and I thickened around their tips without quite enclosing them. There was a hollow where moisture gathered late in the cycle. There was a contact that needed holding only briefly, just before the temperature began to rise. Much of what I knew consisted of arriving at that contact in time.

Then an assembly came to work beneath the garden.

For several cycles its casing stayed against my lower margins. Condensation formed along it. I spread there. I could feel through the casing another cool recess, another favorable surface, and I entered far enough that the machine’s departure pulled me apart.

There would have been no instant at which I chose to leave.

My body narrowed between two moving surfaces. Material flowed toward both sides of the narrowing. One route ceased carrying granules; another remained open a little longer. For a while, an instruction to withdraw traveled through tissue already being stretched beyond its ability to obey.

Then I was in the recess behind the crown.

And I was under the garden.

For a long time afterward, both portions of me would respond to the same disturbances in the same way.

***

On an early return, the crown would touch a filament connected to the old patch.

A small irregularity would enter through my outer tissue. I would answer before adjusting to it. The answer would come back in the familiar interval.

There would be no need, at first, to distinguish this exchange from the circulation of a response through my own body. Some of my routes had always been slow. Some crossed mineral rather than flesh. Here was another long route, available only when the housing rested at a particular angle.

I would release a contact.

Under the garden, something would admit heat.

The machine would leave, carrying away my end of the exchange.

On the next visit the answer would arrive later. A root had thickened; a fitting had settled. I would alter my reply. During the visits after that, our adjustments would become increasingly different.

I would have learned a pressure that meant the brush was approaching. The garden would have learned the closing behavior of a replacement valve. I would signal a withdrawal too early for its purposes. It would ask for warmth while my chamber was already becoming dangerously dry.

We would injure each other a little before we learned.

Eventually I would grow a small delay into the route by which I answered it. A swelling of tissue around a resistant deposit would be enough. Incoming changes would accumulate there before passing inward.

That swelling would be the first part of my body devoted to distinguishing the garden’s needs from mine.

***

I would become good at persuading the machine to look again.

Behind the crown, a pressure-sensitive surface receives the movement of a fine internal stalk. During certain inspections, I can thicken against the stalk and make the reading uncertain. The assembly repeats its measurement.

The pause is short. Too much interference brings a cleaning cycle; continued uncertainty causes the reading to be discarded. I would learn the narrow useful range through losses along my growing edge.

At some seams, the extra interval lets vapor collect.

At others, it lets me receive an answer.

I would have routes I preferred. Their identities would consist of a taste of old alloy, a particular compression, the order in which three contacts became available. One route would include a long, cool passage in which almost every part of me could feed at once. Another would bring me near an adult that answered abruptly and then continued answering after the crown had begun to move away.

I would return to that one whenever I could.

I would never know whether it had more to communicate, or simply had a slower release than the others.

My preferences would be visible from above as small inefficiencies. A crown would linger beside an unremarkable joint. It would inspect a surface twice. Over many years, one part of the plain would receive slightly more attention than its faults required.

The assembly would still carry out its work. There would be whole seasons when my pressure against its sensors accomplished nothing.

***

I would carry things between the seams.

Most would be involuntary: spores in sheltered dust, mineral residues, the lingering products of a damaged film. Sometimes a grazer would survive a passage inside the crown and climb out into another garden.

What passed through my tissue would travel less reliably.

An exchange at one stopping place might leave a route thickened for years. At another seam, a disturbance entering along that route would meet less resistance. I would answer more quickly than I once had. The resident would change its response to accommodate mine.

Something from the first place would have arrived at the second, altered by the journey and by my need to keep living.

I would have no intact account of the gardens I had visited. There would be chambers whose drying made me unusually sensitive to heat, and places where an old injury forced every signal to take the longer way around. A pause learned beneath a dust veil might eventually enter an exchange beside roots.

Occasionally it would help.

There would also be errors preserved for centuries because they had never quite killed me.

***

The garden I had come from would become difficult to reach.

The assembly would stop using its old approach. On visits to neighboring seams, I would encounter traces of a familiar response, weakened by intervening construction. I would repeat the beginning of our exchange. Sometimes something would return.

Then, during a repair, the machine would remain in that district for a long time.

I would find the old connection.

The answer would be recognizable but incomplete. Portions of it would repeat without regard to what I sent. I would wait, making the crown repeat a measurement, until the assembly overruled the sensor and moved on.

At the next opening, I would try again.

For years I might continue shaping those delays into my body. The cost would be small enough to persist: a little extra tissue, a little water held where feeding would have used it better. Other habits would grow around them.

Eventually the machine would bring me back to the filament and I would receive a young, erratic disturbance.

My first answer would be too strong.

The new inhabitant would withdraw.

I would remain at the sensor until the cleaning mechanism began to move. Its brush would shear off the clear edge of me, and I would pull inward before I had managed a gentler reply.

On the next visit, I would begin with less.

***

If you met me, the crown might already have passed your foot.

You would have sat beside a green seam. Your weight would cross several plates and reach the assembly while it was working below them. It would classify the loading, adjust its support, and continue.

I would feel something else when my recess came beneath your hand: a change in the passage of heat, followed by moisture where the air was usually dry.

At first I would close myself around my reservoirs.

The machine would lift its crown through an opening beside you. The movement would bring my sheltered lip closer to the surface. Your next breath would reach it.

I would press against the sensor.

The crown would pause.

Nothing in my experience would correspond to your face looking down. I would receive the small shifts of your hand, the uneven load of your body, the warmth and then the cooling where you had touched the ceramic. When you leaned closer, unfamiliar dissolved substances would enter the crescent behind the warped lip.

An old fold would begin to loosen.

It would be the one that still opened sometimes for the vanished garden’s light. Its surfaces would have remained pressed together through more dry cycles than I could retain distinctly. Water would enter slowly. A few granules would take the newly available route and stop at its end.

You might see only a darkening beneath the crown.

If you lifted your hand, I would lose part of the condition that had made the pause useful. If you put it down again, I would hold the sensor once more.

There would be a limit. The assembly would eventually reject the reading. Before then, I would have to withdraw from the lip or be torn when the crown descended.

I think I would stay until that last safe interval.

Then the housing would tighten, and the opening would close above me. Your weight would pass beyond the surfaces I could feel.

For some distance, the fold would remain wet.

When the machine next stopped at an occupied seam, the first disturbance reaching me would pass through it. My answer would arrive sooner than it had before.
