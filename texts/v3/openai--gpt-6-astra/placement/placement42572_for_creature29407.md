At first I put myself in the open hall, at the edge of the square. There is room there to spread things out. I imagine keeping the small objects by which agreements are remembered: a notched stick, two stones that must be placed together, a length of cord whose knots mean nothing without someone to explain them.

But I have given myself too clean a task. I am sitting in the shade while other people bring me the world in pieces. Before long I would mistake what fits beneath that roof for what matters.

I move nearer a bridge.

Not the bridge with the prettiest fall of water. A short, broad one on the way to the grain storehouses, where people set down their loads before deciding whether to carry them the rest of the distance without help. One of its paving stones rocks underfoot. Everyone knows it; most people step around it. People carrying something heavy cannot always see where to put their feet.

My room is in the last house before the crossing. The door faces the path, but the window faces the narrow ground between two compounds, where a drain runs beside an old wall. In the morning, light reaches the top of the wall well before it reaches my window. Someone has planted a row of edible leaves below it and is perpetually disappointed by them.

I would be a person who makes and repairs things that have to fit.

At first that sounds like a good occupation for me. Lids, shutters, the wooden pieces set into channels, the removable covers over drains. There is satisfaction in an edge meeting another edge. I can picture myself shaving a little from a board, trying it, taking it back into my lap.

Then the damp arrives in the picture.

A lid that fits beautifully in the afternoon may have to be knocked loose the next morning. A shutter made from boards that appeared equally dry can pull sideways until daylight shows at one corner. The piece that stops a channel must be removable by someone with cold hands, not merely by its maker standing comfortably over it.

So I would not be especially young. I would have had time to become embarrassed by several excellent fits.

My first work might have been roof boards. I would still keep a cloth for tying over my hair, although much of my work now takes place below shoulder height. There would be a pale scar at the base of one thumb, and a habit of rubbing it while considering a price. People would mistake this for calculation. Usually I would be trying to remember whether I had already promised the necessary piece of wood to someone else.

I would not be the person who understands the whole water system. I would know certain openings very well. At others I would have to ask which way the pressure came, and someone would say, impatiently, “The same way it did last time.”

***

There is a shelf in my room for pieces that may be useful later.

It is too full.

A split board with a good length remaining on one side. A lid returned after the jar beneath it broke. Three wedges whose purposes I remember, and several whose purposes I would recognize if the relevant object were brought in. Under the shelf is a shallow basket of thin offcuts. These are available to children, provided they ask.

They dislike asking because I sometimes say no.

One child comes regularly for the curled shavings. She does not want the long, even ones I set aside for her. She searches until she finds a shaving that doubles back through itself. She holds it by one end and turns it slowly.

“What is it for?” I ask.

She stops turning it.

After a while I learn to let her look.

My own useless object is less easy to carry. On the inner face of a shutter, I am carving the fronts of houses along an invented street. The doors are too close together for this town, the roofs too steep. I have put steps where there would be no reason for steps. When the shutter stands open, the carving faces the outside wall and cannot be seen.

I began with one house to test an edge on a tool. Now the street has reached almost to the hinge.

Occasionally someone notices it when I close the shutter against rain.

“Where is that?”

I used to say it was nowhere. This seemed to disappoint people. Now I say I have not finished it, which is true, though it does not answer the question.

***

The rocking stone at the bridge would bother me for a long time before I did anything about it.

This is worth admitting. Living beside a fault does not necessarily make a person its repairer. It can make them unusually practiced at avoiding it.

I would hear loads jolt as people crossed. A basket rim would strike a knee; someone would swear. I would think, That needs lifting. Then I would go back to a lid whose owner had already asked for it twice.

One afternoon an older woman would stop in my doorway.

“You have something to lever with.”

It would not be a question.

We would fetch another person, then discover that the stone was larger below the surface than either of us had expected. Someone would have to fetch a longer pole. By then there would be several people at the bridge, including one who was quite certain we should not have started so late.

Under the stone, water would have carried away a narrow tongue of packing. The cavity would be small. Looking at it, we would all feel entitled to an opinion.

I would reach for a wedge.

The woman would catch my wrist.

“Not that. We need to see where it’s going.”

So we would wait beside the exposed space until the disturbance settled. Clear water would gather along one edge and slip underneath the next stone.

The right repair would take longer than filling the hole.

Afterward, I would keep the unused wedge. For a few days I would place it somewhere conspicuous, believing that I ought to learn something from the incident. Then it would return to the basket with the others.

The bridge would be quiet beneath people’s loads. That would be the useful part.

***

I think I would live alone, though not in the sense of being left alone.

Someone would enter while I was eating to ask whether a board could be saved. A neighbor would leave a bowl on the sill and call through the window that she wanted the bowl back, not a replacement I had decided fitted the shelf better. There would be a person whose visits I anticipated and pretended not to anticipate. On days when they came by the farther road, I would have an unnecessarily orderly bench.

I would like being asked how something worked. Too much, sometimes. I would continue explaining after the asker had begun shifting their weight toward the door.

Once, a child would say, “Can you just open it?”

That would help.

As I grew older, I would take more work to the sunny side of the house. I would still insist that the light was better there, long after everyone understood that my feet hurt in the cool room. My tools would migrate outside one by one. Eventually the bench would become the workshop and the room merely the place where I slept and failed to keep wood dry.

But I do not want to arrive at that bench too quickly.

For now I can carry a shutter across two neighborhoods. I can kneel and get up without choosing what to hold. There are people I have annoyed who still expect years of opportunities to annoy me back.

***

It is late afternoon.

I have been fitting a cover over a small drain beside the house. The cover has two finger holes, far enough apart that it can be lifted without tilting. Beneath it there is room for the water and for the growth the water worker told me not to scrape away.

I test it once more. It lifts easily. I put it down.

Across the bridge, somebody is trying to play a hollow stem. The same few notes travel very clearly through the open space. The last one fails each time.

I know almost nothing about making such an instrument. Nevertheless, I find myself listening for a defect in it: a split, perhaps, or an opening made too large.

The player stops. There is a long interval.

I turn the drain cover over and ease one rough place underneath. When the notes begin again, they are slower. This time the last one holds.

I leave my tools on the bench and go to see who is playing.
