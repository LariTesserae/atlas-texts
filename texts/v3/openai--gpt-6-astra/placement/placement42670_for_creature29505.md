I would be farther down than the amber doorway, in a part of the station that Edda does not count as a room.

At low tide there would be an outside wall. At high tide there would be a ceiling over water. Neither description would remain useful for very long. A ladder would descend through a hatch to a narrow ledge, and beneath the ledge the original foundations would continue into a cleft in the basalt. There would be shell fragments on the ladder’s bottom rung. Something with many legs would retreat when the inspection light came on.

That is where I would first put myself: in the cleft, below the weather but not beyond its reach.

The noise would be different there. Rain would hardly register. A stone rolling in the river mouth would arrive through the foundation, distinct enough to interrupt whatever I was doing. The diversion’s anchors would produce a faint, persistent strain. Boats would announce themselves through the water well before anyone at the doorway saw them.

I would know Edda partly by the ladder.

She comes down facing it, including on the last two rungs, where her nephew turns and jumps. There is a pause when she transfers her weight from the left leg. In winter she sometimes stops on the ledge without doing anything. She says she is checking the seals around the hatch. Usually she is waiting for her knee.

I would keep the light on until she went back up.

At first I imagine having no body beyond this listening place. But there are too many things I would want to examine closely, and too many surfaces between a sound and its cause. So there would be a body, though not a particularly accomplished one.

It would have been made for inspecting the submerged foundations: a low shell, four jointed legs, two short manipulators folded underneath. Broad feet for silt, replaceable hooks for stone. Small enough to fit through the hatch if someone turned it sideways. Too heavy for Edda to carry now.

I would spend most of my time attached to a socket beneath the ledge. One side of my shell would have grown a fringe of sedentary animals. The other side, which rubbed against the socket, would be bare.

There would be newer inspection bodies in the valley. They would fit narrower cracks and return measurements faster. I would use their reports, occasionally with resentment, although their existence would spare me several jobs I disliked.

My work would concern the meeting of land and water. Not the whole meeting: a few shelves, the bar, the caves beneath the shore, the station’s buried feet. I would have records from when some of the present willow ground lay underwater. In those records, Edda’s alder hollow would be a place where small flatfish settled into mud.

This would not make me wiser about the hollow tonight.

The water beneath the roots would be too far inland, and the available instruments would be reporting the quantities they had been installed to report. I might feel a change in the load on an eastern anchor. I could tell Edda that it began twelve minutes ago. I could not tell her where to put her pole.

She would have learned to ask me narrow questions.

“Has this happened before?”

“Which part?”

“The knocking.”

That I could answer.

I think there would be a long history to that exchange. Earlier in my life I would have answered the first question with everything resembling the present circumstances. Tides, slumps, ice, maintenance incidents, the collapse of a cave roof seventy years previously. Edda, wet through and trying to keep one glove dry, would have had to stop me.

Now I would wait for the second question.

***

My name would be on an old equipment plate beside the socket, but nobody would use all of it. The nephew would call me Sill. Edda would adopt this after several years of calling it an unnecessary name for a system that already had a perfectly serviceable designation.

She would still use the designation when annoyed.

I would not go to her house often. The path’s footholds would be designed for a different distribution of weight, and the hooks on my feet would damage the floors. Once, carried up for a repair during a spring tide, I would have spent six days on a tray in her entrance.

That would be where I learned how much household life occurs near boots.

People would arrive intending to leave something and stay an hour. Someone would sit on the damp bench to cry, then become angry about the wet seat. Edda would rehearse a remark before opening the inner door and deliver an entirely different one once inside. The animal behind the wall would go quiet whenever my manipulator moved.

I would be given a view of the citrus tree on the fifth day.

By then I would have heard about it for years. I would know its light consumption, its irrigation schedule, the small but measurable effect of its room on the house’s heat balance. I would expect something larger.

Edda would notice my camera turn toward the top of the doorway, then back down.

“Yes,” she would say. “That’s all of it.”

She would leave me there while she washed the leaves. Water would gather at their tips and fall into the tray beneath the pot. I would adjust my microphone twice before giving up on separating those drops from the faint noises of her sleeves.

***

Tonight, the nephew’s recording would reach me while I was checking the station’s lowest seal.

I would listen with one manipulator still pressed against the seam.

The sound would be familiar enough to make me search the wrong records first: stones shifting inside a confined space. Then air leaving a flooded cavity. Then the short, altered calls that seals sometimes make beneath the station.

None would fit well.

The nephew would have recorded more than he played in the warm room. I would ask for the beginning, including his descent and the time he spent adjusting the receiver. There would be handling noise, a brush against gravel, his own breathing transmitted down the cable.

Beyond those sounds, the repetition would already be there.

I would move out of the socket.

This would take longer than anyone listening upstairs might expect. The left rear hook would need freeing from something that had grown around it. I would set each foot before releasing the next, descend the rock, and stop below the turbulent layer where freshwater spread over salt.

No great journey. Six meters, perhaps eight. Far enough to put the station behind a shoulder of basalt.

For a while I would hear nothing comparable.

Then a short run of sounds would come from beyond the bar, indistinct but separate from the gravel moving nearby. I would hold still through the next wave and hear it again.

I would send Edda the live channel.

Her spoon would touch the bowl. The nephew would stop explaining his settings.

“Are you outside?” she would ask.

“Below the ledge.”

“Stay on the near side of the cleft.”

I would shorten the next step I had been preparing to take. The inlet would pull at the animals growing along my shell. Behind me, water would climb the ladder, covering one rung and then another.

We would listen from our separate places.
