I would be under the place where the path almost holds.

Not at the great rupture, with its birds and exposed machinery, but a little way off: beneath a strip of ground that takes weight without quite returning to its former shape. From above, there would be nothing to distinguish my dwelling except a shallow accumulation of water. A grazer might sleep on it. I would know that grazer by the uneven pressure of its ribs.

At first I imagine myself attending to the braids. Feeling their changes, replacing a broken attachment, learning which knots have tightened because the water is colder and which because the route beyond them has become dangerous.

But I would not be especially good at knowing when to throw one away.

My chamber would contain too many.

They would hang in the slow current along the wall, far enough apart not to tangle. Some would be useful. Some would have come from passages that no longer admitted a body. A few would have no strands of my own kind in them at all, only vegetable fibers tied around the place where a fringe had decayed.

I would go among these with my jaw lowered, receiving directions I could no longer follow.

Someone sharing the chamber would object. There would be a particular pressure for this: two feet braced, one dragging backward. You are obstructing the water.

They would be right. I would move the oldest braids into a less favorable corner.

For a while, the water would improve.

***

I think I would have spent too long inside a capsule.

Not some immense, legendary interval. Long enough that the individual who placed me in storage could still be alive, but no longer able to make the journey to retrieve me. Long enough for the way home to require another way home.

I would have no recollection of waiting. My first experience would be of an enclosing surface becoming edible, and beyond it the touch of feet trying to prevent me from eating the wrong part.

Those who brought me up would be younger than my unhatched body. This would confer no dignity. I would put my head into drains. I would grip pressure doors at the hinge. I would repeatedly mistake the release of a distant chamber for something small and injured close at hand.

An adult would put a foot over my fringe until the impulse to follow passed.

The one who retrieved me would have a damaged rear pad. When we rested together, one point of contact would always be missing. I would learn their presence partly through that absence.

For a long time I would assume this was what safety felt like: pressure along my side, a gap, then pressure again.

They would take me to the listening wall and lie above me, keeping my back against the thin tissue. At first there would be too much country. Every discharge would move some part of me. My hands would open involuntarily. I would try to crawl away from a sound that was coming through the floor, the ceiling, my companion’s bones.

Gradually I would learn to leave most of it unattended.

One day I would remain asleep through the emptying of the nearest reservoir.

That would be an achievement. No one would mark it except by getting up and leaving me alone.

***

As an adult I would have a long body, narrow toward the rear, with a fold that did not close properly on one side. Small objects would escape from it. I would discover this defect while carrying something important, not before.

The plants on my back would grow more thickly near my head than near my tail. Passing beneath low roofs would comb them into a pale-edged crest that other individuals could feel before they reached my skin.

I would dislike being touched there.

I would tolerate it from the one with the damaged foot.

Most of my work would be ordinary work. Clearing the mouths of small conduits. Pulling a dead root through a hole too narrow for its branches. Holding an opening while another individual passed a capsule through. Eating, and sometimes eating enough that I would have to rest before I could fit through the route by which I had arrived.

There would be mornings when the whole dwelling tasted faintly wrong.

We would move the young first, then our stored food, then the objects whose usefulness was harder to establish. On such a morning I would have to choose among the old braids. With each trip, the passage would become softer and more difficult.

Eventually my companion would wait at the exit with their body across it.

I would press that I intended to go back once more.

They would not move.

We might quarrel. I might put my fine inner fingers beneath their side, where I knew they disliked it most.

Then something deep in the chamber would give way, quietly enough that we would feel the change in the current before the sound.

For years afterward, I would remember which braid I had been going back to fetch.

***

There would be one I kept with me.

It would be short, poorly made, and unpleasant to examine. One strand would swell much faster than the others, bending the whole arrangement against the fringe. Its maker had compensated by tying a knot so bulky that it obscured the smaller changes.

I made it while learning.

My companion had taken it from me, adjusted one fastening, and installed it beside a safe junction. For some time I thought it was being used to direct travelers.

Later I understood that it had been put where its errors could do little harm.

I would retrieve it after the junction changed.

I would carry it through several dwellings, occasionally trying to improve it. Each improvement would make it less legible. At last I would stop touching the knots.

Sometimes, searching my flank for food, I would find it and be briefly irritated that I still had it.

***

I would make the tensioned stems, too, though not where visitors usually gathered.

Behind my dwelling there would be a pocket low enough that I had to flatten my ribs to enter. Three stems would cross it. One would transmit a clean vibration; one would rub against a sheath; the third would become slack whenever a nearby chamber filled.

I would spend long periods trying to make something with these differences.

My first attempts would resemble routes. A held pressure, a quick release, a series of smaller contacts: the way through a constriction, the hesitation before a drop. Anyone listening would begin preparing to move.

I would find this unsatisfying without knowing why.

Over time I would learn to continue past the point where a route ought to arrive. To repeat a pressure after its practical meaning had been exhausted. To let the slack stem remain slack, then bring it into the pattern when the water withdrew.

Sometimes my companion would settle outside the pocket.

They would listen with their damaged foot tucked beneath them. If they fell asleep, their weight would alter the sheath around the second stem. The vibration would become rougher.

I would keep that sound.

Afterward, when they were elsewhere, I would try to reproduce it by pressing the sheath myself. I would never quite manage it. There had been more than weight involved: breathing, the small adjustments of rest, a body responding to mine without waking.

***

I would come late to the edge.

For much of my life I would have regarded it as an exposed place where work was difficult and food unreliable. Those returning from it would carry unfamiliar water, but many deep passages also yielded unfamiliar water. I would have enough to learn beneath ground that could hold me.

Then the individual with the damaged pad would begin going there.

At first they would be absent for one pressure cycle. Then several. They would return with the growth on their head rubbed thin and their front fingers kept tightly folded against their underside.

I would examine those fingers while they slept.

Eventually I would follow.

The edge would disturb me less because of its height than because so little of it answered. On the plateau, even an empty chamber returned the touch of a foot through its walls. Here I would press the lip of roots and feel my own weight travel away without coming back.

My companion would lower themself over it.

I would grip their rear body too hard.

They would send an impatient contraction backward through their muscles. I would loosen my hold but not release it.

Below us the droplets would stand in their interrupted fall. Fine roots would be entering some of them. Others would hang untouched, each carrying a tiny brightness that did not match the light above.

My companion would gather roots around one drop and wait.

I would wait with them, half my body still wedged safely beneath the mat.

Nothing would happen that I knew how to recognize.

We would return hungry.

***

I would go again.

Not every day. There would still be channels to clear, a ceiling to support, disputes about the space occupied by my belongings. My companion would grow slower. I would learn to approach their damaged side when offering help so they could take my weight without turning.

At the edge, I would begin by holding the roots they passed up.

Later I would hang beside them.

My front hands would become very cold. I would have to withdraw them one at a time and hold them against my belly. Once I would lose my grip and be caught by a foot hooked into the defective fold of my flank. For several days the fold would ache when I moved.

Then, on a visit otherwise unremarkable, I would put my hand farther down.

Something would press against the inner divisions of the pad.

I would close them too quickly.

The pressure would cease.

For a long time I would remain extended into the mist, waiting for another contact. Above me, my companion would shift their grip as the cold worked into their limbs.

Eventually they would touch the base of my tail.

We need to go.

I would draw myself up with a little water cupped in my hand.

We would find shelter beneath the root lip. My companion would lower their fringe into my palm, and I would feel its threads moving between my fingers. When they had finished, I would taste the water myself.

Neither of us would start home immediately.

Before we left, I would take the old, badly made braid from my flank and fasten it where the water passed toward the edge. I would keep one foot against it while the strands swelled.

Then I would loosen the bulky knot a little, leaving room for it to change.
