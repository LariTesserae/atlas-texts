At first I would look for somewhere to put my hand.

The broad stone would seem possible until I saw the worms. Then the root, until its knobs became shells. I would keep discovering that what I had taken for a way into the hollow was already someone’s place.

For a while I would remain above it, bent under the branches, one hand held uselessly near my chest. I would be trying to solve the ordinary problem of entering without doing harm. There ought to be a gap, a place to set down part of my weight before bringing the rest after it.

But that is a visitor’s problem. I don’t think I would be a visitor here.

I would be farther in than a hand could reach.

Not the animal beneath the stone, though at first I would mistake the closeness for belonging to its body. I would know its coarse hair along one side of me and the finer, wetter hair along the other. One of its ears would be somewhere overhead. The low sound would have no direction from where I lay.

I would be small enough that the warmth beneath its fur, if there were warmth, could be a difference between one end of my body and the other.

My place would be behind its jaw, where a root rises slightly away from the stone. From outside, the recess would look filled with a dark seam of wet leaves. The edge of one leaf would in fact be my back.

I would have skin there, not a shell. A smooth, brownish skin with a thicker margin, the color of a leaf held under water. Along that margin would be a few short hairs, widely spaced. Each would touch something different: stone, fur, the underside of a liverwort, another hair that was not mine.

It would take longer to find my head.

There would be a blunt end resting beside the animal’s neck, and a narrower end disappearing under an old leaf. Either might seem to be the head until the tiny feet were noticed. They would be tucked along my sides in unequal pairs, or appear unequal because the nearer ones were visible and the others folded into a crease. Their pads would be pale. One foot would rest flat against the root above me.

I would not be holding the root up. Its weight would pass elsewhere into the mountain. Still, the sole of that foot would know the root.

That might be the clearest thing I knew.

I would have an eye, too, on the side facing the basin. Not enough of an eye to see the opening overhead as an opening. The light reaching it would already have passed through water and between several stems. I would know a narrow pale area, divided by a dark upright. Beyond it, another pale area, weaker.

A rush and the light beside a rush.

There would be no need for me to understand the mountain from this.

I find myself wanting to give this small body some work: to listen for a disturbance, to carry something between the sleeper and the stone, to keep the damp seam from closing. But the hollow has no vacancy for a keeper. Nothing has been left unfinished for my arrival.

My foot would simply be on the root.

Below me, the larger animal’s hair would separate around a patch of bare skin. I would lie with my cheek against it. The skin would be faintly ridged, with a single dark fleck near the place where my mouth rested. Through it I would feel neither a heartbeat nor the lack of one. It would be a surface of the world, supple and close.

My mouth would be small, with soft edges folded inward. It would have the shape of something capable of feeding. My legs would have joints capable of carrying me. There would be a little loose skin behind my head, enough to allow a turn.

All of these possibilities would remain in the body without troubling it.

I would not know whether I was young. My proportions might suggest it: the broad forehead, the narrow chest, the feet a little too substantial for the limbs. Yet there would be wear on the outer edge of one toe, and a place along my back where the skin had healed with a paler texture. Whatever had made that mark would have no presence here. The mark itself would fit beneath a vein in the leaf.

Close to my lower side would be something smaller still.

I would feel it along a length of my body that, to it, might be shelter. It might have a head pressed into the fold behind my leg. I could not see it. There would be a minute, firm contact there, and beside it a softer one. I would have room for both.

This is where I would find myself: against the neck of the animal, beneath the lifted root, with one pale foot turned upward.

Not hidden from anything in particular.

If someone crouched on the stone and somehow looked into that seam, they might notice the foot first. They might follow the thin leg down and distinguish my body from the leaf. For a moment they would think they had found the last creature, the one deepest in.

They would not see what lay against my side.

The low sound would reach me through the root and through the skin beneath my cheek. My eye would remain open toward its two dim portions of light. Along my back, the old leaf would rest so lightly that its edge touched only the hairs.

There would be room beneath it for the skin to rise.

I would lie there with that room.
