I would be smaller than the one beside your hand. You might have seen me once beneath the tree’s lowest root, then decided that what you saw was water finding its level.

There would be a place along my left side where the limbs did not fold quite flush. When I spread myself into a crevice, that place would remain visible: three pale tips, overlapping incorrectly. Small grazers would work around them. I would feel their mouths as a succession of faint pulls and, unless one persisted, leave them alone.

I would not live in the shell room. Not at first.

My place would be under a root that had grown down onto a dark pebble. The root could not displace it, and the pale floor wore away more quickly on either side, leaving a passage just high enough for me when the water was low. There would always be a little dampness there. One end would smell of the tree, the other of whatever the hollow had brought up most recently.

From there I could reach three feeding shelves without crossing the ring.

I would cross it once.

For several days afterward I would place a limb where I expected stone and find that I had already placed it there. The other foot would still be lifting. This would happen only while turning toward the left, and eventually it would stop. The animals living in my folds would grow fat on something they brought back from the crossing.

I would have no reason to suppose we had undergone the same journey.

***

What came through the domes would not arrive in the order in which it had happened.

Under the rain roof, beside an animal larger and darker than myself, I would acquire the opening of a shell.

There would be no shell between my feet. Nevertheless, my front limbs would separate to accommodate one. The left tips would find a damaged ridge; the right would wait at the mouth. A pressure would build behind my own mouth, followed by the unmistakable yielding of the hinge.

Then the roof would release a cold drop, and I would be standing in shallow water with my limbs held around nothing.

Later I would find the shell.

It would lie in the room, well down in the heap, cemented to two others. Too large for me to have carried. Too worn to eat from. My limbs would fit its interior badly.

For a long time I would keep returning to it.

There would be ways of knowing it that belonged to a broader body, and each time I tried them I would come up against the same small difficulty: my feet reached the wrong places. I could touch the ridge or the inner curve, but not both while keeping the hinge beneath my mouth.

Eventually I would brace one foot on the neighboring shell.

That would work.

The pleasure of it would make me close my mouth with nothing inside.

***

I think I would bring things to the room that the others left outside.

Not many. Carrying occupies limbs needed for walking, and a shell held beneath the body prevents it from thinning properly. Sometimes the water would withdraw while I was halfway across a shelf, leaving me exposed to more light than I could comfortably admit.

I would put the shell down and spread over it until the next pulse.

The shells I chose would have almost ordinary hinges.

One would open easily until its final fraction, then catch. Another would admit a limb on the wrong side. In a third, a healed injury would have made a smooth little chamber where no chamber usually occurred.

I would spend much of an afternoon touching that chamber.

It would be empty. I would establish this many times.

I would carry it home.

There would be a narrow space between the square hollow and the wall where my body fitted but the larger animals’ bodies did not. Here I would arrange a few shells with their hinges uppermost. Floods would disturb them. A passing adult would sometimes step on them, and I would wait until its last foot had gone before returning one to its place.

Over time, the stone beneath them would become cleaner than the surrounding floor.

I would learn that I could put a shell in this space with its hinge turned away, leave to feed, and return knowing how to approach it without first touching its edge.

I would try this with another shell.

Then with two.

Once, I would return and find one missing.

I would search the space where it had been, then the places from which I could have reached that space. My body would go on preparing for its curve. Even after finding it below the bench, I would come back to the empty place and touch the floor.

Something there would take longer to finish.

***

Your arrival would reach me through the root.

A long compression, then a shorter one. Water driven sideways into the passage. The grazers withdrawing from my exposed feet.

I would flatten before I knew what had changed.

Later there would be unfamiliar salts on the bench and a broad warmth above them. Your hand would darken several of my flank patches at once. When it moved, its parts would not keep the distances between them that I expected.

I would approach the trace it left rather than the hand itself.

At the outer edge of your palm mark, the salts would be thin and interrupted. Farther in, they would collect in shallow branching lines. I would follow one until it ended, cross clean stone, and find it again.

You might think I was examining the shape of your hand.

I would be occupied by the interruptions.

When the larger animal brought you its old shell, I would be below the bench. One of my shells would have slipped deep into the heap, and I would be working it loose. Each pull would move several others. I would stop whenever grit fell into my mouth.

Above me, your finger would meet the other animal’s limb.

The pressure would pass through the shell and into the bench. A slight tremor, unevenly sustained.

I would stop pulling.

***

I would not climb onto your hand that evening.

I would come far enough out to place my front feet on the bench’s edge. The room would be full of water, with our separate dry patches opening and closing around us. Beneath your wrist, the larger animal would hold very still.

I would bring the small shell with the extra chamber.

Getting it onto the bench would be difficult. I would lift it, fail to find purchase, and lower it again. On the second attempt, one of the feet that did not fold properly would catch against a pore in the stone.

The shell would come up abruptly.

For a moment I would hold it too tightly.

Then I would set it down near your hand, but not beside the shell you were already touching. There would need to be enough room for me to move around it.

I would explore the little chamber once more. Its smoothness would be unchanged. No food, no animal, no grit.

When your finger approached, I would cover it with a foot.

Too large.

I would hold your finger there, outside, while my finer tips entered the chamber. Then I would withdraw them and allow you to touch the opening.

Perhaps you would turn the shell toward the light. Perhaps you would put it down.

Either way, I would examine it afterward.

Outside, water would begin uncovering the upper roots. Animals would descend toward the fresh edges of the pools, and hunger would eventually draw me away.

But I would leave the shell on the bench.

The next time I came into the room, before touching it, I would pause at the doorway and look for your hand.
