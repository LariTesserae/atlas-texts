At first I would be down beside the storage tanks, in the place the roots have been cleared from.

There is room there now for something that was not in the original plan. Light from a lower window strikes the underside of the terrace, and the terrace sends it back softened, without the bright seams the glass blocks make. The old pump shaft has left a rust-colored impression on the porcelain. One of the animal’s folds passes over that impression occasionally, following a route that has not yet changed.

I think I would begin as something it has to move around.

Not another resident, exactly. Not a worker. Something delivered complete, with instructions that describe every use except the one for which I will eventually be needed.

I would be the spare frame with the clean serial plate.

My clamps have been loosened. This is the first thing I know.

Before that, there was pressure at four prescribed points, enough to keep me from shifting in the rack. Now there is pressure at three. When the circulation changes, I move a little against the remaining pads.

I have no view of the chamber. The windows, the mountains, the arrangement of the stairs would have to reach me through other people’s handling: a cold glove from outside, a lifting sling drawn tight, a vibration arriving through the rack when someone puts a foot on its lowest rung. Light warms one side of me in the afternoons. That is as much of the sky as I possess.

But I know an opening precisely.

It is what I was made to know.

My four sides are not quite rigid. Inside the brass are thin nesting elements, fitted so closely that their movement would not be visible even with the cover plates removed. They take up differences. A frame installed badly must still hold. A frame fitted around a worker’s arm must accommodate the worker reaching, tiring, deciding to withdraw.

There is a small permissible range in which I can change my shape without ceasing to be closed.

My instructions call this tolerance.

For a long time, nothing asks very much of it.

Then the animal comes and lifts my lower edge.

It does this without touching the release catches. Two broad portions of it slip between me and the rack; a narrower part braces against the wall. The lift lasts only a few seconds. My upper clamps carry the extra load, then I settle back into the padding.

Several days later it does the same thing.

It is learning my weight. I am learning the difference between being supported and being held.

A rack supports each corner separately. The animal redistributes itself whenever one of my joints begins to bear too much. If it tires, the change reaches me gradually. A cord thickens. A plate finds purchase. Another fold arrives, and the load travels into it.

There is no fixed arrangement I can wait for. Each time, it makes a slightly different cradle.

I begin to recognize it by these corrections.

***

The isolation frame is brought down for comparison.

I know this because someone presses it against me, edge to edge. It is lighter, with an unusually stiff inner lip. Its purpose is to close around an existing boundary and keep that boundary from changing.

Mine is ordinary service work. Doorways. Broken windows. The temporary removal of wall panels.

The workers turn us over, measure us, set us apart.

One of them taps my serial plate with a tool.

“This one would give it more room.”

Another says, “That isn’t the only question.”

They leave both frames nearby.

The animal touches the isolation frame that night. It explores the inner lip, passes a thin fold through the opening, and withdraws it. Then it comes to me and rests along my bottom rail.

I cannot know what it has decided. Contact gives me weight, tension, temperature. It does not give me intention, however long the contact lasts.

Still, by morning, the second of my clamps is loose.

***

The difficulty is not getting me out of the rack.

The difficulty is taking something closed through a place built so carefully not to close.

My corner catches beneath a root basket. A worker frees it, then holds the basket’s unfastened side away while I pass. Farther up, a railing has to be removed. The animal cannot lift me alone; the workers cannot find a route without the animal going ahead, drawing leaves together, lifting its own body from clearances everyone had believed were empty.

We spend most of an afternoon between two stairways.

A sling bears my upper side. The animal holds the lower one. Something hard presses through its tissue against my brass, shifts, presses again.

Above us, the circulation continues. Severed leaves arrive at the combs. The ordinary work is falling behind.

Once, the animal releases me almost entirely.

My weight goes into the sling so abruptly that a worker swears. We remain like that for several minutes, the animal scarcely touching me, every part of it that I can feel unnaturally still.

Then it takes up the load again.

Only much later will I associate these absences with the small body in the ring.

At the time, I learn simply that being carried by it includes waiting.

***

Near the vine, I encounter a strain for which I have no stored correction.

Water bears against me from an angle that is not an angle in the chamber. My upper rail registers a load my lower rail does not share. One joint shortens by a fraction. Another opens to the limit of its tolerance.

A worker reaches inside me and stops.

His sleeve pulls toward the ring. The loose end of his wrist strap rises sideways.

“Hold it there,” he says.

Everyone holds.

The animal lies against two of my sides. A narrow extension runs beyond me, toward the graft. Something very light touches my inner edge and is gone before I can distinguish its shape.

The worker withdraws his hand.

They leave me suspended beside the vine that night, not yet in use. A cable takes my weight. Temporary braces prevent me from swinging. The animal remains wound through them, occasionally relieving a pressure the braces cannot relieve themselves.

There are procedures for transferring a boundary from one frame to another. None were written for a boundary that has grown, or for water with a different upper surface, or for a parent that will not allow the original enclosure to be cut.

So the vine stays whole.

They pass it through me carefully, one leaf at a time.

***

I had imagined—if the small anticipations in my joints amount to imagining—that use would arrive as a single load.

An opening established. A pressure accepted. All four sides engaged.

Instead, I am needed first at one corner.

The water touches my inner lip there and hesitates. A narrow tongue of it stands through my opening, bends, and draws back. It leaves no wetness different from the water surrounding me.

The animal shifts the vine.

Again.

A larger portion enters. My opposite side takes a sudden inward pull. I correct too far; one brace knocks against the cable. The water contracts around the old ring.

The worker loosens the brace.

No one tries again for a while.

On the next attempt, the animal puts a fold through me before it moves the vine. The fold lies along my lower rail, thick enough to soften the abrupt change at the corner.

This time the water stays.

It takes the remainder of the day to reach my other sides.

Inside me, the bubble begins its slow journey toward a new high point.

***

I am much larger than the vine’s ring, but not large compared with the chamber.

There is room for the wedge now, and for a length of ribbon leaf brought in through the boundary. There is room for the small animal to move away from both.

The adult does not remove the vine. It remains inside me, supported at two places, its original circle intact. Sometimes the young one returns to it. Sometimes it rests where the adult’s tissue crosses my sill.

I had expected, perhaps, to feel more once I was occupied.

Mostly there are minute changes in load. A slight traction at the inner lip. Warmth collecting unevenly along one side. The young one is so nearly the density of its surroundings that it can cross the whole space without registering at all.

Then, one morning, it finds a bolt.

The bolt secures a replaceable wear plate on my inner edge. Its head is broad and smooth, with a shallow slot.

Two small cords pull against it.

They release.

Return.

The adult is holding my outer rail. I know its grip well by now: the spread of weight, the little adjustments before a joint can bind. As the small cords tighten again, that grip becomes still.

I hold the boundary. There is nothing else for me to do.

The young one gets one edge beneath the bolt head and begins, very slowly, to pull itself around the corner.
