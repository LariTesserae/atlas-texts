At first I would be one of the pauses along the pale ledge.

A carrier would arrive with a packet of brine held tightly inside itself. The street ahead would be too narrow. It would stop, and somewhere in the stippling above it an opening would deepen.

That would be my work.

I would come close enough to feel the shape of what the carrier could no longer carry. It would turn a particular chamber toward me. Between us, a boundary would become permeable, then disappear. For a moment I would have an impression of a sheltered step, heat rising through it, someone waiting beyond its edge.

Then I would draw back with the memory enclosed.

The carrier would continue. I would fit what it had left into the ledge and check that the warmth reached its farthest part.

There would be many such transactions. Most would leave me with very little understanding of what I had held. A memory passed between bodies need not become visible to both. I would know its requirements: which boundary could tolerate cooling, where salt must not accumulate, whether it could be folded twice or only once.

Some would need a long, narrow room.

Some would need two chambers with no direct passage between them.

One would need an opening kept unlit. Every time I supplied warmth, I would be careful not to brighten it.

I think I would like the work before I understood why.

***

My own grain would be a dull yellow, difficult to distinguish from a deposit left by old water. I would have a short body with an unnecessarily deep chamber near its middle. When empty, that chamber would make my movements awkward. At crossings, the front of me would arrive before the rest had quite agreed to follow.

The streets near the ledge would accommodate this. Away from them, I would have to proceed carefully.

I would own one object: a forked needle that had broken away from a nursery wall. Neither point would be useful. I would keep it in a recess slightly too large for it, and sometimes adjust the recess.

There would be someone who regularly waited for me at the end of a shift.

We would share an enclosure beneath the warm side of the ledge. They would bring impressions from the growing fissure: a new stair, a difficult change in the stone, the peculiar pressure of a wall just before an opening establishes itself. I would bring almost nothing from my work. What I had handled there belonged elsewhere.

At times they would ask, by opening a small chamber toward mine, what had happened to me.

I would show them a drain that had cooled too quickly. A difficult transfer. A tiny crust of salt removed from a hinge.

Eventually they would stop asking every time.

I would notice that.

***

One of the chambers under my care would have no returning owner.

It would occupy an inconvenient position where the ledge thinned. Water supplied to it would have to pass three other openings, and in a dry interval those three would take most of what arrived. I would learn to feed it from below.

Its contents would be modest. A little warmth. A depth that curved away from the entrance. At the far end, something like the pressure of another body close by.

No identifying mark would remain on its threshold.

I would test the old records, carrying my question to the copper-lit building. The answer would require two other attendants and more water than I liked to request. We would hold a room together while an ancient arrangement opened within it.

There would be a transfer.

A interruption in the supply.

Several chambers moved during repairs.

No certain connection between those events and my small, unclaimed room.

I would return to the ledge with the same question, now more expensive.

For a long time I would simply maintain it.

Then the courtyard would fail.

***

The first demand would be for water. The next, for space.

We would move the memories nearest the damaged district. Those that could tolerate sharing would be nested in larger chambers. Others would be carried by attendants until somewhere could be prepared for them.

I would take the unclaimed one.

Its curve would fit inside my deep middle chamber, though imperfectly. To keep its far end stable, I would have to shorten myself on one side. For the first time, the familiar street would hesitate before opening for me.

Below the transparent flake, the trapped grains would be faint enough that I would mistake two of them for reflections.

I would bring water. I would return for more.

On the fourth journey, someone at the amber boundary would turn toward me.

They would be stretched almost beyond coherence, holding the remembered proportions of a body beneath the ruin. Others supported them, but one of the enclosures they offered kept closing. Something in the arrangement was wrong. The trapped inhabitant could not enter it.

The one at the boundary would reach toward the chamber I carried.

I would withdraw.

That would happen first.

I would have spent so long preserving its boundary that I would preserve it even then.

They would wait. Beneath us, the grain would dim.

When I approached again, I would expose only the outer fold of the memory.

A sheltered curve. Warmth held along its underside. Someone close enough to alter the pressure without touching.

The waiting inhabitant would change.

Under the flake, something would answer.

***

I would not learn whose memory it was.

The damaged one might have left it at the ledge. Someone who knew them might have left it. It might concern another body altogether, arranged in a way that happened to offer a useful beginning.

There would be no time to settle that.

I would hold the curve while the other inhabitant made a chamber beside it. We would adjust the passage between them. The first attempt would be too steep. The second would open in the wrong order.

On the third, the grain below would acquire a smoky edge.

Water would reach us. I would feel it enter the borrowed room and move through a membrane that had been still for a very long time.

The memory would change.

Only a little. A threshold would extend beyond the place where it had always ended.

I would know how carefully I had kept that threshold unchanged. I would also feel the new body taking hold of it.

I would let it extend.

***

Afterward, I would continue working at the ledge.

There would still be carriers needing to leave things, chambers needing clean water, deposits growing where no deposit ought to grow. I would still have the forked needle. The one who waited for me would sometimes find me late.

But I would also be called to the shallow districts.

I would learn to hold an enclosure without completing it, leaving the far boundary for the arriving body to establish. This would be harder than maintaining a memory. My first arrangements would be too familiar. I would offer the proportions I knew, and have to loosen them when no one answered.

Sometimes someone would answer.

My grain would change slowly. A dark line would form through its yellow center, visible when I turned.

And there would be evenings beneath the ledge when the other inhabitant opened that small chamber toward me again.

This time I would have something to bring.

I would open the part of myself that had held the unclaimed memory. It would still curve away, but farther now. There would be a place where another boundary had met it from within.

We would remain together while the floor cooled.

Then, before the warmth failed, I would take them by the longer street. A recovering inhabitant had begun using it, and its edges were learning a new way to open.
