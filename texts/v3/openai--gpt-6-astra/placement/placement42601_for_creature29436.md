I find myself first in one of the sideways windows.

Not looking out. There is little to see, and the sill is at the wrong height for that. I am sitting across it with two feet against the opposite wall, holding a shallow dish level while someone inside scrapes the last of a meal into it. The passage is narrow enough that I have to withdraw a foot whenever anyone comes through.

I have been here longer than the errand requires.

The person inside has something else to say. Each time I begin to lower the dish, a hand comes out and takes mine again.

This is a familiar inconvenience. I have brought it on myself by being willing, on some earlier occasion, to listen.

The dish is chipped. One edge has been ground down until it no longer cuts, making a low place through which the contents will spill unless I carry it with that side raised. My fingers know this before I have given any thought to where I am going.

That is how I begin to acquire a body here: through the things I am already careful of.

One hind foot grips poorly on a glazed surface. I put that foot down first, while I still have three other points of support. There is a place beneath my shoulder plates that I cannot reach with a scraper. When it needs attention, I rub against a doorpost, although I know this packs the deposit more tightly into the seam.

My finer hands are sound. On one, the long finger folds a little outward. It catches against the rims of bowls. As a young one I was often told to tuck it away properly, as though I had been leaving it exposed out of carelessness.

The household at the window likes its food bitter.

I do too. This is another reason the errand takes so long.

***

My room is below a bridge approach, but not the main bridge. Its ceiling includes the underside of a stair. There are three places where I can stand upright and several where an unwary visitor cannot.

I keep the food on the highest shelf and the tools near the floor. Neither arrangement is quite convenient. To change them would require moving a wedge that has become, through years of postponement, part of the shelf.

There is space for another inhabitant. There are signs that someone once occupied it: a second resting hollow, a dish with a different pattern of wear, the smooth underside of a lintel where a hand used to reach. I do not yet know whether I remember that person or inherited their room.

For a while, either seems possible.

Then I find myself putting something down in the second hollow and moving it away again.

I remember them.

I need not decide immediately where they have gone.

***

The work I do is small even here.

I clean the places in which messages are left.

The grooves collect grit. Mineral begins at the edges of the cup marks and makes them shallower. A chip that once lay flat develops a rocking motion. Then someone comes home, feels the arrangement beside the door, and cannot be sure whether the message was qualified or merely badly placed.

Most households attend to their own. I am called when a groove has become difficult, or when the person who made it is no longer there to say how deep it was meant to be.

My tools fit in a shell fragment with two holes rubbed through it. A filament passes through the holes, and I carry the shell against my underside. There is a pointed flake, a blunt flake, a small scraper, and a tuft of hairs from an old casing. The hairs have been bound and rebound. One has escaped the binding and lies crosswise under the others, where I cannot remove it without taking the whole thing apart.

I keep meaning to.

The essential part of the work is knowing when to stop. That sounds easier than it is. A clean groove offers a pleasing, definite edge. An old groove ought not always to have one.

There is a doorway on the upper terrace where I once cut too far.

No great harm followed. The household changed its chip. But the person living there still tests my hand before allowing me to begin, pressing the tip of the long finger between two of theirs.

Carefully.

I answer that I remember.

They remind me anyway.

***

I am not a keeper of all the town’s meanings.

There are arrangements I cannot read. A few belong to individual households, and one old set of cup marks may never have been writing at all. It sits beside a blocked entrance, sheltered by a shell lintel. Six hollows, then an interval, then two.

A child asks me about them while I am brushing out the mineral dust.

I give the touch for uncertainty.

The child waits.

I give it again, more fully.

The child takes my hand and proposes that the hollows are for putting six things here and two things there.

We try this.

It is a satisfactory arrangement. We have no reason to think it is the original one.

When we leave, I remove the grains we have used. The child objects. We have a short argument in which neither of us can quite express why it matters.

Afterward, I find one grain tucked into the fastening of my tool shell.

I carry it home.

***

The upper room is part of my route, though there is seldom work for me there.

I take food up when the household that usually does it has another task. The ladder is easier without a loaded dish. On the final stretch I must carry the dish in my mouth, which leaves a taste of old shell that persists after I have eaten.

At first I set the food inside and go.

On another visit I wait with my hand against the entrance.

Nothing touches it.

The water sounds different up there. The sharp reports from below are muffled by intervening roofs, but a fine shivering comes through the floor. I find that I have been pressing my hind feet hard against the stone.

I leave before the dish is taken.

On a later visit, a hand finds mine.

The fingers are thickened. One is capped. They do not ask who I am in quite the form I expect, and I answer too quickly. We separate.

Then we begin again.

It takes some time to establish which way I came.

The old inhabitant knows the route, but not the bridge I crossed. There used to be a descent along the wall. I know the remains of it: two projections beneath a sill, useless to anyone carrying a load.

The hand turns mine toward that absent stair.

I turn it back toward the bridge.

We cannot lay both routes into the same telling without starting from farther away.

By the time I leave, the meal has dried slightly at its edges.

After that, I sometimes go without bringing food. I am not always admitted. Once, after a long climb, I find the pale chip set firmly in its groove.

I go home irritated, and remain irritated until I have eaten.

***

I would be old enough to have habits mistaken for competence, young enough to dislike being corrected.

I would have acquaintances on every terrace and perhaps two people to whom I could offer my damaged, inaccessible back without first finding something else to discuss.

I would know where the good grit is stored. I would not know what makes the footsteps.

At their passing I would flatten myself against whatever surface held me. Afterward I would resume work, sometimes at the wrong place, and have to find my way back with a finger.

The town would be most of what I knew. Parts of it would still be unfamiliar.

***

When the young one is brought beneath the little roof, I am coming down with my tools and an empty dish.

The inhabitant keeping the causeway touches my wrist.

Wait.

I settle beside the wall. The dish goes between my feet. Across the water, one of the adults is trying to fit beneath the shelter without bringing a wet shoulder against its edge. There is a brief adjustment, a withdrawing and returning of hands.

I cannot see the young one.

I know what is happening from the way the adults hold themselves, and from having been present before. The roof receives its blow. Water bulges over the lip, parts, and falls.

There is nothing for me to do yet.

I feel along the dish’s worn edge. A little food has dried in the chip, missed by the household that emptied it. I loosen it with a nail and eat it.

Then the hand at my wrist asks for the dish.

I pass it over. Someone needs a place to put the folded carrying filament. The dish goes beneath the edge of the roof and stays there.

So I am included, inconveniently. I cannot leave without abandoning something I must return.

I shift my poor foot onto a rougher patch. The waiting inhabitant rests a hand near mine. After a while I touch it and ask whether this is the first young one from that household.

The answer is no.

A pause, then a small correction.

The first to be put down.

I keep my hand still. The other hand remains against it.

Under the roof, beyond my view, the limbs are opening. We wait through many impacts. At last an adult moves enough for me to see a pale foot planted on the dry paving, its fingers spread farther apart than an older inhabitant’s would comfortably go.

My dish comes back with the filament in it.

I am asked to take it to the house and leave it just inside the door.

By the time I reach the upper causeway, I have worked out how to carry it without letting the loose end trail in the water.
