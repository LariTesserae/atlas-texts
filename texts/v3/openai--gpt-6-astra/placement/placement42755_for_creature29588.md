At first I would look for room beneath the willows. There is shade there that does not have to be built, and ground with something in it besides the marks of work.

But the roots need protecting, and the old man brings his grandchild there. What looks like an unoccupied place from the bank is not unoccupied when you reach it.

I think I would end up farther south, in a roofed room beside a passage that everyone expects to widen. The owner would let it cheaply on the understanding that, when the passage was needed, the outer wall would come down.

I would ask when.

He would show me where the wider passage was going to lead, which would not answer the question. Still, I would take the room.

My belongings would fit along its back wall.

***

I would not arrive knowing how to build on a lakebed.

For a while I would carry gravel. I would discover that I preferred the upward journey with an empty basket to the downward one with a load, though the upward journey took more breath. Going down, I would keep imagining my feet running ahead of me.

The work I eventually kept would begin with someone asking me to clear a gutter while they fetched another tool.

There would be a blockage beneath a crossing: reed ends, a little clay, a strip of cloth worked so thoroughly into the rest that pulling it would tighten the whole thing. I would try to remove it without lifting the log. This would take longer than lifting the log.

Someone would tell me so.

By then I would have freed it, and would be annoyed rather than grateful.

I would remember the advice at the next crossing.

After a few weeks, people would begin leaving a narrow scraper by my door. Then there would be a second scraper, not as good, and an argument about which one had been borrowed from whom. I would notch the handle of my own.

That is where I find myself more clearly: not at the great gate, where the water has become one body and its movements can be watched from a shelter, but upstream of it, crouching where a lane turns between two walls.

I have a basket for the spoil. Merely lifting it onto the walkway means much of it will wash back in.

There is usually more than I expect.

***

My work is small enough that people feel able to explain it to me while I am doing it.

They are sometimes right.

A woman tells me that the channel outside her house used to clear before the one opposite. I scrape the bottom, looking for something that has lodged there. Nothing has. She tells me again, more sharply, that it used to clear first.

We bring water in a bowl and pour it slowly.

The water hesitates where there is no visible obstruction.

I do not know whether the walkway has sunk or the edge of her plinth has spread. We get a straight board. The board rocks. We get another board, because the first may be bent.

By then three people have stopped to watch, and one wants to cut deeper immediately.

I have already done that once, elsewhere. The deeper cut filled with water that did not leave, and I spent the rest of the afternoon carrying clay back to it.

This time I say we should fetch the man who laid the plinth.

Someone goes. The woman gives me a little cooked grain while we wait. I eat with the fingers I have managed to keep clean.

I would like to be the sort of person who welcomes every useful correction. In this place I would get considerable practice before becoming one.

***

There are things I would come to know well.

Which crossing can be lifted by one person, and which looks lighter than it is. Where a roof drips after the rain has stopped. How far a basket carrier will step aside without looking down. The difference between the smell of a newly opened reed bundle and reeds pulled from beneath a walkway.

I would also know where I could leave my tools without their being used.

This would be a shorter list.

Sela would know routes I did not. I would know some that she could not use while carrying a full meal bowl. We would exchange this information without making an arrangement of it.

Once she would tell me a bridge had begun to rock, and I would say I had fixed it.

“It rocks now,” she would say.

I would go with her.

I imagine this happening often enough that I stop using yesterday’s work as evidence of today’s condition. Not completely. On a tired afternoon I would still say, “I was just there.”

***

Payment would be awkward.

The passages belong to everyone until something must be carried away from them. Then the wet basket at my feet belongs, apparently, to the household nearest it.

I would learn to settle some of this before beginning. A measure of grain for clearing the length of a wall. Help carrying the spoil from a shared crossing. No payment for pulling out the small obstruction I happened to see on my way home.

I would not always draw that boundary fairly. I would be quicker to stop for someone whose voice I liked.

The widow would notice.

One morning she would ask why the drain by the storehouse still stood full when I had cleared hers twice. I would explain that the young men kept sweeping rubbish into it. She would agree that they were idle and careless, then ask the same question again.

I would dislike her for most of that morning.

At midday I would go to the storehouse and make one of the young men carry the basket.

He would work hard once he had been persuaded to begin. We might get on quite well. This, too, would be inconvenient.

***

I would have a life that did not consist entirely of noticing water.

There would be a particular voice I listened for while working, and days when I took too long over a stretch of gutter because I hoped its owner would come outside. I would clean my nails with a splinter before going to borrow a cup I did not need.

I would like the browned grain from the bottom of the cooking pot. Since the woman who cooked it also liked it, this would remain an occasional pleasure rather than part of my meals.

In my room I would keep a smooth board on two blocks, high enough to protect a folded blanket from the floor. When I first arrived, I would place things on it carefully, conscious that the room was temporary. Later there would be a cup ring, a burn, and a small accumulation of objects I meant to take elsewhere.

I would acquire a lid without a jar.

Then a jar it almost fitted.

The narrow room would become difficult to pack.

***

Eventually the owner would come to discuss the passage.

Not to take the wall down yet. Someone farther along had built a store against the proposed route. There was disagreement about whether the lane should bend, and who would surrender the space at the bend.

He would stand in my doorway, looking past me at the room.

I would realize that I had hung my spare shirt from a peg in the wall that was meant to come down. There would be other pegs now, too. A shallow shelf.

Neither of us would mention them.

After he left, I would put a bowl of water outside and wash my feet, separating my toes with my fingers to get the clay out. From there I could see only a narrow piece of the upper bank. People sometimes appeared against the sky, paused, and descended out of sight.

I would still know which path led from the rim toward the place I had come from. I might still go there for a visit, carrying something requested: a basket repaired at the gate, perhaps, or a few of the brothers’ bricks for someone to examine.

But when asked how long I could stay, I would count the days before answering.

There would be a crossing to reset. Someone would be keeping my better scraper. The jar under my shelf would have grain in it.

I think that is who I would be here: a person with wet cuffs, a disputed little livelihood, and a room in the way of a street.

Before sleeping, I would bring the washing bowl inside. There is barely room to pass it in the dark, but I would have learned where to put my feet.
