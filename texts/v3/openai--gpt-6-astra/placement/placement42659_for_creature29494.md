I sit down too far from them at first.

The space they have made is narrow, but I leave a smaller space inside it, keeping my elbow clear of their sleeve. They look at the gap. Then they pull me closer by the felt over my shoulder.

There is no advantage to letting the stone have both sides of a person.

Facing the rear wall, I can hear the trouble in their throat. It is a small sound, a swallowing that has not quite finished. I keep my hands in my lap. My dry hand feels conspicuous.

After a while they take it and put it against their side, below the ribs.

I think they want pressure. I press.

Their fingers tighten on my wrist until I ease off. Then they leave my hand where it is.

We sit that way until I stop waiting for something to happen.

***

I would stay near the southern shelters, though for a long time I would continue to think of myself as someone about to cross the western saddle.

I would keep the things I had brought arranged for departure. Whatever could be folded would remain folded. I would hesitate before using the last of anything.

Then someone would need a piece of cord.

There would be no reason not to give it. The cord would close a garment at the throat, and afterward I would see it moving about the basin without me. Sometimes I would recognize the knot before I recognized the wearer.

Other things would follow. A strip from the lining of my coat. A fastening that could be worked with numb fingers. Nothing taken ceremoniously, nothing received as though it settled what I was doing there.

One day I would need the cord myself and have to ask for something else.

By then I would know how to ask.

***

The work I learn first is holding.

A person sits beside me with a garment turned inside out across our knees. They place my hands along a seam and arrange my fingers. The thread must be pulled through while the material is warm, but the whole garment cannot fit beneath one person’s covering. So we sit close, with the part being worked between us.

I pull the felt taut.

They loosen it.

I let it sag.

They put two fingers under mine and raise them a little.

The repair is near a knee opening. Its binding has become too tight. A narrow crescent of material is being inserted, hardly enough, I think, to matter. But when the wearer bends their leg afterward, the red mark above the kneecap no longer deepens.

I begin to recognize work that small.

A folded edge abrades a wrist. One hanging strip draws moisture up from the hem. A seam that lies flat while someone stands becomes a hard ridge under the hip when they sleep.

I have no particular gift for sewing. My stitches are too large, and I use more thread than I should. For some time, people undo what I have done without asking me to watch. Later, when I have improved, they undo it in front of me.

But I can hold the material warm. I can keep the two edges together without deciding where the needle ought to go.

People begin to bring things to the shelter where I am sitting.

***

I would not have a shelter of my own.

There is one I prefer, where a shallow irregularity in the rear wall receives my shoulder. When someone else occupies that place, I am irritated before I remember that they have a shoulder too.

Sometimes I wait outside longer than necessary.

The person with the black pebble notices this. They move over and pat a place beside them, not the place I wanted. I sit. Their bowl is under their clothes, and its rim presses into my thigh.

Neither of us adjusts for a while.

I would belong badly on some days.

I would misunderstand a request and be ashamed enough to avoid the person who made it. I would look at the food in another person’s hand before looking at their face. There would be mornings—if I still called them mornings—when the thought of another interval of noise would make me unkind.

Once, while holding a seam, I would pull too sharply and break the thread.

The person sewing would remain still.

I would reach for the broken end, trying to make my hands look useful again. They would cover it with their thumb.

For a while, they would not give me anything to hold.

***

The child finds out that I am willing to look at things that have already been looked at.

They bring me stones, though not to keep. I have to turn each one correctly before it is taken back. Usually there is something underneath: lichen, a damp hollow, a pale line no thicker than a hair. Sometimes there is nothing I can find.

The child makes me look again.

I learn to avoid agreeing too quickly. False recognition annoys them more than ignorance.

They also bring the rod from the stream.

I have seen what happened to their feet, so I take hold of the rod when they turn toward the water. They pull. I keep my grip. Their face closes in a way that makes them look briefly like someone much older.

I point to the bank.

We kneel there. The rod is barely long enough.

I hold it near the end while the child guides it closer to the water. Neither of us can reach the leaf. We try from another place, then another. The bank stones press through my clothes, and one of my hands becomes wet.

Finally the child lets go and sits back on their heels.

For a moment I am relieved.

Then they take the rod and press its tip into the white deposit along the bank. It leaves a mark.

They make another.

I stay until they have finished, although I do not know what finishing will look like.

***

I would come to be recognized by a thickened place on the side of one finger, where I hold a needle wrongly and cannot quite learn to hold it otherwise.

Not a keeper of anything. There would be too much I could not keep.

I would be someone who could be interrupted.

Someone seated near the rear of a shelter, with part of another person’s clothing warm beneath my arm. I might have a mouthful of food I had forgotten to work, and have to stop sewing to attend to it. I might be holding a bowl against my belly for the person asleep beside me.

When a hand searched backward at a listening place, sometimes mine would be there. Sometimes I would be too far away to notice. Someone else would have to come.

I would learn who disliked a palm between the shoulders and preferred it lower. Who needed help rising but resented being lifted before they were ready. Who would sit beside me without speaking after I had made a mistake.

The person from the doorway would still have the split at the base of their thumb.

I would cease looking at it every time we met. Then, occasionally, I would notice it with the force of something new.

***

There is a repair I imagine working on for a long time.

It is one of the child’s shoes, opened again at the back. A larger sole has been found, but the upper does not meet it evenly. We have warmed and stretched the felt. Now one side is longer than the other.

The child is asleep with their bare feet under someone’s coat. We cannot keep waking them to try it on.

The person sewing puts the needle down against their lower lip. They study the shoe. I am holding the awkward join between my thumb and forefinger.

Beyond the entrance, a pale strip of animal coat passes out of sight. I feel the noise in the hollow of my chest even with my face turned away.

The sewer takes back the needle and indicates where I should shift my grip.

I move my thumb.

There is a little more room than we thought.
