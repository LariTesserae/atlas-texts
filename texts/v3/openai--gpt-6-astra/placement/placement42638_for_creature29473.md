I would first know the country by a weight across my shoulders.

Something overhead would be drinking. With each swallow it would grow heavier, and the place where I lay would become a little flatter. I would have one cheek against warm clay, one cheek in an opening through which cooler air occasionally passed. There would be a taste of old fiber in my mouth.

For a while I would take the weight for part of my body.

Then a foot would cross it.

The pressure would travel from my shoulder to my hip and disappear. A few grains would fall beside my eye. Above me, someone would pause to adjust a burden, and I would discover that I was under the fourth seed-husk step.

I would have been asleep there.

My back would fit into a hollow in its underside. One of my feet would be hooked around a rush, the other planted against the wall. Getting up would require remembering which pressure belonged to which limb. There would be more limbs than I could immediately account for. Something small beneath my ribs would still be holding the food I had fallen asleep eating.

I would bring it forward with my working hand.

A piece of root, sucked flat.

I would put it back.

***

I think I would be one of the inhabitants whose middle hands had only lately become difficult to reach.

They would still try to do things outside my body. If a peg slipped past my chest, both would dart after it beneath the folds, pulling the skin painfully tight. In sleep they would open and close. Sometimes I would wake with a splinter pressed against my breastbone, unable to remember storing it there.

My cheek stores would be unequal. On the left I could carry cord. On the right, an old puncture would have healed into a small, hard pocket that admitted only short things.

I would keep putting long things into it anyway.

There would be no special mark identifying me. A darker patch over one knee. A split in the gripping edge of one sole. Someone who worked beside me often would recognize the way I tested a surface twice with the same foot.

From the entrance, you might never notice me at all.

***

Beneath the fourth step, a fastening would have become inaccessible.

It would have been easy to reach when it was made. Then the stair had grown tall, the neighboring shelf had folded under itself, and a root had thickened across the approach. The knot would now lie at the end of a crooked passage, beyond a warm drop that struck the same place on my neck however I turned.

I could get there because my shoulders were narrow.

That would be the beginning of my work, rather than any aptitude.

Someone would put a short bone point into my hand. Someone else would hold my ankle while I went headfirst between the root and the clay. At the far end I would find three cords tightened into a single dark lump.

One of them would be dead.

I would have to find which.

There would be ways to tell. Living tension moved, even when everything seemed still. A distant worker shifted its weight; a leaf emptied; the basin gave a slow push. Two cords would speak against the bone point. The third would remain hard and mute.

I would worry it loose.

It would take so long that the hand around my ankle would change twice.

When the dead cord finally came free, the knot would release a smell of enclosed water. Behind it would be a space large enough for my fingers.

I would leave my fingers there for a moment.

Nothing would require them.

***

Afterward, they would send me into other places.

I would learn to carry very little. A tool that fitted through a passage beside my head might refuse to come back beside my hip. Cord could swell. A seed could open in a cheek pocket if I forgot it long enough.

Once, I would enter a gap carrying a curved splinter and emerge without it after a considerable struggle.

Much later, from another shelf, I would see pale roots hanging out of the gap. A worker would be using them to steady itself.

I would remember the splinter as something I had lost.

The worker would know it as a handhold.

There would be no occasion to reconcile these accounts.

***

I would get something wrong.

A cord would seem slack beneath my finger. I would draw it toward me, hoping to expose its knot, and feel a sudden answering movement too large to belong to the fastening.

The wall against my back would tilt.

From beyond it would come a soft scattering sound.

I would stop pulling, but the cord would continue to move through my hand.

By the time I got out, a fern bed would have tipped into the passage below. Its roots would still hold it together, a wet slab with leaves crushed underneath. Two others would already be lifting it. I would put my shoulder under the low edge.

We would stay that way while someone passed a new binding around the bed.

Mud would enter the split in my sole. The weight would make my middle hands clench so tightly that afterward I would have to ease them open, one finger at a time.

Nobody would send me back into the passage that day.

The next time, the old inhabitant near the entrance would give me a piece of loose cord. It would close my fingers over it, tug the other end, then let the cord move without tugging.

Again.

A pull. A passage of fiber.

I would learn the difference slowly.

***

There would be somewhere I went when no one needed my shoulders to fit.

Behind the red ledge, a clay flake would stand away from the wall by the thickness of my body. Its outer face would be bare. On its inner face a green film would grow in branching, delicate tracks.

The white animal would sometimes be there.

I would know it by the touch of its feelers against my heel. I would move my foot and it would proceed, carefully lifting itself across the shallow print I had left in the mud.

For several visits, I would watch it eat around a single brown spot.

Eventually the spot would fall out.

A thin light would enter through the hole.

I would put my eye against it and see a portion of the country I had never reached: the underside of a leaf, a hanging loop, water trembling on a gray-pink wrist. The wrist would move away. For a little while there would be only the loop.

I would return to look again.

Sometimes I would sleep there, with the hole beside my face. Whoever passed on the other side might see an eye in the clay and assume there was work underway.

***

In time, I would grow too broad for the passage beneath the fourth step.

I would discover this while trying to leave it.

There would be an unpleasant interval with my chest caught behind the root and my feet finding nothing useful. I would empty both cheeks. I would turn my head until the healed pocket hurt.

Someone would reach in and take my hand.

For several days afterward, I would approach the opening before remembering.

Then I would begin showing a smaller inhabitant how to go in. It would carry the bone point awkwardly, presenting the sharp end to every surface it passed. I would take it away, turn it, return it.

When its feet disappeared, I would catch one ankle.

Inside, there would be scraping, then a long silence.

I would feel the impulse to tug.

Instead I would lower my chest against the ledge and make my grip easier. Through the clay, the mallet blows would arrive in my ribs. Beneath my arm, one of the little hands would begin patiently combing a frayed end of cord.

The ankle would shift.

I would let it.

Far inside the fastening, the younger one would be making room to turn.
