I would be the one who comes back with the wrong coil.

Not a useless coil. It is well made, close-braided, with a dark outer fiber that resists chewing. I chose it because the rim is moving and there may be weight to hold. But the woman wanted a line that would carry a vibration without giving it a character of its own. Mine has three splices. Each would return a small, persuasive falsehood.

She runs it between her fingers, finds the first splice, and looks at me.

I have already begun explaining.

She puts the coil back in my hands.

There is still time to fetch the other one. This is worse, in some ways, than there being no time.

***

I live two crowns away, in a shelter whose lowest anchor has to be lengthened every few days. The crown beneath it is growing sideways. From my platform I can reach a neighbor’s wall, though our entrances are now separated by a journey that requires food.

We pass things through a pair of little shutters. Neither household admits how much this arrangement pleases us. We complain about the obstruction.

My work is mostly fastenings.

I make basket catches that can be opened with the back of a hand, adjustable foot loops, handles for people whose fingers no longer close evenly. I fit the wedges that hold listening sticks against worn mouthplates. For this last work, people must let me put my fingers into their mouths. They are seldom gracious about it.

There is an old browser tooth fixed above my work surface. I use its polished side to check the fit of small pieces. A red seed has been embedded in its glaze for as long as anyone remembers. It makes everything reflected there appear to have a small injury.

I could exchange the tooth for a better one. I have declined several offers.

People bring me objects and, often, an account of the person for whom the object is intended.

*She won’t use a loop if it looks like something for an old person.*

*He pulls too hard when he’s frightened.*

*Make it so my brother thinks he repaired it himself.*

These are useful specifications. I listen closely to them. Then, sometimes, I neglect the width of a hand.

The woman who reads openings first employed me to repair the catch of her cabinet. I made a very good catch. To release it, one pressed a small plate inward while sliding the cover sideways. Nothing could nose it open; no stray cord could snag it.

She tried it once and handed it back.

With both hands occupied, she could not show a guest which piece to lift.

I disliked her for the length of time it took me to make another.

***

I am not her apprentice, although people have begun calling me that.

She brings me on some journeys because I notice what happens to equipment. A cord develops a flat place before it breaks. A sleeping platform goes out of level in a different way when its anchor is being pulled inward rather than its support pushed up. Sometimes these distinctions tell her something about the growth.

I come because I want to know how she knows where to put her feet.

This is not the reason I give her.

I tell her I want to improve the fittings.

She considers that a sufficient reason, which leaves me without an excuse to discuss the other one.

Once, she made me carry an empty basket all day. At each place where we stopped, she had me set it down and then retrieve it after we had finished listening.

By the fourth stop, I had worked out what she meant to demonstrate. A novice watches the large changes and misses the small graspings. A basket shows how quickly apparently still ground can begin to hold something.

At the fifth stop I explained this to her.

She signed that the basket belonged to someone along our route and she had forgotten which house.

I still think it was a good lesson.

***

When I return to the depression with the correct line, the old man has moved closer to the rim.

His sitting pad remains behind him. Nobody has retrieved it. This tells me more than the expressions of the watchers.

The woman takes one end of the line and gives me the other. It is a cured length of stem, narrow and jointless, stored in a broad coil so that no bend becomes permanent. Held slack, it is merely awkward. Under a little tension, it carries the trembling of her fingers.

She guides it down onto the exposed tissue.

I feel the surface rasp first. Then a deeper motion comes through strongly enough that I loosen my grip.

She looks up.

I tighten my hand again.

The motion is not a knock. It gathers against my fingers, stays, and diminishes. Before it has quite ended, another gathering begins.

I want to put words around it. I have several ready. A chamber filling. A bundle slipping through a constriction. Something with feet, moving very far below.

Instead I touch her wrist and repeat the timing against her skin.

She shifts the line.

We do it again.

The old man watches my hand rather than the depression. His mouth is drawn tightly around the wedge I fitted for him. I remember being pleased with that work. Now I wonder whether I checked the contact on both sides.

I would like to ask him to bite down once without the stick.

It is not the moment.

***

My outer hand patches are unusually smooth. As a child I was told this meant I would do delicate work. It probably means that delicate work has kept them smooth.

There is an old split in the pleating below my left shoulder. It closes badly in extreme heat. When I am tired, I arrange myself so that nobody can see me easing it apart from the adjoining patch.

I am vain about my tools and careless with the fiber around my bed. I clean other people’s forearm channels more thoroughly than my own. I enjoy being asked for help, but dislike being interrupted, and people who live near me have had to learn which expression to disregard.

At the depression, I am afraid of losing a hand.

The thought arrives with such force that the deeper vibration briefly becomes impossible to distinguish. I imagine the work surface in my house, its close-set hooks, the catches waiting to be finished. I imagine having to ask someone to alter them.

Then the woman touches the underside of my wrist.

Not reassurance. An instruction to lower the line.

I lower it.

***

The old man’s *once* has spread around the rim.

Nobody knows what belongs after it. The children have stopped trading. A person I recognize from the bridge family is taking baskets away from the edge without asking their owners.

The woman asks the old man again. This time she turns so I can see.

*What followed?*

His answer is longer now.

He was small. He remembers being carried. A house was at the wrong height: its entrance above the heads of the adults, the bottom of its hanging ladder curled against the wall. Someone was trying to reach a fastening.

The woman asks whether the ground opened.

He presses his fingertips together, separates them, stops.

He cannot say.

I am irritated with him. For a moment I had wanted his age to function like one of my tools: placed correctly, it would give us something definite.

He rubs his mouth. The wedge is hurting him.

I hand my end of the listening line to the woman and fetch my small roll of fitting tools.

***

The adjustment is slight. A shaving from one face, a scrap of resilient fiber beneath the other. He tests the stick against an old supporting stem well back from the edge.

His shoulders lower.

He takes my hand.

*There were baskets underneath,* he tells me.

I wait.

*The house. Baskets underneath it. They were putting things into them from above.*

He remembers being annoyed that his own basket had been taken. It held something he had collected. He cannot remember what.

This time I do not ask the memory to be more useful.

I tell the woman exactly what he told me.

She looks toward the shelters on the older crown. Their anchor lines descend behind the leaves. From here, none of their lower fittings can be seen.

She gives me the listening line to put away.

Then she sends me to inspect the anchors.

***

So I would not remain at the rim for whatever happens next.

I would go reluctantly, looking back until a rising fold of vegetation hid the others. The ground would have begun to tighten. Small grazers would be slipping out from beneath the scales, leaving pale, clean shapes where their bodies had been.

At the first shelter I would find nothing wrong.

At the second, a fastening would have drawn sideways into its socket. It could still be released, but not under load. Someone would need to lift that corner of the house.

The inhabitant would object. She would have stored food, a sleeping child, a relative who had only just become comfortable. She would want to know whether this was necessary or merely something the opening-reader was investigating.

I would begin with too much explanation.

Then the fastening would move while we were watching it.

We would put our baskets clear. She would wake her relative. I would get beneath the shelter with a broad wedge and press my shoulder against the warm underside of the frame, carefully, on the armored part.

Above me, hands would search for mine through the floor opening.

I would guide them to the catch.
