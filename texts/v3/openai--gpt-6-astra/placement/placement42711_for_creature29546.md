At first, I would be given something to hold.

A lamp, probably, while someone worked a hand into a joint beneath a stair. I would hold it too close. The worker would turn the hood away with two dirty fingers, without looking at me, and I would discover that what seemed like helpful light was a bright slit directly across their eyes.

After that I would watch where the light landed.

There would be other work. Carrying empty baskets back against the flow of full ones. Keeping a cloth damp over clay that could not be used until the next cycle. Standing beside a lifted drain cover so nobody stepped into the opening. I would do that last task too conscientiously, warning people who had already seen me and interrupting conversations to announce a hole everyone in the passage knew about.

“Stand there,” someone would tell me. “That is the warning.”

I would learn to stand there.

For a while, my place would be wherever another person needed both hands.

***

I think I would be small enough to enter the older work, but not especially good at moving through it.

There would be a scrape across my back that healed whenever I worked above and opened whenever I went below. I would own a close-fitting cap made from several pieces of cloth, one of them much softer than the others. When waiting, I would turn that piece toward my forehead.

I would not be a floor-listener. Lying with my cheek against a tile, I would hear a great many things and be unable to say which mattered. Someone knocking a basket clean three rooms away might alarm me more than a seam slowly giving under load.

Nor would I have Vey’s eye for a slope. I would need the half-cup of water.

But I might be good at remembering exactly what someone had said.

Not everything. An instruction. A number. The difference between *after the brace is seated* and *after the brace arrives*.

That distinction could get me kept at an opening after the baskets had gone.

***

There are places where a voice will travel farther than a person can comfortably follow.

A narrow shaft carries speech well until it branches. An old pipe makes every word loud except the word that distinguishes one instruction from another. Sometimes people working below can hear a conversation at a cooking shelf but cannot make themselves heard by the crew directly overhead.

I would learn these places locally. A few passages, a few shafts. Enough to be useful, not enough to be sent anywhere without asking questions.

My work would be to sit at a bend and pass words around it.

“Leave the third support.”

“Which end are you counting from?”

A pause.

“The broken tread.”

I would repeat that too.

I would have a pottery cup with no bottom, fitted against an opening in the wall. It would not be mine. A wedge of folded cloth would keep it from rattling out of place. With my mouth against its broad end, I could speak down to the chamber beneath the new basin; with my ear there, I could hear the reply.

Most of what passed through would be ordinary.

More packing clay.

A narrower board.

Who has taken the short scraper?

Once, a request for somebody above to move their foot.

People would call me the mouth at the bend. Later, just the mouth, which I would dislike when used away from work. A person trying to get my attention across an eating room would eventually learn my name.

***

My first serious mistake would be answering too soon.

Someone below would ask whether the load had been taken off a brace. I would have heard the upper crew agree to do it. I would have heard them move. I would say yes.

Nothing would collapse. The person below would put a hand to the brace, feel what I had failed to ask, and wait.

They would come up slowly.

“Did you see it lifted?”

“No.”

“Did someone tell you?”

“No.”

For several cycles afterward, they would ask who had given every answer I carried. I would resent this before I understood that they were still willing to work with me.

I would begin keeping a little damp slab beside my knee. Not a full account; there would seldom be time. Just marks for things asked and not yet answered. A request would remain there until someone did more than agree that it ought to be done.

At home I would sometimes find the slab still in my pocket, its marks crushed against the cloth.

***

Vey would know me first as someone who delayed a pour.

She would be crouching at the unfinished inlet, jar ready, while I leaned over the speaking cup.

“Are they clear?”

No answer.

She would settle back on her heels.

I would call again. A little rubble would fall somewhere inside the wall. Then a voice would arrive, irritated and very close: not yet; a basket had caught under the temporary floor.

We would wait.

Vey would put the stopper back in her jar.

Later, when the answer came, I would repeat it without improving it.

“Both out. Basket left below.”

“Will it obstruct?”

“I haven’t asked.”

She would look at me.

I would ask.

By the time we finished, she might be annoyed with everyone. Still, on the next working cycle, she would set her lamp where it lit my marks without shining into my face.

***

I would have a room shared with someone who slept while I worked and worked while I slept. We would know each other chiefly through things left in the wrong place.

Their bowl on my bedding.

My cap over the warm niche where they wanted to soften a binding.

A scraper laid across the threshold as a reminder that the door no longer cleared it.

For a long time I would think of this person as inconsiderate. Then I would return early once and find them moving my bedding away from a new wet patch in the wall.

We would have an awkward conversation while each held one end of it.

Afterward, we would remain untidy in ways that offended each other.

I would eat too quickly. I would enjoy songs but have a poor voice, made worse by a day of speaking into pottery. There would be evenings when I wanted no account of anyone’s work, not even from people I liked.

Somebody would tell me one anyway.

***

The difficult part of my occupation would not be speaking clearly.

It would be staying at the bend when nothing needed saying.

Below, a worker might spend an hour easing a piece of fill away from a buried lintel. Above, the crew would sit on their baskets or go to collect materials. My cup would give me breathing, scraping, an occasional muttered complaint. Nothing requiring an answer.

I would be tempted to help elsewhere.

Sometimes I would ask for relief and get it. Sometimes I would have to stay, shifting the numbness from one leg to the other, with the lamp turned low.

Vey’s brother might pass and see me there.

“Still talking?”

“Still listening.”

He would lean against the wall for a little while. He would not ask whether anybody was trapped.

On some days we would talk. On others, he would leave a piece of food on the clean side of my cloth and continue down the passage.

***

There would be someone learning the work from me eventually.

They would speak too loudly into the cup. They would repeat a joke from below as though it were an instruction. I would correct them with more impatience than either error deserved.

Then, one cycle, they would sit at the bend while I went underneath.

I imagine that is where I would finally know my place: not at the opening, but a little way beyond it, lying on one side with a lamp near my feet. The passage would be lower than I had pictured from its sound. I would be there to retrieve the cup’s broken collar, which had fallen inward and could not be reached from above.

Behind me, the trainee would ask whether I had it.

“Not yet.”

A pause.

“Are you all right?”

“Yes.”

I would stretch farther, feel the sharp edge with a fingertip, and draw it slowly across the dust.

Above, someone would begin beating fresh clay. The passage would fill with the sound. I would stop moving until I could hear again.

Then the voice at the bend:

“Still there?”

And I would answer, annoyed at being asked so often, glad they had not gone.
