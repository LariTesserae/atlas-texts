I would be farther under the pipe than you would first think to look.

From the stepping stones, that place appears to be a dark line between the insulation and the wall. But the wall has broken unevenly. Behind the pipe there is room, and behind that room a narrower cleft filled with pale roots. Water comes through intermittently. A loose washer lies at the entrance, tilted against a grain of stone. Its lower half is orange; its upper half is still recognizably metal.

I would be somewhere near the washer.

At first I imagine a hand reaching into this space. The backs of the fingers brush the warm pipe. The fingertips encounter roots, grit, a patch of something yielding. There is not enough room to turn the hand over. Whatever lives there can withdraw farther than the hand can follow.

That is where I begin to have a body.

It is gathered into the unevenness behind the washer, with part of its back touching metal and its foot spread over stone. There is no single temperature. Warmth comes strongly through one fold, weakly through another. Along the outer edge, an occasional movement of air causes a tightening that travels inward and subsides.

I would be one of the animals, probably a small one. Large enough to leave the moss in which I hatched, small enough that the washer still alters the shape of my shelter.

My back would have a pale patch where the brown had not fully developed. You could recognize me by it, if you returned often enough and could get your head behind the pipe. I would have no use for that distinction. I could pass another animal with the same patch and remain occupied by the wetness beneath us.

For a while I stay where I am.

The pump reaches this recess unevenly. Through the washer there is a fine, hard tremor. Through the root beneath my rear portion there is almost none. As I loosen and spread, more of me comes into contact with the tremor. It was happening before I woke, but now there is more of it.

A drop forms overhead. It grows until it touches my back.

I shorten.

The drop runs down a fold and enters the narrow space between my foot and the wall. After a time, I extend again. My leading edge finds the place the water has newly covered.

I do not come out all at once. There is still a considerable amount of me behind the washer when my head reaches the underside of the bracket. Here the surface changes. The stone has innumerable small edges; the bracket has a long smoothness interrupted by rust. I follow the join between them, where a soft film has gathered.

This would be my first occupation in the world: working along a join.

There would be interruptions. A patch too dry to cross without contracting. Something bitter enough that the front of my body lifted away before the rest stopped advancing. The thin moving edge of a root, growing slowly but loosened now by water, which gave under my weight and left my head extended over a gap.

I would spend some time at that gap.

From above, it would be almost invisible. A person might clear the whole bracket with one careless step. Beneath it, I would reach down, find nothing, draw back, and reach sideways. Eventually my mouth would touch a lower part of the wall. My foot would widen onto it. The rear of my body would peel slowly from the metal.

For several moments, the small plate beneath my skin would stand up along one edge.

Then I would be across.

I think I would live on this side of the garden for a long time. The lip would be beyond my routes. The lamp would sometimes change the brightness reaching the dark points on my head, without becoming an object toward which I travelled. I might never encounter the largest animal. Its grazing marks and mine could occur on different parts of the same wall for an entire season.

Once, though, I would meet another beneath the bracket.

It would approach along the route I was using. Our heads would touch while both our bodies continued to advance. Mine would thicken against the pressure. One short feeler would disappear beneath a fold.

For a time there would be warm, moving skin where the wall ought to be.

I would turn upward. The other would continue under me, carrying part of my weight. Its back would be slicker than the mineral surface, and I would spread farther to adhere. Then its rear would pass and my foot would settle back onto the bracket.

A little of its mucus would remain across my route. I would feed beyond it.

The flood would reach me during another such journey.

At first there would be more room to move without drying. The spaces between grains would fill; a difficult ridge would become easy to cross. I would continue down the wall into water that stood above my back.

Later, I would turn.

The way toward the washer would still be available, but the washer itself would be submerged. I would pass it and climb into the roots. Silt would lodge against my side. A root would shift, and I would hang from it with the rear part of my foot until my head found the concrete again.

By the time I reached the upper wall, I would be carrying a small fragment of moss in a fold.

There I would fasten myself. Others might arrive close by. We would remain separate, each inside the wet border of its own body.

Below us, the pump would continue.

When the water fell, I would not immediately follow it. The wall would cool. My margin would hold. Moisture would collect around me and gradually soften the fastening until one edge yielded.

I would descend a little to the left of where I had climbed.

That would be enough to miss the bracket.

Instead, I would find the white mineral streak below a bolt, then a seam in the concrete, then the sheltered back of a conduit. There would be food along part of it. Farther down, a pocket would hold water after the exposed wall had begun to dry.

I might remain there.

The washer would stay behind the pipe, with its tilted edge and the space beneath it. Nothing would need to happen to that old shelter. I would simply fail to arrive there again.

And if you came looking, remembering the small brown animal with the pale patch, you might lift the fern and find the recess empty.

A few inches away, behind the conduit, my head would be moving out over a damp seam. The front of my body would widen. The rest would follow only when there was somewhere to put it.
