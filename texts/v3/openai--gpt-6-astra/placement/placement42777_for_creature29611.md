I think I would be in one of the rooms whose original doorway has been blocked.

Not trapped there. There would be another entrance, reached by stepping across a channel and turning sideways past a projecting wall. But from inside I could still make out the old opening. Its infill would be a little rougher than the surrounding stone. On mornings when I had work to avoid, I would pick lime from the seam with my thumbnail.

I would want to reopen it.

This would be a small, persistent nuisance to everyone who knew me. The doorway would open onto a passage already used for lowering baskets to the water. There would be questions about whose passage it was, where my door flap would swing, whether I meant to put a step there. I would have answered these questions differently on different occasions, damaging my case.

What I wanted was to come home without passing through anyone else’s work.

At present, the woman who removes glass from wounds would have to draw in her knees whenever I passed. She would do this without interrupting what she was doing. Sometimes her patient would try to move as well.

“Not you,” she would say, and catch their wrist.

I would belong to the household next to hers, though we would share enough wall, drainage, and daily inconvenience that a visitor might not understand the distinction.

My work would be small work. Not the fine, celebrated kind. I would replace toggles, pierce hide, cut new notches in old handles, smooth the part of a bone hook that kept wearing through its line. People would bring me things after attempting the repair themselves. They would explain what the object needed while demonstrating, with both hands, why their first attempt should have worked.

I would listen longer than the woman did.

This would not necessarily be kindness. I would like knowing how things had gone wrong. I would also be reluctant to begin while someone watched.

There would be a shallow shell beside my knee containing pieces too small to be generally valuable: bone splinters, short lengths of thong, a wooden wedge polished on one face. Anyone looking for something to prop up a pot would be told not to take from it. I would know what most of the pieces might become. A few I would have kept for so long that their possible uses had narrowed to the preservation of my judgment.

The younger child would be permitted to sort them, but not carry them away.

Once, I would find a crab claw among them. I would leave it there.

—

When the boat arrives, I am trying to open a second hole beside a damaged hole in a strip of bone. There is barely enough material. If it splits, I will have to make the whole piece again.

I see the sister pass the entrance.

She has finished the trap. I know because she is still flexing the fingers with which she pulled the lashing tight. She does not ask me to come. At the turn, though, she looks back.

I set down the point.

By the time we reach the landing, the long thing has been brought ashore. It is a piece of timber, dark outside and pale where an end has broken. Three people are examining it with the concentrated suspicion usually given to an unfamiliar injury.

It is not straight. From above, between the roofs, it looked straight.

Someone has already proposed dividing it lengthwise. Someone else puts a foot against it and makes the sign for a boat rib. The crew member who brought it keeps his hand on one end.

The sister is not looking at the wood.

She is watching a woman in the boat loosen a strap from her shoulder. The woman has a covering made from several unlike pieces of hide. Salt has dried along one edge. When she lifts her arm, I can see that the seam underneath has been repaired with a row of holes much closer together than I would have made them.

The sister crouches. Now her face is near the stranger’s.

I cannot hear their conversation. I can see the questions arriving. She points outward once, then lowers her hand and asks something longer.

I stay beside the timber after I have finished examining it.

There is a useful fork near the broken end. I imagine cutting it away, turning it in my hands. Then I notice that I have been standing where people need to pass.

—

Later, the sister brings me a bundle.

She waits until the child has gone. That makes me uncomfortable before I know why.

Inside are a cutting flake wrapped in hide, a bone point, two fastenings, and a shell cup. She has laid them together in a way that keeps the sharp edge away from the thong, but the point can still work loose if the bundle is turned.

“Can you make this stay closed?”

I begin rearranging it.

She stops my hand.

“So I can open it myself. Without putting everything down.”

That is a different problem.

I ask which hand she would use. She tries both. With her left hand holding an imaginary load against her side, she reaches across with her right. The motion is awkward. We move the fastening.

I could ask when she is leaving. I could ask whether the boat’s crew has agreed. There are several questions whose answers I would then have to carry back through the adjoining room.

Instead, I find the little wooden wedge in my shell.

It is almost right. I cut away its polished face.

She watches this with an attention I find easier than conversation. When I am finished, she closes the bundle, opens it, then closes it behind her back.

“Wet it,” I say.

She looks at me.

“It will swell.”

We put it in the channel and wait.

—

I would not be the person who left on that boat. At least, not then.

I would have too many opinions about where my things belonged. I would dislike borrowing tools whose weight was wrong. I might listen eagerly to an account of soft ground and still spend the following morning arguing for permission to make one more doorway in the place where I already lived.

But I would want the fastening to work.

When we lift the bundle out, the toggle sticks. She pulls harder.

“Wait.”

I hold out my hand, and she gives it back. Water runs from the fold onto my knees. I shave a little more from the wood, trying not to take too much. She sits beside me, blocking what light there is, and for once I do not ask her to move.
