I would find myself in the passage that was not meant to be a passage, where the two supports have worn back from one another.

I would have gone in facing the wrong way. There is room to turn my head but not my shoulders, and for a while I would be occupied entirely by the problem of getting through: one arm raised, the other trapped against my ribs, my cheek finding the shallow hollow where other cheeks may have rested.

The sound would make it difficult to judge how much force I was using. I would think I was easing myself forward when in fact I was pushing hard enough to hurt.

Here, before I knew there was a creature, something would yield.

Only a little. Enough to let my lower arm come free.

I would take it for an accident of the masonry. Then, having passed through, I would put my hand back into the gap to understand it.

The place that yielded would already be hard again.

*

I think I would be human there, at least at first: an inconvenient body with a wet interior, carrying little pockets of coolness that would soon be lost. I would have knees that needed room the building did not provide. My teeth would hurt. I would keep swallowing to clear a pressure that swallowing could not clear.

There would be things in my pockets that had ceased to be useful.

A folded piece of paper. A key. Something tied with thread.

I would take them out in the central chamber and lay them on the slab, partly to inspect them and partly because arranging small possessions is a way of making a place temporarily habitable.

The key would trouble the creature.

I would not understand this immediately. It would touch the slab, withdraw, touch it again farther along. A lobe would rise beneath its jaw. I would pick up the paper, then the little tied thing, trying to discover what I had done.

When I lifted the key, its breast would settle.

The key had been trembling against the brick. I could not hear it. I would put it in my pocket and close my hand around it.

That would be the first thing it taught me.

*

I would initially mistake its attention for a request.

This is a habit I would have brought a very long way: to believe that if something notices me, I must either give it something or get away.

I would show it my empty palms. I would offer the paper. I might even speak, though only once.

At the closing of its jaw plates I would stop.

Thereafter I would try to make my movements plain. Before standing, I would lean forward. Before touching a wall, I would hold my hand near it. These gestures might mean nothing to it. But they would slow me enough to notice what happened next.

It would begin to approach while I was sitting.

Not from in front. A broad end of a limb would appear beside my heel, fitted to the junction between floor and pier. Sometimes it would remain there. Sometimes, after I shifted, it would disappear.

I would learn not to turn quickly.

The first part of it I could recognize reliably would be a dark patch inside a fold. Even then I would not know whether I was recognizing an individual or simply one portion of a body much larger and more distributed than I understood.

I would give it no name.

I might try one silently, then find that the name made me expect it to arrive whole.

*

When it offered the hand, I would take it badly.

Too firmly, with the quick enclosing pressure by which one person assures another that contact has been made.

Its fingers would flatten. For an instant there would be almost nothing inside my grip.

I would let go.

The hand would remain extended.

This time I would place mine beneath it.

There would follow a long adjustment. Its low thumb would find the side of my wrist. One finger would lie across two of mine. Another would curl into the hollow of my palm without touching the bottom.

I would feel my own pulse become conspicuous.

Then I would try to make it steady.

Of course that would make it faster.

The creature would change its hold, following the change, and I would be embarrassed by the exposure of something I could neither conceal nor perform correctly.

It would take me some time to understand that it had not asked for a steady pulse.

*

I would not become its interpreter. There would be no moment when the chord resolved into sentences or the six piers disclosed the purpose of the building.

But I might learn a few positions.

One place beside the slab where I could sit without making it draw its limbs closer. One way of putting my feet down that did not close some route of pressure it was using. One shallow recess where my back hurt less.

And it might learn something of my arrangements.

That the projecting joint in the middle of my leg did not fold sideways.

That pressure on the soft place below my ribs made me pull away.

That sometimes I withdrew my hand only because my fingers had gone numb.

I imagine this last distinction taking the longest.

Each time I loosened my grip, it would spread its fingers a little farther, searching for the lost contact. I would flex my hand, wait, and offer it again.

Eventually it might leave its hand resting beside mine during the interval.

Not reaching.

Available.

*

I could not stay indefinitely. My body would bring that fact into every contact, whether either of us understood it or not. Thirst would alter my pulse. Fatigue would make my steps less careful. The building would remain warm.

I would need to search for a way down.

But before that, there would be a time beside the slab when I no longer tried to improve what the creature felt.

My heart would stumble, recover, hurry for no useful reason. Its fingers would accommodate each change.

With my free hand I would touch the nearest pier. I would feel almost nothing except heat and the enormous, unvarying insistence of the sound.

Between those two contacts I would sit: one hand receiving more than I could distinguish, the other giving more than I knew how to say.

For a little while, that is who I would be there.
