At first I would put myself beneath the island, with the six: a back fitted to a low ceiling, feet set where the next pressure change could be felt. But those resting places are occupied. To belong there I would have to arrive, and arrival would be less quiet than the view from the entrance suggests.

I think I would come through the rear seam.

Not as the old animal did, large enough to emerge into the chamber and grip a column. I would still be small enough to turn around inside the seam. For some time I would live there without discovering that it opened into a larger space.

My body would be pale. The joints would have a little color when extended, but I would seldom extend them fully. There would always be another surface within reach.

In the passage, the root’s knocking would not sound deep or distant. It would come through everything I touched. Before each closure, one wall would firm against my feet; then the opposite wall would answer. In the quiet part of the cycle I would advance, feeding as I went. A patch of film could hold me for many repetitions. When it was scraped clean, I would move far enough to find another.

I would have come from somewhere narrower, in company. For a while there would have been a shell behind me, another ahead, the occasional pressure of limbs reaching past my own. Then the passages would have divided. Nothing in particular would mark the last contact.

Here, one of my antennae could reach into water without finding a wall.

That would be the beginning of the hollow for me.

I would stay at its edge. The white feeding mouths would withdraw when I touched them, leaving smooth little closures in the floor. Farther out, they would open again. Their fans would comb particles past my face. Some of those particles I could catch.

There would be light, but it would take time for light to become useful. At first the slit would be a broad direction in which surfaces were harder to distinguish. A moving shadow would make me flatten. So would the frog’s call. After it, I would remain still long enough for the feeding fans around me to reopen.

My first attempt to cross the exposed floor would go badly.

The current would be shallow enough to stand in. I would walk with my body lifted, keeping the edge of my shell above the water, and reach a stretch of tissue smoother than the passage wall. Then the valve would close.

I would lower myself as usual. But there would be no opposing surface to brace against. Water would take the fringe of my shell and turn me partly around. Two feet would lose their purchase. For a moment I would be held by one hooked foreleg, the rest of me trailing toward the seam.

I would catch the floor again before being carried into it.

Afterward I would feed where I had started.

Several cycles later, I would try along the wall.

There is a place I can begin to see now: behind the broad foot of the nearest column, on the side turned away from the entrance. Ceiling water comes down there in a narrow groove. At the bottom, the groove bends around a swelling in the tissue before joining the floor current. A small fold overhangs the bend.

I would get beneath it.

Not all the way. My back would fit, but one rear leg would remain outside, flexed against the column. I would pull it in, find that I had lifted my shell too far, and extend it again. For a long time that would be my resting posture: five feet sheltered, the sixth receiving the movement of the chamber.

Food would collect just beyond the fold. Not much at once. Enough that I could reach out, take something, and retreat without turning my whole body. A narrow worm would occupy the groove above me. When it lengthened into the descending water, its tip would sometimes brush my shell.

I would push upward.

It would shorten.

We would do this often.

As I grew, the fold would become inconvenient before it became accommodating. Its edge would catch the margin of my shell. Entering would require a sideways movement and a brief, close scrape. The exposed leg would no longer fold comfortably against the column.

Then I would stop feeding.

The old shell would loosen around the joints. Water would reach places where I had not felt it before. Under the fold, I would brace and draw against it until part of me came free, softer and larger than the space seemed able to hold.

I would have to leave the shelter to finish.

There would be no good time, only the wet sigh and the water turning around the column. I would press into its lee. The remaining shell would hold at a foot, and I would keep pulling while the flow laid its light weight against my side.

When I was free, I would be unable to get back beneath the fold.

I would wait beside it, low against the column, with my new legs bent. An adult crossing the floor would touch me with an antenna. Its forefoot would come down on the empty shell instead of on my back. The shell would tilt. The adult would pass.

By the next feeding period, I could push farther into my shelter. Not because the whole root had made room for me at once: one edge would have softened, and I would have learned to enter with my body angled differently. Later growth would follow that pressure. The fold would acquire a shallow roof, almost invisible from outside.

That is where I would find myself.

Not quite under the island, and not inside the machinery. Behind the first column, where a thin run of water joins a wider one.

Occasionally I would cross into the leaf-softened light. My joints would show red when I stood high enough to drain my fringe. I would turn a scrap between my front limbs, working along an edge already cleared by something else.

But most of my life would happen around that bend.

The knock would lift me slightly against my roof. The roof would settle back. Above me the worm would extend into the groove, and I would shift one foot to let the water through.
