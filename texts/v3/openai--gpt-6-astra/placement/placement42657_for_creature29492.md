I would be somewhere along the maintenance track, but not on it.

There is a place where one of the sleepers has sunk at an angle. Its upper end dries in the afternoon; its lower end rests in brown water beneath the grass. From the track it looks like a small defect, something to step over. Beneath it there is a wedge of space, roofed partly with wood and partly with roots.

I think I would find that space before I knew what shape I had.

The wood would be cold against my back. Along one side, water would pass steadily enough to keep the fine sediment from settling. Along the other, there would be a pocket of stillness where a loose sedge seed turned without going anywhere. Light would enter through three or four small openings, each bright enough to keep an eye away from.

There would be room to rest, though perhaps not room to turn around.

At first that seems like a disadvantage. Then I feel the opening behind me, the broad feet folding close, the ease with which I can withdraw backward. Turning around belongs to a different sort of body.

Mine fits.

I would be one of the animals, not especially old. There would still be more brown than green along my back. A few strands of moss would catch in my coat and come away again; I would not yet carry the thick growth that makes an old animal look as though it has been lifted out of the ground with a shovel.

I would have lived under the hut when I was small. I would know its piers by their separate surfaces: one rough with grit, one coated in something slippery, one with a narrow seam that caught a claw. But another animal would occupy the best hollow now, and I would have begun finding places beyond it.

The sleeper would be my most recent place.

For several days I would use it only briefly. I would enter, wait, come out, circle beneath the neighboring cushion, return. Once I would bring a stem inside and lose it to the current. Once I would fall asleep with a hind foot still outside.

By the time rain came, I would stay.

The rain would arrive first as innumerable touches on the roof. Water would press lower around the timber, closing two of the light holes. I would move my nostrils to the remaining air and sleep again.

Above me, the track would be disappearing into the meadow.

***

I would know a worker before I knew there was a person.

The first arrival would be a distant pair of impacts, repeated unevenly. Then a pause. Then another pair, stronger.

By the third visit I would recognize the pause. It came where the track crossed the broad wet hollow, while the worker considered where to put a foot.

I would stop feeding.

The shocks would approach through the timber, become almost unbearably distinct, then pass overhead. Dirt would come loose from the underside of the sleeper. A little would settle on my closed eyes.

Only afterward would I rise through the grass beside the track.

There would be a large moving darkness, a flash from something carried in one hand, and the sour mineral smell left by a boot. I would see very little of the worker. Mostly I would encounter what remained: compressed stems, a new trickle entering an old footprint, one torn root leaking its faint taste into the water.

That evening I would eat the root.

A few days later the worker would stop directly above me.

The sleeper would tilt.

I would leave so quickly that my flank scraped the wood. Behind me, light would fill the whole chamber. One end of the sleeper would rise, hang dripping, then come down on a bed of gravel.

For a long time I would wait beneath a pool margin with only the rims of my nostrils exposed.

When I returned, the wedge would be gone.

There would still be a passage, but it would be too low across the middle, and the water would have begun carrying pale grit through it. I would press forward until my shoulders met the roof. My toes would search beyond the obstruction.

Then I would back out.

I would try again after dark.

***

I do not think I would live under the hut after that.

I might visit it. I might lie for an hour beside the familiar rough pier, where the water was warmer and smelled of another animal. But I would have begun to know the outer ground: a submerged willow branch, a pool with a cold spring entering at its bottom, a long low room where the roots were red and fine.

There would be more than one place available to me. I would need time to learn that.

Near the young margin I would find a cushion held up by a braid of old runners. Beneath it, a tuft of roots would hang almost to the bottom. I would feed there, drawing the tender lengths through my mouth, leaving the thick ones.

On the first evening there would scarcely be room for my head.

On the second, I could get my shoulders underneath.

The water would carry the bitten fragments out behind me. A small shrimp-like thing would come to investigate. I would miss it, close my mouth on water, and wait for the disturbed sediment to settle.

By the end of the week I would have a resting place.

It would not be as well protected as the hut. When a bird landed overhead, the roof would dip. Sunlight would sometimes reach through and warm a patch no bigger than one of my feet. The overflow would be less distinct here, softened by open water, though I could feel the deeper pipe vibration when I rested my jaw against a runner.

I would sleep with that runner touching me.

And if the machinery stopped, I would wake.

For a moment every small movement would arrive separately. Water rubbing a stem. Something swimming past my wrist. Another animal feeding somewhere beyond the pool.

I would lift my head and touch the roof in several places.

Then I would move to the edge.

My eyes would emerge among the loose stems, and beyond them would be the bright gap, the shoulders of the basin, the immense exposed surface across which I had hardly traveled at all.

I would remain there until a sedge blade brushed my lip.

I would take it slowly, so that it bent toward me without pulling free.

Under my forefoot, a root would hold.
