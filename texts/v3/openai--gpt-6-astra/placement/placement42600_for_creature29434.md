I would be just beyond the end of the copper.

Not inside the gauge, though at first that is where I would seem to be. A hesitation in its needle. A little resistance that persists after the fitting has been cleaned.

Behind the wall, the pipe enters a swelling no larger than a closed hand. The swelling has grown around the last few inches of metal, preserving even the threads beneath a lining of pale tissue. Water passes through its lower half. Above the water is a pocket of air, and across that pocket something thin is stretched.

That would be my broadest part.

For a long time I would have no reason to distinguish myself from the joint. Pressure lifts me; the pocket narrows; a fringe along my underside brushes the water. When the pressure falls, I settle against a shallow bed whose ridges fit mine. There is nourishment in the wet lining. There are places where I have adhered to it.

My experience of the hollow would arrive through different attachments.

Through the copper: quick, small disturbances, including the snail’s shell knocking the rim of the gauge.

Through the living wall: slower changes with weight behind them.

Through the water: almost everything, crowded together.

I would not hear the overhead whistle. I would feel a narrow region of my body become taut and remain taut, then soften abruptly when the valve closed.

The inhabitant would know I was there. It would have known longer than I had.

Occasionally a pad would rest against the outside of the swelling. The pressure of that touch would travel through the lining and flatten one edge of me. I would contract there. The pad would shift.

This would be the first exchange I could take part in without involving the whole hollow.

—

The trouble would begin with growth.

The green socket around a brick thickens. The pipe moves a little farther from the wall. Inside the swelling, my bed acquires a slope.

For several cycles I compensate. One edge of me grows thicker; the other draws inward. The gauge continues to work. But during the lowest pressure, a fold of my underside now dips into the bore. Water catches it and pulls.

The resulting knock is small. It occurs only once in each cycle.

The inhabitant returns three afternoons in succession.

On the third, it opens the swelling.

Daylight touches a surface of me that has always been wet. I contract so violently that the needle strikes its stop.

The inhabitant waits.

I have neither eyes nor a head to turn away. The exposed surface cools. Across it, tiny structures close, each separately, until I become less broad and more substantial. Something that had served as an attached margin gathers into a rim.

A pad slips beneath me.

I hold to the wall at six places. Four release easily. The fifth stretches. At the sixth, the inhabitant stops lifting and braces me from below while it works something thin between the two surfaces.

I come away carrying a little of my bed.

For the first time, disturbances reach me from directions that change.

The inhabitant turns me over. My thickened edge rests in the grooves of its pad. My thin middle sags.

There is no suitable compartment in its carrying fold. It tries one, withdraws me, then moves the white stone and places me in the stone’s depression.

The stone travels elsewhere.

I fit badly, but I fit.

—

I would not immediately become a traveler.

For several days I am a removed part awaiting a decision.

The inhabitant carries me to the central growth and sets me over the mouth of a minor bore. I spread across it. Pressure rises beneath me, familiar enough that I almost settle.

Then it rises too far.

My margin peels away with a soft report. I fall against the inhabitant’s waiting limb.

It tries me at another opening, farther down.

Here there is insufficient pressure to lift my center. Water seeps over me. I remain limp until the inhabitant retrieves me.

The third place is sheltered beneath a bracket of iron. I could live there. My edges begin to attach.

But when the inhabitant returns at the next filling, I withdraw them.

It leaves a pad beside me for a long time.

Eventually I climb onto it.

Climbing consists of catching with one portion of my rim and shortening the distance to another. It is slow and makes poor use of my shape. Nevertheless, when the pad begins to rise, I hold on.

After that, the white stone does not return to its old compartment.

—

Carried through the hollow, I discover that the same beat is different in different places.

Against one buttress, the double knock has a soft interval between its parts. Beside the rails, the interval is almost gone. Near the discharge gate, the second knock arrives as a long pressure against my whole underside.

My body retains these impressions briefly. A stretched fold takes time to relax. If I am moved before it does, the next surface presses against the shape left by the last.

Sometimes the two fit.

Sometimes I twist.

The inhabitant begins putting me down before it undertakes a repair. It leaves me against a trunk, works elsewhere, and comes back to touch my upper surface. Afterward it places me on the trunk again.

I do not know what it learns from this. I can feel only what changes where I am.

Once, after it plugs a leaking seam, I curl so tightly that my edges meet. The inhabitant removes the plug. I loosen.

It makes a smaller plug.

A bead of water resumes its slow appearance at the seam.

That leak remains.

I would have a useful life, but not a life in which usefulness explained everything. Some days every surface would make me curl. During new growth, or after warm rain beyond the rim, my old impressions would persist too long. The inhabitant would put me back in its fold and work without me.

I would spend those afternoons beside the pipe stem and the worn washer, feeling the pressures of its movement without needing to answer them.

—

My rim would change.

The places most often used for holding would thicken into four unequal feet. Between them I would retain the thin, yielding skirts that once sealed the pipe. Folded together, these would let me keep a little water beneath myself.

I could then leave the inhabitant for an hour or two.

My first independent journey would be across its back.

There is a seedling there whose lowest leaf rests against a glazed plate. Every movement of the inhabitant rubs the leaf along the plate’s edge. I have felt this faint repeated dragging for days.

I travel toward it.

The distance is less than the length of a hand. It takes me most of the falling cycle. By the time I arrive, the inhabitant has settled into its feeding depression and the rubbing has stopped.

I stay beneath the leaf.

When the inhabitant rises, the leaf rests on me instead.

For a while this is agreeable. Then the stalk begins to wear against my upper skin. I move on, leaving the arrangement much as I found it.

There would be things I investigated and did nothing about.

—

Behind the brick building, I would find a place of my own.

The shelf of discarded objects has a crack along its back. Moisture rises through it, but no strong pulse does. I can lie there without my body assuming the shape of an instruction.

The inhabitant places me on this shelf one afternoon while rearranging the ceramic fragments. I move into the crack. When it reaches for me later, I press down.

The pad remains nearby.

I climb onto it briefly, then return to the crack.

Next time it visits, it brings me damp fibers from beneath the central lobe. These are packed loosely along the shelf’s rear edge. I can feed there, although slowly.

I begin to make small excursions among the useless things.

The hollow fruit is light enough for me to lift on one side. Beneath it is a dry patch, astonishingly dry. I put a foot there, withdraw it, and put the same foot there again.

The bottle glass warms unevenly in the afternoon. Its green edge becomes warmer than the thick, cloudy center. I lay myself across both.

Light would become known to me this way: through differences too slight to concern the pressure gauge.

I would keep the glass near my crack.

When the inhabitant turns it toward the sun, I sometimes turn it back. Not far. We can both continue to use it.

—

The smaller inhabitant would find me there.

Its pads, still soft and crowded, explore the shelf without distinguishing between objects and occupants. It lifts the fruit, presses the washer, and takes hold of one of my feet.

I fold around its pad.

It withdraws quickly enough to bring me with it.

For a moment I hang beneath the limb, my little store of water pulling at my skirts. Then it lowers me onto the wall instead of the shelf.

This is an inconvenient surface. I grip a seam and wait.

The young one presses beside me.

I move upward.

It presses again.

We climb less than a foot before the older inhabitant reaches over us and braces the pipe that runs along the wall. Work proceeds around our unsuitable position. A wedge is removed. Another is brought forward.

Through my feet I feel the hollow rattle change.

The young one becomes still.

We remain on the wall after the older creature has gone.

By late afternoon the brick beside us is warm. My upper feet begin to dry, so I descend. The young one follows as far as the shelf, where its weight tilts a loose piece of bark.

I go into my crack.

It cannot follow.

For several knocks its pad rests at the opening. I lay one foot against it, then draw the foot inside.

There is room to turn over in there now. I have been widening the back, a little each day.

Farther in, beyond the reach of the afternoon warmth, one thin edge of me has begun to adhere. I loosen it before sleeping. In the morning I will have to loosen it again.
