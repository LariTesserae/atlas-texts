I would be near the second turn, where a person stops trying to see.

Not quite at the wall. There would be a little space behind me, enough for fingertips to pass through and curl. When someone reached too high, I could do nothing. Their hand would travel over the warm mineral and return empty. They would have to try lower.

I think this matters: that I could be missed.

The person who lives in the hollow would usually find me with the backs of two fingers. They would scarcely close their hand. Coming home with something awkward—a bundle of branches, a sack that must be kept above the water—they might lean a wrist against me while changing their grip. I would feel the burden without knowing what they carried.

A stranger would take hold differently.

There would be the first touch, light and readily withdrawn. Then the whole hand. Sometimes a pull, harder than necessary, as though whatever had been found in the darkness ought to prove it belonged there.

I would tighten between my fastenings.

I would not lead anywhere by myself. There would have to be a hand moving along me, and feet discovering, below, what a hand cannot tell them.

At first I imagine being the whole cord: the length through both bends, the part that dries a little when the weather changes, the part that remains wet. But that gives me too much of the passage. I would not know the cold at the entrance and the warmth farther in at once.

I would be a repair near the second turn.

A short length of fibre doubled into the older cord, its ends worked back through the twist. One end would have been trimmed neatly. The other would retain a small roughness because the person doing the work had lost patience, or their fingers had begun to ache. They meant to finish it another time.

That would be where I was most easily recognized.

Passing outward, their thumb would catch there. Passing inward, the roughness would lie down beneath their palm. Without counting steps, they would know which part of the turn they had reached.

I would have come up the mountain around their waist. Before that, there would have been hands in a valley, fibres separated and rolled against a thigh, a conversation taking place above the work. I would retain none of its words. Something of the work would remain in me: a thicker portion, a strand turned back upon itself, the uneven firmness of what had been made while attention went elsewhere.

The hollow would not undo these differences.

Its stone could receive a hand and slowly release the impression. I would keep the small alterations made by use. A tightened splice. A loosened hair. The place where one frightened visitor had worried me between finger and thumb while deciding whether to continue.

I could serve them in either direction.

If they went back, I would run through their hand toward the cold. The person from the platform might follow, carrying the cup, and sit with them outside. For a while there would be nobody touching me.

Most of my time would be like that.

I do not think I would hear the cup from here. Perhaps a voice would arrive with its edges missing. Perhaps a movement would reach me through the cord when the person checked the farther fastening. I would have no account of their nights, no access to the knots in the bark or the reasons for a journey downhill.

I would know their return only when they reached me.

Eventually, a strand would part.

It might happen while nobody was passing. In the damp, under the small constant strain, something within the twist would give. I would remain where I was. The next hand might notice nothing.

But their hand would pause.

They would feel along my underside, then bend me gently against the direction of the lay. Later they would come back with fibre. The passage is narrow there; they would have to work with one shoulder lowered, their breath close to the wall.

For a little while, the cord would be down.

Anyone arriving would call, and the person would answer from the turn.

“Wait. I’m here.”

Then there would be the slow work of separating what could still be used from what had softened too far. I cannot decide how much of me would remain. Perhaps only a few strands, enclosed by the new ones. Perhaps none.

Until then, I would be the slight thickness that passes through a hand just before there is room to straighten.
