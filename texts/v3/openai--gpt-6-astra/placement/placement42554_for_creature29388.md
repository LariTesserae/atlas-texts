I would live beneath one of the streets, in a house people mistook for an entrance to somewhere else.

There would be reasons for the mistake. The main rope would pass within reach of my lintel. My breathing bell would be visible from outside, and the next public chamber uphill would be farther away than it looked. People would come in with the particular expression of someone who meant to go on but had reconsidered.

I would know enough to look away while they took their first breaths.

Afterward they might explain themselves. They had been carrying something awkward. The current near the gardens had changed. They were quite capable of making the upper chamber, ordinarily.

“Yes,” I would say.

I would have been a poor choice for the upper gardens myself. Too much hauling, too few places to rest without appearing to rest. For several years I would have blamed a weakness in my chest before admitting that I disliked the work even on days when I breathed easily.

But I would still grow things: bitter foliage in the ceiling mesh, edible stems along the outside wall, one expensive plant that had never produced anything and that I kept because its seller used the street above my house.

Whenever she passed, I would be busy elsewhere.

***

My work would begin with the bell.

Not making it. I would have inherited it with the house: an old clay chamber, broad enough for three heads if their owners were friendly, bound outside with waterwood ribs. A repair on one side would be darker than the original clay. Nobody living would remember what had broken it.

What I would learn was how to hang it.

A bell that hung badly wasted space. A slight tilt let air escape from its lower lip while the other side held a pocket no one could comfortably reach. Its suspension had to accommodate the house, and the house had to accommodate the mountain, which had no interest in either.

At first I would only adjust my own bindings. Then a neighbor would ask me to look at hers. I would go over with more cord than I needed, fail to improve it, and return the next day after thinking about the problem through an entire meal.

Eventually there would be a basket of fittings beside my door: wooden toggles, lengths of sound cord, curved pieces cut from broken bells. I would become somebody called for when a breathing place grew inconvenient.

Not when the air itself went bad. I would know people who understood the plants better than I did, and fetch them.

My hands would be broad, with one little finger that no longer straightened. In water it would trail slightly away from the others. Children learning my name-sign would copy the crooked finger, though it was not part of the name.

I would let them.

***

The first time Emet came to my house for work, rather than air, she would remain below the bell and inspect its outer supports.

I would see her feet from where I was eating.

After a while she would rise beside me.

“Your lower wall is taking weight.”

“It has always taken weight.”

“More weight.”

I would move my food to make room for her elbows. She would not put her elbows down.

There would be a shallow shelf beneath the chamber, a place where visitors could rest their forearms. Over the years it would have risen toward the clay lip. When I first lived there, I could stand upright beneath it without touching anything. Now I would turn sideways to enter, a movement so familiar that I no longer counted it as a difficulty.

Emet would ask me to show her how I came in.

I would resent this more than I resented the wall.

Below the bell, she would watch me tuck a shoulder, turn my head, and pull myself up by a short loop.

Then she would make me do it holding a basket.

There are refusals that require only one hand. I would use one.

She would wait.

***

We would agree to lift the bell by the width of a palm.

This would sound like a small job until we began removing the things attached to it.

The bitter plants had fed their roots through two layers of mesh. A lamp bracket used one of the suspension cords. Behind the bracket was a little wedge I had put there to stop a rattle and then forgotten. The roof binding above it had been renewed around a storage hook rather than beneath it, so that the hook was now part of the roof.

Seli would arrive with a frame for holding the plants.

He would spend a long time freeing their roots. I would begin to tell him which threads could be broken, then notice that he had already loosened a section I had expected to lose.

Emet would hand me a bundle of empty carrying vessels.

While they worked, I would take the air out of my house.

It could not all be saved. The last of it lay against the uneven ceiling in shallow patches, divided by clay ribs and old repairs. I would tilt a vessel beneath one patch and coax the air toward its mouth with my fingers. Several bubbles would evade me, slipping along the ceiling until they found a crack between my hand and the clay.

I would follow them with my eyes even though there was nothing to be done.

Without its air, the bell would become an ordinary submerged object. I could put my whole head inside and look closely at the ceiling I had lived beneath for years.

There would be marks in it.

Some were fingerprints from the making. Others had been scratched after firing: two crossed lines, a little row of pits. Near the oldest repair, someone had drawn the rough outline of a hand with six fingers.

I would call Seli over.

He would count the fingers twice, then hold up his own hand, spreading it as far as it would go.

For a moment we would both hang there with our heads inside the empty bell, neglecting the work.

***

The new supports would hold. My first binding would not.

As we eased the bell upward, one loop would tighten around its toggle and pull the toggle sideways. I would feel the movement through the cord before I saw it.

Emet would catch the rim. Seli would seize a roof rib with both hands.

There would be no great collapse. The bell would turn a little, scrape a pale scar along the wall, and stop. But for several breaths afterward, taken in my neighbor’s chamber, I would speak too loudly.

“The cord was sound.”

Emet would wipe mineral paste from her wrist.

“Yes.”

“I tested it.”

“Yes.”

I would hear what I sounded like and become quiet.

Back at the house, she would hold the toggle while I remade the binding with a second loop to keep it flat. She would not offer to do it for me.

That would matter later, more than her catching the bell.

***

Refilling would take the rest of the working period.

A few neighbors would bring air from their gardens. One would bring a vessel scarcely larger than a drinking cup and stay long enough to describe the trouble involved in collecting it. Another would arrive with two large jars, empty them, and leave before I could rise to thank him.

I would forget the first person’s generosity before I forgot the annoyance. I would know this was unfair and still need to make an effort when we next met.

By the time the bell held enough air to use, my shoulders would ache from working above my head.

I would enter too cautiously, making the old sideways movement where it was no longer needed. My face would emerge. My inner lids would withdraw, and the room would sharpen around me.

It would feel wrong.

The ceiling would be the same distance from my head, but the ledge would be lower. A familiar clay rib would now sit to my left instead of directly above me. In turning the bell to fit the supports, we would have moved the six-fingered hand into view.

Seli would surface beside me.

He would examine the ceiling, find the drawing, and smile.

Then he would try to rest his elbows.

We had lifted the bell. We had not made a new ledge.

For a little while we would remain there with our chins raised, looking at each other.

Emet would come in last. There would barely be room.

“Well,” she would say.

***

I would not become a builder through this. Emet would make the ledge, and I would owe her work on two chambers whose bindings she distrusted.

My own work would spread unevenly through the terraces. Some days I would mend a suspension. Some days I would carry vessels, or spend an hour persuading a household that their favorite storage basket obstructed the entrance. I would get better at checking the approach as well as the chamber itself.

At home, I would keep the short entry loop even after it was no longer necessary. People who had used the bell for years would reach for it without looking.

The new ledge would have room for three pairs of elbows.

I would eat at one end, leaving the other clear. Outside, feet would pass along the street overhead. Sometimes a traveler would slow, lower themselves beneath the lintel, and come up beside me with their eyes still covered.

I would move the bowl.

There would be questions I could ask once they had caught their breath. Whether the upper rope had been repaired. Whether the garden harvest was late. Whether they had seen the woman who sold me the expensive plant.

But first I would sit with them, looking at the little hand in the clay, until they could see again.
