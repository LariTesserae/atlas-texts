I would be farther in than the animal with the bitten crown, but not down in the salt basin. Somewhere water still arrives in pulses, with intervals long enough for the smallest suspended grains to settle.

There is an old tube there, lying almost sideways. Its builder began on the lower valve and grew toward an opening that no longer exists. Three successive rims record the turn it tried to make. The last is pressed against the base of a cup.

I would first seem to be something inside that tube.

Not the occupant. That body is gone. Not quite a new occupant, either: nothing large enough to withdraw when touched. A little dullness on the inner wall, perhaps, where the lime should show through clearly.

Come closer, and the dullness has an edge.

It extends around a grain, stops at a fracture, resumes on the other side. It is thickest along the underside of the tube, where a narrow seam admits water. Here and there its surface lifts into a translucent fold. A particle touching one of these folds remains for a while, then moves inward without any visible mouth having opened.

I think I would be that lining.

At first there would be very little of me. A settled body, flattened almost out of recognition, spending what it had carried to make its attachment secure. The hairs that brought me here would be gone. There would be no means of lifting away again.

For a time I would have no opening through which to eat.

Water would pass. The stored material within me would grow less. Along one margin, cells would draw together around a small inward turning. The fold would deepen. Eventually a few short arms would loosen from one another and rise into the hollow of the abandoned house.

My first mouth would open inside someone else’s doorway.

The water there would not be good, but it would be sufficient. Enough for a second feeding body to thicken behind the first. Enough for the living layer between them to extend farther along the lime.

From outside, I would still look like a stain.

I would not have the shell’s shape available to me. Even the old tube would be too much to know at once. There would be the pull against one set of arms and the weaker pull against another. Food entering here. An injury there, followed by contraction. A stretch of my underside meeting a surface on which it could hold.

Two mouths at opposite ends of me might open into quite different water.

One would receive fragments loosened by the passing crustacean. The other would spend a long interval sorting mineral grains from almost nothing. Between them, through the thin common tissue, nourishment would move. The poorly placed mouth could remain alive without its own catch being enough to sustain it.

I would grow unevenly.

At the fed end, a low cup would acquire a firmer rim. At the other, the arms would shorten. Some of what had made that feeding body would be withdrawn into the lining. For a while its opening would remain as a small puckered place. Then there would only be a thicker patch against the wall.

Nothing would have decided to abandon it. Growth would continue elsewhere.

A worm would enter the tube one dim interval, pressing its flank against me for nearly my whole length. My mouths would close. Along the narrowest part, the living film would tear.

After the worm had passed, the torn edges would contract separately.

This is where being a lining becomes less simple. For a time I would be two patches with no tissue between them. Each would retain a mouth. Each would feed. The injury would have divided the routes within me as thoroughly as a fallen partition divides the water outside.

The worm’s groove would collect fine material. New growth might cross it.

Or a different settler might fasten there first.

Then my two portions would extend around a body neither could absorb, their edges approaching on the far side of it. Perhaps they would join. Perhaps they would stop just short, leaving a seam scarcely wide enough for a single cell to pass.

I would have no way to count what I had become.

Eventually the ice would press the shell.

The old tube, already weakened at its base, would split lengthwise. One piece would settle lower. The other would remain caught beneath the cup. Much of my lining would stay attached to both.

For the first time, water would strike surfaces that had grown in shelter.

Some arms would be damaged before they could fold. A loose strip of tissue would lift and remain stretched between the separated pieces. Through it, briefly, the two portions would still communicate.

Then it would part.

On the lower fragment, grit would begin to accumulate. The surviving mouths would reach upward through it. One would clear itself; another would close beneath the deposit.

Above, along the newly exposed fracture, food would arrive before there was any apparatus ready to take it.

That is where I would begin thickening.

Not across the whole broken edge. Only in a shallow notch where the current slackened enough. A bud would swell there, supplied by the mouths still sheltered inside the tube. Its first arms would be short, its rim almost soft enough to move with them.

Beyond it would be a passage I had never touched.

The arms would separate slowly. Water would enter between them. A grain would be turned away, another held, and beneath the new mouth the pale living edge would advance a little farther over the break.
