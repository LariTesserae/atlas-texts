I would arrive from above, where the last warmth is still in the stones.

For a while I would stay among the scrub at the edge. There is no single place where the plateau ends. There are bushes growing out over nothing, slopes that appear to continue until I lower my head, cracks breathing cooler air against my forefeet. A moth passes below me. Farther down, birds cross a space whose other side I cannot yet make out.

The noise rises through the ground.

I go along the rim, looking for a way that does not require a jump.

Here is a carob root exposed by the loss of the soil around it. Here is a sloping shelf, then another, with a dark interruption between them. I stretch down until my front claws find purchase. For a moment my body bridges the gap, belly clear of stone. Then my hind feet follow, and the sky becomes smaller.

There is a smell on the next ledge that stops me.

Not prey. Not the old, scattered traces left where animals have passed without lingering. This is concentrated at a projecting corner, where a body has rubbed and returned.

I bring my nose close, withdraw it, approach from the other side.

Someone lives here.

—

I would be another marten, but younger than the female beneath the pigeon chambers. Both my ears would still be whole. The fur of my throat would divide differently, one white finger reaching farther down my chest than the other.

I would have come some distance across the plateau, sleeping in places I did not use twice.

The caverns would be the first shelter large enough to enter without knowing where its shelter ended.

At first I would keep finding apparent ways through that were not ways for me. A ledge narrows, and I follow it too far before discovering that the last stretch is only a stain on the wall. A hole admits my head and shoulders but bends where my ribs cannot follow. I back out with limestone dust along my flanks.

From below, a fig looks easy to reach. Close to it, I find a smooth bulge of rock between the tree and every foothold I can use.

I sit back. I look elsewhere.

Above me, something moves briskly behind a curtain of leaves. I hear the branch take its weight, then release it.

I remain still long after the sound has stopped.

—

What I know at first is scattered.

A beetle beneath a flake of stone. The stale feathers in an unoccupied hole. A shallow pocket where I can put all four feet together and turn around. One exposed crossing that I make too quickly, dislodging grit into the court.

The falling grains are almost inaudible. Nevertheless, I stop on the far side and listen.

It will take time to learn what matters here. At the rim, a wingbeat made me crouch. Now hundreds pass overhead, and I begin to let them pass. The little scrape inside a cavity holds me motionless.

I wait beside that cavity until my forelegs grow tired.

When I move, the pigeon escapes before I have properly begun. Its wings hit the opening, astonishingly close. I snap at feathers and strike my chin against stone.

For a few seconds the wall is full of departures.

I enter the hole afterward. There is an egg pushed into a shallow hollow behind the nesting material. I eat there, with my hindquarters still close to the entrance.

It is not enough, but it changes the night.

—

I would not live beside the female’s feather bed. Her routes are too frequently renewed with her smell. Even where I do not meet her, there are places I leave quickly.

I go farther into the broken caverns.

The noise loosens as I pass beneath an arch. Individual calls disappear first. Then the whole colony seems to draw away at once, though the opening is still visible behind me.

Here I can hear my own feet when I cross loose grit.

I dislike the broad floor. I follow its edge, beneath shallow overhangs, entering each promising recess. Most are scarcely deeper than my head. One contains a shed snakeskin that catches on my whiskers. Another smells of nothing but dry stone, and I spend a long time examining it.

Beyond a fallen block, a root comes down the wall.

It is thin, dead at its exposed tip, thicker where it enters a seam. I climb beside it and find a narrow shelf hidden from the floor. The shelf runs behind the block. At its far end is a space where I can curl up.

No feathers. No fur. A little powder beneath my paws.

I turn twice, lie down, get up again.

From the entrance I can see a strip of the opposite wall and the lower corner of the arch. Nothing can approach across the floor without entering that small view. Something coming through the crack behind me would be another matter. I investigate the crack until it pinches shut.

Then I return to the shelf.

—

For several nights, this is where I would find myself: not in the richest part of the basin, but close enough to reach it.

I would begin to have a route. The place where I struck my chin would come before the rough slope, and the rough slope before a crossing beneath the bats. I would learn to skirt one patch of loose stone. I would find the fig from its other side.

Once, carrying a fruit in my mouth, I would hear the older marten moving above me.

I would lower myself against the ledge.

She would pass without coming into view. A few fragments of bark would fall, one touching my back. I would wait with the fig held so firmly that its skin split between my teeth.

Later, in the quiet chamber, I would eat what remained of it.

Near morning I would climb to the shelf, nose the entrance, and go inside. A little of my own smell would be there now. I would scrape the loose dust aside with my forefeet and settle with my tail over my nose.

Below me, the floor would remain empty.

When I woke, there would be one pale grain caught in the fur beneath my eye. I would wash it away before going out.
