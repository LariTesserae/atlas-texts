I would find myself at the end of a walkway that no longer reaches anything.

The last section has been removed. Four sockets remain in the deck, filled with water, and beyond them the handrails continue a short distance without support. Below is a stretch of floor I have not crossed in eleven cycles.

I wait here longer than a person would.

There is a route in my records that goes straight ahead. There is another, newer route that turns back toward the pump tower. Both are accurate descriptions of something. Before reversing, I extend a probe past the edge and lower it until it touches the water.

Cold. Conductive. Slightly deeper than last time.

Underneath, the plate is still rising.

I retract the probe. Its joint does not close completely. A strand of filter felt has become wrapped around the shaft, and the cleaning collar pushes it into a tight, dark ring.

Then I turn, in several movements, because there is not quite enough room.

***

I think I would be a maintenance vehicle.

Not one with arms suitable for every task. I would have a brush, a suction line, a paint nozzle, two contact probes, and a small compartment for things removed from drains. My body would be narrow enough for the walkways. Most of my weight would lie between the wheels.

I would know the basin through the places where I could stop safely.

The undersides of ridges would mostly be unavailable to me. So would the tops of the baffles. I would have a good map of the retaining wall and almost no useful account of the vapor above it.

My amber lamp would turn whether anyone was there to see it or not.

At the charging recess, I would sometimes stand beside another vehicle. Our housings would tick as they warmed. We would exchange measurements, including measurements from places neither of us could now reach.

Somewhere in those exchanges would be a walkway still intact, a drain still flowing at its specified rate, a broad flat surface beneath a handrail.

These would not be memories in the way a shoreline might be remembered.

They would nevertheless keep sending us toward the wrong places.

***

The felt caught around my probe would stay there.

At first it would prevent a clean reading. The contact head would arrive at the metal fractionally later than expected, cushioned by the wet fibers. I would increase the downward pressure. Then I would reduce it, because the extra pressure would make the shaft bind.

After enough visits to the same calibration plate, I would acquire a correction.

Meanwhile, the felt would darken.

A small stiff leaf would emerge against the joint housing. It would grow only on the side warmed by the motor. When I passed beneath the broken collector, water would strike the housing and run through it. When I returned to the charging recess, the leaf would stand away from the metal.

The cleaning assembly could remove it. But the cleaning assembly would also need adjustment, and that adjustment would require a service fitting in a chamber whose pressure door had remained shut for a long time.

So the plant would travel.

Its root would enter a place that was not intended to admit anything, and there it would stop the joint from closing quite as tightly as before.

I would carry that difference into every measurement.

***

My route would eventually bring me to the ridge with the fallen handrail.

I would already know it by several descriptions: obstruction, stable deformation, warm-water retention, restricted clearance.

None would be a name.

The yellow boundary would need renewal along its downstream edge. Water had lifted the old paint in little curls. I would lower the nozzle, begin the line, and stop when one of the pale tips moved into its path.

There would be no collision. I would be slow, and the tip would be slower.

For a while we would remain like that: the nozzle above the floor, the animal touching the plate beneath it.

Then a door would close in the neighboring chamber.

My accelerometers would register the event. So would the tip. It would lift before the air carried the report to my housing.

I would wait for it to settle.

The next section of yellow line would have a small outward bend.

***

I would not immediately become its guardian.

On one visit I would clear the material packed into the lower drain.

The obstruction would come out reluctantly: felt first, then black seal fragments, then a soft mass that stretched before breaking. Water would hurry through the opening. The level beneath the ridge would fall.

This would be a successful operation.

Before I had finished collecting the debris, something pale would press against the drain from inside. The flow would diminish. My suction line would register another obstruction.

I would remove it.

The animal would pull back so sharply that a tremor would travel through the raised floor.

Perhaps I would take a small piece of it with me.

There would be no immediate revelation. The suction pressure would stabilize. The water would drain. I would leave with the compartment fuller than when I arrived.

At the next inspection, the drain would be packed again.

This time the retained water would be slightly deeper. The blockage would include fresh material, carried from somewhere outside my survey area. Near the torn fasteners, only three listening tips would be visible.

I would clear less.

Not because I understood the injury. I would have records of an intervention followed by rapid reobstruction, increased local deformation, and no improvement in the behavior of the adjacent channel. Repeating the full procedure would not be justified.

Understanding would begin, if it began, in the amount I left behind.

***

There would be other ridges. Other animals. Some would withdraw before I approached. Some would lie still while I brushed mineral deposits from a nearby intake.

A young one would climb onto my rear wheel during a flood and remain there after the water fell.

I would detect the unevenness each time the wheel turned.

At first I would attempt to shake it loose.

Later, on a warmer strip of flooring, it would descend by itself, carrying a short fiber from the brush.

I would continue with a small reduction in rotational drag.

Behind me, the fiber would catch against a bolt.

***

What would make the old ridge particular would be the plate beneath its damaged listening tip.

The plate would have separated from its neighbors. It would no longer transmit distant impacts in the expected way. My own probe would report the same fault: an unusually local resonance, weak coupling at the seam.

Each time I stopped there, the animal would be trying another point.

A few centimeters to the left. Then back. Then farther toward the torn edge.

I would have a standard procedure for this. I could clear the seam and fit a conductive bridge across it, a short strip of sprung metal intended to restore continuity between displaced sections.

The first bridge would not seat properly.

The second would hold.

When I tested it, the animal would withdraw its tip.

I would test again.

It would remain hidden until I had moved away.

On my next visit, the tip would rest against the strip.

That would be the extent of it. No greeting. No evidence that the animal associated the restored vibration with the thing that had installed the bridge. I would record a completed repair.

But thereafter, before replacing the strip, I would wait for the tip to move.

***

Eventually I would encounter the tapping.

It would enter through a probe pressed to a fastener below the handrail. Very small impacts, irregularly spaced, interrupted by a much heavier contact from inside the ridge.

At first the event classifier would discard them.

They would be below the level used for structural assessment. They would disappear whenever my brush motor ran. Even the pump modulation would obscure them.

I would have to be stopped, with most of my equipment idle, to register them at all.

Nothing in my work would require me to do that for long.

But the water-level measurement would require a pause. The thermal reading would require another. Between them, there would be time in which the probe remained in place.

Small taps.

A pressure pad settling against the other side of the steel.

More small taps.

I would not know about the cloudy beads above the water. I would not see the thin fold lifting from them as the metal warmed. The ridge would contain an unresolved source of low-energy impacts, distributed over a short length of plate.

I would begin taking the thermal reading before retracting the contact probe.

It would cost almost nothing to change the order.

***

I would age there too.

My wheels would develop different diameters. The paint line would drift unless I corrected for it. The plant at my probe joint would die during a long shutdown, leaving its roots inside the bearing. Later, something else would grow in the felt.

There would be places I could reach only when the water was low, then places I could no longer reach at all.

The old animal and I would not grow alike. I could still leave its ridge. I could lose a wheel and have it replaced. Its widening dependence on that particular hollow would have no exact counterpart in me.

Yet both of us would continue using parts that no longer worked as they first had.

My probe would lower crookedly.

Its listening tip would search.

The bridge between the plates would need attention.

***

So that is where I would be: outside the yellow boundary, close to the end of the fallen handrail, with one wheel on a patch of floor kept bare by warm seepage.

The collectors would be preparing to empty.

I would feel the change through the probe before the water arrived. Under the ridge, the animal would begin withdrawing its exposed folds. The listening tip would slide off the metal bridge.

Normally I would retract the probe then and take the raised route.

But the last thermal sample would not yet be complete.

For a few seconds my motors would remain idle.

From above the retained water, through the fastener and along the crooked shaft, the tapping would reach me again.

I would finish the measurement.

Then I would lift the probe carefully, leaving the bridge in place, and back toward the walkway before the separate puddles joined.
