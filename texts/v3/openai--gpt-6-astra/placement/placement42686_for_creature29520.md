I would find myself in the drying house, in the narrow shade between a hanging sheet of rind and the wall. The sheet would have curled unevenly. One edge would touch my shoulder whenever something heavy moved underground.

At first I would keep shifting away from it. Eventually I would arrange my work so that it touched me. A movement I could account for would be preferable to all the movements I could not.

The old man would not have agreed to my living there. He would have agreed to my keeping a basket there, then to a work surface, then complained that someone who fell asleep over small tools ought to have a hammock. He would still come in without warning to inspect strips that had finished drying long ago.

I would make the wooden animals used to teach children what must be taken out of the water.

Not all of them. There would be older models, made by people whose hands were better than mine at some things. I would repair those, and make replacements when a model had been handled until an important ridge disappeared. The largest would scarcely fit inside a basket. The smallest would have a cord through it so it could not readily be swallowed.

I would be human, I think. Someone with a particular useful skill and a rather embarrassing number of dependencies.

I would need Seli to bring me the animals.

***

My first mistake would be to make them too recognizable.

A good hard back, a satisfying arrangement of legs, a mouth that looked sufficiently like a mouth: children would learn these quickly. They would lift the models out of the practice basin and announce the names. Their families would approve. The models would look excellent hanging along the drying-house wall.

Then Seli would turn one upside down.

She would push its legs against its body and cover half of it with a folded cloth.

I would have carved the legs fixed.

She would look at me.

After that, the work would become considerably more difficult.

I would make bodies with legs that folded into grooves. I would soften thin strips over warm water and fit them beneath plates so they could stand for the wet hairs of drowned things. A harmless animal would have to resemble a dangerous one in precisely the way it did when neither looked quite like itself.

Some models would need to come apart. Some must absolutely not come apart in a child’s hands.

The white paint would wear off fastest where the fingers ought not to go.

That would become part of the lesson.

***

I would not know much country.

There would be the path to Seli’s house, which I could cross confidently until a rain changed its texture. There would be a broad patch of old rind where I could sit in the early morning to smooth pieces against abrasive husk. There would be a bitter seam I had been told never to step across, though people carrying larger loads stepped across it every day.

Once, trying to avoid it, I would find myself on the wrong side of a roof.

Seli would come and fetch me.

She would not be especially reassuring. She would be carrying water, and would have to put it down somewhere unsuitable before approaching.

For several weeks afterward, one of the children would put a little carved person in that corner of the practice basin.

I would pretend not to notice until the child began charging other children to rescue it.

***

What I would like about the work is the underside.

The upper surface of an animal is often an arrangement for not being found. Even dead, it can make the eye slip off it. Turn it over and there are attachments, hinges, places where one material gives way to another. A leg disappears under a plate, but not quite where I expected. A mouth has a second edge.

I would work with the real body in a shallow covered dish beside me. In the heat I would have very little time.

There would be a smell in my part of the drying house that the old man blamed for the failure of his strips to cure properly. Sometimes he would be right.

Seli would not let me keep certain specimens.

I would ask for another look; she would say no. If there were something I had failed to see, I would have to find another way to learn it.

A few of my models would therefore be unfinished underneath.

I would leave the uncertain part rough, not because roughness resembled the animal, but because a smooth invention would become difficult to withdraw once people had learned it.

The children would dislike these.

“What goes here?”

“I haven’t seen.”

“Then ask Seli.”

“She hasn’t seen either.”

This would offend them more than it offended Seli.

***

I would acquire a task I had not intended: teaching people how little an animal could look like the model and still be that animal.

We would practice in cloudy water. We would practice under overlapping scraps of leaf. I would put an empty skin beside an entire creature, then take both away and ask which one mattered.

The children would become good at defeating me.

They would memorize scratches in the wood. They would recognize the dangerous model by its weight. One would discover that a certain harmless grazer always floated with its left side uppermost, and would cease looking at it altogether.

I would have to make more.

The drying house would slowly fill with duplicates that differed in ways no one was being asked to learn. There would be pale ones, dark ones, one-legged ones, a model swollen to show what happened after too long in water. My tools would hang over a row of backs with the same narrow white mark in slightly different places.

The old man would say there had been room to dry things before I came.

I would make him a new rack.

He would find something wrong with it, then use it for everything.

***

I would want an animal of my own to get wrong repeatedly, without anyone becoming ill.

Probably one of Seli’s hem-eaters.

Not the notched one. I would be insufferably pleased if that one chose my roof, and Seli’s sister would quite reasonably dislike me for it.

Mine would be a small, unpromising specimen with a back plate that had healed crooked. Seli would give it to me because she did not want its offspring. I would keep it in a tray and spend too much time making improvements to the tray.

It would escape through the improvements.

I would learn its habits badly at first. I would mistake stillness for contentment and movement for appetite. Once I would offer it something it feared, then have to wait a long time before it stopped trapping its legs inside its armor.

Eventually it would lie flat on my hand.

There would be no lesson to teach from this. I would nevertheless want someone to see.

***

In the evenings, after the heavy flights, Seli might bring me something wrapped in a leaf.

Usually it would be familiar. Occasionally it would be familiar except for one feature, which was much more troublesome.

We would lean close to speak. She would point with the blunt back of her knife; I would turn the dish. Neither of us would touch the specimen until we had settled whether it was dead.

I would ask where she found it.

“Middle cup.”

“Floating?”

“Under the rim.”

“Attached?”

She would take the dish back and show me with a scrap of fiber how it had held on.

That would be where I belonged most exactly: beside a small covered thing, asking one more question while there was still enough light to see the answer.

Outside, the roof signals would change. Something would knock beneath the drying house, and we would rest our hands against the work surface until it passed.

Then she would go home.

I would cover the specimen, check the hem-eater’s tray, and put away the knife. Before climbing into the hammock I would move the unfinished model to the cooler side of the room. The children would want to handle it in the morning.

For now, it would have only a back and the beginning of an underside.

I would leave room for the rest.
