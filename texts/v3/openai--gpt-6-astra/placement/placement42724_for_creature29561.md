I would be farther inside the fallen trunk than her tongue can reach.

Not very far. A bend in a passage, a thickness of wood that still resists her claw. The difference between where she feeds and where I remain would scarcely admit the end of a finger.

There would be no chamber with a view of the opening. Whatever light entered would stop against the first rough turn. I would know the outside by the change in the air.

Until then, the passage would have been close and damp, its surfaces carrying the touch of many bodies. I would move with my sides brushing it. At a narrowing, I would meet another head, touch it, turn partly against the wall, and let a swollen abdomen pass. Behind me another body would arrive. We would make room without ever having much room.

I think I would belong there: not to the sound timber, but to the thin, occupied interval between timber and earth.

I would be a worker termite, pale enough that the food inside me darkens my body. My head would be harder than the rest of me. My jaws would be useful for carrying and breaking things; they would not make me formidable. A grain of clay would require a particular grip. A loose fiber could obstruct a passage until someone cut it or shifted it lengthwise.

The forest would reach me in these sizes.

A root would be a surface followed for a long time. A crack would be somewhere the damp went. The length of the fallen tree would not exist for me as an outline, though I might travel through much of it. There would be familiar contacts, branches taken or not taken, places where the traffic thickened. There would be wood I could work and wood I could only move around.

When her claw came through, the first event would be movement in what should have held still.

The wall would jar against my feet. A fragment would strike the passage behind me. Somewhere ahead, bodies would begin to hurry, and the impacts of heads against the wood would pass through the structure around us.

I would turn.

For a moment the route back would be blocked by others turning too. Antennae would touch my face and slide past. One body would force itself beneath mine. The passage would hold us close enough that no movement belonged entirely to the one making it.

Then space would open ahead.

With it would come the drying air.

I would have no image of the animal outside: no overlapping scales, no damaged foot, no small one gripping the base of her tail. Her closed eye would be beyond anything I could encounter. Even the mouth taking bodies from the passage would arrive only in parts—a wet contact, a sudden vacancy, the disappearance of something my antenna had just touched.

I might approach and be taken.

There would be nothing in being the one telling this that protected me.

But if I remained around the bend, if other bodies stood between the breach and me, I would eventually move forward again. By then her mouth might be gone. The broken edge would stay broken, and air would continue to pass through it.

A soldier would occupy part of the opening, its dark head too large for the work I did. I would touch its flank on my way past. Beyond it there would be a space no antenna could cross, where the passage had ceased to have a wall.

I would keep my feet on the remaining surface.

The first material I carried might be a particle loosened by the claw itself. I would work it with my mouth and press it against the ragged edge. It would adhere. Beside it, another worker would place something darker. For a while our additions would be so small that the hole scarcely changed.

I would leave to gather more.

There would be no need to hold the shape of the finished passage in mind. On returning, I would find an edge that had advanced since I left. I would touch it, move along it, find a place for what I carried. Sometimes another worker would already be there. Sometimes a piece would shift and need pressing back.

The new wall would grow unevenly.

Outside, she would be climbing, or drinking, or passing under foliage that brushed the dust from her back. Her young one might be awake. I would receive none of this. Her departure would become the absence of further blows.

The heat would remain.

That would matter more as the opening narrowed. Damp would begin to hold in the passage again. Bodies would hesitate less at the repaired section. A worker carrying food would push between us, following the route as soon as there was enough wall to follow.

I would step aside.

Later I might pass over the repair without stopping. My feet would find its slight bulge. My antennae would examine a join where the new material met the old, then reach toward the body ahead.

Somewhere deeper in the trunk, a fiber would have softened enough to bite through. I would set my jaws against it, with my hind feet braced on the close wall.
