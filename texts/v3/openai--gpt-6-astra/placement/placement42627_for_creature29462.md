I would find the seven nests in the shallow saddle, and spend too long deciding which were occupied.

One would have a doorway almost closed by a fallen piece of roof. There would be a dark space beneath it, and in that space something with the color and finish of the surrounding wall. I would take it for another piece of building material until it drew back slightly and the darkness widened.

That is where I think I would be: just inside, with my forehead under the broken edge.

At first I imagine myself small enough to pass through the narrow chambers. I follow a turn out of the light, finding the wall with one shoulder. There is a shelf overhead, polished underneath by backs. The passage smells more strongly of salt as it descends. Somewhere ahead, bodies are moving, and their feet reach me through the floor.

But the nest would not be a place I explored. I would already have worn part of it smooth.

My body would fit a low opening between two chambers. One chamber lies toward the outside and empties quickly after sunrise. The other holds its moisture longer. Across the opening, I can rest with my feet drawn close and my forehead against a shallow recess. Air passes over my back and through a small gap beside my flank.

I have enlarged that gap myself, though I also keep trying to close it.

When dry air touches the wet band around my mouth, I press against the nearest edge and leave a little material there. Later, another body needs to pass. It pushes between me and the wall, wearing the fresh bead away. Over many such repairs and interruptions, the opening has acquired its shape.

There is a ridge in it that catches one of my hind feet. Each time I settle, I lift that foot a little higher.

***

I would be an adult builder, old enough for the hardened surface of my forehead to have a smooth central patch. One digging limb would be shorter than the other. It was injured before the doorway narrowed, when there was still room for two bodies to pass without either turning.

I can move through most of the nest. In the upper passages, though, I lose my purchase where the floor steepens. My shorter limb searches while the others hold. An animal behind me presses forward. Eventually I find an edge.

I go up there less often now.

The low opening is a good place for me. Food comes past it after rain, carried inward in damp, irregular pieces. There are soft fragments left along the floor. Other builders bring warmth against my side. When the outer chamber becomes too dry, I need only draw backward by half my length.

I would defend this position.

Another adult sometimes approaches from the moist side and sets its forehead beneath my abdomen. It has done this often enough that I begin to brace at the first touch. We remain wedged together, its feet pushing, mine spread against the floor. The air gap closes. Animals collect behind us.

Once, it dislodges me.

For the rest of that warm interval I lie in a larger chamber, touching the wall on only one side. Every passing body makes me contract. I keep turning toward a current of air that reaches first one flank, then the other.

When I return, the opening is unoccupied. I press into it so firmly that a flake comes off the roof.

I eat the flake. It softens slowly against my mouth.

***

I would know very little of the plateau.

Light enters in particular places. Rain announces itself along the floor before it reaches my body. The wall sometimes cools from the outside inward, and sometimes a hundred returning animals warm it long after sunset.

The southern edge, the clouds below it, the broad oval that no animal crosses: none of these would have a place in me. Even the next nest would be beyond almost everything I had learned.

Yet I would distinguish changes too small for the visitor at the doorway.

A draft has begun to reach the opening from below. It brings the smell of an unused chamber. I lower my head, turn, and press my mouth into the corner. The surface there is rough. I leave a bead, move sideways, leave another.

This takes much of the evening.

Behind me, young builders climb over one another in a pocket in the wall. Their weight comes and goes against my back. One finds the gap beside my flank and pushes its head through. I shift away from it, exposing the repair before it has stiffened.

The youngster scrapes some of it off as it passes.

For a while I remain where I am. Then the dry current reaches my mouth again, and I turn back to the corner.

***

There would be intervals when I leave.

After a long rain, dampness fills the passages so completely that my usual place no longer has a distinct edge. Bodies move through it in both directions. I move with them, out through the broken doorway and onto the skirt of old nest material.

Outside, I am scarcely higher than the debris.

The ground holds too many separate contacts. Loose grains roll under my feet. A plant touches my back and I stop beneath it, pressing down until the leaf slides free. Farther on, the padded rim of a grazer yields against my forehead. I follow it into shade.

I feed there. For some time, that is enough.

When the grazer begins to lift, the change passes through the folds beneath its shell. I withdraw. Light touches the wet band of my mouth, and I turn toward the nest, whose lower wall still holds the rain.

Finding the doorway takes longer than it should. I encounter a sealed entrance first and work my head against it.

Eventually I return to the low opening. A small body is resting there. It gives way under my pressure and disappears into the inner chamber.

My short foot catches on the ridge.

I lift it, settle, and leave a narrow passage for the air.
