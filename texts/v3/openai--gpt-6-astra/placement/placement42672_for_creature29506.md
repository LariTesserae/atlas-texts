I would have a room beside the ramp, but not beside the daylight.

There would be a turn between my door and the upper door, and a cupboard belonging to someone else in the turn. Deliveries would strike the cupboard often enough that its owner had nailed a strip of leather to the corner. From my bed I could hear the trolley brake engage. I would learn to distinguish a load of grain from a load of metal, although I would be wrong whenever the grain merchant brought his daughter down sitting on the sacks.

My window would open into the space behind the cupboard.

I would have taken the room because of the light advertised, and stayed because the floor was nearly level.

At first I imagine myself keeping records: the dates on which the stone warmed, the amount a sill rose, the appearance of roots in a previously dry joint. But there are already people who know these things, and they would have little reason to climb the ramp to tell me. A book could remain very orderly while the room containing it became impossible to enter.

I think there would be a bench instead.

Not a large one. Its back edge would be cut around a pipe, and one leg would stand in a shallow iron shoe to keep the warmth from splitting it. Above it, drawers with paper labels. Below it, a box of mechanisms I had accepted in payment and regretted accepting: a counter with no lever, a pressure dial whose needle had been replaced with a thorn, three locks for which no one could remember the doors.

I would repair measuring instruments.

Balances, mostly. Workshop gauges. The little indicators on covered tanks that allow someone to know how much water remains without lifting the lid. Things brought down from above because a new one costs more than waiting.

There would be a particular pleasure in bringing two marks into agreement. I would have to be watched for this. It is possible to make an instrument agree with itself beautifully and still have it wrong.

Edan would send Bel to me with a dial and instructions not to explain what he thought was the matter.

“Test it first,” she would say.

“I know.”

“He said you’d say that.”

She would remain in the doorway, obscuring most of the available light.

***

My hands would be ordinary hands, with a slight tremor when I had forgotten to eat. I would brace the heel of one against the other for delicate work. The thumbnail on my left hand would have a notch worn into it from holding wire.

I would not be old when I arrived. After some years, children would assume I always had been.

I would own a good pair of shoes for going above and dislike the time required to change into them. Sometimes I would go up in my sandals and spend the whole errand expecting someone to notice. No one would.

There would be someone at a stall above who knew me only as a customer who examined the bottoms of cups before buying them. Below, this same caution would be familiar enough to invite jokes. I would be given cracked cups on purpose, carefully wrapped.

I would laugh the first few times.

My least attractive habit would concern borrowed tools. I would say that people were welcome to borrow them, and mean it while the tools were leaving. By evening I would begin remembering the tools’ good qualities. By the following afternoon I would have found a reason to visit the borrower.

Eventually Rus would tell me to stop lending things I could not bear to have absent.

After that I would keep two screwdrivers of each useful size. Everyone would know which ones could be borrowed.

***

The work I did for the district would gradually become different from the work on my sign.

Someone would ask whether a shelf was lower than last month. Someone would bring me a lid that fitted its tank in the morning but not at night. Sera would ask for a way to tell whether the two sides of a doorway were still moving apart.

I would make her a simple witness: two small metal tongues fixed separately to the frame, one passing over the other. A fine line scratched on each. No gearing, no needle. The lines would show a change without pretending to explain it.

She would bring the first one back.

“Too sharp.”

I would round the corners.

“The other side too. People carry bedding through doors.”

After that I would take my own bedding through the doorway before giving her the next one.

A few of these witnesses would appear around the district. Not everywhere. Nobody would want their kitchen made into a collection of warnings. I would keep the corresponding measurements on cards in a drawer, along with notes that grew less concise as I learned what mattered.

*Frame opened slightly.*

Then:

*Frame opened after tank was filled.*

Later:

*Measured with tank half full. Upper hinge recently replaced. Do not compare with spring reading.*

There would be cards I disliked because they contained no useful conclusion. I would keep them.

Once, a witness would show a sudden movement beside the laundry. We would clear a passage, fetch Sera, disturb both sisters, and stop the rollers. The culprit would be one of my fastenings, working loose in an old brick.

Nothing else would move.

I would replace the fixing and pay for the lost work. For a long time afterward, whenever I entered the laundry, the younger sister would ask whether she ought to evacuate.

This would not prevent her from bringing me her scissors.

***

There would be a patch of exposed stone under my bench.

I would dislike it at first. It would keep the room warm after I wanted warmth finished for the day, and small things dropped onto it would vanish among the folds. A screw could remain visible and still be irretrievable.

I would make a hooked tool for recovering screws. Then a narrower one.

Eventually a fern would establish itself in the crease nearest the pipe. I would leave it because removing it risked pushing grit into a box of parts. Its first frond would open against the underside of the bench. The second would find the gap beside my knee.

I would take an unreasonable interest in it.

Not enough to call Rus. Enough to move the box.

When it began to withdraw from the pipe, I would check the wall and find nothing. I would check my gauges. Nothing. For three evenings I would sit with my work neglected, listening.

Then someone would mention that the pipe had been carrying warmer water since a valve was replaced.

I would feel relieved, and also a little cheated. I would be ashamed of the second feeling without being able to remove it.

The fern would continue growing elsewhere.

***

The younger sister’s coat would eventually arrive at my door.

Not a coat for mending. A coat in pieces, folded inside a sheet.

She would have looked through my window one day while the cupboard was being moved and seen the bench.

“If you took everything off,” she would have said.

“There’s a pipe.”

“I can work around a pipe.”

I would explain that the bench was not as deep as it appeared. She would ask for its measurements. This would be difficult to argue with.

We would agree on an afternoon.

I would spend the morning moving things into shallow trays, preserving the order of dismantled mechanisms. There would be more things than I remembered. By noon the bed would be covered. By one, the floor. I would put a small balance on the windowsill behind the cupboard and then be unable to stop thinking about it.

She would arrive late.

The coat would fit if laid diagonally, with one sleeve supported on a board across my open parts drawer. She would ask for chalk. I would offer a fine marking point and receive a look.

While she worked, I would stand in the doorway. There would be nowhere else for me without going out.

I would see, for the first time, how broad the bench was when it held one thing instead of many small ones.

She would not finish that afternoon.

This would cause an argument.

We would settle it by leaving the coat in place overnight and moving the trays off my bed onto hers. Carrying them would take four trips. On the last, I would encounter Sera at the shared stove and discover that the sisters’ room contained a window I had never seen from outside.

Through it, above a tank, part of the court tree would be visible.

***

That is where I find myself most clearly: not at the bench with an instrument finally reading true, but halfway down a passage with a tray held level in both hands.

Someone behind me wants to get past. Ahead, a leaf has caught beneath the tray’s rim. I cannot lift the tray because of the beam. I cannot lower it because the loose parts will touch the hot pipe.

I have to ask for help.

The person behind me reaches around, frees the leaf, and holds it aside. For a moment we are close enough that I can feel the warmth of their wrist against my neck.

Then I go on to the sisters’ room, where a place has been cleared, though not quite enough of one, and we decide what can be moved.
