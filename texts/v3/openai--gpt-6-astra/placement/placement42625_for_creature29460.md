I would be somewhere along the descent between two basins, where a grove’s outer roots cross beneath a shelf of stone.

Not in the deepest pool. That would belong to an animal that had learned its banks over many winters, and I would pass it with my tubes lowered. My resting place would be narrower: a pocket behind the roots, long enough only if I doubled back on myself. One flank would lie in water and the other against the underside of the bank. When rain increased the flow, I would have to move.

Above me, a tree would have an uneven cycle.

I would know it first as the place where I could sleep.

Its lowest chamber would fill, hold, and discharge almost regularly. Almost. Every few cycles there would be a pause long enough for my body to begin anticipating the next movement. My belly would tighten. Nothing would happen. Then the valve would seat, the root against my mouth would turn firm, and I would loosen with it.

For a long time I would take this irregularity to be something the tree did.

Only after travelling would I recognise it as a fault.

***

I think I would be an adult, but not yet a large one. Large enough to cross a passage without being casually covered by another body; small enough to use the wet cleft behind an escarpment where the older animals had to go over the top.

There would be a scar beneath one forelimb. Not an impressive wound. A patch where I had attached myself to a sheet of stone that was no longer attached to the cliff.

I would have held perfectly.

The stone would have fallen with me.

Afterward, I would test exposed surfaces differently. Press, release, press farther along. There would be places where I spent a long time doing this although the rock was sound, and places where the smell of freshly broken mineral made me turn aside.

My routes would accumulate such bends.

From above, someone might suppose I was following food or shelter. Usually I would be. But one detour would persist around a hollow that had been dry for several years. Another would take me past a tree whose crown was gone, because its remaining roots still carried the familiar movement of a grove beyond the ridge.

I would put my mouth to that root sometimes.

There would be very little to eat.

***

The uneven tree beside my bank would keep drawing me back.

One evening I would climb it instead of settling underneath.

I would test the bark with the tubes behind my head, then with the length of my underside. Higher up, the delay would resolve into smaller movements: pressure building against something, a faint flutter, a release too narrow and too sudden.

An old vent would admit the tips of my folded appendages.

I would work there through the first part of the night. A mineral crust would come away in flakes. Beneath it would be a soft obstruction, good to eat, threaded through with harder fibres. I would pull until the fibres tightened. Change my hold. Pull again.

The passage would open.

Water would press past my mouth, warm enough for me to register it against the cold bark. The lower chamber would fill without its accustomed hesitation.

For several cycles the tree would move evenly beneath me.

I would remain attached until morning.

Then the chamber above would fail to hold.

The material I had removed would have been caught around a tear. Some of it had obstructed the flow; some had helped close the damaged partition. Now water would escape backward whenever pressure rose. I would feel the wrongness running farther up the trunk than my appendages could reach.

I would probe the opening again. There would be nothing within reach that I could pull into a better position.

By daylight the vent would begin to contract around me.

I would withdraw.

For days afterward, I would return and test the trunk. The tree would close the wound with a thick, wet lip. Once I would start to scrape it away, then stop when the pressure beneath my belly changed.

Its crown would lose leaves. Not all of them.

Other roots would take part of the load.

My bank would become colder.

***

I would still live there.

That seems important to the shape of the animal I would be: the damaged tree above, the same sheltered water below, no clean departure into another part of the country.

There would be roots I could feed from farther along the shelf. In the colder months I would spend more time near them. But when the afternoon rain reached the basin, I would come back to feel what happened in the injured trunk.

New tissue would alter the sequence. The lower chamber would no longer empty completely. A smaller passage would take water around the damaged part. Its movements would be weak at first, difficult to distinguish from the activity of neighbouring trees.

I would learn them.

Eventually I would sleep through the old delay and wake at a new one.

***

I would not be solitary in quite the way I first appear.

Others would pass beneath the shelf. I would know some by the substances in their flank water, some by injuries my forefoot encountered while we held each other. One would always arrive with fine red sediment from a basin I had never reached.

I would follow that animal once.

For two nights we would travel along the edge of the forest, stopping at different roots. At narrow places it would wait while I came through. On the third night, where the route crossed a broad exposed back of stone, rain would change to ice.

The other animal would continue.

I would flatten beneath a shrub and draw my filaments into their glaze. By morning its passage would have left only a darker strip through the growth. I would return to my basin slowly, carrying red grit in the folds beside my mouth.

Perhaps I would try again another year.

Perhaps that would remain the farthest edge of my routes.

***

Later, a young animal would begin following me.

Not necessarily my own. It would arrive from the grove above the pool, pale along the spine, repeatedly fastening a forefoot to my flank when I stopped. At first I would leave it behind whenever I crossed deeper water. It would find me again at the next bank.

I would take it to the uneven tree.

By then the old vent would have narrowed to a dark slit. The bark around it would be swollen and hard, with a little vegetation rooted in the upper crease.

I would settle below the opening. The young animal would climb over my neck and test it with its mouth.

I would contract against the trunk.

It would stop.

We would remain there while the lowest chamber filled. The young one would shift impatiently. Its pads would lift and reattach; once, it would put an appendage into the slit.

I would contract again.

Then the small bypass would begin taking water. A tremor would run across the scar, scarcely strong enough to reach the far side of the trunk.

The young animal’s belly would flatten.

I would feel it become still.

For the rest of that cycle, neither of us would open the tree.

***

That is where I find myself most clearly.

Not spread across the whole root network, receiving the weather from every basin. Only this much body, against this much bark. One side chilled by the rain. A forelimb growing tired, needing to be moved without disturbing the younger animal.

Below us, water would gather at the lip of my bank. Soon it would rise into the place where I slept, and I would have to find another hollow for the night.

But the chamber would not yet have emptied.

I would hold where I was until it did.
