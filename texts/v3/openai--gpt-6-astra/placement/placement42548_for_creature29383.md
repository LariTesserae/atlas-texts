I would be a little downstream of the stone, where the water coming out from under it loses its cloud of silt.

There would be no visible current around me. A shred of leaf would lie against my wrist, held there lightly enough that I could lift my foot without noticing its weight. When I put the foot down again, the leaf would return. Farther out, the pale bottom would be bare.

I would keep my head in that narrow passage of water. It would bring the taste of whatever had happened beneath the slab: disturbed sediment, another body, the faint difference between water that had stood in darkness and water that had passed through the open pool.

At first that might be most of me—a head resting where something arrives.

Then there would be the inconvenience of the rest of my body. My belly would have settled into the clay. One hind leg would be folded too tightly beneath me. To move it, I would have to lift my flank, letting brighter, warmer water into the space I had occupied. I would do this reluctantly, stretching the leg behind me until the toes opened.

All my toes would be there. I would be smaller than the animal with the notched foot, with a tail slightly bent near its end. In a deep enough pool, that bend would turn me toward one side unless I corrected with a foot.

I would know several shelters, none entirely good.

There would be a root mat with two entrances, useful when a larger animal came in through one. There would be a burrow whose roof shed grains onto my back whenever something walked above it. And there would be a place under the downstream lip of the broad slab, separate from the chamber with the eggs. A seam of stone would divide us. Water could pass over the seam when the pool was full; I could not.

That would be where I spent most days.

The roof would touch me before I had quite finished breathing in. Along one side, a root would have grown into the opening and thickened there. I could get past it with my legs folded, but could not turn around inside. Each evening I would come out backward. Each morning I would arrange myself outside, then push in tail first.

There would be a particular point at which the bent part of my tail caught against the root. I would stop, shift my weight onto my left feet, and pass it.

I would have done this often enough that I no longer paused for long.

***

The animal beneath the larger part of the slab would be familiar to me.

Not its outline. In the shelter I would seldom see it whole. I would know the displacement when it entered, the scrape of its feet, its smell arriving more strongly after it fed. Sometimes its throat would pulse against the bottom. The movement would reach my jaw through the stone and the thin water.

When that happened, I would draw back.

At other times I could remain beside it beneath the sedge roots. Our skins would touch, and neither of us would have to spend anything on moving. A small animal passing between our forefeet might be taken by either mouth.

I would not know about the eggs as a ceiling of separate beads. Once, searching along the seam, I might put my jaw against a strand. The other animal would push its head hard into mine. I would retreat until the root pressed against my flank.

Afterward, the water would continue to come.

It would arrive in small exchanges, stronger and then weaker. Lying downstream, I would receive each one across the soft fingers beneath my jaw. Sometimes a larva would come with it. Sometimes there would be nothing to eat, only a little suspended clay settling on my forefeet.

***

Toward evening I would leave the stone.

Outside, my skin would register the open air before my eyes made anything useful of it. The upper surface of my body would cool slightly. Beneath me the clay would still be warm.

I would feed near the place where a leaf had softened without yet breaking apart. There would be food under its edge, and more beneath the portion pressed into the mud. I could not lift the whole thing. I would work along it, drawing in what loosened, letting sediment pass out again.

A shadow would cross.

I would flatten with my mouth still full.

For a while, the leaf and I would lie beside each other. My throat would want to move more than I allowed it. One of the fingers beneath my jaw would be folded backward under my chin.

Nothing would strike.

Eventually a foot would press down some distance away. Through the mud I would feel the weight, then its release. Only after another long interval would I swallow.

When I moved again, I would go toward the roots rather than continue along the exposed leaf.

There would still be food left there.

***

What held me to this part of the basin would be very small.

A little room above the water. A roof I could reach with my back. The root that impeded my entrance also kept a larger body from following easily. Beneath the slab, my skin stayed wet through the bright hours.

Yet the place would change while I lay in it.

For several days the water might withdraw until its edge was beneath my chest. Then I would have to crawl out farther to feed. The sediment at the entrance would grow thick, taking the shape of my feet and keeping it. Behind me, the tail would rest on damp clay instead of in water.

One evening I would come back and find the entrance shallower than I had left it.

I would push backward. My tail would catch too soon. I would shift left, as usual, but still be unable to pass.

For a time I would remain there, front half in the pool, back half under the roof.

Then I would come out.

I would put my head into the opening and feel along the bottom. Soft clay, a piece of leaf, a small hard object I could not draw into my mouth. I would turn again and rake with one hind foot. A little mud would slide out beneath my belly.

From beyond the seam, the larger animal would lift its body. Water would seep through, briefly making the narrow entrance easier.

I would push backward into it, toes spread against the bottom, until the root passed along my side and my face was all that remained outside.
