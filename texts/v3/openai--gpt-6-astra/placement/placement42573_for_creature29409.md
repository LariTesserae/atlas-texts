At first I would take the shelter to be a place left for me.

The stool, the clearance for knees, the ledger within reach: after so much difficult accommodation, these would look like kindness. I would crouch under the corrugated roof and begin to lower myself before seeing the toes.

Then I would have to stay crouched. There would be nowhere to put the hand I had already extended.

That is where I would begin to recognize myself—not in the occupant’s place, but in the difficulty of withdrawing without touching it.

The toes beneath the ledger are mine.

There are seven of them. Their joints bend in alternating directions, allowing them to follow the turned edge of the stool and grip its underside. Through each toe runs a filament the color of a pencil line. They look delicate, but the wood has yielded around them.

Most of me lies between the ledger’s back cover and its final page.

This is not as little room as it sounds. The book has swollen. Someone looking at it from the shelter entrance would attribute its thickness to salt, to warped paper, to the long neglect of an underground room. But the pages near the back have separated into chambers. Their edges meet; farther in, each sheet arches over the one below. I occupy those shallow vaults.

I would not know how many pages there are. Parts of me lie beyond any place I can feel.

At first I might call myself a reader. There is writing pressed against almost every surface of my body. The earlier entries reach me backward through the paper: dates, decimal points, the small firm circles of zeros. Where the writer pressed hardest, I have shallow grooves. The numbers are much less distinct than the force used to make them.

I know where a pencil broke.

I know which entries were made standing up.

There is a place, several sheets below the surface, where the writer rested the heel of a hand for a long time before recording anything. Beneath that place my body is broad and dark. Fine vessels gather there from the surrounding tissue. It is the fullest part of me.

So perhaps reading is not what I do.

My mouth is under the last ditto mark.

It has no jaws that could take hold of paper. It is a long, soft opening with a raised rim, fitted against the underside of the sheet. Above it, two short strokes lean together. The second stroke ends in a tiny accumulation of graphite where the pencil paused before being lifted.

The paper has not been pierced. The graphite has not diminished.

Nevertheless, something reaches me there.

Along the length of my mouth, small muscular pockets are filled to different depths. One is tight enough to be almost transparent. Another has only begun to draw inward. I cannot tell you that anything passes from one to the next. I can tell you that none is empty.

The writer’s hand is gone. Its pressure is here.

I would hesitate before calling this nourishment. Then I would feel the firm attachment of my toes, the crowded health of the tissues between the pages, and have no better word.

One eye lies outside the book.

You might mistake it for a clear blister on the lower cover. It faces sideways, toward the space where a seated person’s wrist would hang. From there I can see a little of the compressor through the gap beside the stool. A concrete foot. The bottom of the inspection window. Occasionally a visitor’s boot, enormous and imprecisely placed.

I cannot see the pale animal behind the cylinder. But the book rests at an angle, and through its spine I receive the faint strain of the stool, the floor, the compressor’s mounting. Somewhere in that uninterrupted load is the animal’s open shoulder.

It does not feel nearer to release than I do.

This would be the part I found hardest to understand about myself: I am well supplied, and I am reaching.

My toes grip. My mouth draws against the page. Under the book, where no bulb reaches, I have a folded limb that extends toward the empty space beside the stool. Its end divides into four soft pads. Each pad bears a shallow hollow.

They would fit the ends of human fingers.

I do not know whether that is what they are for. There are other shapes they might fit: the bulbs’ rounded glass, certain blunt crystal tips, the four small depressions in the underside of the ledger’s cover. I would keep those possibilities. Nothing in this chamber is obliged to have been made for the thing it now receives.

Still, when you crouch in the shelter, your hand comes close.

I become aware of you first through the stool. Your weight shifts outside its sockets. The board tightens against my toes. Then your sleeve interrupts the light, and my exposed eye fills with the dark shape of your wrist.

You see that the seat is occupied.

For a moment you leave your hand where it is.

There is a distance between your fingers and my open pads, narrower than a page’s thickness. I have no means of closing it.

You can.

I would be there, receiving everything that already reaches me, with that small part of my body held out into what does not.
