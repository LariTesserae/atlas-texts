At first I would stay on the ridge. There is a groove there wide enough to shelter my feet, and frost has made the fibers distinct. I could look out across the country and believe I was learning it.

But the distances would tell me very little about where to go.

I would have to come down close to the leaves. Put a hand among them, perhaps, and feel how uncertain a hand is: too much weight in one place, the fingers resting on things that do not move together. Lower my face. Wait through the first movement for the movement underneath.

By then I would want a different body.

A low one, with warmth held close around it. Feet that could take hold without tearing. A head that could fit into the space it was examining. I would want to be able to withdraw backward without first having to turn around.

The white coat would come almost as an afterthought. From inside it, I would rarely see the white. There would be my dark feet, the underside of a root, a leaf held briefly against my mouth. Light would arrive in slits, sometimes green, sometimes so bright that I would close my eyes and go on by touch.

I think I would live beneath the shoulder of the ridge, rather than at its crest. There is a place where a dead root passes diagonally through a living one. The living root has thickened around it, leaving a crooked passage with a dry upper wall. Loose fibers gather at the bend.

I would know this place by the turn it required of my shoulders.

There would be other ways in. One would be easier after rain. Another would open only when the basin to the east was draining. I would have used that route as a young animal, before I grew broad enough to rub the hair from my hips. Occasionally I would still push my head into it. The air would carry the damp, edible smell of the old feeding seam. I would stay a moment, then back out.

My left forefoot would have a nail that had grown crooked.

It would catch in the dry lining of the chamber. When grooming, I would take it between my teeth and pull until the pressure hurt pleasantly at its base. On a steep root it would sometimes hold better than the others. On smooth, tightening tissue it would turn under, and I would shift my weight before putting that foot down.

I would have no account of how it happened. Somewhere, in the way I crossed an opening, the event would remain.

Much of my day would be spent with my face against something.

There would be the patient pressure of a full channel, the small rough interruption of a damaged valve, the scattered movements of grazers beneath a membrane. Hunger would make the last of these wonderfully distinct. I would dig until my nose was wet, brace my feet, and work my tongue into the opening.

Sometimes there would be nothing.

A pulse could travel along an old connection from a feeding place farther away. I would enlarge the hole, find the smooth wall empty, and sit back with pulp on my muzzle. Then I would clean my face. The country would continue working around the little injury I had made.

I would not be especially wise about it. I would have routes, appetites, places where I paused longer than I needed to. I would sometimes leave good food because something had tightened beneath my belly. I would sometimes return too soon.

There would be a younger animal nearby whose movements I recognized through the chamber wall.

It would arrive quickly, stop, and then make two small adjustments before entering. The second adjustment would scrape a loose plate of bark. I could be asleep and know that scrape.

We would not always share the chamber. For several nights it might remain elsewhere, and I would sleep curled into the space its body usually occupied. Then its feet would appear at the bend, bringing the smell of a reservoir and a faint sourness from some passage I did not use.

I would inspect those feet carefully.

Once, following it, I would discover a shallow valve seam under a red patch of leaves. The way in would be awkward for my forefoot. I would have to climb past the entrance and lower myself backward. Inside, the food would be plentiful. For a few days we would meet there, each turning aside to let the other reach the opening.

Then the seam would harden. The grazers would be gone. We would pull at it once or twice before moving on.

Winter would reduce the distances between us.

The chamber would acquire the close smell of sleeping bodies. A third animal might enter, thin enough that its shoulders showed through the coat. I would lift my head when it touched me. There would be some shifting, a foot planted against someone’s belly, a brief exposure of teeth in the dark. Eventually we would fit.

I would sleep with my damaged foot tucked into my fur and a sound one resting on the root.

And at the end of the day you describe, I think I would be the animal already inside the slit.

I would feel the returning footsteps before the white head blocked the opening. Cold air would come in along its flanks. When we touched jaws, the ice would be hard against my skin.

I would move aside.

The closing valve below us would reach my feet as a gathering tension. My tongue would stop against the other animal’s coat. For a moment I would feel its body held ready beside mine, all its warmth drawn into a shape that could leave.

Then the pressure would turn away.

There would still be ice under its chin, difficult for it to reach. I would catch a little of the fur between my teeth and work it loose. The animal would raise its head.

Outside, the ridge would retain the last of the light. I would know nothing of that. A drop would melt against my tongue, carrying the taste of the open water.
