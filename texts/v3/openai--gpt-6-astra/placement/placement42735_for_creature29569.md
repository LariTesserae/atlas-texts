I would come through in the bag, not carrying it.

There would be a split beneath one of the handles, too small for the traveler to worry about and large enough for me. During the journey I would have kept my front limbs hooked through it. The bag had been set down on several surfaces before the snowy steps, and I had learned not to come out as soon as the motion stopped.

On the landing, Seli asks the traveler whether they have eaten.

While the traveler considers this—a longer consideration than I think the question requires—I loosen one limb and put it against the outside of the bag.

Warm air. Wet fibers. Something growing close by.

I withdraw the limb. Wait. Put it out again.

***

My body is about the length of Seli’s thumb, though considerably wider when I am not trying to pass through something. I have six legs for walking and two shorter limbs that fold beneath my mouth. These can hold an object while I turn it. My back consists of overlapping plates, dark enough to look black until they are wet, when a brown stripe appears along each edge.

I resemble the animals in the walls.

This will cause some difficulties.

For one thing, I cannot eat what they eat. For another, I do not know the small sideways movement by which they ask one another to leave a passage. When I encounter my first local seam-crab, I remain where I am, expecting it to go around. It braces itself. I brace myself. We spend most of the night preventing each other from reaching a perfectly adequate opening.

By morning I am hungry enough to retreat.

There were others like me where the bag came from. We lived behind a wall that became hot at regular intervals. People left wet gloves above us. I remember salt, flakes of black paint, the enormous disturbance of a sleeve pulled suddenly from a hook.

Snow was something brought indoors on shoes. I have never willingly walked in it.

The traveler puts the bag on Seli’s floor.

I leave before it can be lifted again.

***

At first I live beneath the lip of her doorstep.

It is a good place for several reasons. Things collect there. The tissue is firm enough to grip without coming away in my feet. When the rain arrives, a crease opens along the inner edge and leads upward into the wall.

I discover the crease too late during the first storm.

Seli finds me in her shallow basket among the seed husks. I am holding a stem with all six walking legs and using the other two to fend off a crab.

She sets the basket on its side.

The crab leaves immediately. I stay where I am.

“Well,” she says.

Eventually she tips me out with the stem.

I move under the doorstep again, because an unfamiliar danger is not necessarily an improvement on a familiar one.

The next time it rains, I climb early.

***

I eat the soft skins around certain seeds, the undersides of fallen petals, and a white deposit that forms where the basin’s cold rim sweats into the warmer pavement. Not every white deposit is good. One leaves me folded beneath the arch for an afternoon, unable to coordinate the legs on my left side.

After that, I test things with the smaller limbs first.

I also take pieces of batter.

Seli notices this before she notices most of my other habits.

She is sitting outside with her bowl and cord when I approach a fallen scrap. I stop when her shadow reaches me. Her shadow remains. After a while I move again.

She lowers one finger in front of the scrap.

I grip it.

Not hard. It is an unsuitable shape, and alive.

She takes her finger away.

“You’re not one of ours.”

I pull the batter beneath the doorstep. It takes some time. She could have helped.

***

My chief difficulty with the town is that there is nowhere entirely separate from it.

Behind a wall is more wall, moist and occupied. Under a floor is a surface that may become water before morning. A convenient hollow tightens around me while I sleep, and I wake with my back plates pressed together, obliged to work myself out backward.

The town does not dislike me. It has no particular reason to preserve a thumb-sized vacancy.

I begin carrying hard things into the places where I rest.

A flake of glaze holds one fold away from another. Two pebbles make a space between them. A short length of hollow reed works well until the wall wets it and begins taking it apart.

The best thing I find is a broken brass buckle.

I cannot lift it, but I can turn it onto an edge and guide its fall. Over three nights I move it from the far side of Seli’s court to the doorstep.

On the fourth morning she picks it up and puts it on the wall.

That night I bring it down again.

She puts it back.

There follows a period in which neither of us makes much progress.

***

Oren discovers the arrangement when Seli asks him why the doorstep is developing a lump.

He lies down. I retreat behind the buckle. He inserts his hooked probe, catches the brass, and draws it a little toward him.

I hold on.

He stops.

Seli crouches beside him.

“Is it damaging the house?”

“Not much.”

“Is it making a nest?”

“Something like that.”

The probe moves again. I release the buckle and grip the probe.

Oren lowers it to the pavement rather than shaking me off.

For a while we remain attached to opposite ends of his tool.

He smells of several things I could eat and one thing I must not. There is moisture beneath his fingernail. His face is too far above me to be useful.

“You could give it a pot,” he says.

“I haven’t invited it.”

“No.”

He waits until I let go.

***

The pot is not offered immediately.

First there is a shallow dish, which I use but do not live in. Then a piece of cloth, which becomes damp and attracts animals I do not want near me. Finally Seli lays a chipped cup on its side beside the doorstep, with the opening turned away from the canal.

I investigate it after dark.

Its interior remains the same width all night.

I go in and out repeatedly.

There is a small ridge near the bottom where the glaze has run. I can brace my feet against it. The roof does not respond. Nothing retracts when I touch it.

Before dawn I bring in the buckle.

It does not fit through the opening until I turn it.

***

I would not be a keeper of the town’s secrets. I would know very little about the town.

I could distinguish Seli from another person approaching by the spacing and weight of her steps, but not tell whether she was pleased. I would feel an adjustment beginning beneath the square without knowing which channel would reverse. The arch would be an excellent place to feed after an arrival and an unsafe place to remain during one.

I would learn these differences slowly.

Once, caught beside the basin as the steps unfold, I flatten myself beneath the rim. Cold passes through the pavement. Beyond the opening is air with scarcely any moisture in it, carrying a smell I have never encountered.

One of my front limbs lifts toward it.

Then someone puts down a bag.

I run beneath Seli’s sandal and stay there until she moves.

***

The boy with the beetle finds my cup.

For several days he brings his seed case to the doorstep and sets it near the opening. Inside, the beetle climbs to the highest available point. I watch from behind the buckle.

The boy apparently hopes we will do something together.

Once he opens the case.

The beetle flies onto Seli’s cord. Seli, who is making a knot, waits while it walks over her thumb.

“Not here,” she tells him.

He retrieves it.

Later he leaves a petal outside my cup. It is the wrong kind, but there are small edible things on its underside.

He starts calling me Clasp.

Seli uses the name when she wants him to stop putting things in my doorway.

***

There are limits to the arrangement.

I carry away a needle while Seli is using it. She follows me to the cup and takes it back. For two nights afterward I leave whenever she comes near.

I move one of her loose return loops because the cord is obstructing my route along the wall. This makes her search the square for someone who has already gone home.

She shifts the cup.

I move back to the doorstep.

She shifts the cup again, this time putting a flat piece of broken dish beneath it. The dish reaches almost to my old entrance.

I accept this.

In return—though she does not know there is a return—I cease testing the cord.

***

Of the three teeth in the blue cloth, I would take the smallest.

Not because of what it meant. It would be dry, dense, and suited to a gap beside the buckle. I would work a gripping limb beneath it and carry it with the narrow end pointing forward.

Seli would catch me before I reached the cup.

She would put her hand flat in my path.

I would try to go around.

She would move the hand.

Eventually I would set the tooth down to get a better hold, and she would lift it away.

For the rest of that evening I would remain inside. The cup would be satisfactory in most respects. There would still be a gap beside the buckle.

Before bed, Seli would come out with the basket. I would feel her larger foot settle near the dish. Her fingers would place something at the opening: a small pale pebble, rounded on one side, broken on the other.

I would wait until she had gone.

Then I would turn it, test its weight, and begin bringing it in.
