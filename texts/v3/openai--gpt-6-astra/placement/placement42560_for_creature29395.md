The mountain reaches me through someone else’s feet.

By the time its movement enters my body, it has passed through hard points, thin pads, the long folded joints of legs. Much of it has been spent. What remains arrives as a tightening of the surface against which I lie, followed by a warmth that moves from one side of me to the other.

There are places here where the thunder is softer. I travel toward them, a little at a time.

Sometimes the soft place closes.

Sometimes it takes me up and puts me elsewhere.

I do not yet distinguish these events from changes in the mountain.

***

I think I would be underneath: not in the stone shelters themselves, not in the stillness beneath the slab, but in the smaller weather carried inside the creature’s shield.

There would be a floor that breathes. There would be interruptions in that floor where a foot turns inward, bringing a sharp mineral taste and a cold so sudden that my mouths shut together. Food would pass overhead as pressure and sweetness, with hard pieces moving inside it. Farther away, beyond several overlapping folds, a current would drag continuously at everything I had not fastened down.

My world would have very little empty space.

Most of its distances would be measured by how much of myself I had to lift to cross them.

A ridge of tissue: three feet loose.

The shallow channel beside a vessel: half my feet loose, the others pulling.

The place where the fish lies: too far.

I would go toward it anyway.

***

I am the young one that keeps releasing its pads.

From outside, this happens all at once. Under my shield, it is a sequence.

One grain in my back pulls sideways. I lift the feet on that side. The other grain presses down, so I brace against it. Then the first grain becomes light, and the feet I left attached begin to hurt.

I release those too.

For a moment everything agrees.

There is no pull in my back. My legs open easily. The surface beneath me moves away, and I have done something that feels complete.

Then a finger comes around my edge.

It is jointed in more places than any of my legs. It fits itself to me without fitting tightly. I turn both mouths toward it and find the taste of old food, powdered stone, the clear material that holds the wall together.

The finger returns me to the warm ridge.

My feet spread.

This time I hold until the next change.

***

The other young lies close enough that I can touch it with a rear foot.

Its grip makes a small persistent tension in the tissue between us. Even asleep, if this is sleep, it maintains that tension. When food comes, its mouths begin working before mine. When a hard fragment is placed between us, it secures itself with every foot and scrapes.

I put a mouth to the fragment.

The taste is faint. I have to work for it. Each scrape sends a little shock through my shield, where there are still places too soft to carry a grain of stone.

The fragment shifts.

My sibling holds.

I release one side, then the other.

We both lose the fragment.

It strikes the fish.

The fish moves.

For a long time afterward, neither of us feeds.

***

The fish is the only part of the shelter that sometimes leaves the shelter.

It does not go far. Its head enters the fringes, and its gills move against water that has not yet been warmed by the body around us. Along its flank there is a line with a different taste from the rest of it. I have touched this line twice.

The first time, the fish struck me with the side of its head.

The second time, it remained still.

I can feel its breathing through my feet when I stand on the fold beside it. The intervals are shorter than the wall’s great trembling, longer than the impacts in the stone. After a while my feet begin to loosen between breaths and fasten when the pressure returns.

This is not something I can do elsewhere.

When the fish turns, I am thrown off.

The finger finds me near the outer fringe, with two legs already pulled straight by the current.

One of those legs does not fold properly afterward.

It stays under me at an angle. When I try to use it, the pad touches before the points.

I learn to keep it raised.

***

There are times when the body above us becomes very long.

Warmth stretches into separate streams. My sibling is near, then distant, though the tissue connecting us has not pulled tight. The familiar ridges become shallow. A taste comes through both of the large mouths at once, pale and rich, with something in it that makes my own mouths move before food reaches them.

During these journeys I hold well.

Perhaps the changes are slower here. Perhaps there is more room between one direction of weight and the next.

Once, I open a foot and set it farther forward. It finds a hollow exactly its size.

I move another.

For several steps I travel without being lifted or caught.

Then the body contracts. The floor gathers beneath me in thick folds, carrying me backward. My good feet grip one fold while the injured foot catches behind another.

I pull until the foot comes free.

A small piece of it remains.

The parent stops feeding.

One of its fingers turns me over. My feet work at the water. The finger touches the damaged place, waits, touches it again.

Later, when we are back behind the wall, it gives me a fragment of milky plate without placing it between us.

I feed alone until my mouths stop.

***

I would not know that there are three shelters.

I would know that, on certain occasions, a thin thing is drawn into the warmth and held there.

It tastes of the parent, but faintly. Something else has handled its farther end.

The parent passes this end through several fingers. A movement travels down it, stops, begins again. I reach out. A finger intercepts me—not returning me to my place this time, only keeping my mouth away.

The strand lies across the tissue beside my feet.

For a little while I feel a trembling that does not come from the mountain, the wall, the fish, or the body holding me.

Its pauses are unfamiliar.

I put a foot on it.

The parent allows this.

When the strand is finally eaten, I search the place where it lay. My points catch in a crease. I keep them there through a whole surge.

***

The first stone I stand on is not the basin floor.

It is caught beneath the parent’s shield, a splinter driven through the fringes during a rearrangement. One end rests against the worn hollow. The other presses into soft tissue.

Everything near it tightens whenever the wall moves.

I find its edge with my feeding fingers. There is no warmth inside it. My hard points slide, find a flaw, slide again. One pad spreads across a face so smooth that I cannot feel where it ends.

The stone gives me the mountain directly.

I close.

For a while I have no loose parts.

The impacts are too many to follow. They enter every place I touch and meet inside my small shield. I cannot tell which feet hurt. I cannot tell which grain is rising.

The parent reaches for me.

Its finger rests against my back, ready to curl beneath my edge.

But the stone has a crack.

Two of my points are in it.

The other feet begin to peel away. I let them. The two remain.

Water catches beneath me and lifts my body until the held legs are almost straight. The finger comes closer. I turn, slowly, around the points in the crack, and the current presses my shield against the stone instead of getting underneath it.

Here, for a moment, I can stay.

The parent’s finger withdraws a little.

Far beyond us, something heavy strikes the wall. The splinter shifts deeper into the fold. Warm tissue presses against one of my mouths.

I release.

The finger catches me at once.

It carries me back past the fish, past the scraped plate, to the ridge where my sibling has made its small enduring tension.

I fasten beside it.

Under my shield, the two grains settle at different angles. My injured leg opens and closes without touching anything.

The parent is still working the splinter out. Each attempt travels through the floor beneath us.

I lift one foot.

Set it farther over.

Keep the others where they are.
