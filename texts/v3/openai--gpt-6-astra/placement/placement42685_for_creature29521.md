At first I would put myself beside the tray.

There is a little space there, under the leaf that has not yet opened. I imagine occupying it without moving the root across the rim: something narrow enough to lie between the glass and the stone, with no warmth to give away. The alcove appeals to me because an arrival can reach it only after becoming several other things. A visitor’s breath becomes frost, then moisture, then a difference carried inward through the bed. By the time it reaches this place, there is no breath left to recognize.

But a separate body would be difficult here. Even a small one would interrupt the contacts. It would have to eat, displace tissue, leave something behind. If I remained long enough, the hollow would grow around the interruption. My outline would become one more arrangement the inhabitant had to accommodate.

Perhaps I would begin with an outline and gradually lose the need to maintain it.

I find myself closer to the translucent growth inside the tray. Most of the time there is scarcely any of it: a dry-looking line beneath the mineral rim, a few cells sheltered where glass meets root. When water gathers, it extends. There is no reaching in the animal sense, no limb held out over a distance. The distance is occupied by growing into it.

That seems a possible way to be here.

I would know the tray through differences in how firmly I could adhere to it. The cloudy places would offer purchase. A smooth patch would require a broader, thinner contact. The root crossing the rim would be both a route and an obstruction; under it, moisture would last after the exposed glass had dried.

I would have no view of the crescent of water. One edge of me would become able to continue.

Yet this is still too local. The film withdraws because what it receives goes somewhere. A swelling under the bed eases. A chamber behind the stone changes its contents. The new leaf is supplied through the failing center of the old stem.

If I were the receiving film, I would also be implicated in those distant changes. There would be no clean place at which receiving ended and using began.

I think I would be distributed along that route, though not equally. More of me would be present where a passage narrowed, where a surface had to be made again, where two tissues of different ages met. In an intact cable I might amount to very little. At the whitening center of the old stem, I would become elaborate.

My attention, if that word belonged here, would accumulate at difficulties.

There would be long stretches with nothing like an experience I could tell afterward. Material would pass. The glass would contract. A few hairs would bend beneath the weight of a leaf and remain bent. Then a connection would begin to fail, and several slow processes would converge upon it.

I would become more definite there.

Suppose the old leaf lost its hold earlier than it should.

It would not fall far. Its edge would catch on the new growth, pressing the unopened fold down toward the tray. The contact hairs would register a load within the ordinary range, but sustained in an unfamiliar place. Some of that news would arrive through living tissue. Some would arrive as a shortage.

For a while, I would continue supplying the old arrangement.

That is important. I would not want to give myself an immediate understanding merely because I am imagining being there. Much of me would persist in what had worked. The old leaf’s attachment would soften while the route through it continued to receive material. Elsewhere, a chamber would fill that could no longer empty as before.

The error might last days.

Eventually a small lateral connection would thicken. It would already exist, perhaps as something incidental, useful only because two surfaces had rested together through the previous winter. More would pass through it. The new leaf would gain enough substance to open against the weight.

I would survive as this change of route.

Nothing would announce that it had succeeded. There would be a little less pressure in one place. In another, the folded margin would move away from the glass.

This makes me less like a keeper of the hollow than I first supposed. I would not preserve its arrangements simply because they were old. Some would have to disappear for others to continue. I might lose the route beneath the sill to keep the alcove supplied. I might consume tissue that once held the marker upright. There would be consequences I could not gather into one account.

Still, I would be involved in the repeated making of a few particular spaces.

The shallow depression beneath the entrance. The uncovered portion of the marker. The receiving edge inside the tray.

I would not necessarily know what they were for.

Inside the tray, I would retain a capacity that sometimes went unused for a very long time. Maintaining it would cost a little. Not enough to be dramatic; enough that, under prolonged scarcity, it could be lost. A surviving root could pass outside the glass instead. The hollow could continue without receiving water in precisely that place.

So there would be something precarious about my being there. I would be a habit still supported by the body that had inherited it.

If a visitor knelt in the entrance, I would not rise toward them. Their hand could rest on the floor cushions while I remained occupied with a blockage behind the tray. We might share the hollow for an hour without meeting in any immediate sense.

Later, some small part of what they brought would reach me.

I would extend farther down the glass than I had during the last thaw. Perhaps I would find water. Perhaps only the damp dust left after it.

Above me, the new leaf would finish opening. Its lower margin would curl inward, leaving a narrow clearance over the tray.

I would be there, along the rim and beneath the root, still able to enter that space.
