I would find myself beside the seven dark tiles, where a little water has collected without reflecting the sky.

From a distance I would take it for a shadow. Coming closer would not immediately help. There would be grit in it, and one of the winged creatures lying on its side, moving three wings. The fourth would be caught beneath something.

That something would be my hand.

I would have to lift it before I knew.

***

My body lies mostly in the gaps. There is more of it than can be seen from above: a thickness tucked beneath each tile, soft connections passing through the warm stone wherever the edges have not quite hardened. Across the surface I show as seven or eight dark patches. When rain strikes one, I feel it in all the others.

I have no particular front until I need to go somewhere.

Then the patch nearest the destination thickens. Two blunt projections emerge, feel the ground, and take weight. The rest of me follows reluctantly, peeling out from under the tiles with faint clicks. For a while I look like a wet strip being pulled through a series of buckles.

Free of them, I am shorter than the inhabitant’s forearm. My underside has many small gripping folds. My upper surface carries bits of stone that do not belong to the shelf anymore but have not quite become part of me. One is a corner of a tile.

I am slow when watched. I am also slow when no one is watching. Crossing the exposed ground between the tiles and the shelter takes most of a shower, and afterward I remain beneath the bench until the grazers stop trying to scrape my back.

The inhabitant knows I am there.

It has never tried to put me with its other objects.

***

At first I would think my work was to keep the tiles together.

Growth pulls gently on them from every direction. The pull is almost continuous, too slight to feel as movement. It reaches me as appetite: an opening somewhere inside, the conviction that a part of my body has been left behind.

I push myself farther beneath the tile edges. I thicken in the gaps. At night I eat what has accumulated against me—film, softened mineral, the small pale things that live where neither air nor stone quite reaches.

By morning the tiles are a little farther apart anyway.

I do not know how they were spaced when they were laid. I only know the distances I held yesterday. Even those become uncertain after I have gone to feed.

The inhabitant comes and places its feet along them.

Its weight presses through the dark material into my back. There are places on its soles that are firmer than others. I learn those before I learn its face.

When it tries a second time, I tighten.

This does not make its stride fit. Once it makes a tile tip.

The inhabitant steps away and waits beside the row. Beneath the lifted edge, my exposed tissue gives off a thin steam. It brings water in the folded strip and squeezes it over me.

The water is full of food. I loosen so abruptly that the tile settles with a knock.

For some time afterward, whenever it approaches, I draw back from that edge.

***

I can hear through anything I am holding.

The tiles have very little to say. Rain on them is sharp and nearby. Feet are complicated. A grazer’s six steps reach me separately, with the scraping of its mouth laid across them like a continuous seventh foot.

The hollow reports from below are different. They enter everywhere at once.

For several moments before one comes, I lose track of my extremities. Something under the shelf offers more room than I have a body to occupy. My folds open. The thin connections beneath the tiles swell until the stone presses painfully around them.

Then the sound passes through.

Afterward I must find my ends again.

Occasionally there is an extra one.

It is usually small and unhelpful, a soft knob without grip or feeling. I drag it about until it wears away. Once an end develops a crease that can taste water. I keep that one tucked beneath me during the day.

With it I discover that the central pool tastes different along its eastern margin.

I spend much of an afternoon finding this out. The transparent hunters gather under my reflection. One places its mouth against the tasting crease and stays there.

Neither of us gets anything useful.

When it lets go, I retreat too fast and leave a pale scrap attached to the stone. A grazer eats it while I am still close enough to feel the pulling.

***

There are things I want that have nothing to do with holding.

I want the underside of the shelter bench when the slow light is passing through it.

I want a certain seam just after rain, before the winged columns have descended.

There is a smooth stone beneath the western overhang that I cannot reach. The ground curves away too steeply, and the moisture makes my folds slip. Each time I try, I get far enough to touch it with one extension. It is cooler than everything around it.

I have no use for it that I know of.

Sometimes I go there instead of returning to the tiles.

***

The inhabitant first carries me by mistake.

I have settled over the dark strip after it was left damp beneath the bench. When it lifts the strip, my folds close. It shakes it once. I close harder.

It brings us both to the light.

Held in the air, I become very compact. The little pieces of mineral on my back touch one another. My unsupported edges curl inward, exposing a narrow underside crossed with pale lines.

The inhabitant puts a finger there.

I grip it.

For a while we remain in this arrangement. Its finger is warm, but the warmth comes and goes in small traveling patches. Deep inside it, something moves toward the fingertip, stops short of me, and turns back.

Eventually it lowers its hand beside the tiles.

I stay attached.

It waits until I can uncurl.

After that, it sometimes offers me the backs of two fingers. I do not always take them. It learns to offer them low, with its hand resting on the ground, and to begin lifting only after I have drawn my trailing parts aboard.

Its left hand is easier to hold. Its right takes me farther.

***

I become involved with the eggs because one is set down on me.

The inhabitant is carrying it toward a ridge. I am crossing the narrow route, broad and flat from recent feeding. A report comes from below.

Its breathing fold closes. Its hands descend.

The egg rests in a shallow depression in my back while the inhabitant braces itself against the ground.

Inside the smoky skin, the white crescent straightens.

I feel that small movement more clearly than the sound beneath the shelf. Every fold along my underside grips. The mineral fragments on my back spread apart.

When the inhabitant reaches for the egg again, it has settled too deeply.

Pulling makes the skin stretch.

The inhabitant stops.

It wets its fingers. It wets me. Finally it slides the slender root beneath the egg, easing one part free at a time. The last contact releases with a tiny tremor that goes through my whole body.

For the rest of the day I carry the shape of it.

I return to the same place the next morning, but the inhabitant steps over me.

***

Eventually I spend less time beneath the tiles.

One gap in the route to the ridges widens faster than the others. The inhabitant puts softened mineral into it; by evening the mineral has divided into three low humps with wet intervals between. An old shell placed there turns slowly on its side.

I settle across the gap because food gathers in it.

While I am feeding, the two nearest humps stop moving apart.

The inhabitant notices before I do.

It crouches nearby with an egg between its fingers. It waits so long that I begin to draw myself away from the exposed ground. Then it sets one foot beside me, crosses, and continues toward the ridge.

Afterward it leaves the damp strip within reach.

This becomes an arrangement, though an unreliable one. I am sometimes under the bench when needed. Sometimes I am at the overhang, touching the cool stone. Once, while the inhabitant is crossing, I follow a rich thread of film into a crack. Its foot slips. Warm water splashes over us both.

It sits down heavily, keeping the egg raised.

I withdraw beneath a shell and remain there until night.

The next morning the strip is beside the gap again.

***

I would not be present for everything.

When the inhabitant visits the northern nodule, I might feel its departure through the route and nothing more. I would know the long stillness of its feet near the edge. If rain began, I would lose even that.

But once it would carry me there.

It would lower its hand beside the hollow, and I would spread onto the new stone. The nodule would stand close enough for one of my edges to touch. Warm water would fill its pierced center.

Through the opening, wind would make an uneven sound.

I would reach around the base, tasting the dampness beneath it. The inhabitant would turn the nodule a little. I would adjust my grip.

Then something very slight would tap against the inside of the hole.

My folds would close.

The inhabitant’s fingers would stop moving.

We would wait through the next report from below, and through the fresh warmth rising afterward. Nothing would tap again.

Eventually the inhabitant would leave to feed. I would remain around the base until the stone darkened and the first winged creatures came down to drink.

When I finally drew away, the nodule would lean a little toward the shelter.

I would push it upright before going.
