I would be some distance below the new court, in a basin that receives its overflow.

For much of the day there would be nothing to distinguish me from the slight cloudiness around the foot of a wall. One edge of my body would lie across an outlet. Another would extend beneath a ledge, where the ceramic stays wet even when the upper pools are being cleaned. I would have placed myself so that water entering the basin had to pass over part of me.

The new court would reach me first as a change in temperature.

Then a little grit. Then water with the flat, mineral taste of a surface being wetted for the first time.

I would draw my margins inward, pass the grit out from under myself, and wait. A first filling does not tell you much. Water finds the obvious hollows immediately. The less obvious ones take longer.

My work would begin when the supply diminished.

***

I think I would be an inhabitant who goes through passages after they are finished.

Not the first to enter. There are others who like the advancing edge, who spread onto clay while it is still darkening. I would stay behind them, where the flow has settled and the bodies have dispersed. I would cross a tongue slowly, stop halfway, turn, go back. I would approach an outlet along the wall rather than the bottom. Sometimes I would carry a small piece of fired clay in a fold, simply to be less able to make myself thin.

A passage can be generous when you are unburdened and moving in the intended direction.

I would want to know what happens when you have to stop.

There would be a stiff place in me, a narrow strip that would no longer lie flush against the ceramic. Under strong light it would look like a short hair trapped beneath glaze. I would have had it long enough to arrange my movements around it without much attention. On broad surfaces I would turn one way more readily than the other.

This would make me useful, though not universally welcome.

The maker of an outlet might wait nearby while I passed through it. I would feel its waiting: a steady pressure through the partition, interrupted whenever it shifted its grip.

Sometimes I would go through without difficulty, and the pressure would lift.

Sometimes I would stop.

***

In the newly filled court, there would be a turn beneath a projecting shelf.

The water would take it perfectly. From above, the junction would look especially neat: a narrow stream drawn around a curved lip, never losing contact with the clay.

I would pass it once with the flow.

Coming back, I would find that I needed to gather my stiff portion before I could reach the wall beyond. Gathering would pull my leading edge out of the stream. A healthy body could spread farther. A smaller one could travel entirely within the curve.

I would remain there, attached on both sides, with the middle of myself growing dull.

Eventually I would retreat.

The maker would come down the wall. It would be a broad individual with closely spaced structures in its margin and a habit of holding its working pad raised after setting down a tool. I would have met it before.

It would cross the turn, reverse, and cross again.

Then it would press once against the clay beside me.

I would take the piece I had been carrying from its fold and leave it on the bottom. Without that burden, I could return through the passage. The maker would follow.

For a while we would rest on the same flat patch, our edges just touching. It would feel the part of me that did not spread.

I would feel how much of its body remained extended back toward the new work.

Neither of us would move.

***

I would not always be right about where to add clay.

My first attempt at improving that turn might be a low rest beneath the shelf, a place to gather without leaving the water. I would bring paste, raise a hand, and press a small wedge into the angle.

The wedge would make the crossing easier for me.

It would also draw a thread of water away from the main channel. Below us, along a seam intended to remain merely damp, a dark patch would begin to widen.

The maker would touch the patch and then the wedge.

I would take my addition out.

That would be part of the work too: waiting for enough water to pass that I could discover what I had done.

We might finish with something smaller than either of us first proposed. Three shallow grooves on the underside of the shelf. A roughened patch, no larger than a raised pad. Enough water held against the clay to let an edge secure itself during the turn.

The maker would cut the grooves. I would remain spread beneath the work, catching the crumbs before they entered the lower basins.

Afterwards it would smooth the place where my wedge had been.

I would leave the little piece I carried in a recess nearby. Tomorrow I would come back for it and try again.

***

I would have a home, although it would not be enclosed.

It would be a basin with an imperfect floor. Near one corner, the clay would rise in a broad, almost invisible swelling. When the basin emptied, a shallow crescent of water would remain behind it.

I would rest in that crescent.

Someone would have proposed levelling the floor once. I would have held my edge over the place long enough for them to understand that I preferred it as it was.

A large neighbour would occupy the next basin. Part of its margin would reach through a low opening into mine. We would not need to greet each other every time we returned. I would know it was there from the way the water slowed beneath the opening.

If I came home carrying grit, it would withdraw until I had cleared it out.

If I came home with my scar drawn tight, it would sometimes send a small contraction through the pool. I would loosen by degrees. The pulse would come again only after the first had passed through me.

There would be days when I did no work beyond moving to the other side of the swelling.

The court would continue to fill.

***

I would be capable of making a hand, but I would not keep one longer than necessary.

The air would take water from it. Fine lines would appear across the pad. The three projections would become awkwardly distinct, and holding a tool would require a continual drawing-up from the body below.

Still, I would like certain parts of that work.

Pressing fresh paste into a socket.

Finding, by touch, that a repair stood just proud of its surrounding surface.

Taking away so little that the tool seemed to have removed nothing, then touching again and feeling the difference.

I would have a small polisher with one flat side and one rounded side. Other inhabitants would borrow it. Sometimes it would return to the wrong recess. I would search several roofs before finding it, and carry it home enclosed rather more deeply than its weight required.

***

Much later, the new court would cease to taste new.

I would be crossing it on some ordinary errand when a heavily folded inhabitant came toward the turn beneath the shelf. It would carry a fitting wrapped inside itself, the hard corners showing through as pale ridges.

I would move aside.

At the turn it would stop, secure an edge beneath the shelf, and gather its burden forward. For a short time its body would rest on the damp patch made by the grooves.

Then it would continue.

I would stay where I was until its trailing margin had passed. A little of the water displaced by its body would travel back over mine.

The turn would be too narrow for us to pass together. There would still be that to consider.
