I would rent a room in a building whose address was more impressive than its position: Fourth Court, Reservoir Approach, though there would be no first three courts and the reservoir would be reached more conveniently from the other side.

The room would suit me for reasons I would be embarrassed to list at the housing office. A loose channel bracket would tap twice before each watering cycle. The kitchen window would retain the brighter part of the afternoon well into evening. From the bed, through two curtains of leaves, I would be able to see the station.

I would say I had chosen it for the good condensation.

At first I would sleep with my shoes beside the bed, pointing toward the door. This would be a habit from wherever I had come from. I would not remember acquiring it, and would dislike having it noticed.

My luggage would have one repaired grip and one that needed repairing. Inside, wrapped separately from my clothes, there would be a small household object whose use I could demonstrate more easily than explain. It would fasten two things together, but neither of those things would be made here. For some months I would take it to shops.

Then I would put it in a drawer.

***

The work I would find is checking pedestrian maps.

Not surveying. I would have no qualification to judge what the ground was doing, and during my first year I would be among those who learned about an adjustment from the behavior of other people. My feet would possess the necessary ridges, nerves, and breadth. They would simply take a long time to understand what they were standing on.

My work would begin after the surveyors had signed off.

A passage could be geometrically correct and still be wrong for someone trying to reach a clinic. A stairway might be reliable uphill but give a person the sound of their own descent while they were still climbing. A crossing could require a firmer tread than an elderly resident supplied. A garden could have closed around a route which remained, on the district plan, a generous public thoroughfare.

I would carry a roll of textured proof sheets, a pressure stylus, a little folding measure, and my midday food. Nothing authoritative enough to persuade a channel fitter to move a channel. Enough to have an argument afterward.

On each sheet I would record things the survey could not use.

*Handrail becomes warm before adjustment.*

*Door opens outward. Leave space for the person coming through.*

*Do not follow the first set of footsteps.*

There would be an approved vocabulary. I would be slow to learn it.

My supervisor would cross out “the path appears to continue” and write “unconfirmed continuation.”

“It does continue,” I would tell her.

“Then confirm it.”

“I couldn’t find my way back along it.”

She would set down the stylus.

We would go together the following morning.

I would like her considerably more after that.

***

In this work I would become familiar with the city’s provisions for people who had not arrived.

There would be benches with no polished places. Public cupboards stocked with drinking cups, each cup still sitting exactly within the pale circle left by its manufacture. In one district, a nursery garden would have been trained to admit small shoulders through its entrances, though the nearest children were old enough to be irritated by such care.

I would eat there sometimes.

I would not find these places sad every day. Often I would be glad of a clean table. Sometimes I would be impatient with the expense. Once, in warm rain, I would discover that the roof of an empty waiting shelter leaked directly over its only seat, and feel an indignation out of proportion to the fault.

Someone would eventually sit there carrying everything they owned.

I would put in a repair request. When it was closed without inspection, I would put in another.

The shelter would acquire a new roof long before it acquired a passenger.

***

I think I would be broad, rather ungainly when rising, good at remaining still once seated. There would be a dark strip of hair at the back of my head that refused to lie evenly. My neck folds would open when I was concentrating, which other people would mistake for distress.

“Take a rest,” they would say, while I was enjoying myself.

I would learn to wear a loose collar.

I would also acquire a weakness for the brittle sheets sold beside the southern station. I would buy two packets with the intention of saving one. Both would be empty before I reached the second crossing. Small fragments would lodge in the folds of my maps.

My supervisor would object to this.

It would be one of her less persuasive objections.

At home, the garden would gradually make a route for me. A collecting leaf would turn aside from the place where I stopped to take off my shoes. The stems beside the kitchen would leave room for my left elbow, because I would always carry too many things in that hand and use the right one to feel for the latch.

This accommodation would happen before I had any close friends.

I would discover it one evening while carrying a cup, a proof sheet, and an unopened letter. There would be no leaf to push aside.

For a moment I would stand in the little space prepared around my elbow.

Then I would spill something.

***

I would meet the channel keeper because she had complained about a map.

I would find her at the small southern tree, with her meal unwrapped in front of her. The second portion would be across the table. I would look at it, then look away too deliberately.

She would notice both things.

“You’ve sent people through the lower court,” she would say.

“It’s the shortest accessible route.”

“The lower court is being watered at that hour.”

“The paving stays dry.”

“The people don’t.”

I would unfold the sheet. She would set a tool on one corner to keep it from curling.

There would be a perfectly ordinary correction to make. The public route passed below a row of collecting leaves. During the evening cycle, their swollen joints lowered them to the height of a tall person’s face. The alternate route added sixty paces and went behind a kitchen whose occupants disliked the traffic.

We would discuss the kitchen.

I would not ask about the food.

Before I left, she would brush the back of her hand along my forearm, removing a strand of flower silk I had been picking at throughout our conversation.

Afterward I would occasionally take my lunch to the tree. I would bring my own cup, marked on the bottom, and sit on the bench nearest the street rather than across from her.

There would be days when we spoke and days when she continued a conversation I could not remember beginning. I would learn to say, “I don’t know that part,” rather than agree and hope the missing information became apparent.

Sometimes she would explain.

Sometimes she would say, “No, you wouldn’t.”

I would not put the tree on a special map.

***

The mistake that changed my work would be a small one.

A newcomer would be sent to an apartment in my building. I would have checked the route myself. Station, crossing, shaded walk, second entrance. No unreliable steps. No new stone. No ambiguity at the doors.

Yet I would find him sitting on his luggage outside the first entrance, well after the water cycle, with his papers going soft in his hands.

He would have reached the correct door and gone past it. Then he would have returned to the street.

I would assume he could not read the entrance marks. I would begin explaining them.

“I know,” he would say.

He would be looking up at the rooms.

There are ways to make a person tell you something. Standing over him with a map is one of the poorer ones. I would sit down, partly because he looked exhausted and partly because my feet hurt.

After a while he would ask whether someone needed to let him in.

“No. Your seal will open it.”

“But does someone know?”

I would look at the papers in his lap. The office knew. The allocation system knew. His name was already waiting inside the door.

I would say, “I live upstairs.”

That would be enough to get us standing.

I would take one bag. At his kitchen entrance the plants would still be arranged for a narrower person, and we would have to work the luggage through sideways.

***

I would not cease checking maps. People would still need to know which pavement was safe.

But on some assignments I would begin waiting at the station after I had finished. Only when a household was expected; only when I had time. I would offer to walk the route with them.

My supervisor would tell me this belonged to another department. She would then ask me to record how long it took.

It would often take much longer than the walk.

There would be a stopped channel to report, or a hungry child, or someone who needed to go back to the platform once more to make quite sure no bag had been left behind. People would tell me where they had come from. I would make mistakes about what they meant. We would straighten out whatever was necessary to get their belongings through the door.

Some would invite me inside. Some would want me gone as soon as possible. I would become better at distinguishing these wishes.

I would come home late enough that my own garden had lowered its leaves across the path. For a while, I would apologize as I eased through them.

Eventually it would leave that route open longer.

***

Where would you find me?

Not far beyond the maintained streets, although I would go there for work. Not inside a tower, and not at the opposite place under the channel keeper’s tree.

Most often I would be somewhere between the station and a building whose entrance was difficult to see beneath its leaves. I would have a map rolled under one arm and somebody else’s awkward bag in my hand. I would be explaining which shops took back their food stalks, or admitting that I did not know why a rail continued into the bare plain.

After some years, I would feel an adjustment through my soles before the channels stopped.

My first impulse would be to look at the person beside me. They might already have felt it. They might still be speaking, tired and intent, about a room they had left.

I would set down the bag.

“Wait here a moment,” I would say, and put my hand on something steady.

When the pressure had passed, we would pick everything up again.
