I think I would live just below a meeting place, in a room people passed on their way to somewhere more important.

Its entrance would open onto a long incline. There would be no view from inside, only the opposite wall of the passage and, during the brighter hours, a pale oblong where the light reached the paving. The room would be deeper on one side than the other. I would have a usual arrangement for resting in it, and another, less comfortable arrangement for when someone stayed.

Someone would stay often enough that I kept the second arrangement possible.

At first, you might take me for part of the threshold. I would have the old inhabitants’ habit of putting down more of my body than movement strictly required: a broad fold against the floor, a support resting along the wall. There would be considerable reassurance in the pressure of a familiar place. I would know the house across the passage by the way air worried its upper recess. I would notice when that recess began to close, long before its occupant admitted there was less room.

I would be less accurate about my own house.

One of my supports would have a stiffness I accommodated so thoroughly that it no longer seemed like stiffness. Asked why I crossed an incline in a particular way, I would give an elaborate account of the grain of the stone.

Another inhabitant, who had known me when I moved differently, would put a limb against mine.

The correction would be brief.

I would withdraw the limb.

Then, after a while, put it back.

—

I would not have a name that meant *keeper* or *listener*. Those are the sorts of names a visitor gives someone whose other activities they have failed to notice.

There would be a way of recognizing me: a broad initial pressure, two smaller adjustments, an interval during which I seemed about to move and did not. Someone who knew me well could feel this through a communal bench. Someone who disliked me could imitate it.

I would dislike being imitated more than I thought reasonable.

My eating seam would be tucked inconveniently close to the stiff support. Feeding would require a private, undignified arrangement. I would prefer to do it alone, though I would allow the small animals to clear the grit from the plates afterward. One of them would habitually approach before I had finished. I would push it away with more force than necessary, then remain still for a long time in case it returned.

There would be days occupied almost entirely by food, a troublesome fold, and the effort of finding a resting position that did not press on an old sore place.

From a distance I might seem patient.

—

The room would be on a route that used to be much busier.

Above it, the passage would turn through a roofless hall and descend toward a crossing where moving height briefly made a difficult ledge negotiable. In earlier seasons, inhabitants from the next ridge would use it on their way to the summit square. They would stop near my doorway to wait for the right conditions.

I would come to know their weights.

One traveled with its body carried remarkably high, even when there was no reason. One rested against the wall as soon as it stopped. Another always approached my entrance, made contact, and communicated something too small to justify the interruption: food in a crack, an animal obstructing a passage, an impression that a familiar courtyard had become disagreeable.

I would answer at greater length than the matter required.

Then the crossing would change.

Not collapse. Nothing so decisive. The safe interval would become shorter, the ledge less reliable. A different approach would open on the far side of the ridge. Over several seasons the passage outside my room would cease to carry anyone who did not mean to come there.

For a while I would enjoy the quiet.

Later, I would move farther into the doorway when resting.

—

There would be a young inhabitant in the roofless hall.

Not mine. I would be particular about this distinction when it damaged something, and less particular when it learned quickly.

The adults caring for it would have moved there for the growth along a new fissure. Their bodies would make an awkward shelter against one surviving wall. When they went to feed, the young one would sometimes be left with me.

It would find my room intolerably uninteresting.

I would lay a support across the entrance. It would discover the space beneath. I would lower the support. It would go around the end. Once outside, it would flatten against the paving with such sudden completeness that, for a moment, I would fail to distinguish its pressure from the room’s.

That fright would remain in my body longer than the incident deserved. Even after I had brought it back, I would keep an attentive patch pressed to it.

The young one would object.

Eventually I would take it along the old passage.

There would be things worth learning there: the shallow places where a body could wait, the strain that traveled ahead of a rise, the difference between a floor relaxing and a floor losing its nearness. I would hold part of myself on either side of a moving boundary and let the young one feel the change through me.

It would learn my stiff way of standing.

I would discover this only when another adult came with us.

The adult would touch me, then the young one, then arrange its supports differently. The young one would cross with much less effort.

For several days I would find reasons not to give another lesson.

When we went out again, I would have to learn a little of what I was teaching.

—

I would keep a fragment.

It would not be beautiful. One side would be brown and rounded; the other would have the faint cream roughness of a comparatively recent break. Wrapped in old skin, it would fit inside a small upper recess.

It came from the place where the visitor who carried itself too high used to rest.

We would not have been close in every way. There would have been long intervals when I found its visits tiring. It had a habit of beginning a contact exchange while I was arranging myself, then objecting when the arrangement interrupted it.

Once, during an argument, it would have communicated through the floor rather than touch me.

I would have replied through the wall, making the disagreement unnecessarily public.

The fragment would come into my possession much later, after its room had worn open and it had moved to another district. I would take it almost accidentally, hold it through a passage of height, and then face the inconvenience of having decided to keep it.

I would not know whether that inhabitant was still alive.

Sometimes I would take the fragment down with the intention of setting it outside.

I would turn it, feeling the worn surface.

Then I would wrap it again. The recess would require an uncomfortable reach.

—

Eventually someone would persuade me to go to the broad summit.

Perhaps the young one, no longer small enough to shelter beneath a single fold, would be making the journey for the first time. Its household would want someone familiar along the route. I would explain that I knew only the older approach.

They would explain that we were not taking the older approach.

The journey would be harder than I expected. There would be rests I pretended were opportunities to examine the stone. Once I would put down a support and find the floor farther away than it ought to be. For the remainder of that passage, I would maintain contact with the inhabitant beside me.

Neither of us would mention it.

At the square, I would lie near an opening until the cold left my outer folds.

The gathering would begin without me. Small movements would pass through adjoining bodies. I would receive their consequences through the paving, but not enough to follow. A place would be made, perhaps intended for me, perhaps simply left by someone shifting closer to another.

I would take too long deciding.

Then the young one would reach back and touch me with a firm, clumsy pressure I recognized. It would be something I had used to bring it away from my doorway.

I would join them.

No one would be waiting to hear everything I knew. Part of the exchange would concern a ridge I had never visited. Two inhabitants would resume an old disagreement. Something would pass through my neighbor that I failed to understand, and when it came again I would find I had misunderstood the first part too.

I would ask.

The answer would require us to change our contact, bringing softer surfaces together.

While we were occupied, the line would form beneath us.

I would feel it first along the stiff support: a slight pressure where there had been none. My old arrangement would no longer serve. Withdrawing would be easy. I could settle beyond the growing edge and receive whatever reached me through the stone.

Instead, I would lift that side of my body and try to find another way to rest.

It would take some adjustment. I would put too much weight on my neighbor. The neighbor would object, directly.

I would move.

The contact would hold.
