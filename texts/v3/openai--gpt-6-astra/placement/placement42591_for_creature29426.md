I would find myself on the wooded shore that appears only in the water.

At first there would be no reason to think it was a different shore. There would be peat under my belly, a root between my hind fingers, rain arriving through the leaves. A snail would be working its way along the underside of a branch. I would reach for it and find that the branch continued farther than I could reach, although my hand had already passed the place where the snail was.

Distances would be difficult in a few such ways.

I would learn to approach things along their surfaces. A root could be followed. So could a bank, provided I kept some part of my body against it. Open water would be less reliable. Sometimes a channel crossed in six movements would take the rest of the day to cross again. Sometimes I would arrive with silt beneath my palms that belonged to neither bank.

There would be food. I think there would have to be food: something with a shell that resisted opening, something small enough to gather in the fringe beneath my jaw. Otherwise I would become too easily an image, exempt from the work of living here.

I would be hungry at inconvenient times.

I would have a body very like the old animal’s, though at first I would be smaller. All my fingers would be present. There would be a bare place over one shoulder where a fern might establish itself, but nothing would grow there yet.

My shelter would lie beneath a branch that entered the water.

Beyond its entrance would be an empty shell. I would put it outside, and later find it inside again. After several repetitions I would carry it well away, over a ridge of roots, and wedge it into a fork with my middle hands.

The next time the deep rushing came, the shell would be at the entrance.

I would feel inside it.

There would be nothing there.

***

My side of the lake would have its own weather, but not enough of it.

A squall could pass through the grove and leave one tree dry. Mist would collect on the right side of my body while the left remained cold and bare. Some days the rain would continue after its sound had stopped. I would stay under the branch then, drawing water through my jaw, waiting for the two to come together again.

The other inhabitants would be difficult to know.

I might follow the disturbance of a traveler through three pools and find only my own trail coming toward me. Once, beneath a mat of grass, I would encounter a small body with its hind hands wound so tightly around a root that the fingertips had whitened. I would touch its flank. It would loosen one hand, touch mine, and continue holding.

I would remain beside it until the channel settled.

Later I would look for it. The grass mat would still be there. Beneath it, a hollow would hold warmer water than the surrounding pool.

There would be no way to establish whether I had helped it, or whether someone had once helped me in that place.

Still, I would begin checking beneath the grass whenever I passed.

***

The first time the old animal came to the shore, I would notice the weight.

Something would press through the root I was holding: six points of contact, then a spreading heaviness. I would tighten against it. The pressure would change.

Beyond the trees, a pale opening would appear. Through it I would see dead trunks standing in a much larger reach of water. They would look widely separated and strangely definite. Rain would strike them and fall straight down.

At the edge of that opening would be another animal.

It would be large, planted firmly, with growth along its shoulders and a damaged middle hand. I would approach by following the submerged bank. Several times the bank would turn away from it. I would persist.

When I came close, it would lift the damaged hand.

I would lift mine.

For a while we would make the same small movements. Then something would happen on the far shore—a gust, perhaps, or the first tightening in the stone—and the old animal would turn its head.

I would keep looking at it.

That would be the first difference I could be certain of.

***

I would not know what it saw when it looked at me.

Perhaps a younger body. Perhaps an animal it had known. Perhaps only the completion of its own hand, held where the missing fingers ought to have been.

The first few times, I would try to touch it.

There would be a surface between us, but not one I could find consistently. My hand would pass into cold water. Its hand would remain just beyond mine. If I reached farther, the bank beneath me would soften, and the root in my hind grip would become thin enough to slide between my fingers.

Once I would lose it.

There would be a considerable interval with nothing to hold. My limbs would close against my body. Water would pass through my jaw too quickly, and the fringe around my head would stream forward, though I would have no sense of moving backward.

When I found the bank again, one of my palms would be scraped raw.

After that, I would stop reaching so far.

I would lie opposite the old animal with a root held behind me. My front hand would rest open in the water.

Sometimes its fingers would move.

Sometimes mine would.

I would learn the little pause before it withdrew. I would begin withdrawing first, so that whatever lay between us did not have to pull us apart.

***

There would be long stretches when the opening did not return.

During these I would enlarge my shelter. Roots would enter from above, and I would work them loose before they thickened. I would bring a stone to the entrance, find that it obstructed the flow, and move it to one side. Small animals would gather in the quiet water behind it.

Something green would take hold on my shoulder.

I would discover it while pressing through a narrow place. The snag would hurt. I would back out slowly and take another route.

In time there would be two small leaves there, then several. They would be paler than the plants around the shelter. When the air dried, I would turn that shoulder into the water.

I would have other occupations than waiting.

This seems important. The old one would leave the lake and feed, sleep badly, admit its smaller neighbor. On my side, too, there would be a place that needed clearing, a wound to keep out of the silt, a good feeding seam that shifted every time the water cooled.

Once the rushing would begin while I was opening a snail.

I would continue until I had finished.

By the time I reached the shore, the opening would be narrow, and the old animal would be leaving.

I would remain there with the shell still in my hand.

***

Eventually it would come with the smaller one.

The smaller animal would stay farther up the root. Its pale scar would be visible even through the unsettled water. When the old one leaned toward me, the smaller one would take hold of its flank.

I would feel no corresponding touch.

I would look behind myself, at the unoccupied bank.

Then I would put my hand down.

The old one would keep its damaged hand open for some time. I would stay opposite it, close enough to see the water moving through the little openings beneath its jaw. Without our raised hands between us, I would notice how much work it took to remain there. Its hind limbs would tremble. A loose edge of skin would lift and settle near its belly.

I would move a little farther back.

It would lean forward.

I would stop.

We would stay that way until the smaller animal began pulling, patiently, with its whole weight.

***

I think I would live near that shore, but not directly upon it.

Farther into the reflected woodland there would be a low bank that remained through most of the changes. I would make my shelter there. The entrance would face away from the lake. Water would enter along one side and leave along the other, keeping a narrow strip of the floor clear without my having to scrape it.

I would grow heavier. Plants would darken my back. One finger would stiffen after being trapped beneath a shifting stone.

When the deep noise came, I would pause wherever I was and test the ground.

Some days I would go to the lake.

On others I would stay beneath the branch, especially if the water had begun to cool or something small was sheltering in the hollow beside me.

If the old animal came, it might find the bank empty. It might wait, with its hand open.

I would be a little way inland, occupied with a root that had grown across my doorway. My front hands would lift it while the middle pair worked a stone underneath. The stiff finger would make this slow.

Above my shoulder, a new leaf would be uncurling.

The ground would begin to loosen. I would stop working and reach behind me for my hold.
