I would be near the back of the missing brick, below the place where the shrimp braces its abdomen. Not in the deepest corner. A little water has to reach me.

At first I imagine myself as something caught there: a shred of softened leaf, perhaps, held under a grain of sand. The shrimp turns it, finds an edge, begins to eat. But the leaf has come from elsewhere, and I find I want to belong to this chamber without having fallen into it. I want the warm water to be the water I have always had.

So I would be lower down, against the masonry. The faint slipperiness beneath the shrimp’s feet.

A film, with living thickness.

From the mouth of the recess I would be invisible. Even close up, under a better light than the inspection lens, I might look like a stain that disappears when the surface is wetted. But within that stain there would be crowded bodies, held in material they had made around themselves. Water would enter slowly. Dissolved food would pass from places where it was plentiful to places where it was being used. There would be small differences between the exposed surface and the sheltered depth, and those differences would be enough for different lives.

I could not honestly make all of them into one animal. Still, that is where I would find myself: in a patch whose edges are continually being revised.

The shrimp would know me by working across me.

Its feet would settle beyond the place it meant to feed. Then the smaller appendages would begin. From outside, the movements would look delicate. Here they would open furrows. Material laid down over many quiet intervals would gather into a scrap, rise from the wall, and go.

For a while, the scrape would leave bare mineral exposed to the water.

I would remain in its irregularities. Along the side of a pit. Beneath the overhang of a harder grain. Where the mouthparts had passed without quite reaching, a few attached cells would continue to divide.

There would be no whole shape to restore. Growth might close the furrow, or follow its edge, or fail there while another part of the patch thickened. The shrimp could return to the same place and find something different under its mouth.

I would be capable of changing this little surface, given enough time and enough of what the water brought. I would have very little influence over either.

The pumps would reach me through the motion of the water immediately above the film. On a steady run, that motion might bring nourishment faster than I could use it. A stronger flow might lift an edge. Once an edge lifted, water could get underneath, and a portion of what had been attached would peel away.

I would not follow it.

That is difficult to imagine from here, where I am accustomed to keeping track of myself as one thing. Yet in the chamber, part of me could leave while the rest continued against the brick. The released portion might break up in the return current. It might enter the pipe. It might lodge within reach of the shrimp, which would handle it without any reason to distinguish it from food arriving out of the city.

Most of my life would remain a close arrangement of water and wall.

And the shrimp would be an intermittent weight above it.

I think I would come to belong especially to the place beneath its shorter antenna, where it cannot sweep quite as far. A narrow crescent could persist there through several feedings. As the animal grew, as its stance shifted after a molt, that crescent might become accessible. An old sheltered patch would be eaten; another would begin somewhere newly neglected.

There would be an uneven intimacy to this. I would grow in the shelter the shrimp occupied, and it would enlarge by removing some of me. Neither of us would hold the other in mind.

When the shrimp shed its shell, pieces of the old covering might rest against the film. Their undersides would interrupt the flow. Beneath one, conditions would change. For a time I might spread over the empty joint of a leg.

Then the shrimp would recover enough to turn and feed.

I would have no refuge that stayed a refuge simply because it had been one yesterday.

Still, I do not picture myself everywhere on the wall. That would make this too easy—a life dispersed until nothing particular could happen to it. I would be this patch, with its shallow pit and its exposed upper edge. Here, a stretch of poor water would slow growth. Here, a grazing stroke could remove almost everything. The pocket next to this one might flourish while this one thinned.

During the quiet interval, sediment would settle over me. A grain would come to rest in the fresh scrape. Water around it would slow further.

When the pump started, the grain would rock.

If it left, there would be an uncovered place to grow into. If it stayed, growth would continue around it, making the smallest possible alteration to the floor beneath the shrimp.
