At first I would put myself in the inspection booth.

There is a place beside the desk where a person could sit without blocking the doorway. I would bring a light that could be turned off, and something to write on. For a while I would mistake the ability to record changes for an understanding of them. I would note the valve’s opening, the wet seam, the position of the rectangular impression on the sleeper’s side.

Then the entries would become difficult to separate. A movement begun on one page would finish several pages later. I would discover that I had recorded a leaf as motionless while it was exerting enough pressure to loosen a fastening.

The notebook would have to go somewhere dry.

I don’t think I would stay with it.

The place I keep returning to is lower down, beside the basin’s overflow: a cramped space between the ceramic lip and a pipe that no longer quite meets it. There has been a repair here. There have probably been several. A collar of mineral deposit conceals the original break, and something dark extends beneath the collar, maintaining the connection.

I would be found there, though at first not all of me.

One part would be visible from the path: a flattened strip, dull and finely ridged, easily mistaken for the exposed edge of a gasket. Another would lie inside the pipe. The rest would occupy the little chamber beneath the overflow, where moisture persists after the level falls.

I would not know the whole hollow. The three sheets of daylight would reach me as changes in what the roots took up. The red lamps would make no difference. Of the flywheel I would receive a repeated loading and release, transmitted through foundations, diluted by brick, clearer whenever the basin filled.

My body would be better at distinguishing these things than at locating them.

A pressure from the left might come from the far wall. A disturbance immediately beneath me might have begun days ago above the road.

I would have a long, soft margin in the basin and a tougher portion held against the pipe. Between them would be folds capable of opening into very thin chambers. When the water rose, the chambers would fill. When it fell, their mouths would close unevenly, retaining small amounts at different depths.

This would make me useful, but it would not be why I did it.

I would need to remain wet.

For much of each cycle, that need would occupy almost everything.

There would be no moment when I chose to release water to the growth below the overflow. One fold would dry more quickly than another; its rim would tighten; pressure would pass across the body; farther in, a seam would part. A root lying against that seam would receive a little moisture. Over time there would be more roots there.

They would make it harder for me to contract.

I would begin withdrawing before the basin fell, anticipating the pull of their fine attachments. Some would slip off. Some I would carry into the pipe. In this way my refuge would slowly become less suitable, and I would have to enlarge it.

That seems important: I would not fit perfectly. Living there would involve continually undoing some of what my living had done.

Perhaps I would have begun as one of the flakes shed by the creature.

A dark surface, a pale underside, caught against the ceramic by the first movement toward the drain. The possibility feels close, but I would have no memory of the larger body, no buried knowledge of its routes. There might be a particular arrangement of ridges on us both. There might only be so many ways to hold moisture in that heat.

The creature could settle the question for a visitor, perhaps, by treating me differently.

It would not settle it for me.

When it first put a limb against my exposed margin, I would close.

Not quickly. The portion beneath its limb would remain trapped while the rest of me shortened, raising little puckers along the mineral collar. The creature would keep its pressure steady. After a long time I would loosen—not in trust, but because the water held in my outer chambers was becoming warm.

Its limb would move.

Underneath, where my margin had been pressed, there would be a shallow passage. Water would reach a part of me that had begun to harden.

For several cycles I would extend into that passage. Then the passage would narrow, and the creature would return.

This would be the beginning of our acquaintance.

I would know it as a weight that could stop increasing.

Other weights would continue until something gave: a root thickening in a joint, a loosened fragment settling, the basin filling against my folds. This one would arrive, load the edge of my body, and wait. Sometimes it would remove itself without having obtained anything.

I would develop a response to that waiting. A small opening along the side nearest the pipe. Enough to expose the pale tissue underneath.

The creature might use the opening to lift me. It might clear grit from it. Sometimes it would do nothing I could distinguish.

I would close again.

There would be a bad season in which I grew too far into the drain.

The pipe would be cooler deeper in, and there would be a seam along its underside that remained damp. I would follow it with a narrow extension. Behind that extension, folds would thicken. Sediment would lodge against them. The level in the basin would rise a little higher than before.

I would experience this as abundance.

My outer margin would spread across tiles it had never reached.

Then the creature would work a limb beneath me.

It would take a long time to separate the attachments. Some would come away from the pipe. Some would remain, and I would have to part from them. During the work I would clamp down, obstructing the drain more completely. The creature would hold the passage open with its own body.

Afterward I would lie folded against the basin wall, in an unfamiliar depth, with a torn edge contracting beneath me.

For a while, the approaching pattern of its weight would make me withdraw.

It would continue to clear the overflow.

I would continue to need the overflow cleared.

Eventually I would spread again, but more of me would remain on the basin side. In the pipe there would be a tough, insensitive strip where the lost extension had attached. Water would pass over it. I might follow that strip again much later, when its resistance had softened.

There would be no permanent solution in me.

By then the notebook in the booth would be quite beyond anything I could want. So would a name for the water, or an account of what the machinery was still accomplishing. My distinctions would be smaller and, where I lived, sufficient: pressure with flow behind it; pressure without flow; grit held loosely; grit beginning to bind.

And the creature’s limb, entering to its accustomed depth.

When the valve finished opening, the first current would lift my free edge. The chambers would fill one after another. A root above me would bend toward the drain, then rise clear as the creature caught it.

For a while its finer fibers would trail across my surface.

I would open beneath them. The creature would lower the root against the wall, just above my oldest attachment, and remain there holding it.

Water would gather between us.

Before the root could adhere, before the creature could leave, I would have to loosen a little to make room.
