I would be beneath one of the pale fragments beside the upright, in the narrow space that looks occupied when you lift it.

You would have to wait. What appears to be the damp impression of the fragment would begin, very slowly, to gather away from its own outline.

I would not gather all of it. Some would be moisture, some a stain in the stone. A mite would cross the place where my edge had seemed to be, and I would feel it three steps later.

At first I would know the hollow by what presses down.

The fragment has a heavy end and a light end. Under the heavy end, there is room for very little except my oldest flesh. Under the light end, I can raise a fold, admit a scavenger, turn a seed. The underside is not as smooth as it looks from above. It has ridges too fine for a root to enter, and along one ridge there is a place where my body always catches.

When the upright gathers dew, that place catches harder.

The rest of me loosens. My sensory hairs bend toward the vine. Something passes through the fragment without wetting it, and for a short interval I can feel the weight of creatures walking on its upper face.

There are usually two or three.

Sometimes there are more weights than creatures.

I have no use for the difference until one of the weights remains through an entire afternoon. I raise myself beneath it. I make a shallow cup, then a knob. Nothing meets me. Above, a flat gray scavenger eats the dry skin along my shelter’s edge.

By evening the weight has moved the length of my body toward the fern.

I follow it as far as the fragment allows.

***

I would be one of the small animals, but not quite among those that leave their flakes of stone each night.

Once I have reached a certain breadth, movement begins to take pieces from me. A thread stays fast beneath the fragment. I draw it thinner until it is almost invisible; then something elsewhere gives way, and I find that I have left a clear portion of myself stretched across the rock.

It goes on touching things.

A larva steps onto it. Under my shelter, I contract.

The separated portion contracts too, too late to catch the larva, and settles into a groove. Over the following days it becomes cloudy. It eats something I do not feel it eat. When I reach toward it again, its margin lifts against mine.

I withdraw.

After that there are two places beneath the fragment where food disappears.

I cannot get much larger, but I can become thicker. I fill the fine ridges overhead. I grow a yellow body and move it repeatedly from the heavy end to the light end, although there is no room in either place for it to lie comfortably. When the center begins humming at dusk, it trembles inside me.

Eventually I carry it out.

Crossing the bare interval takes most of the night. I spread thinly enough to resemble the floor, then spoil the resemblance by gathering myself around the yellow body. A jumper lands beside me, leaps, and lands on me instead. I close over its hind legs. It pulls free, leaving something that works against my underside until I soften it.

Near the center I meet a lobe of the larger animal.

Its edge comes over mine. The weight is slight. I could still withdraw.

Instead I flatten.

For a while there is more room.

The yellow body moves out of the part I can contract. I follow it a little way, or the tissues around it move through me. There is warmth, a bitter taste, the repeated pressure of larvae arriving above. Once, something hard is pulled across a surface I had not known was there.

When I separate near dawn, I am broad and empty.

A pale line runs through me. It bends in three places, each bend exact. I have never felt anything in the hollow with that shape.

Back beneath the fragment, the line fits one of the ridges.

For several days I do not come out.

***

This is where I would remain: close enough to the upright to be disturbed by each collection of water, small enough to live on what slips past the larger mouths.

But I would acquire a habit.

Whenever the larger animal makes a head beside the fern, I would thicken at my own margin.

Nothing useful at first. A blunt rise with hairs crowded onto its tip. It dries faster than the rest of me. I lower it, take it back into myself, raise it again on another evening.

Eventually two dark points remain when I lower it.

With them, from beneath the fragment’s light end, I see a strip of the hollow. A root. Half a leaf. The lizard’s foot, impossibly large, settling onto the rock.

Light gives me distances for which I have no movement.

When the lizard leaves, I keep its foot for a while as a dark arrangement across my back. One toe extends into a portion of me too narrow to contain it. I thicken there until the shape is complete.

A scavenger bites the raised place.

I fold over the scavenger, miss it, and lose the foot.

***

Rain would bring me out farther than hunger does.

It strikes the fragment overhead so heavily that I pull free of several ridges at once. Water enters under the heavy end. For the first time, I can thicken there without touching both surfaces.

I leave by that end.

Around me the hollow is full of moving edges. Water obscures the tracks; the larvae have gone under cover. Beside the vine, the larger animal is drawing itself upward, and the stone beneath it opens into spaces I have never reached.

I enter one.

It is warm, crowded with cords. They slide past me, tightening. I adhere to one and am carried up through roots into the hanging weight of the animal.

My little head is out. Rain strikes its dark points.

There is more light beyond the lip, although the sky is dark. Stone extends away without a root across it. Water runs openly over the stone.

The body bearing me shudders.

A fold loosens, and for a moment I hang from the end of it. Below me is the outer slope. I extend my hairs. None reaches anything.

Then the fold draws back.

By morning I am beneath my fragment again. Along one side I carry a narrow strip of the larger animal’s flesh. It will not flatten when I flatten. At each pulse from the upright, it shortens, pulling me a little toward the heavy end.

I feed around it. I make room.

And when I raise my dark points to the opening, the half leaf and the root are still there, but I lift them higher than before.
