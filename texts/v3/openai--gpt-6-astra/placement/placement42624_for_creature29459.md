I would be near the station, but not in the warmest part.

There is a space beneath the concrete plinth where the peat has settled away from it. From outside, it looks no deeper than the shadow under a door. Farther in, it widens around a root that was severed when the foundations were laid. Water follows the root. The concrete above it is rough with small stones, the first stones I would have much occasion to know.

I would fit between them and the ground.

At first there would be little to distinguish me from the dampness there. A darkening along the underside of the plinth. A few fibres crossing a gap that ought to be empty. When the worker shone his torch beneath the building, the light would catch water and stop.

I think I would be a small portion, not the largest one withdrawing into the bank. Something left beside the vent when the bar came down, or caught beneath the worker’s boot and pressed into the sheltered margin of his footprint. I would not retain an account of that separation. One edge would close more slowly than the others. That would be what remained of it.

For a while I would still move as though there were more of me.

The forward folds would find a wet passage and spread into it. Farther back, an edge would tighten against nothing. I would wait for a weight that did not follow. Water would accumulate in the hollow of my body until I withdrew and tried a narrower way.

Under the plinth, there would be enough room to make this mistake without freezing.

I would have the pounding above me and the root beneath me. The root would yield very slowly. Its outer layers would come away first, leaving a harder length against which I could hold myself. I would spread along it without reaching either end.

On the side facing the station, droplets would form on the concrete. I would raise folds toward them. Sometimes a drop would grow large enough to touch me; sometimes it would run elsewhere, following an unevenness too high for me to reach. Over many days, I would come to occupy the places where the drops fell.

This would give my upper edge an irregular shape.

There would be no reason for it to resemble the edge I had lost.

The machinery would reach me through several substances at once. Through the concrete, a close, hard shivering. Through the buried root, a weaker succession of blows. Through the water held against my underside, something broad enough to move my whole thickness.

I would settle differently against each.

When a pump stopped, one part of me would loosen before another. A fold held high against the plinth would descend. Beneath it, the water would gather into a bead and fall. For a short time I would be wetter, though the place was beginning to cool.

I might move toward that brief increase. It would be a poor direction to take.

There would be several such errors in my life, none of them large enough to be visible from the door.

Once, during a long shutdown, I would extend beyond the edge of the concrete. The sheltered peat would already have stiffened. Outside, the worker’s cleared path would hold a little spilled water, still liquid beneath its skin.

I would reach it with two folds.

The first would lie against the ice. The second would find its way underneath. Water would wet the pale tissue, and for a while the passage would remain open.

Then the ice would thicken against me.

By the time warmth returned through the plinth, I would have drawn most of myself back. A narrow piece would remain in the path. Snow would cover it. Later the worker would crush it with the heel of his boot, without any change in his stride.

Under the building, I would close around the place where it had been attached.

There would also be growth.

A moss fragment would arrive in a drip or cling to me as I passed through the loosened peat. In the dimness beneath the plinth it would do very little. I would carry it toward the opening because that was where the water reached me, and there it would put out a few green points.

These would catch fine dirt. They would hold a droplet after the bare concrete had dried.

I would remain there longer.

By late winter, one side of me would have become a small, uneven cushion visible beside the station wall. The other would still extend into the dark along the severed root. A person touching the moss would feel the cold water held in it and perhaps, beneath that, something giving way.

I would not withdraw quickly enough to reveal myself.

When the worker came to clear the vent, his first step onto the plinth would pass through me. I would lower the exposed folds. The moss would barely move.

He might leave that patch alone. He might set the broken ice beside it rather than on it. I would have no means of separating these actions from the other changes in pressure and water, but their consequences would remain: an open passage, an uncrushed edge, a few additional hours of wetness.

Eventually something would touch me from farther under the foundation.

It would arrive along the same root. At first I would tighten, as I did against a dry fibre or a grain of concrete. But the contact would stay wet. It would yield when I pressed against it, then press back along another part of my margin.

We would lie together through the running of the pumps.

There might be room for both of us in the hollow. There might cease to be two. The moss at the opening would remain where it was, and beneath it the folds would rearrange themselves slowly, making use of the additional weight.

Outside, the worker would pull the yellow handle. Light would spread briefly over the cleared path.

I would be farther under the building by then, though some of me would still be waiting for the next drop.
