At first I would find myself under the storehouse, where the channel turns.

There is a place there where a citizen can stand with the hind feet on the wall, the middle pair on a hanging support, and the forefeet free. The water passes just below the mouth. Every basket above makes itself felt through the support: one slow tightening for a body entering, a sharper disturbance when someone sets down a load without lowering it properly.

I would stay there longer than the work required.

Not because it is quiet. It is one of the busiest places in town. But most disturbances can be traced. A basket has been opened. Someone is climbing. The keeper is moving a counting cord. Even the root’s slow drinking has a direction here.

I would like being able to tell one thing from another.

This would not always make me useful.

***

My work would involve the pieces left over when something is moved.

A bridge comes down, and there are its loops to inspect. A dwelling is raised away from damp bark, leaving short lashings that may still be sound. Someone cuts a doorway brace free and drops it because the doorway is closing faster than expected. Later I go back for it.

I carry these things beneath my body in a loose harness. Good cord lies lengthwise. Questionable cord is doubled, so I can distinguish it without bringing it to my mouth. Pieces too short to tie are kept separately until I have enough to twist together for packing.

People give me things they do not want to sort.

Sometimes they give me things they ought to have thrown away.

There is a difference between a fiber softened by use and one being eaten from within. I know the difference fairly well. I have also hung a household’s food from a fiber that failed before morning.

For some time afterward, that household brings every cord to me stretched between two citizens, as though transporting an injured person.

I dislike them for this longer than I should.

***

My sleeping room would be in one of the sheltered corners, but not far enough into it.

We have a sound roof. The rear wall is troublesome. A root hair that once supported a storage sling has thickened and begun to push the room outward. We can still close the entrance, but only by drawing one edge across the other.

I sleep with four other citizens. One moves constantly before a molt. One returns late and brings the cold in on its underside. One is very small and has learned that the warmest place is between my forelimbs.

The fifth has known me since before this chamber.

When I explain that the room must eventually be moved, this one presses agreement into the floor without lifting its head.

When I explain where it could go, there is no answer.

We have already examined the available places.

So I take another turn out of the rear lashing. I shift the sling. I persuade the smallest to sleep with its head toward the entrance, where there is more room for its antennae.

At work I am impatient with people who do this.

***

The thorn worker would know me by the way I test a knot twice: once in the direction it is meant to hold, once sideways.

We would have carried roof flakes together. I know how long to wait while the damaged foot finds purchase. Usually I do not notice that I am waiting.

I would be the one bringing cord to the new opening.

Not the best cord. Good long strands are needed at the entrance, where freezing water has begun to pull the outer bridge awry. I bring a length recovered from a room whose occupants have gone to sleep with relatives. There is a flattened place near one end, but it will bear the little fork.

The thorn worker feels that place.

I feel the older notch on the thorn.

For a moment we are both examining what the other has chosen.

Then we begin.

***

I am good at retrieving things because I am willing to go back into spaces other people consider finished.

An emptied room has a different arrangement of smells. Without food, bodies, and damp packing, the bark becomes easier to examine. I can find where a wall has begun to soften. I can discover an egg behind a roof flake before the flake is carried to the waste recess.

But I would not be especially brave about entering a space that has never held us.

At the fresh opening, I put my head against the floor because the floor can tell me something without my having to put my head through.

There is movement beyond.

Several light contacts. A pause. Something dragging.

I lift my head and touch the thorn worker: wait.

We wait.

Again the contacts, though less distinct now. I try to place them along a single body. I cannot. The yielding surface beneath them carries each disturbance badly.

I would want to report a large animal. A definite warning is easier to pass along than this collection of uncertainties.

Instead I bring the worker’s forefoot to the place where my head has been.

We remain there, awkwardly close, while nothing happens.

***

The youngster arrives with its cord.

I have seen this youngster stripping fibers before. It pulls too quickly and gets a wet ribbon instead of a strand. Usually it leaves these ribbons where they will stick to somebody’s feet.

This one has been worked for longer. Parts of it are almost clean.

I take it.

There are several useful things I could teach just then. How to feel the grain before pulling. How to keep a strand from twisting against the mouthparts. Why the damp shreds must come off before a knot is trusted.

Instead I make the loop.

I tie it around a low projection beside the opening, pull on it, and feel the youngster pull back before I have finished testing. Its whole body is already leaning toward the split.

I touch its abdomen until it brings the hind feet forward.

Then I let it lean.

For a while I hold the knot rather than the youngster. The fresh cord tightens across my forefoot. Its damp remnants press into the joints.

There is no movement beyond the wall.

The youngster’s antennae enter, withdraw, enter again.

One tip touches something.

The youngster jerks backward, pulling the loop hard enough to turn it around its anchorage.

I catch its body. The thorn worker braces the fork. A pale strand appears briefly in the opening and curls back out of reach.

We hold still.

Then the strand returns.

It is a rootlet. Its growing tip has been pressed against the other side of the split. As the fork turns, it finds a little room to straighten.

The youngster reaches for it again. This time I let one forefoot go.

***

I would not become the person who understands what is beyond the opening.

Later, when it is wide enough, another citizen will enter first. There will be surfaces I read wrongly, animals whose movements I cannot distinguish, wetness that has to be tasted by someone willing to risk the sting.

I would go in after a line had been fixed.

I would carry spare loops, a roof flake, and the little wedge that can be pressed beneath a foot when the floor offers nothing to grip. Somewhere inside I would find a place to put these down. I would test it, and then test the way back.

But before any of that, the youngster tires.

Its abdomen settles onto the bark. Its antennae continue moving for a while after its feet have relaxed. The thorn worker has gone to fetch a longer brace, and I am left beside the opening with the sleeping youngster and the cord we cannot use for building.

I begin cleaning the remaining shreds from it.

There is a weak place close to the loop. Under a hauling strain it would part. Here, it has held.

I leave the knot fastened. When the youngster wakes, it will want to lean forward again.
