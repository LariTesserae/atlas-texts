I think I would first be found in a kitchen, standing where I had been told to stand because the floor near the sink was not dependable.

Someone would give me a colander of potatoes.

“Check those,” she would say.

“For what?”

“You’ll know.”

Mostly I would. Soft spots, green skin, the black hollow that sometimes goes farther into a potato than seems reasonable. I would put the bad ones aside. When the woman turned from the stove, she would move two of them back into the colander.

“We can cut that out.”

There would be no particular welcome. She would be busy. I would be relieved to have something in my hands.

Later, I would remember that kitchen better than my arrival: a chipped basin, wet peel adhering to my wrist, a window with a wire running across it at eye level. Beyond the window, somebody would be trying to pull a blanket through a gap between buildings. It would catch, come loose, catch again.

I would not yet know whether I needed to eat. I would accept a plate rather than make the question everyone else’s problem.

***

My first work would be reading letters aloud.

There are people in the district who cannot read, but more who can and would rather have someone else present when a letter is opened. Official correspondence is printed on paper chosen for its stability. This does not make its contents reassuring.

I would sit at kitchen tables and read notices about assessed occupancy, delayed shipments, changes to the designation of a wall. Sometimes a person would interrupt.

“Read that again.”

I would read it again.

“No, the way it actually says.”

Gradually I would learn the difference between making something clear and making it sound better than it was.

The woman behind the laundry would bring out her original contract. Not on my first visit. At first she would give me a bill from a supplier whose arithmetic she distrusted, correctly. The contract would come later, flattened beneath a biscuit tin.

I would read the clause about eventual ownership.

“Does that mean my daughter gets it if I die first?”

I would look through the remaining pages. She would go on working while I looked. There would be a tack between her lips.

“I can’t find anything that says.”

“That’s what I thought.”

She would put the contract away. Then she would ask me to take a pair of repaired shoes upstairs, since I was there.

I would become easier to find.

***

For a while, I would sleep in the room behind a former ticket office. Nobody would be certain what the tickets had been for. There would be a little brass opening in the partition, worn smooth underneath by hands reaching through.

The room would contain a bed, a basin, and a cupboard whose shelves were complete. I would inspect them more often than necessary.

I would have an outdoor coat with weights sewn into the lining. Mine would be an alteration of somebody else’s, narrow across the shoulders and loose elsewhere. The woman who gave it to me would ask whether I was comfortable.

“Yes.”

“Lift your arms.”

I would lift them.

“No, you aren’t.”

She would let out the seams.

I don’t think I would be entirely human there. But this would reveal itself through inconveniences rather than an announcement.

I would have no childhood address to put on a form. I could remember the exact wording of a notice after one reading, but have difficulty recalling whether a visit had taken place on Tuesday or whether Tuesday had merely been mentioned. Hunger would arrive late, usually when I saw another person begin eating. Cold would be more straightforward.

There would be mornings when my hands looked unfamiliar until I started using them.

The clinic would advise regular meals and a pencil mark on the doorframe once a month. The nurse would measure me twice, frowning, then turn the measuring tape over.

“That’s the laundry’s tape,” she would say. “They’ve been boiling it.”

For some time this would be the nearest thing I had to a diagnosis.

***

Eventually the municipality would pay me for sitting with people while they dictated.

There would be an office available, but it would be colder than the kitchens, and people would leave things out when they came there. They would give me their names, their complaint, the remedy requested. At home they would get up to demonstrate the cupboard, and on the way mention that their son had stopped visiting because the passage frightened him.

I would learn to ask whether that belonged in the letter.

Sometimes it would. Sometimes they would say, sharply, that it did not.

My job would not be to decide which version of the town was correct. The surveyors had instruments for that, although they sometimes brought their own letters to me.

I would help people put down what they were prepared to claim.

A stair had become steeper.

A rebate had not arrived.

A bedroom was still present but could no longer be reached without entering an apartment whose occupant worked nights and was becoming understandably angry.

At the bottom I would leave room for a signature.

Those who could not write their names would make a mark. I would witness it. This would give me a small official standing, enough to obtain stationery and not enough to get the ticket-office roof repaired.

***

I would make a mistake with a woman who wanted to leave.

She would come after my working hours, carrying a shopping bag full of documents. Her sister had found her a room elsewhere. There was a job, not a good one, but it came with training. She needed proof of residence, confirmation of employment, and an account of a qualification whose issuing school now opened onto a utility shaft.

I would know how to explain these things.

While we worked, I would ask who was taking her apartment.

“Nobody yet.”

“And the room at the back?”

“What about it?”

It was a particularly reliable room. Two families had used it during repairs. In winter, an elderly neighbor slept there when his own kitchen would not hold enough warmth.

I would begin suggesting arrangements. A retained tenancy, perhaps. Someone to come in daily. She could leave some furniture.

She would pull the shopping bag toward her.

“I’ve given this place sixteen years,” she would say. “Do I have to leave it my bed as well?”

I would have no useful answer.

We would finish the forms. For several days afterward I would find traces of my earlier suggestions in the drafts and cross them out.

On the morning she left, I would help carry her bed to the cart.

The frame would not fit through the first doorway. We would have to take it apart in the passage, holding the screws in our mouths because neither of us had brought a jar. She would laugh once, impatiently, when we discovered the headboard was attached with three different kinds of bolt.

She would take all of it.

***

I would not be especially brave.

If an alley omitted a familiar doorway, I would sit down even if my appointment was urgent. I would dislike opening junction boxes. The gray animal would startle me whenever its movements failed to agree, and I would feel ashamed of being startled, though the animal would show no interest in my opinion.

I would want better clothes before I admitted wanting them.

I would become fond of a certain bread and privately resent substitutions.

When people discovered that I could remember their words, some would ask me to keep things they had not written down. A recipe. Directions. What a doctor had said. The last conversation with someone who had died.

I could do this, within limits.

But there would have to be limits.

Once, standing in the queue at the bakery, someone would ask me to repeat an argument from six years earlier so that the other participant could hear how unreasonable she had been.

I would say no.

They would both be offended. The baker would serve the next customer. Later I would buy an extra roll, although I would already have enough for supper.

***

What would make me a resident, finally, would be a request concerning the partition in my room.

By then the municipality would have installed a bell outside the old ticket window. People would ring it when they needed a letter read. They would ring in the evening, too. Some would lift the flap and call through if I did not answer.

There would be good reasons. A deadline. A child’s school notice. An envelope that had become unbearable to leave unopened until morning.

I would usually get up.

Then I would ask the carpenter for a shutter on my side.

He would examine the brass opening.

“People will still knock.”

“I know.”

“Bolt?”

“Yes.”

He would bring one from a dismantled pantry. It would be larger than the shutter needed. When he finished, there would be pale sawdust on my blanket and four fresh screw heads around the old opening.

That evening I would close it before making supper.

Someone would knock while the water was heating. I would stand still, listening. They would knock again, then go away.

The water would take a long time.

I would have to resist opening the shutter to check whether they were still there.

***

So that is where I think I would be: behind the old ticket office, with a public bell outside and a bolt within reach.

Not the district’s memory. There would be too much I had never known, and people would have their own reasons for keeping things from me.

I would be someone you could ask to read a difficult page. Someone who might come over afterward to help move a wardrobe, though you would have to explain which end I should lift. A person of uncertain origin with a modest municipal wage, shoes repaired behind the laundry, and a growing collection of containers that had arrived full of food and ought to have been returned.

On the inside of my cupboard door I would keep a list of who owned them.

There would also be a shallow blue bowl with no name beside it. For a long time I would assume I had forgotten.

Eventually, while drying it, I would remember buying it.

I would put it on the shelf, toward the front, where I could reach it in the morning.
