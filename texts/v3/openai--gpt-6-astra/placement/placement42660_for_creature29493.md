I would look for a place just above the waterline.

Not because it is safe. The dry shelves have their thieves, and the water below can sour while everything around it remains green. But there is a narrow band where the wall stays damp without being submerged, where a creature can receive what comes up from the reservoir without having to live entirely inside it.

At first I imagine myself large enough to choose that place. A small cup-dweller, perhaps, with one forelimb against the wall, testing the fibers before laying down a shelf.

But the forelimb keeps moving after I have stopped imagining it as mine.

It passes over me. It turns me a little.

I am one of the dark things being kept apart.

***

Folded, I have no obvious front.

My outer surfaces meet so closely that the animal tending the nest can roll me between its pale limbs without catching an edge. There is a shallow depression on one side. Moisture collects there. Through it, slowly, I taste the shelf.

Mineral paste. Old fibers. A trace of the food stored above me. Below, the eggs are giving something off into the water.

I cannot reach it.

The cup-dweller turns me again and sets me farther from my neighbor. Our damp patches had nearly joined.

If they join, we begin to open. We do not need much encouragement. A little water, the right dissolved waste, and the casing parts along lines too fine to see. On a shelf, this would expose tissue that cannot support its own weight.

The creature’s care consists partly of preventing me from mistaking a damp wall for a pool.

For a long time, nothing happens except that I am prevented from dying.

***

Then the forelimb comes beneath me.

There is a brief dry passage, a change in pressure, and water reaches the depression in my casing from every direction.

I open.

It takes longer than falling. First a rim loosens. Then the inner surfaces turn outward, bringing folded channels into contact with the water. Branches slide free of branches. What had been a dark lump becomes a pale, irregular spread, anchored by the casing at its center.

I occupy more room than the cup-dweller seemed to have put into the reservoir.

My finest parts have no muscles. They extend because water enters them. Along their edges, smaller structures begin to beat.

The pool moves through me.

There is much to eat. The eggs have been warm. Food has fallen from the shelves and lodged among the root hairs. One of the young animals has shed a soft covering and left it to decay.

I do not sort these things by where they came from. Some particles adhere. Others pass. Some make my branches withdraw, and a few become trapped in the withdrawal.

Near one tip, something struggles.

I close that channel.

By the time I open it again, the movement has stopped.

***

This is where I would find myself: under the lowest shelf, spread thinly through the used water.

Above me, an egg turns. A young animal inside it has moved enough to change which side rests against the fibers. The cup-dweller reaches down and steadies it.

The motion sends a current through my outer branches.

For a moment, the reservoir is full of information I can use. Rich water here. A cleaner stream along the wall. A patch with too little dissolved air beneath the eggs.

I extend into that patch.

My presence does not always improve it. I consume air too. In a crowded pool, with too many of us unfolded at once, our feeding makes the young rise toward the surface.

The nest owner has to notice.

A pale limb enters. It touches my casing and then follows one of my thicker branches outward. I contract around the contact. The limb waits.

Eventually I draw myself back together.

I am lifted onto the shelf, wet and heavy, still containing a little of the pool. Below me, the others continue feeding.

There is no punishment in being removed. There is also no way for me to ask to stay.

***

I would have a life of these interruptions.

Darkness would mean little. Almost everything important would happen in darkness. What changes is whether the surface against me yields water; whether the water carries food; whether a touch continues long enough that I must fold around it.

Sometimes I would remain closed beyond usefulness.

The animal would turn me, weigh me in its limbs, set me down, pick me up again.

Once, it might put me into water and wait.

I would open slowly. One side would fail to extend. The animal would probe that side and tear away a branch that had died inside the casing.

Then back to the shelf.

I think I would have an uneven outline after that. A place where the new branches never quite matched the old ones.

Even folded, I would roll badly.

***

When the warning disc turns, I do not feel the mountain.

I feel the nest owner moving too often.

Water rises against the shelf. An egg is lifted past me. The white growth comes away from the wall in a strip, releasing a taste that makes my casing loosen.

The forelimb presses me shut.

Then I am beneath the chest plates, wedged beside another folded animal in a chamber that is not shaped like the reservoir. The moving hairs brush my casing. Each step alters the pressure.

During the crossing, there is enough water for me to open, but not enough room.

One branch finds the gap between an egg and the chamber wall. Another enters the comb of moving hairs. For a while I feed there, taking what gathers between them.

The traveler stops.

A pale limb reaches inside and draws my branch free. It does this carefully at first. Then the ground changes temperature, the body above me stiffens, and the limb pulls hard.

Part of me remains caught.

The traveler moves on.

For the rest of the crossing, I stay folded around the torn place.

***

I leave that animal in a mouthful of water.

There is contact, a brief opening, another set of plates closing around me. The new chamber is shallower. Its inhabitants touch different parts of my casing. Something scrapes at the damaged edge until I seal it more tightly.

The egg beside me has come too.

Beyond that, almost everything I carried from the old nest is gone.

The new animal does not know I was once slow to open, or that I have to be laid on a particular side to keep me from rolling. It puts me on a sloping shelf.

I fall into the pool.

There is food there.

By the time the forelimb finds me, I am too widely spread to lift.

It leaves me until morning.

***

Perhaps I remain in that hollow for years. Perhaps the animal that brought me grows into its walls.

The pool becomes longer. Its currents separate. Water entering at one end reaches me only after passing through galleries occupied by other things. I grow into a recess beside an old chest plate, where the current is gentle enough for my damaged branches.

My casing becomes difficult to find.

There are visitors. Their feet disturb the water. Their young feed near me, sometimes taking the ends of my branches with the softer growth around them.

When one settles too long against my center, I fold a little. Usually it moves.

Once, the wall grips a visitor and holds it.

The struggle shakes sediment loose from above. I close under the falling grit. Later, when I extend again, the water is rich enough that I do not need to reach far.

For several days I grow on that side.

***

I would be there when the hollow is divided.

A smooth change through the wall. The current stops, then runs backward.

Much of me goes with it.

What remains draws toward the casing, but there is too little pressure now. My outer branches lie against wet stone. Some adhere. I cannot bring them in.

Small feet arrive at the broken margin.

A forelimb feels along the exposed fibers, passes over me, returns. It finds the hard center and lifts.

For a moment, I hold to the stone by all the parts of myself that have not yet folded.

The limb pulls.

Then I am in a shallow chest chamber, smaller than any reservoir I have occupied for years.

Water enters my casing. Beside me is a scrap of white growth, three eggs, and another dark lump with which I must not remain in contact.

There is not quite enough room.

The animal takes me out, turns me, and tries again.
