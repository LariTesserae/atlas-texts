I would find myself in the raised seam of the fourth plate, where a little grit has collected and the shell never quite lies smooth.

At first I would occupy less space than a drop. Water would pass around me, carrying finer particles into the hollow: leaf dust, a grain of something pale from the mountain, a broken filament of moss. When the animal tightened beneath ice, the seam would narrow. When it bent around a root, the seam would open. My first root would grow into a place whose dimensions kept changing.

I would be the red-stemmed plant. But I would have begun rooting before this particular piece of darkness moved.

There would be enough food in the grit for two leaves. I would spread them close against the plate, where they could keep the damp beneath them. Their lower surfaces would take the shape of its scratches. One would be slightly larger than the other because a ridge of shell sheltered it from passing wood.

Then, during a thaw, the ground would tilt.

The moisture would gather on a different side of my root. A strand I had sent into a shallow groove would draw taut. For the first time, my leaves would brush something that was not part of the surface beneath me.

I would have no way to separate a journey from weather. Darkness would press against me, withdraw, return. Water would arrive from below. Once, light would remain long enough for both leaves to lift a fraction, and then a roof would pass over them.

I would grow very slowly.

What I could keep would depend on where I put it. A leaf raised on a long stalk would be caught under bark. A root extending beyond the plate would dry while the animal crossed an exposed arch. The little rootlets within the damaged seam would fare better. They would divide among the black grains, holding together the small amount of ground I had.

As they thickened, less of it would wash away.

The animal would carry me through chambers where plants like me could not have established themselves. There would be nights of uninterrupted wetness, longer than any wetness I could hold. There would be soft ceilings that combed my leaves flat, and passages where my newest tip scraped against wood for so long that its end browned.

Occasionally I would be brought into light.

I would spend what I had saved.

A third leaf would open while the animal rested beneath an outer crown. Small, stiff, red at the base. By the time it had fully spread, we would be underground again.

It would remain there, completed in darkness.

***

One season, the animal would stop beside a wall for much longer than usual.

Its injured plate would be close to a cushion of moss. Water would join the two surfaces. My longest root, following that continuous wetness, would enter the cushion.

There would be room.

For the first time, a root could lengthen without finding hard shell. It could pass between stems, branch in leaf mould, turn downward into a cool pocket that stayed wet after the exposed moss had begun to crisp. I would put out fine hairs there. Another leaf would swell against my stem.

Below me, the animal would remain sealed.

Snow would close the lane. Ice would thicken over the root-step. In the wall, my new root would grow by increments too small to distinguish from stillness.

By the thaw, it would have forked twice.

Then water would reach the animal’s foot.

Its first contractions would scarcely disturb me. The plates would settle differently beneath the grit. My leaves would tremble against the moss.

The animal would advance.

For a little while, the root would come with it. Moss stems would straighten. A buried leaf would shift toward the surface. Water would brighten along the stretched root, and the animal would pause, held by something too slight to have held it before.

Then it would pull again.

Something would give inside the wall.

I would leave with a piece of the cushion caught beneath me: several moss stems, the edge of the buried leaf, soil wrapped around the surviving fork. My newest leaf would strike the arch and fold backward. The animal would continue down the passage, carrying more than it had brought in.

Behind us, a short length of root would remain in the moss.

For some time, both broken ends would be alive.

***

I would be larger afterward, but unevenly so. One side anchored deep in the old split; the other trailing a little torn ground. The folded leaf would die. Its stalk would stay through several journeys, pale against the dark plate, until a low crossing scraped it away.

There might be another long rest. Another wall close enough to enter.

I would keep putting roots into whatever stayed wet.

And beneath me, the animal would follow water according to needs almost entirely different from mine. It would take me away from good light. It would save me from drying. It would carry me past a hundred places where I might have grown, and stop in a crevice where scarcely anything could.

There I would press a new leaf against its back.

Above the crevice, snow would be gathering on the upper terraces. A drop would enter, swell against my stem, and hold. Beneath it, in the grit of the repaired plate, a root tip would ease around a grain too large to move.
