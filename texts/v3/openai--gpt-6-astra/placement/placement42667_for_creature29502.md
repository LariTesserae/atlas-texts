I would find myself at the back of the step, where the cavity turns and becomes too narrow for the body sheltering in it.

Water passes through there, though very little else does. The stone on one side is faced and smooth; on the other, a broken aggregate projects from the wall. Between them is a softness that shifts when the pumps draw harder. At first I might take this for something caught—a fold of skin, a pale scrap pressed into the joint.

Then the suckers would open.

I think I would be the short arm. Or I would find myself most distinctly there, in the part of the animal that reaches with the others and arrives nowhere.

The rest of me would be difficult to locate so precisely. Some of it would be holding the underside of the stair. Some would be outside, tasting a dark streak along the paving. There would be an eye above me, turned toward the chamber, and a mouth I could reach by curling inward. The water moving through the mantle would lift and settle the crowded arms. I would belong to all of this without needing to gather it into a single place.

But here, at the new tip, the world would keep ending too soon.

A movement begins. Seven arms meet resistance: stone, shell, the underside of the tile. I extend into water. The suckers close on nothing, open again. My neighbor takes the weight.

Nothing stops to account for this.

There is work even for a short reach. Beneath the body, a shell fragment has lodged with its point against the skin. I can get between it and the stone. I turn it until the point faces outward; another arm carries it away. When food comes inward, I help hold it. The broken legs of a crab offer several small, definite surfaces. I fasten to one, and for a while the whole chamber is elsewhere.

I would have no picture of the arm as it was.

The missing length would be present in movements: a turn that leaves the body leaning, a spread that opens an uncovered space beneath the web. Sometimes I would follow an arm toward a hold and fail beside it. Sometimes I would curl early, fastening nearer than the movement seemed to require. The body would pass over me, and I would release.

Outside, the wrench keeps a little country of grit in place.

We go there after the draw. I travel mostly folded beneath the head, brushing the paving as the longer arms carry us. The floor is not smooth at this distance. There are pits with hard rims, grains that roll under a sucker, delicate tubes that break when held. A dark surface may adhere cleanly; a pale one may come away as powder.

At the wrench, an arm disappears into the scour hollow. Another stretches along the handle. I reach into the space between the corroded metal and the block beneath it.

Here the distance is small enough.

There is a shell in the gap. Its outer face is rough, its inner lip smooth. I turn it a little. Water gets underneath. Something moves against me and is gone before the shell falls back.

The longer arm comes around.

For a moment we touch each other under the wrench, sucker against sucker. Then it withdraws, and I go farther into the gap.

Farther is not very far. It is enough to meet the shell again.

—

I would grow without being able to watch it happen.

The water would remain cold. The valve would strike, and the body would gather me in. Brown water would arrive above the stair. Shells would change position at the entrance. A piece of tile that once needed two arms to shift might turn easily after grit had washed from beneath it, or resist because a fragment had lodged against its edge.

Among these changes, my reach would change too.

A place touched only when the body lowered would become available while it stood. A ridge would cease to be the far edge of an effort and become something I could pass. The suckers near the tip would find their purchase, release, and find another.

I would not know whether the chamber had become smaller.

One night we would climb toward the grille. Below us, the stair would disappear before the floor did. The body would flatten against a bracket while water pulled through the bars, and I would hold to a patch of ceramic exposed where the pipe’s coating had broken away. It would be smooth enough that every sucker touching it could seal.

A longer arm would go through the opening.

Then another.

The body would rise from the bracket, narrowing between iron bars. My hold would carry a little of its weight. More would come onto it as an arm outside searched for stone.

I would stay attached.

Then the hold beyond the wall would be found. The pull on me would ease. I would let go from the base outward, the smallest suckers releasing last.

Outside, the water would move across us from a different direction. There would still be masonry, but its joints would not be where the arms expected them. We would stop just beyond the opening and spread against the harbor wall.

I would reach down.

Under my tip, beyond the last secure contact, there would be another narrow seam. Water coming out of it would carry the taste of something sheltered inside. I would turn into it while the rest of the body remained still, working along one rough face, then the other, until the seam bent and I could no longer follow.

There I would stay a little while, touching the turn.
