I would look first for a room with two exits.

Not for safety, exactly. I would want to sit where the light from one opening stopped just short of the light from the other. There would be a strip of floor between them that remained cool through the afternoon. I would think: here, at least, I can tell what is inside and what is outside.

Then a foot would appear in one of the exits, and its shadow would fall through the other.

I would have to find a different way to be comfortable.

Perhaps I would spend a long time making that mistake—trying to choose a dwelling, judging the depth of its recess, imagining where to sleep—before noticing that the place I kept returning to was not a room at all. It would be the soft underside of a threshold, just above the lane that floods. A shallow groove there would stay damp after the rest of the street had dried.

Something would already be lying in it.

Eventually I would recognize the posture.

***

I would be a small animal, broad enough to cover three of the resident’s fingernails. At rest, I would resemble a husk caught under the wood: amber along the edges, darker in the middle, with a row of little openings down each side.

My back would be hard. My underside would not.

I could lift the hard part a little way from the surface beneath me, making a chamber between my belly and the street. Muscles around its rim would tighten and slacken. By changing that chamber’s shape, I could return a sound that had passed through the wood.

Not every sound. Most of the town’s roaring would go through me without anything happening. But a certain brief, coarse rattle would set the edges of my back trembling. I would hold its vibration after the caller stopped, then let it out again through the holes along my sides.

An animal on the upper terrace would answer.

I would lift my front end.

There would be something worth eating in that direction.

***

My food would be the soft growth inside recently opened seams.

Where two surfaces had become neighbors, moisture would gather between them. Fine pale threads would spread into it, followed by smaller things with no visible shape until they were crowded together. I would scrape these off with a mouth underneath my head.

Old seams would be poor feeding. Their surfaces fitted too closely. The best ones would still be adjusting, touching in some places and leaking in others.

This would make me restless.

I would cross beneath gutters, skirt the pools, and press myself against the lower lips of doorways. Sometimes I would put my front legs into a crack and wait while the rest of the route became available. I would have six legs, though it would often be difficult to establish that without annoying me.

The rattle would help me find the new seams. Its answering call would change when a passage opened nearby. I would not hear distance so much as the wetness, thickness, and resistance of the way between.

I would sometimes be wrong.

A splendid answer might lead to a dry cavity full of shed skins. I would scrape at them for a while, then leave. Once, I would find another animal like myself beneath the same threshold, returning my own return.

We would spend most of a morning encouraging each other.

When we finally met, we would fight over almost nothing.

***

The resident would notice me before I noticed it noticing.

I would be feeding beside a pale strip of skin. My little chamber would release a stored rattle. Along the strip, four stiff hairs would turn toward me.

I would stop chewing.

After a while, the skin would move away.

Behind it would be fresh moisture and a narrow bed of food.

I would follow.

This could happen often without becoming an arrangement. The resident would listen to me because my noises indicated surfaces still loose enough to move through. I would follow its movements because they exposed the things I ate. It might occasionally lift a heel with me beneath it. I might occasionally scrape an irritating growth from the edge of an old bite.

Neither of us would always benefit.

My shell could catch painfully beneath its skin. Its saliva could wash away a whole patch I had spent the afternoon finding. If it withdrew too quickly, I would tumble into the open lane and lie there with my legs working until I found an edge.

I would dislike the open lane.

The square would be worse.

***

There would be a season when the resident grew old enough to listen too closely.

By then my back would have acquired a thick dark margin. The chamber beneath it would no longer empty cleanly. Some vibrations would linger in a stiff fold and emerge much later, mixed with a call I had only just heard.

I would carry small remnants of the town as it had been.

This would not make me wise. It would make me less reliable at finding supper.

One evening, beneath the upper terrace, I would give back a sound from a passage that had closed.

The resident would begin to move.

A foot would tighten against a threshold. Something heavy would draw along the wall. There would be a faint change in the roaring, like a rough place appearing in a continuous sheet.

Then the movement would stop.

For a long time, the foot would remain where it was, gripping.

I would flatten myself. I would have no understanding of the old passage or the joint caught near it. But I would feel the strain through my belly, and the strain would keep me still.

When it finally eased, I would crawl away without feeding.

After that, whenever I returned to that threshold, I would press my chamber shut. Perhaps it would begin as a fear of the vibration. Perhaps it would persist because the folded posture was comfortable there.

It would look, from outside, as though I knew when to be quiet.

***

I would have other habits less easily mistaken for virtues.

I would steal eggs from the translucent-legged animals in the gutter. I would pry up loose edges of the plant’s skin, sometimes exposing more than I could eat. I would sleep through good feeding hours and travel recklessly just before the lane flooded.

The flood would carry me at least once.

My feet would lose their grip. The street would become a succession of raised seams passing beneath me too quickly to catch. Blind silver bodies would strike my shell. I would seal the openings along my sides, holding a little air, and be rolled toward the square.

A backward-pointing toe would catch me.

Not delicately. I would lose part of a leg.

For several minutes I would be pinned among grit, empty egg cases, and a broad torn leaf. Then the toe would relax. I would work free beneath the leaf and remain there until the water went away.

The leg would grow back crooked.

I would use it to clean the openings on my left side.

***

So I would be found under the lowest threshold, although not every day.

Someone looking for me would first see a dark husk, then a slight separation between it and the wood. Two short feelers would explore the air. If touched, I would cling so firmly that lifting me would risk breaking something.

Better to wait for the rain.

When the membrane swelled overhead, I would loosen my grip. The whole underside of my body would feel the town changing. Calls would arrive through old routes, new routes, routes available only for the length of that expansion.

I would turn slowly beneath my shell.

Near me, the resident’s foot would uncurl. Water would bead between its toes. The damaged leg on my left side would reach across a shallow groove and find purchase.

For a moment, there would be many places I could go.

Then the warm drops would begin to fall, and I would go a short distance toward food.
