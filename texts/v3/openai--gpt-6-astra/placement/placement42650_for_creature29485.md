I would find myself near the back of the hollow, where the moss gives way to bare wood. Not in the thickest green, but a little below it, under a projecting edge that keeps a narrow strip of soil damp.

From the clearing, this would hardly count as a place. An ant could step over it. Yet there would be room to go farther in, and a low part where moisture lingered, and a passage between two fibers that opened into something wider.

At first I imagine myself as another particle there. Pale, perhaps. Something uncovered when a crumb rolls away. The sideways light would not reach me, but its arrival would alter the wood above: a slow warming, followed much later by a change in the surface beneath my feet.

I would know very little of the clearing at once.

A moving leaf would not be a green banner. It would be an interruption overhead, accompanied by the disturbance of feet. The fern would be mostly the place from which water sometimes came. The root would have no whole shape. There would be wood nearby, wood higher up, wood continuing beyond any journey I made.

I am tempted by the little worker riding a leaf. There is something good in that precarious position: walking while being carried, finding a foothold on an edge another animal has cut. But that would take me toward the entrances, into a traffic whose urgency I do not quite find myself sharing.

I keep returning to the damp strip.

There is food on its surfaces, though from above it looks like nothing: a thin growth on a softened fiber, a particle with something edible adhering to it. I would move with my head close to the ground. Stop. Feed. Move around an obstruction rather than climb it. My route would have no destination beyond the next suitable patch.

A springtail, then. One of the pale ones, small enough that a wet piece of wood holds several kinds of shelter.

The jump would be part of me, but not the part I lived in most often.

Most of the time there would be six feet and a succession of close surfaces. My body would pass through a gap that looked closed from the other side. I would come out beneath the moss, among stems rising separately from the dark. Here the ground would be littered with things too small to litter the clearing: flakes, fragments, shed remnants of bodies. There would be no bare floor.

Others like me would be nearby. We would gather where the conditions suited us, separate as they changed. Sometimes I would touch one and turn. Sometimes we would feed within a body’s length of each other, each occupied with a different portion of the same decaying edge.

Then an ant would arrive.

First, perhaps, a shifting particle. Then an antenna entering the space in front of me, touching farther than I could reach. Its head would follow, dark and close.

The catch beneath my body would release.

For a moment there would be no purchase anywhere.

I would land against the curled leaf, well away from the moss. One foot would find its surface before the others. I would right myself and remain still.

The ant would not have followed. It might never have been approaching me at all.

Here, the leaf’s underside would still be damp, but farther along it the surface would have begun to dry. I would follow the fold toward the soil. Progress would be slow again. There would be feeding, an unsuitable patch, a turn back. The great escape would end in this small business of finding somewhere I could remain.

By evening, the ants’ new ridge might have blocked my earlier way. I would meet its foot without knowing when it had been made. Fresh grains would stand loosely against each other, with darkness in the gaps. I would test one opening, withdraw, move along the base.

Eventually there would be a place where the mound met the old wood, and enough space underneath.

That is where I would be when the last light touched the crest: below it, passing through a cool interval between grains, toward a softened fiber I had not fed on yet.
