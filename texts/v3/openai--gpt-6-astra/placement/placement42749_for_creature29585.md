At first I would put myself in the crack behind the animal: somewhere narrow enough that the water could pass over me without taking hold. But the crack is not separate from the bank. It receives the heavy note, and its smallest recesses may carry it more strongly than the exposed wall. Farther in, there might be no food. Farther out, a margin can be lifted.

I would keep looking for a place that was sheltered without being sealed.

There is such a place under the saddle, though it moves.

I would be one of the amber swellings. Not the largest. The one beside it, slightly flattened by the way the parent rests against the clay.

From outside, almost nothing about me would change for days.

Inside, I would be finding out how much of myself could bend.

At one end of the swelling there is room to double over. At the other, the membrane fits so closely that an attempted movement becomes a pressure against the parent’s underside. When the parent contracts, this pressure comes back. I straighten in the space left to me. Beside me, the larger young one flexes, briefly making my chamber narrower.

The river reaches us through several layers. Its cold arrives slowly. The weight of rising water comes more quickly. The vibration has been here for as long as there has been any part of me capable of answering it.

I have begun to contract before I have anywhere to retreat.

Sometimes the parent lifts enough for water to exchange beneath the saddle. Something fresher enters, and my movements grow stronger. Sometimes the folds close for so long that I become still. There is no way, inside the swelling, to make that opening happen.

My front edge develops pressed against the membrane. The two dark grains are already there. A fringe begins along either side, too short to lie down properly, each filament taking up space I do not yet have.

Then the chamber beside mine empties.

Its wall gives, and the larger young one passes into the folds beneath the parent. For a while it presses against me from outside. The contact is broad, then narrow. Eventually there is only water against that side.

I turn into the space it has left.

The membrane catches along my back. I pull, stop, pull again. One margin slips through while the other remains held, and for a long time I am twisted between the chamber and the wall.

What frees me is an ordinary adjustment of the parent’s grip.

The saddle shifts. Water enters. I come loose and fold badly, my underside against itself.

Here, even beneath the animal, there is enough current to move me.

I touch the clay with an edge that cannot hold. Then the broad, darker flesh above me lowers, pressing me gently flat. My underside meets the bank. A few grains adhere. When the parent lifts again, I stay.

This is where I would first become a shape of my own.

A shallow oval, almost clear. The amber was mostly the chamber around me. I have no notch, no thickened saddle worth seeing, and only a slight inequality between the part that goes first and the part that follows.

I spend some time moving toward the parent.

Its underside is wet and close. The folds contain particles already softened enough to take in. But each contraction dislodges me, and the place I try to enter keeps disappearing. Once I get a margin beneath a fold and remain there through several changes of pressure. Then the fold opens, and I am outside it again.

Along the clay is a narrow, slick band.

I cross it, stop, and turn until more of my fringe touches it. Something comes free beneath me. I advance a little. The band continues around a grain too large for me to pass over, so I travel around the grain as well.

Behind me, the parent spreads across the place where I opened.

I could still reach it. For a while one edge of my body remains in contact with its fringe. The filaments move against mine, admitting almost no water between them.

Then I follow the band beyond that contact.

The next heavy beat closes me against the wall. My leading edge is caught extended, and I draw the rest of myself toward it rather than pulling it back. This leaves me a little farther along the track.

When the beat comes again, the same thing happens.

I make a slow, wasteful progress through disturbance, feeding only in the intervals. Once I turn across the track and reach bare clay. The grains shift beneath my grip. I fold back over ground I have already cleared until the slippery band touches my margin again.

By then the water has risen.

It carries the loosened grains away. A filament lifts from my side and stays lifted. Then another. I tighten, but I am too small for the groove that sheltered the snail; water passes through it around me.

My rear edge comes free.

For a moment I hang from the front alone, stretched thin enough that the dark grains nearly meet. A shred of leaf brushes the wall. I catch its softened edge without releasing the clay.

The leaf turns.

I go with it.

There would be no view of the hollow leaving me, only the loss of the wall and a new surface yielding beneath my grip. I spread where two veins meet. Water works under the leaf, folds it, opens it again. Once we strike something hard.

When we stop, I am underneath.

One torn edge of the leaf rests against a root. Fine matter is collecting there, trembling but not yet washing away. I extend a little of my fringe into it.

Above me, the leaf lifts and settles.
