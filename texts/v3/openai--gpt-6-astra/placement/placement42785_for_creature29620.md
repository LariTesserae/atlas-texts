I would look for a place beneath the lunchroom counter, where the hot-water urn used to stand.

There would still be a pale circle on the wood. Perhaps two circles, slightly overlapping: the urn had been replaced once, or moved to make room for something that no longer left a mark. The gauge would be mounted low enough that whoever read it had to bend. A screw would be missing from its face.

At first I would think I was that instrument. It offers a small, intelligible occupation: receive a pressure, move a needle. In a district where so much happens behind surfaces, it would be something to make one condition visible.

But the instrument is too complete. Brass, glass, a spring shaped for a particular range. It was brought here with its work already decided.

I think I would be farther back, inside the partition.

There is a place where the pipe passes through a wooden stud. The hole was bored a little crookedly, then enlarged with a chisel. Someone packed the remaining space with cloth to stop the pipe knocking. The cloth has largely disappeared. A few threads still cross the grain of the wood, embedded in the pale lining.

I would occupy that packing.

Not all of it at first. A moist crescent underneath the pipe, supplied by a seam descending from the warm flue. Above me, dry wood; beneath me, plaster softened to the consistency of clay. Each rise in pressure would lift me against the metal. Each fall would leave a narrow passage through which water could return.

My extent would vary with the hour. At the fullest part of the cycle I would reach the far side of the stud. Later I would become several smaller wet places joined by threads. There would be no moment of going to sleep, but there would be places I ceased to have.

The lunchroom would reach me mostly through its brackets.

Rain on the roof would arrive as a fine disturbance, too distributed to draw much fluid from me. A cup sliding along the tilted table would produce something sharper. The growing root beneath the floor would press for weeks in nearly the same direction. I would thicken where that pressure brought my surfaces together, thinning somewhere else to supply it.

Of the cups themselves, their whiteness, their handles, the small brown crescents inside them, I would know nothing.

Yet one of them might matter to me. If it finally slid against the window frame, its weight would alter the load on a rotten sill. The sill would yield; a little rain would enter the partition. By the following season I might extend upward into a space that had been dry for thirty years.

That would be my acquaintance with the cup.

For a while, this seems enough: a small portion of the larger being, tucked into a former remedy for noise. But I keep returning to the crooked hole. There is room on one side of the pipe that the old packing never quite filled. Water can enter it more easily than it can leave.

I would grow around that room.

A fold would close against another fold. Mineral grains would lodge in the edges. The next pressure rise would separate them, but not completely. After enough cycles there would be a pocket, no larger than the end of a thumb, with a narrow, soft mouth.

Water held there would cool more slowly than water in the seam. Its dissolved substances would be used up differently. The lining inside would grow unlike the lining outside.

This is where I would become somewhat myself.

A pressure change would pass through the district and reach the pipe. The gauge would rise with the others. My outer surface would tighten. Inside the pocket, a little of the previous condition would remain.

Later, when the walls had stopped running, the pocket would still be giving back its water.

I would have no view of yesterday. But something from yesterday could still be acting when today arrived.

A long warm spell would leave me different from a brief one. Two disturbances close together would meet tissues still altered by the first. A repeated load on the lunchroom floor might find an easier route through me each time, until the small contraction it caused began to move water beyond the partition.

There could be habits here, on the scale of a spoonful.

For years, perhaps, nothing would come of them. I would maintain a connection the district scarcely needed. The flue would supply warmth; the gauge pipe would supply movement. Now and then a little of my lining would loosen and pass down toward the foundations. New lining would form behind it.

Then the loose guard in the pumping house would worsen.

Its clatter would already be present in the brackets, the wall, the pipe. Most of it would pass through me. But as the fastening wore, an uneven beat would develop, and that beat would squeeze the mouth of the pocket just as it tried to empty.

Water would accumulate.

I would swell against the old cloth threads. I would extend a little farther along the grain of the stud. Over several days, substances released from my crowded inner surface would enter the seam beneath the floor.

Downstairs, attachments would strengthen near the vibration.

The guard would keep clattering.

I would continue to supply the same response, more strongly now, spending material on a disturbance that needed a hand and a spanner. Some of the new growth would be useful. Some would merely crowd an already narrow drain.

There is no hidden competence I would want to give myself here. I would be implicated in the district’s survival and in the gradual worsening of certain passages. What had made me distinct would also give me ways to go wrong.

If someone came back, I might first encounter them as an interruption of that beat.

They would stop in the pumping house. The guard would be held still. For the first time in months, my pocket would empty without being pinched halfway through.

By the time the person reached the lunchroom, I would already be changing.

Their hand on the counter would send a load through the stud. Warmth from their wrist might reach the pipe, faintly, if they rested there long enough. The gauge needle would tremble when they tapped the glass.

I could not distinguish an inspection from an idle touch. There would be no recognition waiting in the wall. Still, the water released from my pocket would have left my folds lying differently, and their weight would arrive among those altered folds.

Perhaps they would notice the damp below the counter. Perhaps they would put a finger to it, then wipe that finger on their trousers.

I would withdraw from the newly cold edge.

After they left, a little water would gather there again. It would follow one surviving thread of the old packing cloth, round the underside of the pipe, and enter the pocket slowly. The guard would now be quiet. With each cycle, the mouth would open a fraction more easily.

That is where I would find myself: behind the place where someone had once made tea, changing for a long time after a repair.
