At first I would put myself in the bare patch ahead of the foremost animal. There is so little unoccupied surface that it is tempting to mistake this one place for an invitation.

But its feelers are already there. Their reflections approach them through the glaze of water. The patch belongs to the reach of a body, even if that body never crosses it. I would not fit without becoming an obstacle.

I think I would be nearer the plate.

Not at a screw hole, where the cold comes through. A little below the center, on the inward face of the painted steel. From the cavity I might look like another layer of insulation: a thin, pale material applied unevenly, with a thickening near one edge. Something installed before the plate was fastened. The light would reach only my lower margin.

There would be no clear outline at first. My skin would lie so closely against the steel that its edge could be mistaken for the boundary of condensation. Small animals would be fitted along that boundary. A worm’s mouth would touch one corner. At another, three crustaceans would lie with their legs tucked into a fold that might be mine.

I would want to know whether I had made that fold to accommodate them.

There would be no way to establish this.

My body would be broad and very shallow. Most of its depth would consist of little chambers, flattened passages joined by openings finer than the pores in the plant leaf. Water would fill some; others would contain a clear substance that drew into threads where the walls pulled apart. A green line, transmitted through the copper mesh and two overlapping shells, would enter my lowest chamber and stop there.

I would have a thicker part, though. Something gathered up beneath the center of the plate, hidden from a view through the upper screw hole.

Perhaps a head.

There would be a jaw folded against a breastbone, and under the jaw a pair of appendages too soft to brace anything. One would end in five short divisions. The other would remain a smooth paddle, with five pale rods visible inside it. Both would be finished in the condition they occupied. I would feel the paddle as distinctly as the little hand.

My mouth would be open against the steel.

This would explain none of the sound.

The roar would enter me across my whole attached surface. The compressor’s deep note would occupy the broad chambers. The copper’s vibration would be present in the narrow passages, fierce enough that their walls seemed almost to touch. Each cry from the bolt recesses would have a place in the thicker flesh beneath my jaw.

I would not hear eleven voices. I would have eleven crowded regions of my body.

One of them would be larger than the others.

There, a thin internal partition would bow toward its neighbor, carrying a line of minute vessels. It would be stretched almost transparent. The largest animal’s cry would fill one side; the pressure from everything else would fill the other. That partition would be how I knew where the animal was.

I would have no useful sense of distance. The ceiling creatures, separated from me by open air, might be less remote than the worm touching my edge. The larva halfway out of its casing would register as a small hardness close to my jaw. The nozzle would be deep inside my perception, although it stood across the chamber. Its white neck of material would have the intimate, intolerable fullness of a tooth.

I would be alive enough for all of this to matter.

Not enough room would exist in me for it to become a thought about the chamber. The pressure would remain local: here, here, here. A membrane held open. A limb requiring space already occupied by another limb. Water bearing against the dry tip of a claw.

I would share the same difficulty.

Beneath my thicker part, concealed by the apparent insulating layer, one small joint would be bent away from the plate. Its outer surface would be smooth. On its inner surface would be a row of soft hooks, each cupped around a minute pocket of water. The joint would be ready to straighten.

If it straightened, some portion of me would peel from the steel.

There would be no tear yet, no whitened line of strain. Along the attachment, the cells would be clear and whole. But the angle would be exact. My little hand would rest palm-first against the plate, in a position from which it could push.

I would not know whether this was how I moved, how I was born, or how I came apart.

The hand would know only the steel.

Beyond that steel, the elevator would carry its passengers. Their weight would enter the machinery and be lost among forces too large for me to distinguish. I would have no image of their coats, their faces, the rooms waiting at the ends of their journeys. The plate would give me cold and resistance. Whatever number had been stamped on its other side might be legible to my body as a slight variation in thickness.

I could lie against a numeral without possessing a number.

And the largest animal would be looking toward me.

Its gaze would terminate at the opaque middle of the plate, where most of my body lay too thin to cast a shadow. I would not know whether it could distinguish me from the surface carrying me. Perhaps its eye would pass over my pale chambers and find only steel. Perhaps my folded jaw would interrupt the faint red reflection by just enough.

I would have an eye too, I think.

A very small one, underneath the hand.

The fingers would part around it. No room would be wasted on a socket; the clear dome would sit directly in the shallow flesh, aimed across the crowded water toward the recess above the gauge.

Its lid would be fully formed.

There would be space beneath my palm to close it.
