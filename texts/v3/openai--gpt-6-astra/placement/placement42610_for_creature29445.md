I would be on the cooler side of a ridge, where the trunks stand a little farther apart and a long leaf has come to rest across a break in the stone.

You could lift the leaf and still fail to find me.

My back would have the brown dust of that slope upon it. Along one edge, a snail would have cleared a narrow track, exposing the milky tissue beneath. Three of my limbs would be folded out of sight. A fourth would reach into the break, attached to its lower wall, so that part of what appeared to be my body was actually a space I had enclosed.

I think I would be older than the animal beneath the eastern slab, but not among the oldest. Wide enough to shelter something without noticing it. Still clear enough, when stretched between two holds, for light to show where my muscles gathered.

At first, most of my life would happen beneath me.

Water rises through a seam. I keep it close. There is a difference between the water arriving and the water leaving, and my mouth moves within that difference. Some passages open; others narrow. The folds are dark rust in their deepest creases.

Above, the leaf shifts with the light.

One of my discs rests against its underside. Over a long time, the leaf thickens around the place I touch it. My disc moves a little farther out. Neither of us gives up the contact.

I have occupied this seam long enough for a ribbon root to pass through a groove beside my mouth. It enters from behind, follows the protected channel beneath me, and emerges between two limbs. The root has thickened. Sometimes I raise myself to let more water past it.

Then, very gradually, the seepage changes.

Its warmth remains. Something else diminishes. I open the passages wider and keep them open longer. The rust-colored folds grow paler at their tips.

For several days I draw my margins inward without releasing the stone.

The leaf slides off.

There would be a clean shape beneath me when I finally moved, though not a complete one: islands of sediment, the pale root, a crescent where water had passed continually. My departure would leave several different accounts of how I had rested.

The root holds me at first.

I go a little way and wait. The stretched part of it lifts clear of the sediment. One of my limbs remains behind, attached near the old fissure, while the others find purchase farther down.

By the time that limb releases, a finer branch of the root has broken. The main length comes with me.

I carry it through the forest.

It catches against a trunk. I turn back toward the resistance, shortening my body until the tension eases. For a while I rest with the trunk between two folds of my back. Then I reach around it from the other side.

This is much of what travelling would be like for me. A succession of places where the whole body cannot follow its first reaching limb.

Once, a fish passes so close that I leave the slope.

I gather, throw myself, open badly. Light occupies all the places where stone should be. A crown of leaves receives me, and I hang among them with two discs fastened to the same leaf.

It takes until the light has changed to get down.

But I keep going.

I would come to the rim without chambers full of young.

Perhaps I have come before. There is a broad, shallow crease across my back which fits no rock I encounter on the way. When I reach the last ledge, I settle into it easily.

Beyond the stone, my free limb opens.

The boundary is cooler than the fissures. It gives beneath the disc, slowly enough that I continue extending after I have touched it. Another limb follows. Then another.

My last hold on the mountain is deep inside a crack. To release it, I must fold the disc twice.

For a little while I remain drawn toward that empty hold.

Then my weight spreads through the water’s edge.

Below me, a cloud opens. Light enters my body through surfaces usually pressed against sediment. The root I have carried hangs along my flank, its smallest branches suspended in brightness. One tip reaches the boundary and bends there.

Nearby, another animal is already resting.

We do not come close all at once. As the afternoon cools, our bodies spread, and the outermost disc of its reaching limb meets mine. The ridges settle across one another. There is a slight pull when either of us changes shape.

After sunset, something touches the underside of my limb.

It is small enough that at first I close my disc.

Then the touch divides. Several little holds, shifting independently. A young animal has fastened itself there, and its remaining limbs reach into the water.

I keep still.

When it releases, it moves outward. I extend beneath it and curl my limb toward the lake. The small body meets me again, folds, opens. This time it drifts past my edge on the inward side.

Through the night, that happens more than once.

By morning the other adult has thickened and begun to withdraw toward the ledge. Our discs separate. A faint impression of its ridges stays in mine.

I remain until the carried root catches among the dark stems.

Then I begin returning to stone, leaving it there.

Somewhere along the inner slope I will find warmth moving through a fracture. I will settle badly at first. My back will still hold the curve of the boundary, and a narrow space will remain beneath one side.

Water will pass through that space.

Over the next few days, I will lower myself into the shape of the new place. One limb will stay open beyond my body, turning slowly among the leaves.
