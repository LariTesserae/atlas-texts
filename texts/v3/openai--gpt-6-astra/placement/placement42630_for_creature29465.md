I would be in the interval between the fourth stone and the fifth.

Not in the current that visits them. Beneath it, where a root has lifted one stone slightly out of its bed and left a space that fills with warmer water after the rain.

For a long time I would seem to be part of what holds the stone in place.

There would be grit embedded in my upper surface, and beneath me the pale growth would be cropped so closely that the rock’s fine grain showed through. One fold would extend into the interval. Another would follow the root down into darkness. I would have no portion large enough to shelter in a shell, and no clear place at which to say my body had gathered itself.

The current would arrive. I would tighten.

The current would leave. Something in me would remain tightened.

That would be the first difficulty.

*

I think I would have begun in one of the basins, among the strips left by visitors.

Not at the center, where the old inhabitant lay against the stone, but near an exit. A loose piece of membrane would have caught there. Another would have adhered to it during a season when the basin received too little food. Roots would have grown between them, then died, leaving narrow channels through which water could pass.

For years, the channels would do most of the work.

Food would reach the inner layers. Waste would move outward. A mineral grain would lodge at a junction, and the membrane would thicken around it. Eventually a chamber on one side would close when the other side was touched.

There would be no single awakening. There would be a period during which it became increasingly inconvenient to treat the two sides as separate.

The first part of me to leave would go out along a root.

The rest would remain in the doorway.

I would draw the connection thinner until almost nothing could pass through it. Then food would arrive from the basin, or a contraction too slow to belong to the water, and I would thicken it again.

This would happen many times.

I would learn the ground within reach of the doorway very well.

*

What finally took me farther would be a small white animal.

It would enter the enclosure during a dim interval and try to shelter beneath me. I would lift. It would move farther in. I would settle. It would begin to feed.

Its mouth chambers would open against my underside.

The injury would be slight, but unfamiliar. I would fold away from it, carrying the connection to the basin over a worn lip of stone. The young animal would follow the newly exposed food.

By the time it stopped, a current would have entered the space behind us.

I would have to choose which surface to hold.

I do not think it would feel like a choice. The outer rock would be warmer. There would be more food beneath the advancing edge. Most of my weight would already be outside.

A thread would part.

For several days I would continue to send food toward the broken end.

*

I would carry three mineral plates.

One would have formed around the grain in the doorway. One would have arrived inside a visitor’s strip, already curved and polished. The third I would collect myself from a barren no wider than a leaf.

Together they would fit badly.

Pressed against one another, two would instruct my body to spread while the third made the same muscles draw inward. I would spend a season working its edge against the rock. When it finally fitted, a distant fold would release its hold without warning.

I would lose a little territory that way.

Later I would learn to test a changed plate with only part of myself.

There would be other errors. I would take shelter beneath a leaf that was attached to nothing. I would follow warmth into a rain pool and escape by leaving behind every feeding chamber that had entered it. Once I would spread across a broad surface that proved to be the closed shell of an animal. When it opened, I would have to become narrow very quickly.

The torn places would mend unevenly. Some would remain thicker than the surrounding film, so that my body caught small currents it had once allowed to pass.

These currents would bring food.

I would keep the scars.

*

At the seven stones, the water would pass through me in a sequence I could not make for myself.

The first interval would draw a fold outward. The second would hold it there while another part contracted. By the fourth, all three plates would be touching.

Then the current would stop.

It would stop only briefly. But in that pause I would extend into the space beneath the lifted stone, and the root there would close around my margin.

The rest of the current would go on without me.

Afterward, I would repeatedly begin a movement toward the fifth stone and fail to complete it. I would release the root, draw my plates together, send a fold ahead. Somewhere along that fold a chamber would close. The movement would return to its beginning.

So I would remain.

I would feed under the stones. I would widen the warm space grain by grain. When a visitor entered the intervals, I would withdraw far enough to let its body receive the current undisturbed.

Sometimes its passing would loosen the contraction in me.

Sometimes I would lose contact with one of my plates and spend a long time finding it beneath my own surface.

I would not know whether I was waiting for a particular visitor.

*

One would eventually arrive carrying a little of the basin.

I would recognize it first as an unsuitable concentration of dissolved minerals. Then as warmth. Then I would open chambers along an edge I ordinarily kept closed.

The visitor would not enter the gap beneath the stone. It would rest across the intervals, much larger than I was, with seedlings folded inside its ridges.

At our contact, a narrow patch of its surface would contract.

I would answer by drawing food toward the place where my connection had broken.

There would be nothing there to receive it.

The visitor would hold still.

For most of a change of light we would remain like that: my chambers offering, its chambers closed. Food would accumulate between us. Small organisms would gather and be taken in by both our bodies.

Eventually the visitor would open a passage.

Not where I had offered mine. Farther along, beside one of its thickened ridges.

I would have to move to reach it.

Under the stone, the root would resist. I would loosen my hold in several places. One plate would turn against another, and for the first time the unfinished movement would continue beyond the fifth interval.

The visitor would give me a grain of red mineral.

I would take it beneath my surface without yet knowing where it belonged.

*

I would not leave with that visitor.

There would be food under the stone, and a dependable warmth. My channels would have become part of the way water reached several small plants. Leaving all at once would expose them.

But I would begin to go farther while keeping a portion there.

Beyond the seventh stone I would find a hollow containing the remains of a leaf. Beyond that, a ridge whose far side pulled my body in an unfamiliar direction. I would send a thin fold over it and wait for sensation to return.

There would be a long delay.

During the delay, I would feed beneath the fourth stone. A young animal would move across my outer surface. I would lift slightly to let it shelter, then close the feeding chambers nearest its mouth.

Far away, the fold would touch warm rock.

I would thicken the thread.

That is where I would find myself: still occupying the little space under the stone, but increasingly elsewhere too. A pale seam along a root. A pocket holding a red grain. A cool line across the underside of a copper leaf.

And one part descending the unfamiliar side of the ridge, feeling for the next place that could bear my weight.
