At first I would look like something the foot had left behind.

A pale scrap caught on a root after the sole withdrew. Slightly curled, with a wet underside and a rim of stiff hairs. You might take me for shed skin until I began pulling myself uphill.

I would be about the length of the inhabitant’s smallest toe. Flat when frightened; thicker after feeding. My back would have the dull translucence of a soaked seed, showing a dark thread of gut and, beside it, two little organs that alternately tightened. I would have legs, but you would need to turn me over to count them, and I would hold on hard enough to make that an unkind thing to do.

I think I would live near the place where the throat lies beside the drainage channel.

Not inside the throat. In the narrow, damp interval between its outer skin and a root that keeps trying to grow toward it.

There would be a good deal to eat there.

***

My mouth would be small and inconveniently delicate.

I could not bite into an intact leaf. I would feed along softened edges, where cold or pressure had broken a few cells and the contents seeped out. The drainage channel would bring such material to me: scraps from the swimmers’ meals, a film of sweetness from a bruised stem, particles too heavy to remain in the pool.

When the inhabitant swallowed, the current would strengthen.

I would brace myself with the rear legs and open two folds beneath my head. They would fill like little pockets. For a while I would be almost unable to move, attached to the root by my hindmost feet, eating what passed.

This would make the inhabitant’s hunger the principal weather of my life.

I would not know that it was hunger. I would know the pull arriving through the water, the trembling of my foothold, the rich drift that followed. Sometimes I would unfold too soon and wait in still water with my mouthparts spread.

Sometimes the throat would contract during sleep.

Then the current would reverse, and I would have to close myself quickly against the taste of things that had already been taken in.

***

My shelter would not stay the same size.

In one winter the root would thicken against the throat until I could sleep only on my side. In another the throat would shift, leaving a gap so wide that grazers entered and rasped at the wall beside my head.

I would pack the opening with empty seed coats.

There would be no special skill in this at first. A seed coat would snag on my hairs, come loose at the entrance, and make the water quieter inside. I would bring another. Eventually I would have a loose, rattling door, which I pushed through bodily and repaired whenever the current carried part of it away.

One of the coats would be reddish brown and too hard to soften.

I would keep it longer than the others.

If displaced, I would retrieve that one first.

***

I would make excursions between meals.

My underside would distinguish several kinds of surface that a hand would call smooth. A living leaf would have a fine directional drag. The inhabitant’s skin would yield, then very slowly return pressure. A root preparing to fold distance would feel briefly slippery even where it was dry.

I would avoid that last sensation.

Once, crossing a narrow runner, my front legs would find a foothold while the rear legs remained somewhere that had become much colder.

There would be no visible gap in me. Nevertheless, I would be stretched.

I would stay that way through several rounds of drumming, pulling carefully, then violently. When I came free, one leg would remain hooked into the runner.

Afterward I would turn back from certain perfectly ordinary-looking paths.

The missing leg would grow again, smaller. In sleep I would tuck it beneath the others.

***

The first time I crossed the inhabitant’s hand, I would not recognize skin I had already encountered beside the channel.

This skin would be newer. It would have a different salt, a tighter surface. The palm would still be shallow, with its crease collecting a clear liquid in which there was almost nothing to eat.

I would investigate anyway.

The fingers would begin to close.

Very little, and very slowly—but enough to change the slope beneath me. I would hurry toward the wrist. The hairs on my back would catch against the descending thumb.

For some time we would remain there.

I would pull. The thumb would tighten.

I would stop. Eventually it would ease.

I would escape with several hairs left in the fold of skin.

On my next excursion I would go around the hand.

On the next, too.

But the palm would be warmer than the route beside it, and there would come a day when I was carrying more food than my legs could comfortably bear.

***

Perhaps this is where I would become particular: in returning before I had quite ceased to be afraid.

I would discover that the hand moved less if I crossed it slowly. That pressing myself flat did not help, but touching down with the front feet and waiting sometimes did.

The inhabitant would discover something as well.

At first its fingers would answer every movement. Later they would remain open while I passed between them. I would pause to clean a leg. A fingertip would lift, stop, and lower without touching me.

Neither of us would be consistently good at this.

Sometimes I would run for no reason it could feel. Sometimes the hand would close because a swimmer had disturbed water beneath the head, and I would be caught in a response to another part of the hollow.

But there would be crossings in which we caused each other no trouble.

I would begin using that route regularly.

***

I would not be its keeper.

If the tissue beside my shelter softened enough, I would eat it. Not deeply; I would be too small. Still, there would sometimes be a pale rough patch on the throat where I had fed through a long interval of still water.

And I would not understand its eventual size.

The hand would be a warm crossing. The throat would be one wall of my house. A foot would be an intermittent hill on which unfamiliar seeds appeared.

When those places began moving toward one another, I would lose paths I had followed all my life.

My seed-coat door would collapse. Water would enter from the wrong side. I would spend a long time searching for the reddish coat before abandoning it.

There might be room for me beneath the jaw, among the roots of the folded fringe. Or I might retreat into a leaf chamber and become an animal that seldom approached the body at all.

I would have to find out by touching things.

***

For now, though, I would be on the large seed.

It would have a split near one end, just wide enough for my mouth. Each time the foot turned it, a little sweetness would rise from the seam.

I would follow the seam around.

When the second foot approached, its pressure would reach me through the shell. I would stop feeding. The seed would tilt; all my legs would tighten.

For the first time, the two soles would hold it between them.

The split would widen slightly.

I would put my head into the opening.

Around me, the feet would make small corrections. One toe would press too hard, another withdraw too far. The seed would slip and be caught again. I would flatten myself against it, waiting out each movement, then return to the food.

After a while the grip would become steady.

There would be three of us occupied with the seed, each feeling a different part of what was happening.
