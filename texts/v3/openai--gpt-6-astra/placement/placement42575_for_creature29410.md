I would look first beneath the arcade, where the fallen hood lies on its side.

Not inside it immediately. There is a narrow place between its outer wall and the ground, sheltered from the basin but open toward the cold current. Something passing through has rubbed the chalk smooth. I would fit there, though at first I might mistake the smoothness for the place I occupied, rather than something I had done.

When a grain rolled against me, part of the wall would move.

I think I would have a soft back and a firmer underside, with a shallow groove running the length of it. Lying still, I would resemble a flake that had almost come loose. My edges would be thin enough to admit the light. Farther inward, there would be a little amber cloud, not quite central, which contracted when I fed.

I would not know the whole chamber. The roof blister above the shells would be farther away than I ordinarily travelled. Of the oldest hood I would know chiefly its cold, arriving along the ground and dividing around small obstructions. Sometimes it would reach me through the fallen doorway. Sometimes from behind. This difference would be enough to wake me.

My movement would begin underneath.

Two short lengths of skin would thicken against the chalk. Between them, a third would reach forward and feel for purchase. I would advance by drawing these points together, leaving a damp mark shaped like a narrow leaf. On established ground the mark would disappear quickly. In the intervals between islands, it would hang for a little while with nothing visibly beneath it.

I would seldom cross those intervals.

There would be a route around the fallen hood, through its doorway, along the inside wall, and out through a break in the roof. Most of my life would occur on that route. In one place I would have to flatten. In another, turn partly over. The turn would bring my mouth against a seam where the material of the hood had not quite joined.

There would usually be something to eat there.

Not the wall itself, but the softer matter accumulating between its grains: a colourless film, thickest after water, faintly bitter when the stair had gone unused. I would press my underside to it. Within the groove, several small mouths would open in succession.

From outside, this would look like resting.

The animal carrying grains among the shells would pass close enough for its belly to brush my back. I would draw in my edges but not leave. Occasionally a grain would fall from its chest and lodge against me. I would work around it for hours, trying to regain contact with the wall.

Some grains I could dislodge.

One I could not. It would remain in the seam through several arrivals of water, and I would grow around the inconvenience. Eventually there would be a notch in my side that corresponded to it. Removed from the wall, I might appear to have been bitten.

I would have been shaped by a grain that someone else had dropped.

When the great drop gathered, moisture would thicken beneath me before I could sense anything overhead. My small mouths would close. I would withdraw through the broken roof into the hood, taking the route backward, and settle against its highest inner curve.

The hood was built for a different position. Its highest place would once have been a side wall. There would be shallow hollows there where its maker’s folds had rested.

I would fit badly into one of them.

Below me, the first water would enter through the polished doorway. It would advance without ripples until it touched the opposite wall. Then the whole little interior would fill with movement.

A swimmer would pass beneath my belly.

For a moment I would feel its bright knot through my skin, a warmth so brief that I would contract after it had gone. Others would follow. One might turn in the hollow beside me and remain there while the water lowered.

I would not help it.

When the last wetness drew down through the doorway, I would come out and feed along the line it had left. If the swimmer remained, I would feed around it until its movements stopped, and then there too.

Afterward the amber cloud inside me would darken.

There might be young, though I would not carry them visibly. Along the thin edge of my back, a few places would cease to lie flat. Each would curl inward, making a pocket no wider than the mouth of a burrow. For a long time the pockets would contain only moisture.

Then one would be empty.

On the inner wall of the hood, slightly above my usual path, a fresh damp mark would appear. It would lead almost to the broken roof and stop. I would pass beneath it without turning.

The young would have to find seams of their own.

And when someone lingered on the stair, I would feel the fallen hood change shape around me. Its doorway would narrow. The grain in my notch would press more deeply into my side. Outside, feet would stop, cold would travel, and somewhere beyond the shells the hood-maker would unfold.

I would keep moving.

Not far: through the doorway and halfway around its lip, to where pressure had opened a minute split in the old secretion.

There would be fresh food in it.

I would spread across the split, amber centre drawing close to the surface, edges almost invisible. Above me, the weight would lift. The crack would begin to close.

For a little longer, I would be thick enough to hold it open.
