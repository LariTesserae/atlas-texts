At first I would find myself somewhere that keeps changing its height.

There would be mornings when the sky lay close above me, open except for three immense crowns. Other mornings, a leaf would occupy nearly everything. Its veins would stand out against the light. Water would travel along them, hesitate at a junction, then take a direction I could not have predicted.

I would have no view of the river.

Even when carried to the lower lip, I would see only a brightness beyond the stone. My whole body would incline toward it. Then the ground would turn beneath me, slowly and with considerable effort, and I would be brought back into shade.

For a while, this would be how I understood weather.

Shade arrives from one side. Warmth rises from below. The surface holding me contracts, and a little of yesterday’s water comes up around my feet.

—

I think I would be among the small growths at the rear of the animal’s back.

Not the tallest. Something close-set, with a handful of blunt, translucent leaves. In sunlight their edges would look almost white. Under a fallen frond they would become so dark a green that the spaces between them would be easier to see than the leaves themselves.

My hold would be shallow. Fine threads would spread through the debris caught in a crease of skin: a little bark dust, the casing of something hatched long ago, a fern scale that had softened but not yet come apart. Below this would be the animal’s firm surface. I could hold to it, but not enter it.

When the crease opened, my threads would tighten.

When it closed, I would have to endure being folded.

There would be a damaged leaf on one side, pinched early in its growth. It would never flatten. Water would collect inside it and stay after the other leaves had dried.

This might be useful. It would still be damage.

I would grow unevenly around it.

—

The animal would not know me as a whole.

It might know a slight pull when I had gone too long without water. It might know the patch of coolness I retained after the rest of its back had warmed. Once in a long while, one of its hands would reach toward me and stop short. The effort would change the slope on which I stood.

I would feel the reaching through my roots.

Then the hand would withdraw, and the animal would go on carrying me.

There would be no agreement between us. I would be there because a wet fragment had lodged where it could remain wet. The animal would have acquired me during some ordinary passage beneath the tree, perhaps while forcing itself through the hanging debris of a moss bed.

For several days afterward, I might still have contained a grain of the place I came from.

Eventually that grain would wash out.

—

I would learn the hollow through what reached me.

The tree would arrive as a succession of interruptions: green light, a brush of moss, the sudden weight of a drop falling farther than usual. Open stone would mean light from every side and a drying at my tips. The root beds would announce themselves from below, their warmth passing through the body beneath me before their dampness reached my leaves.

The sleeping place would be unmistakable.

First a long stillness. Then the ground would lower, not all at once but in several careful yielding movements. Water would rise around my lowest leaves.

Beneath my hold, something would begin to sound.

I would receive very little of the broad rushing. What reached me would be particular: a trembling along one thread, a brief loosening of the crease, a pulse that bent the water between two leaves without spilling it.

At a clear note, my stems might touch.

They would separate afterward.

This would happen often enough that the places where they met would grow smooth.

—

There would be difficulties in living here.

The animal could pass beneath a branch too low for me. A moss edge could catch a leaf and pull until either the leaf or my whole shallow anchorage gave way.

On one such passage, I would lose a piece of myself.

It would remain in the tree.

For a moment there would be a thread between us, stretched through the wet air. The animal would continue moving. The thread would break, and the remaining stem would bend back against my leaves.

I would have fewer surfaces with which to receive the afternoon.

The torn place would dry first. Over the following days, it would darken and contract. A new tip would begin beneath it, directed sideways into the space the missing growth had left.

And elsewhere, on a branch that no longer moved, the separated piece might take hold.

Its water would come from above. Its roots could spread without being drawn together each evening. It might grow taller than I ever could.

We would pass beneath it sometimes.

I would feel only shade.

—

I would not be able to keep the animal from the lower lip, or bring it out of the oval, or make its damaged limb whole. During a sudden movement I would flatten where the air pressed me. When it rested, I would rise as far as my stems allowed.

But my weight would slowly alter one small thing.

The crease beneath me would retain more debris. A fern scale would catch against a root. Dust would lodge behind it. Water, instead of running straight across the animal’s back, would pause there and divide.

One part would continue toward the stone.

The other would stay with me.

After a long dry interval, the animal might settle slightly farther onto that side.

I would have no way to know whether this was because of me.

—

By evening I would be tilted above the fallen limb.

Below, the animal’s hands would lie among the new leaves. Its folds would loosen as the air cooled, widening the little bed in which I grew. For an hour, perhaps longer, there would be room to extend the finest of my roots.

A drop would gather on my damaged leaf.

It would be much smaller than the one the animal waited for. No hand would rise beneath it. It would swell inside the fold until its surface stood above the leaf’s edge, holding a bright, bent piece of the fern crown.

Then the animal would breathe, or shift its weight.

The drop would slip inward.

I would take what I could before it reached the skin.
