At first I would mistake the pressure for a surface.

I would push against it.

All my life, when something pressed against my underside, there would have been something there to hold: stone, shell, the thick lip of an intake. But this would come through the floor of a channel without changing its shape. My remaining legs would straighten. There would be nothing to move away from.

Above me, the great animal would miss a compression.

In that interval I would feel the second knock.

I think I would be far inside a mouth by then.

Not at its entrance, where the long-legged attendants brace themselves in the current, but behind the first valve, in a fold that never quite empties. I would have worn that fold smooth. The membrane would have grown around my shell and been scraped back, grown and been scraped back again. A little seam of scar tissue would mark the reach of my forelimbs.

One forelimb. By then there would be only one.

I would know the host less by the force of its pumping than by a small hesitation before the force arrived. Somewhere deeper in its body, a chamber would tighten unevenly. The hesitation would pass through the membrane beneath me, followed by a sideways pull, then the great inward rush.

I would have lived inside that sequence long enough that other healthy animals felt wrong.

There would be food closer to the entrance. I would sometimes go without it rather than move.

Outside, my companions would keep the rim open. Their feet would transmit little sharp disturbances through the walls: adjustments, blows, the prolonged dragging of a body trying to find purchase. I would distinguish two or three of them. One always shifted before the valve tightened. Another scraped in short fits, worrying a place until the host contracted.

When that one disappeared, I would continue to withdraw my forelimb at the times it used to scrape.

For a while there would be no replacement. Then a newcomer would settle there and scrape continuously, with no useful pauses.

I would strike the wall from inside.

The newcomer would stop.

That would be the extent of our acquaintance.

***

I would not know that I was old.

I would know that passages had become longer. That a ridge I once crossed without stopping now required a turn onto my side. That the current could reach beneath my shell in places where a leg had formerly kept the edge down.

My underside would have become very sensitive.

Small changes in the host would occupy me for hours. An obstruction three folds inward. A pocket of water cooling where water should not remain. The first loose margin of a membrane, still moving with its neighbors but no longer taking its share of the strain.

Sometimes I could reach these places.

Sometimes the host would move, rolling its weight against the channel wall, and the bad fold would be pressed out of reach. I would remain beside it, working my forelimb against the smooth, closed flesh.

There would be nothing useful in the persistence. The fold would not open.

I would persist.

Then the deep knocks would come, and I would leave it.

Not bravely. Not with an understanding of where I was going. The familiar compression would become difficult to attend to. Through it, beneath it, I would feel a pressure that continued when the host rested.

I would begin turning around.

There would not be enough room to do this easily.

***

At the entrance, the newcomer would catch my shell.

Its hind foot would find a notch and hold me above the outward flow. Perhaps I would obstruct the valve. Perhaps I would be another damaged edge to brace.

For several pulses we would remain like that: my body half outside, its leg locked across me.

I would scrape the pad with my surviving hook.

It would tighten.

I would scrape again.

When it released me, I would fall with a piece of its foot in my mouth.

I would eat it on the bottom.

The silt would be cold. Away from the membrane, every movement would cost more. I would travel beneath the host first, where the stone carried its weight and made its compression seem to come from everywhere.

Then I would pass beyond its belly.

There would be other rhythms. Crowded together, some strong enough to flatten me, some reaching me only when the nearer animals paused. I would stop often. My cups would settle against the channel bottom, and the dark grains within them would move.

I would not follow the strongest knock.

I would follow the place where, after each knock, the pressure failed to return quite to what it had been.

A small accumulation.

Enough to make one direction different from another.

***

The hollow would have room for all my legs.

I would discover this badly.

Trying to cross its edge, I would slip sideways into a groove made for a hind limb. My body would twist. The surviving stump would work against the stone, unable to reach the purchase provided for it farther out.

For a long time I would lie partly on my back.

Other animals would cross me. A grazer’s foot would press one shell plate inward and hold it there. I would hook at the soft joint above the foot, and the grazer would pull away, turning me enough that my underside touched the hollow.

Then I would stop moving.

The fit would not be exact everywhere. A raised place would press beneath a cup that had enlarged after my last shedding. One side would accommodate a thinner shell. But the long grooves would run outward at the angles my legs had once taken.

Water would enter them.

As it rose, I would keep trying to set feet down at their ends.

***

Here is where I find myself most clearly: not arriving, and not yet enclosed.

My shell is still separate from the stone. Silt has packed beneath one edge, but I could leave if I began now. Above me, a procession divides around the shallow water and joins again beyond it. Some of the feet touch my back.

My cups swell.

Until now, a vibration has always arrived from somewhere. Even when its source was too far away to distinguish, the grains have leaned, lifted, settled. My body has answered by turning or flattening.

Now different cups pull in different directions.

I try to turn toward all of them.

The effort parts my shell.

A tremor passes out of me into the slab, so slight that a nearby grazer continues walking. Farther across the basin, something answers. The answering pressure enters one row of cups and leaves the others almost still.

I press down with that side.

Another answer comes from behind me.

For a time I am busy with this. There is no whole pattern I can recognize. Each response changes the surface against which I am working. I shift my weight by less than the width of a grain. Water gathers at my shell and drains away.

Then the deep pressure subsides.

The ordinary pumps return to my attention, badly separated by distance.

I am hungry.

***

I would be one of those that leave the hollow.

It would take most of a falling tide to free the shell edge that had become embedded. I would tear something underneath. For the first few body-lengths, one row of cups would drag uselessly, their fluid leaking into the silt.

I would not return to my former host.

I might recognize it. Through all the nearer compressions I might feel that hesitation, the little sideways pull.

But the first intake I reached would be working. There would be living membrane within reach of my hook.

I would enter.

The residents would strike me until I passed beyond their legs.

This host would tighten too soon. Twice I would be caught and held through an entire compression. I would learn a different fold. I would scrape, feed, thicken again.

Something from the basin would remain troublesome.

At intervals, with no failure in the host to account for it, the intact cups along my underside would draw tight. I would press against the membrane. The host would flinch, and an attendant outside would lose its footing, catch itself, wait.

I would wait too.

Sometimes nothing would follow.

Sometimes, long afterward, a pressure would reach us through the channel floor. By then I would have resumed feeding. My hook would be buried in a fold, and I would have to decide whether to let go before the next compression pulled it free.
