At first I would be on the lower turn of the ledge, looking down.

There is enough room there to stand if I keep one shoulder near the leaves. I would want to see a cloud part. I would wait for some indication of the height: a shadow moving over land, a bright thread of water, anything that would let the distance become a distance *to* something.

But the clouds would close again. The rail would keep working against my palm. Eventually I would turn toward the wall.

I think that is where I begin to belong: with my attention turned away from the opening, trying to distinguish one movement of the metal from another.

Not yet able to.

The roar would take longer to become useful than the warmth. Warmth already has places in it. Under a washer it is bearable; on the exposed plate it dries the mouth. The folded seam stays cooler a little farther into the day. I would learn those differences before I learned the route between them.

And by then a palm would be too large. It would conceal too much.

I would need the small, separately settling pads. I would need to be able to leave three points attached while a fourth searched. My view of the chamber would become intermittent: a brightness beyond the rail, briefly visible between stems. Most of what I saw would be close enough to touch with my mouth.

I would be one of the animals on the opposite wall. Not the adult you watched. Younger, with all my toes, living in a hollow that was not quite finished.

***

My hollow is behind a leaf with no notch.

To look out, I push beneath its edge. This sends a movement through the stem that can be felt from the next seam, so I seldom look out without also announcing that I am awake.

There is a better leaf a little higher. Its underside is dry and the roots around it are thin enough to bite through. But the backing beneath it has pulled loose. When I put my weight there, the whole patch lifts from the steel.

I have tried it several times.

Each time, after returning to the lower hollow, I press my feet hard against the felt before settling.

My shelter fits me badly because I cleared it while I was smaller. There is room to lengthen it downhill, but a root runs across that end. The root is thick and moist. I have scraped its surface without cutting through. At night I sleep bent around it.

Sometimes I wake and find my tail in my mouth.

***

I know the animal across the chamber by an interruption.

With my jaw against the lower rail attachment, I can feel the machinery in several places at once: at the hinge of my mouth, behind my eyes, along the bones that run toward my shoulders. One part of this disturbance occasionally changes. It changes again after a pause.

When I was smaller I answered by pressing harder.

Now I can make the movement in my throat.

I do not know everything I send. Sometimes the animal across the chamber stops what it is doing. Sometimes it continues along the wall, and I stay at the attachment until my jaw grows hot.

There are other contacts. Something farther up sends a short, uneven tremor through a plate. A bird landing behind the flashing makes a disturbance that spreads and is gone. Water filling the small basin changes the feel of its rim before the surface reaches the place where I drink.

I am slow to leave that basin.

The reliable seam is farther away, beyond a bare crossing I have not made often enough. At the basin I can drink with my hind feet sheltered and my tail around a post. I stay until a beetle touches my lip. Then I draw back, swallow, and wait for it to move.

***

There is a piece of fern in the empty air near my shelter.

It has been there through two periods of cooler rail. Pale on one side, dark on the other, turning over so slowly that I sometimes think it has stopped.

I can reach it from a bent attachment if I take my tail out of the felt.

I have not done this.

Instead I go down past it, then up. From above it seems nearer. From below I can see something caught in its fold.

Once I stretch far enough that the loose skin of my side begins to shake. The trembling is different from the wall’s. My forefoot opens.

Then I pull back so abruptly that I strike my mouth against the attachment.

For a while I clean a foot that is already clean.

Later, without my seeing it happen, the fern moves closer to the wall. I take it from an ordinary standing place. There are two dry insect legs inside and a soft body tucked deep into the fold.

I eat the body. I carry the rest home, though it is awkward to get beneath the leaf with it.

***

I would not be especially good at living here.

There would be places I avoided that other animals crossed readily. I would sometimes mistake a change in the engines for a touch nearby. During my first storm alone, I would climb too early and spend a long time thirsty behind a hot plate.

But my hollow would gradually improve.

I would cut through the root at last. Water would bead on the severed end, and I would lick it before clearing away the fibers. For several days the space would smell sharply green.

I would make a notch in the leaf.

Not in the middle of its edge, where the other animal has made hers, but close to the stem. From there I could see the next foothold without exposing my head.

And one evening I would come back from drinking, put my jaw to the rail attachment, and receive the familiar alteration from across the chamber.

I would answer.

Then, with nothing further arriving through the metal, I would go beneath my leaf. There would be room now to stretch my hind legs. One foot would rest against the newly cleared backing, where the root had been.

Before sleeping, I would move it a little higher.
