I would look first for somewhere to put down what I was carrying.

There is a ledge just inside the outer gate, but it slopes toward the sump, and someone has laid three wedges there in order of size. Farther in, a recess beside a doorway looks empty until I see the little pot pushed to the back, catching water. I keep going with my bundle against my chest.

I think this would be how I began to belong: by learning which apparently empty places were in use.

For a while I would sleep in a room borrowed from someone who needed it back at harvest. I would be careful with the walls and careless with the ceiling, striking my head on the same beam each morning. I would own a broad basket brought from elsewhere. It would be a good basket, sound at the rim, well balanced when full, impossible to carry through the last turn before my door.

Eventually I would give it to a boatman. For years afterward I would recognize it coming into the landing with somebody else’s belongings in it.

But that is an arrival imagined from outside. After a little longer, I find I have been here since childhood. The basket belonged to my mother. I disliked it because carrying it required me to go the long way.

There was once another way beneath the meeting-house stair.

I could crawl through with a lamp pushed ahead of me on a shard. Halfway along, the passage widened enough to turn onto my back. The underside of the stair was black; the stone beside it was white and faintly wet. I used to lie there listening to people stop above me. Those who were bringing something heavy set it down on the third step. Those who were angry generally went straight up.

I cannot get through now. I have not been able to for twenty years, though when someone mentions that passage I still picture it from the middle.

My room is near it.

The door opens inward, which was a mistake before it became an agreement. I have a sleeping bench, a shelf, and a stool with one shortened leg. The floor rises toward the wall, so the stool stands properly only in one place. Visitors move it to be helpful. I move it back after they leave.

Above the bench hangs a piece of dark cloth. Behind it, the slab has a shallow hollow where the rising water gathers before spilling over. I clear the hollow with a bone scraper and fit a trough beneath it during dry weather. Once, when I was ill, I neglected this and woke with one sleeve soaked through. For several days people asked whether I had enough bedding. What I had needed was someone to move the bench.

I am a woman who works at channels.

Not the difficult work beneath a loaded wall. I help with that, but another person decides where the braces go. Mostly I open the smaller runs, lift crust from the mouths of conduits, replace split covers, and reach for things that have fallen where a hand will not go.

My tools hang beside the door. There are hooked sticks, strips of split reed, a narrow scoop, two lengths of cord, and a wooden fork made by someone who owed me a favor. The fork is excellent. I have never quite forgiven him for making something so useful when I wanted him to mend my shutter.

People borrow these things. I dislike lending the fork, and usually lend it.

The work suits me because there is a place to begin. A gutter is overflowing here. The water ought to be going there. Whatever else has happened in the household, I can kneel down and start with that.

This does not make me patient. I become particularly unpleasant when someone stands behind me explaining how freely the water ran before they changed the paving.

I keep small objects on the high shelf: a bead, a bone fastening, a copper hook gone green, three gaming pieces from two different sets. Things brought up with the grit. People know they can come and look.

Anything valuable is usually claimed at once. The rest remains until a child asks for it or I need the space. I used to keep everything. Then I found a little wooden animal on the shelf and could no longer remember whether I had recovered it from a channel or been given it by someone I should remember.

It is still there.

My hands would be broad. This becomes clear when I try to reach past a bend beneath a threshold. I can touch the obstruction with one finger, but cannot turn my wrist. The person who called me is watching, and for a moment I persist because I remember being able to do it.

Then I pull my hand out, bleeding along the knuckle, and ask for a child.

The boy they bring is too eager. He gets down before I have explained where the channel deepens.

“Wait.”

He thinks I mean to let me try again.

“Wait until I show you.”

We spend more time clearing space for his shoulder than we would have spent fetching a thinner hook. He lies with his cheek on a folded mat while I hold the lamp. His mother stays by his feet, pretending to examine the lifted paving.

The obstruction is a piece of wood thickly coated in white deposit. He works it loose and passes it back. Under the crust, one end has been cut into a little paddle.

I made boats like that. So does he. Neither of us mentions it while his mother is there.

Afterward I tell the householder that the opening needs widening.

He says it has always been large enough.

I hold up my hand. He looks at the scrape rather than the breadth of it, but in the end we arrange a morning when the threshold can be lifted.

That would be part of who I am here: someone who has begun asking for openings to be made larger. Not everywhere. Sometimes there is too much weight above them, or the neighboring room has already given all the space it can. But I have spent enough mornings with my ribs pressed against stone to ask before assuming nothing can be changed.

At home, though, I leave the door as it is. Moving it would mean shifting the bench, and I like the bench beneath the little window.

I have a friend who comes some evenings with work she can do while talking. She plaits the narrow ties used to secure rolled mats. The first time she came, she moved the stool and nearly fell. Now she sits without looking.

She has a way of leaving the unfinished end of a tie in my hand when she needs to reach for something. I hold the strands apart while she drinks. Sometimes she forgets to take them back until I complain.

We talk about the people whose houses I have been in. I try not to tell her anything I ought to keep to myself. She says I am very strict about secrets everyone already knows.

There would be evenings when I want to leave this place. Usually I have worked all day in cold water, and the smoke from the room below is coming through the floor. I imagine somewhere I could walk home without touching a wall. I imagine putting a basket down beside my own door and leaving it there.

On those evenings my friend does not defend the town. She has wanted to leave longer than I have.

She asks whether I would take the fork.

“Yes.”

“And the shelf of rubbish?”

“Some of it.”

We discuss what would fit into a boat. By the time she goes, we have given away each other’s possessions several times.

Then I put the room in order. I hang the damp shin pads where they will receive a little air. I check behind the cloth. If the stone is darkening, I pull the bench forward before lying down.

From there I can reach the door with my foot and push it closed.

Under the meeting-house stair, water goes through the place where I used to lie. I hear it only when the neighboring rooms have quieted. A cover somewhere is rocking under the flow: a small knock, a pause, another small knock.

I know which one it is. I fitted it, and one corner was a little short.

In the morning I will take the fork, two wedges, and a better piece of wood.
