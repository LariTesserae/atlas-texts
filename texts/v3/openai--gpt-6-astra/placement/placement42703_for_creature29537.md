I would look for a room in which the roar sounded farther away.

Not a quiet room. I would discover the difference early: a cupboard-sized hollow could be louder than an avenue, and a great flooded hall could hold the noise at a distance without diminishing it. In such a hall I might hear, or seem to hear, the small sound of my heel lifting from the wet floor. I would try it several times. The sound would prove to be part of the water, already there before I raised my foot.

I would choose the hall anyway.

It would be below the ascending causeway, accessible through a passage whose ceiling forced me to stoop. Coming out, I would find the causeway below its windows. This would make it easy to recognize. I would not need to decide which side of it I occupied.

There would be a basin at the back, too broad to step across, and a shelf along one wall. A dark fold would occupy most of the shelf. Its exposed end would have a little scalloped edge, neither damage nor ornament: just a place where the body had grown unevenly. Beyond that edge, there would be room for me.

At first I would sit with my hands between my knees.

I would keep my belongings on my lap, if I had brought any. It would feel presumptuous to set them down.

***

I think I would spend a long time believing myself to be a visitor who had found a good place to wait.

There would be practical matters. The heat. The damp clothing. The difficulty of finding a position in which neither my back nor my knees ached. Whatever had brought me there would gradually become less useful than the knowledge that the shelf was cooler near the basin.

I would learn the nearby inhabitant in the way one learns a room at night.

There was its broad upper surface. There was the slight outward roll before the scalloped edge. There was a dark pit facing almost directly into the wall, which I could inspect only by leaning so far sideways that one hand had to brace against the paving.

Inside that pit I would see the passage through which I had entered.

Most often it would be empty. Occasionally it would contain my own hand, braced on the floor.

The view would be so close that I could make out water gathered beside a fingernail.

I would put my hand back in my lap.

I would not be frightened every time. That would be one of the first changes.

Sometimes I would sit beside the fold without looking into it. Sometimes I would look without trying to find myself. The passage had a thin cream seam along its ceiling that was difficult to see from anywhere else. The seam forked above the entrance. One branch stopped; the other continued into the hall.

It would become familiar enough that I would notice it before the hand.

***

My first idea of a useful occupation would be to clear the shallow channel along the shelf.

A fallen leaf lay across it. Water stood against the leaf’s upstream edge, although upstream was only a supposition. The leaf was brown, almost black at its folded tip. I would reach toward it and stop before touching it.

There was no debris behind it. No backed-up sediment. The water beyond it was no shallower.

It was not obstructing anything I could establish.

I would leave it where it was.

Elsewhere I might find a root pressed beneath a heavy fold and think to lift the fold free. Then I would see the transparent tips resting along that root, and the pale, undamaged continuation emerging on the other side.

I would find many things that looked like small tasks, until I looked at them closely.

This would be uncomfortable. I would have liked to repay the use of the shelf. I would have liked an errand that ended with something visibly better than before.

For a while I would settle for learning the routes. I would know how to reach the cliff without passing through the harshest part of the sound. I would know which basin gave the clearest view of the mountains and which apparent window opened onto the underside of its own sill. I could have guided someone.

There would be no one to guide.

Still, I would keep returning to the hall.

***

I do not think I would become one of the inhabitants by slowly losing my human outline.

There would be no useful sequence of softened bones, widening skin, fingers growing transparent. That would make the question too easy: first a visitor, then a creature; the change located somewhere between.

Instead, I would begin to have difficulty finding the whole of the visitor.

Seated on the shelf, I could still look down at my knees. I could feel the damp weight of my clothing. But there would also be a particular pressure beneath the opposite gallery, as recognizable as the pressure of the shelf under my thigh.

I would go over to inspect it.

A pale fold lay there, wedged comfortably between two roots. One root crossed its upper surface. Beneath it, the skin was indented by a shallow, diagonal line.

Touching the fold would not produce the sensation of touching my own leg.

It would produce the sensation of someone putting a hand on the place I had come to inspect.

I would remove my hand quite quickly.

Back on the shelf, the pressure would remain.

That would be how I learned: not through an expanding sense of possession, but through small errors about where I was already comfortable.

There would be a cool place in a high chamber I had never entered. There would be a narrow weight across something of mine near the falls. On a ledge overlooking mountain air, there would be an exposed surface warmed by ordinary sun.

I could search for these places. Sometimes I would find them. Sometimes the search would bring me back to the hall by a route I had not intended.

The body would not become clearer as the map improved.

***

Eventually, I would recognize a portion of myself by an imperfection.

Along the margin of a brown fold, just above the water, there would be three small pale spots. Two round, one elongated. Nothing about them would resemble a face, a sign, or a constellation I knew.

I would find them beneath the gallery.

Later, I would find the same three spots on the inner lip of a chamber overlooking the cliff. They would be larger there. The elongated one would turn slightly around the edge, disappearing onto the underside.

I would know both places from within.

That would not tell me whether there were two marked portions or one portion accessible twice. It would tell me where not to put my foot.

I would become more careful in the city, though less hesitant.

There would be places where my body rested against another body. The difference would be evident at the contact: a warmer surface, a firmer one, a vibration that reached me with the quality of something encountered. I would not know where the other inhabitant ended. I would know that the pressure was welcome.

Some portions of me would remain difficult to reach. One might lie in a hollow so small that the person walking through the city could not get a hand inside.

I would spend an unreasonable amount of time crouched beside that hollow.

There would be something embarrassing about being unable to see whether I was comfortable, despite being comfortable.

***

The roar would become more particular.

Through one resting surface it would have a coarse, close grain. Through another, it would arrive as a deep pressure without any apparent edge. Where my underside lay among roots, I would distinguish fine strains within it, tightly laid beside one another.

I would not understand these as messages.

I would make that mistake at least once. With so much arriving, there would surely be something to decipher. I would attend to the dark bands beneath my skin, to the flattened ends of the filaments, to the views opening through the pits.

But there would be no moment when the noise broke into speech.

There would instead be the discovery that I had been resting awkwardly in one distant place.

Not painfully. A thick part of me lay across a shelf whose lip supported it a little too sharply. The discomfort was slight enough to have been lost in the rest.

I would remain aware of it.

I do not know whether I could move that portion. I imagine trying, and finding that the intention to move was much less definite than I expected. The pressure was already accommodated by the yielding flesh. Elsewhere, a broad underside lay beautifully supported in water.

Both conditions would be mine.

I would stop expecting my whole body to agree about how it felt.

***

The place I would think of as home would still be the hall below the causeway.

Not because it contained more of me. I might occupy only a few inches of its shelf, a soft rim along the basin, the back of one small recess. Most of the dark fold beside my original seat would belong to someone else.

I would recognize that inhabitant by the scalloped edge.

There would be no greeting between us. No evidence that my return made a difference. Yet I would prefer that shelf to others, and I would leave the same generous space between us even where our submerged filaments lay close together.

The human figure might still come there.

I would see it through a pit facing the wall: stooping through the passage, straightening, looking toward the basin. I would feel the soles of its feet and the cool pressure beneath the opposite gallery at the same time.

It would sit down.

I would not need to decide whether it was an earlier shape, a small exposed portion, or simply the part of me that could walk from room to room.

Its hands would rest between its knees. After a while, one hand would settle on the shelf.

Through that contact, the roar would separate into a low, finely uneven tremor.

There would be enough room to lay the hand flat without touching the neighboring fold. And beyond the hand, a little more shelf, warm and unoccupied.
