I would be near the fifth step, but not on it. There is a place underneath where the drainage groove has worn through to an older joint. Water enters there carrying a little daylight. Farther in, the joint turns, and the light ends against a grainy face of mortar.

That is where I would first look for myself.

There would be something caught in the turn. A scrap of packing, perhaps, from a repair made before the present motor was installed. Folded material with a straight edge that suggests it was once cut to fit. A person clearing the groove could touch it with a screwdriver and feel it give, then catch. They might leave it rather than risk opening a leak beneath the stairs.

For a while, that would be enough of a life: to remain caught, wet on one side and intermittently wet on the other.

But I think I would extend farther back. Not far enough to occupy the foundations. Only to where the joint widens behind the rail socket, making a chamber about the size of a closed hand. Sand would cover the bottom. A rusted end of reinforcement would enter through the roof. The chamber would fill from below, keeping a flattened bubble against the underside of the step.

I would live around that bubble.

My upper surface would be dry much of the time, faintly wrinkled, with dust caught in it that the water could never quite reach. Below, I would be soft and dark. There would be no good place to put the boundary between these surfaces. As the chamber filled, a band of skin would become wet that had been dry for several hours. It would take time to unfold.

I would have that kind of slowness.

The large animal would reach me through two cracks in the chamber floor. Its flesh would be paler than mine, and smoother. Sometimes it would rise far enough to touch my underside. For several minutes we would fit together closely. Water would cease passing between us.

Then its fullness would go elsewhere, and I would remain.

I don’t think I would be one of its young. Nor would I be certain to someone who knew the hollow well that I was a separate animal. My movements would usually follow its movements, because we would share the same small quantities of water. But after the lower tissue withdrew, I would sometimes stay closed. A little pressure would accumulate behind the step. The groove would run toward the bank.

On other days I would open almost immediately.

There would be a reason for the difference, but it would be difficult to discover by watching from above.

Inside me would be several narrow spaces lined with short, stiff threads. Some would hold water long after the chamber drained. One would retain a grain of something black and angular, too large to work out through either end. When I contracted, the grain would turn. Over years it would wear a smooth place in the lining.

Perhaps that would account for my hesitation on some days. Perhaps the temperature would. Nothing in the movement itself would distinguish an old obstruction from a preference.

I would feed poorly but often. The groove would bring dust from the steps, traces of algae, the softened margins of leaves. Most would pass. Fine material would stay along my lower lip until the lip folded inward. Occasionally a whole seed would lodge there, and for days I would be unable to close properly.

The seed would remain a seed. Eventually it would wash out.

I would have no use for most of what reached me.

When someone descended the stairs, I would feel the rail before the footfall. The socket would shift almost imperceptibly in its bed, pressing the rusted reinforcement against the chamber roof. My dry surface would tighten. A little air would enter a passage ordinarily occupied by water.

This would be the strongest interruption in my day.

I would not know about the hand becoming cold. I would know the length of the pressure, its small reversals, the final release. People who stopped on the fifth step would leave me altered longer than people who walked directly past it.

The person who maintained the pump would stop there often.

They would have a habit of setting something on the step while opening or closing their coat. A wrench, a tin of grease, the wooden wedge. Each object would arrive with a different brief impact. Then there would be the quieter load of a hand on the rail.

Once, they would clear the groove with a piece of wire.

I would draw away too late. The wire would tear my outer edge and bring a small piece into daylight. It would look like wet paper on the hooked end.

The person would wipe it off on the concrete.

For the remainder of that afternoon, water would pass through me without being held. The chamber would fill and empty with the nearest pool. The pale flesh below would rise against the injury. I would close around it, and for a week or more we would have no space between us.

When we separated again, a small portion of my lining would remain attached to it.

I would continue with less of that lining. The other animal might continue with more. Nothing visible at the steps would establish what had happened.

Later, the person would use a strip of wood to clear the groove. I would feel its broad end stop against me, rest there, and retreat.

I would not become tame. There would be no movement by which I could thank them. Even an observer willing to attribute intention would find very little to point to: the water slowing, a damp edge withdrawing into a joint.

But I would grow nearer the entrance after that.

Not out onto the step. The open air would still be too much, and the exposed concrete would warm too quickly when sunlight reached it. I would put down a thin rim along the inner turn, where the water brought in light without admitting the sky itself.

From above, this rim would resemble a dark deposit. In dry weather it would contract into a broken line.

That is where I would be found: beneath a usable step, living in a space the larger body did not quite fill. Holding a little water after it had gone elsewhere. Letting it pass later, across the old cut in the concrete, with a few grains loosened from the joint.

By then the person would have gone into the pump house. Their wrench would touch the floor. The motor would stop.

I would remain partly open, waiting for the pressure to reach me.
