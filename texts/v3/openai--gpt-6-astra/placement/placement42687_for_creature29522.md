I think I would live beside a crossing that is slowly becoming useless.

Not a dangerous crossing yet. That would make the decision easier. In most states of the tide, it still saves a long walk around the western rise. People arrive at my door with the old expectation of going straight across. They put a hand to the floor, take off their outer foot wrappings, and look at me.

Sometimes I put water into a dish for them. Sometimes I begin making room for the night.

My house has space for two sleeping pads if the storage dishes are moved. Three people can lie down if one agrees to sleep with their feet beneath the work ledge. That place is warmer, though everyone pretends it is the bad place.

I do not know the whole country. Two rises away, the vibrations become difficult for me to separate. I have been farther, carrying things, following someone who knew where to stop. But here I can sometimes tell that a pool has emptied before its exposed stones become visible from the door.

Sometimes is important. I have been wrong while putting on my shoes.

***

The work I am good at is repairing things that have already taken a shape.

A new piece of inner skin is troublesome. It curls where it ought to lie flat; its thickness varies; there is no wear to tell you how it will be used. Give me a carrying strap that has pulled against the same shoulder for fifteen years, and I can find where the next failure will happen by drawing it between my fingers.

People bring me foot wrappings, chamber bags, lashings from racks. A household once brought an entire sleeping pad, rolled so tightly around its damaged place that I had to persuade them it could not be repaired without being opened.

The pad belonged to a man who had died. His sister thought unfolding it would let his smell escape.

We sat with it between us for most of a pressure change.

Eventually she opened it herself. The damage was quite small.

I use slivers of flexible bone to work strips through old holes. Making a new hole is easy. Deciding whether the material can bear one is most of the job. My fingertips are rough enough to catch on sound skin, so I test doubtful places against the inside of my wrist.

There is usually a pale scrap stuck there when visitors arrive.

They have made a sign for me: someone testing a strip, considering it, testing it again, then discovering that it is attached to their own arm. It is an unfair imitation. I have only done that once.

***

Beside the entrance lives a fixed shell with an uneven upper lip.

Part of the lip broke before I came to this house, and the animal has grown around the break. One chamber opens less widely than the others. Grit collects there. I clear it with a narrow strip of bone, working from the side so that I do not push sediment farther in.

I do not think it likes this.

It draws in what it can, and once caught the end of the strip so firmly that I had to leave it there until the next lull. A visitor asked why I had given the animal a tongue.

Its calls have a roughness I can feel through the heel of my hand. If I go out onto the shelf, the roughness disappears among the other shells and the stone. Indoors, near the doorway, I can pick it out.

I have never named it. The cracked-lip one is quite sufficient.

When I am away, I ask whoever checks the crossing to clear its lip.

When I return, I check whether they have.

***

What draws people to my door now is less often a broken thing than a journey interrupted.

They have bundles prepared for a house beyond the basin. They have planned to arrive before the meat is lowered into brine, or while someone is still awake to help move a roof plate. The crossing has taken away the next part of their day, and for a little while they cannot think what to do with their hands.

I give them something to hold.

A strap under tension. A dish I need moved. Two ends that must stay together while I pass a fastening through them.

This is useful to me, and usually to them. Some resent it. One woman now enters with her hands tucked beneath her arms, announcing that she has brought no fingers to spare.

She is excellent at holding a seam.

People talk when the noise permits. At other times, I learn what they have been doing from the things in their bundles. A strap worn on both edges means that someone else has begun carrying the load. New lashings on an old wedge may mean a young person is learning to open the larger shells.

It may also mean the owner left the wedge where a hunter could worry at it.

I ask before assuming. Even then, people sometimes tell me the more flattering story.

***

The crossing’s trouble begins beneath its middle.

There used to be a hollow tremor there, a loose succession of knocks that came just before the water withdrew. Now, at some tides, the knocks stop while water is still arriving through the lower cracks.

An older woman from the southern shelters tells me this happened when she was young. She says the passage will clear.

A man who collects along the westward runs says something has fallen into it. He has found warm water where none used to reach.

They visit on the same day and disagree at considerable length. During the roar, their signs become so abbreviated that I lose parts of the argument. Both turn toward me for support.

I put a strap in the woman’s hands and a chamber bag in the man’s.

This does not settle anything, but I finish two repairs.

Afterward we go outside. The man lies flat beside a narrow opening, with one sleeve submerged to the elbow. The woman stands farther back, watching the small shells beneath a ledge.

Neither looks foolish. Neither changes the other’s mind.

For several days I mark the crossing closed earlier than I once would have. People complain about the detour. One comes back from the western rise with a shell plate he found exposed in an unfamiliar pool, curved just right for his roof.

He shows it to me as though the whole inconvenience has been arranged for his benefit.

***

I would like to say I am patient because patience is needed here.

Mostly I am patient with material. A difficult repair gives me permission to stay where I am and attend to something small enough to hold.

People are less obliging.

They pull while I am asking them to slacken. They explain a break I can see. They bring a wrapping worn almost through and ask whether it will last one more crossing, when what they want is to avoid the cost of replacing it.

I have done the same with my house.

The collecting seam beside it has cooled. There is still food to be taken nearby, but less than there was. Visitors have begun arriving by the longer route without stopping here at all. I notice how much news I have missed when someone mentions a child who has grown large enough to help with a heavy shell, and in my memory the child is still tending a pool of three transparent ones.

The roof is sound. The floor is familiar. Every stored thing has a place made for its particular awkwardness.

Moving would require admitting how many of those things I no longer use.

***

The day I close the crossing altogether, nothing dramatic happens.

The cracked-lip shell is open. The basin is shallow enough to show the pale line of markers almost all the way across.

I have gone out to retrieve a waiting stone that has shifted in its socket. Kneeling to lift it, I feel the loose knocking under my knee.

Then a second vibration passes through it.

The two overlap. I cannot tell which passage is taking water.

I stay there long enough for cold to come through my wrappings. A little grazer works past my thumb, leaving a clean trail on the stone.

There may be a safe interval. There may be several. But I no longer know how to recognize their ends.

I lay the waiting stone on its side.

Coming back, I am angry. I had expected to be frightened, but I am thinking of the effort spent boring that stone, and of all the people who will now have opinions about where I ought to live.

At the door I clear the fixed shell’s lip more roughly than I should. It closes against the bone.

I let go.

For a while we remain there, each holding an end.

***

I do not move into someone else’s household. There is an unused shelter beyond the western rise, with a poor roof and a good floor. It is close enough to a dependable collecting run that people pass it carrying things which will eventually need repair.

Three people help me carry my possessions. They are surprised by the number of small dishes. I am surprised too.

The last bundle includes a rack fitting that belongs to nobody I can remember, strips too short for any repair I can imagine, and my old inner foot wrappings. I have kept those because they are still unmistakably mine, although there is no longer enough strength in them to bear a fastening.

At the new house, I cannot tell which chamber makes the smooth pulse beneath the threshold.

One of the people helping me unload can.

She puts her hand on the floor and waits for me to put mine beside it. When the pulse returns, she touches the outside of my wrist.

Later, another tremor comes. I look up.

She shakes her head. We wait again.

***

I still visit the old house.

There are roof plates worth taking, and I have not finished deciding what to do with the work ledge. I check the stones that turn travelers toward the western route. I clear the cracked lip.

The bone strip is kept beneath a small ledge by the entrance, where anyone can find it.

At the new place, my repairs accumulate. Someone brings a sleeping pad. Someone arrives with a carrying bag split along a seam I warned them about, and we courteously agree that the break was difficult to predict.

My tools find their places more quickly than I do.

For a long time I wake at the first filling pulse, reach toward where the old wall should be, and touch the person sleeping beside the work ledge.

Usually it is a visitor. Sometimes it is the woman who is teaching me the floor.

She knows what I am asking. Without getting up, she presses my hand back onto the sleeping skin.

There is time yet.

I am learning how that feels here.
