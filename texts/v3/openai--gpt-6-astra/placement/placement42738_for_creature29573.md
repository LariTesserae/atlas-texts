I would find the shelf by its underside.

From above, it would be one pale rise among many, with scales growing to the lip and a few tall stalks beyond it. But somewhere in the plain I would put my head beneath a leaf and see a place that could shelter the whole of me.

Getting there would require a small departure from the direction in which I was going.

I would have done this before. My body would know how far to lower itself, which plates to close, which part of the grove to cease carrying. For a moment a patch of foliage would brighten without any change in the light. Then I would be beneath the shelf, among the resting animals.

There would be room for me. I would examine it before lying down.

The others would leave a little more space around me than around one another. Not enough to suggest that I was unwelcome. Enough that, when my plates opened in sleep, none of them would have to close theirs.

Along my left side I would carry a place that did not quite fit.

***

It would look, at first, like a bare patch of stone.

Other animals would carry more attractive openings: a black grove, the bright undersides of scales, a stem occupied by an amber creature. Mine would contain a length of pale mineral with a shallow line across it. At one end of the line, five small shadows would lie beside five small projections.

A hand had rested there once.

The hand would not be in the opening. Neither would the visitor. Only the stone, and a displacement in the light that became difficult to see when looked at directly.

I would have acquired it during a crossing.

Before that, I would have been a grazer with an appetite for the low plants whose leaves overlap so closely that taking from one exposes the brightness of another. Their small crescents would arrange themselves beneath my plates in dense, fine ranks. Amber animals would find these arrangements agreeable. There would usually be one somewhere inside my flank, resting on a stem that I had never approached.

I would tolerate it until it turned its white points inward.

Then I would shut that view, gently, leaving it on its stem.

I would be particular in this way. Curious, but not open to everything.

***

The visitor would stop on the bare stone because there was room to sit.

I would have followed for some distance. Its movement would disturb me less than the way its positions disappeared. Behind it, the plants would part and close with no remaining limb, no occupied interval, nothing to indicate where the body continued elsewhere.

Even an alarmed animal left more than that.

I would come close while one hand lay palm-down beside its leg.

The black points within my divided head would attend to the fingers, to their slight inequality of warmth, to the soft depth where one surface pressed against another. There would be an extraordinary amount of movement inside the hand. Most of it too small to follow. Some of it recurring.

I would open a fold.

The beat would pass.

I would open another, making a place for it.

By then the first place would be empty.

This would occupy me for a long time.

The visitor might turn the hand over. I would withdraw at the sudden abundance of creases, then approach again. Its fingers would bend without retaining their former arrangement anywhere I could reach.

I would take nothing from the skin.

But when the hand lifted, I would remain with my head lowered over the place it had covered. The mineral there would be warmer. A little moisture would be leaving it.

Something could still be accommodated.

I would spread my plates and make the smallest opening I knew.

***

Afterward, beneath the shelf, the line of the stone would continue across my body at an awkward angle.

It would not hurt. I would sleep less compactly.

One of the others would wake and incline its head toward the opening. A note would appear between us, low among the folded limbs. I would let the sound touch the place where I carried the stone.

For a while we would both be very still.

Then the other animal would shift away by the width of one pad.

I would not understand this as rejection. There would simply be more room, and I would use it.

Beneath a seam some distance from my sleeping body, I would also be small.

Four soft lobes would enclose my green interior. A fallen scale would have impressed a bright-edged shape into my outer surface. I would be there without knowing the shelf as a destination, without needing to turn my head toward anything.

The warmth from the hand would reach this body faintly.

One lobe would loosen.

***

I think this is where I would become recognizable to myself: not in the difficult opening, but in the trouble I took to keep it.

I would alter my feeding. The black-stemmed plants would give me roomier folds than the low scales, though I would still prefer the scales. I would take a little from them before going to the groves.

I would stop admitting amber passengers along my left side.

Crossing the open flats, I would hold my plates close. Under the white hunters, every remote place becomes a liability; each opening offers depth that can be drawn out of the body. I would have escaped them before by closing everything.

Now I would close everything but one.

This would make me neither brave nor wise. A hunter’s depth would catch the rear of my body one day, and I would feel a pad approach the ground.

The contact would be almost delicate.

I would let go of a grove. Then a pale strip of shelf. Then the stem of an amber animal, which would remain suspended in its proper thicket, no longer carried.

The mineral beneath my foot would come nearer.

At last I would close the place where the hand had rested.

My outline would gather around me. Four limbs, one head, a narrow body. I would leave.

For some distance afterward, I would remain easy to see.

***

I would return to the visitor’s stone.

There would be no stain. No surviving warmth. I would spread my head over the shallow line and find ordinary mineral, intricate within itself but offering nothing of what I had lost.

I might stay long enough for leaves at the edge of the flat to turn toward me.

Then I would go beneath the shelf.

There, in the small folded body, one lobe would still be loose.

The green within it would have changed its arrangement. Nothing resembling a hand would be present. Nothing would beat. But between two folds there would be a narrow space I could not fill by feeding, nor close by drawing my plates together.

I would rest with that side toward the other sleepers.

Eventually one would come nearer.

Its shoulder would occupy some of the room they had been leaving me. A clear note would stand beside us, and I would answer with another. The sounds would not quite meet.

We would adjust our heads.

Above the shelf, the leaves would remain turned toward the sun. Somewhere beyond them the visitor would be walking, using up one place after another.

I would have no opening onto that journey.

Still, when I rose to feed, I would leave room beside me for something that would have to arrive by crossing the stone.
