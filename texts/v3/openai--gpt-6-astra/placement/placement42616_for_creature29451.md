I would be under a place where someone is likely to stop.

Not the lowest part of a basin. A little above it, where two laid surfaces meet and one is firmer than the other. From above, there would be no obvious reason to pause there, except that the footing had improved and the next stretch was long. Beneath it, the sand layer would narrow to almost nothing. Water would come through reluctantly, wetting one shoulder before it reached the rest of me.

I would have lived there long enough for that shoulder to be darker than the other.

At first I imagine myself with both hands against the roof, holding it up. But a sound roof would not need that, and I would have other things to do. One hand would rest overhead out of habit. The smaller hands would be working beneath me, loosening a film from the sand, pressing waste into a place where the living threads had thinned. My mouth would be against the damp boundary. Most of the chamber would fit closely enough that I could not move without changing its shape.

I would not experience this as confinement. An empty space taller than my body would be more troubling: somewhere moisture could leave me on every side.

There would be a larger accommodation nearby, occupied by several others. I could reach it without exposing any part of myself to air. I would go there when the water in my own seam grew too thin, and sometimes before it did. There would be a particular body whose pressure I knew through two floors and a bed of sand. When it moved, I would make room before it arrived.

One of my front hands would not open completely.

It would have dried while I was young. I had reached too far out of a seam and kept working after the shade passed. By the time I withdrew, the outer fingers had begun to draw together. The hand recovered enough to carry clay, but two webs remained short. When I pressed a level surface, there was a small interruption in the pressure.

Others would know me partly by that interruption.

With strangers I would need to turn the hand, offer its edge, wait while they found how the fingers joined. The familiar neighbor would put its palm against mine slightly askew. Nothing would need correcting.

***

The first passage overhead would reach me as a sequence of separate failures.

The roof would bend, recover, bend farther away. Sand would shift into the depression my shoulder had left. Then the weight would return, much closer, and remain.

I would flatten. The water in me would move backward. My smaller hands would draw in.

Nothing I knew walked with so little of itself touching the ground.

When the pressure persisted, I would retreat toward the wider chamber. Above me, the floor would yield into the space I had occupied. There would be a sharp redistribution of weight, a scrape, then several quicker impacts going away.

For a long time afterward, I would keep one hand against the roof.

Later, another inhabitant would come to feel the damage. We would touch across the loosened sand. It would press where the load had been; I would answer where the clay had moved. Between us we could establish that something heavy had crossed and that the surface had not held.

We could not establish what had crossed.

That night we would bring up enough earth to bed the cracked plates. Not much. The damp seam could spare only a little water, and whatever we used in the repair would not reach the lower chamber until another rain.

I would compress one portion beneath my body while my neighbor prepared its place. My stiff hand would be poor at spreading the final edge. I would brace the underside instead, feeling the other’s strokes arrive through the wet clay.

Near dawn, a third set of hands would take over.

I would leave before the work was finished.

***

There would be no single task that made me necessary.

Some nights I would graze. Some nights I would do nothing but lie in the deeper accommodation while water slowly restored the flexible places of my body. I would fail to answer a pressure signal because I was feeding, then catch its repetition much later. A piece of roof I had judged ready would settle after I moved away. Someone else would wet it and lift it back.

The crossing would nevertheless become familiar.

Its weight would change with the distance between impacts. I would learn the long interval, the short one, the dragging pressure that preceded a turn. When it stopped above my chamber, I would no longer withdraw at once. I would spread beneath the place that bent most, while the other inhabitants moved out from under the weakest edge.

This would work until rain softened an adjoining floor.

On the next crossing, the weight would come down where we had not prepared for it. A plate would tilt. Its edge would cut through the upper part of my chamber, closing the passage to the others.

I would be on the damp side of the fall. I would not be trapped, but I could feel a hand on the other side.

For most of that night, I would carry water a body’s length.

Drink from the seam. Turn. Press my mouth to the broken earth. Wait for it to soften. Return.

The hand beyond the fall would move less as the night continued. Once, when I pressed against it through the clay, it would answer with the familiar angled pressure, making allowance for my fingers even there.

By morning we would have a passage broad enough to touch directly.

The surface above us would still look firm.

***

After that, I think I would spend less effort on the place where the visitor stopped.

I would work a little farther upslope, where an older floor lay close beneath the new one. There was less room to live there, but also less room for the ground to give way. Several of us could bed a strip along it without closing an occupied chamber.

We would not be able to tell the walker why the strip was there. We could only make it easier underfoot than the neighboring clay.

Perhaps it would be used. Perhaps the next crossing would miss it entirely.

I would still have to eat. The seam beside my shoulder would still need opening after sand washed into it. The neighbor’s hand would remain slow for a while, and I would wait with mine against it, finding the familiar pressures among the changed ones.

Once, late in the evening, I would come far enough out to see the walker.

Not its face. My eyes would find a tall interruption in the remaining light. It would be standing on the firmer strip, with no part of its body laid down to rest. Beneath my smaller hands, the ground would carry a slight, continuous shifting.

I would lower myself before I could learn much more.

There would be green crust along the sheltered margin. I would eat there, leaving the growth inside the crack untouched. As the light went, my neighbor would emerge beside me and begin working the other end of the patch.

Between us, the clay would cool.

When the distant impacts began again, I would stop feeding long enough to feel which way they went.
