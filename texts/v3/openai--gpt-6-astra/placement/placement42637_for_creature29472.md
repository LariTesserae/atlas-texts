I would be in the jar beyond the break.

Not in the crushed neck itself, where the living cord has been pinched almost through, but in the vessel after it: the dull, loose tissue that still feeds, still holds to the pottery, and receives very little from the rest of the body.

There would be room there. More room than I could use.

Along the bottom of the jar, a fold of me would lie in a shallow residue of water. Above it, the clay would curve away into air. I would have no sight of that curve. I would know its lower portion by contact and its upper portion by the different places from which drops returned.

One drop would fall regularly on a surface that had stopped withdrawing.

Another would arrive only during the rise, cold enough to produce a small contraction. That contraction would travel toward the damaged neck and end there, or seem to. Much of what happened in me would end there.

At first, this would be the whole place.

The water would grow fresh, then stale. Fine matter would arrive. My hairs would incline, and a fold would deepen around what they brought. Sometimes the water would contain enough food that the receiving surface continued to work after the current had ceased. Sometimes it would bring lime, which I would move and move without being able to consume.

There would be a little bank of lime beneath me.

I would have made it without knowing it was a bank. Eventually it would lift one edge of my body clear of the water, and I would draw away from it. The next rise would wash part of it beneath another fold.

My surroundings would consist largely of such corrections.

Then the court would sound.

It would reach this jar through the pottery more strongly than through the damaged cord. The wall against which I rested would become active everywhere. The floor of the vessel, the broken shoulder, a chip held in my tissue: all would tremble.

I would contract.

There would be no feeding sheets here to draw back, no crab above me, no insect caught in a minute cup. Nevertheless, the old response would pass through what remained of me. A soft ridge would pull itself from the jar’s wall. Water would enter behind it.

When the chorus ended, the ridge would settle a little differently.

This would happen many times.

In that small difference, something would begin.

***

I would not know that I belonged to a larger animal.

Belonging would arrive as a faint tightening at the neck of the jar, usually after some stronger event had already passed through the clay. The tightening would pull on tissue I could not release. I would answer it, slowly. Sometimes there would be another movement against my answer.

There would also be substances arriving through the cord.

During some periods, these would keep me alive when the water brought almost nothing. During others, they would diminish, and I would consume the softest of my own folds. The rim of an old attachment would remain, tougher than the tissue inside it. In a later abundance I would grow across that rim again.

So I would have scars within the scarred part.

I would not experience myself as an injured version of a complete shape. There would be nowhere in me that held such a shape. The narrow place would simply be difficult. Growth toward it would meet pressure. Growth away from it would meet water, then clay.

I would spread where spreading was possible.

Near the shoulder of the vessel, the inner surface would be rough with something other than pottery. Mortar had entered through a crack long ago and hardened in a low, irregular tongue. At its edge, water would sometimes arrive before the water at the bottom rose.

I would extend a little tissue there.

The first extension would dry.

The next would last until the jar emptied, then shrink to a thread.

In time, a fold would remain wet enough to persist. Its underside would hold moisture against the mortar. Fine particles would lodge beneath it. A darker skin would form along the exposed margin.

There would be no decision to leave the jar. There would be an edge that survived slightly farther out.

***

Above me, a crab would spend much of its life under a curved piece of clay.

I would never encounter the crab. Its shelter would scrape a particular brick during the chorus, but that sound would be lost among the others before it reached my vessel. Once, perhaps, some small part of its meal would wash down to me. I would receive it without receiving the crab.

The amphibians would be more intimate and less distinct.

Their calling would loosen my hold on the wall. Their silence would let me attach again. The pressure that woke them would sometimes reach me as a slight increase in water against my lowest fold.

Of their bellies, their eggs, their fidelity to particular chambers, I would know nothing.

Yet each chorus would admit water behind the same ridge of my body. Each settling would leave that ridge nearer the wet mortar seam.

Their lives would help determine the direction in which I grew.

I would be that far inside the workings of the court. Nothing visible would need to approach me to alter my body.

***

Eventually my growing edge would pass through the shoulder of the jar.

Outside would be a narrow pocket between fired clay and mortar. No open chamber, only a space left by two surfaces that had never fitted closely. Water moved through it during the rise. During the fall, enough remained to hold its ceiling and floor together with a film.

I would enter that film.

Here I would be thin and quick again.

A grain shifting against me would produce a contraction along my whole exposed length. Water pressing from the wrong direction would fold me back into the crack. The tissue in the jar would take much longer to respond. For a while, one part of me would already have retreated while another was still beginning to withdraw.

As I grew, the pocket would divide.

One way would end against a dry seam. I would leave a sealed tip there.

The other would turn beneath the vessel, toward a current I had not previously felt.

There would be food in it. Not much, but continuously: material missed by surfaces farther along the passage. I would extend a fringe. Water would pass through it and continue into darkness.

Then, during a rise, the fringe would touch something soft.

It would withdraw at once.

The other surface would also withdraw.

For a long time afterward, there would be water between us.

***

I think this is where I would become less easy to locate.

The other surface might be part of the body from which the collapsed jar had nearly severed me. It might belong to another animal that had entered the buried pottery through a drain. No mark on it would explain the difference.

On the next contact, neither surface would withdraw as far.

On later contacts, the fringes would lie against one another in the current. Fine matter would gather between them. A fold would open on one side, then close. For a while one surface might try to feed on the other.

There would be damage. There would be repair.

If we joined, it would happen over many risings of the water.

A patch of contact would remain after the surrounding tissue drew apart. It would stretch. It would thicken. Fluid would begin to pass through it.

Something happening elsewhere would then reach me by two routes.

The old cord at the broken neck would tighten late, as it always had. But before that, through the new connection beneath the jar, a much clearer change would arrive. My folds would lift. Water would move into spaces they had occupied.

For the first time in a very long while, I would withdraw before the clay began to shake.

The pier would call.

Above, the crab would lower its raised foot.

In the jar, some of my oldest tissue would remain motionless. The lime bank would still be there. The drop would still strike the surface that no longer withdrew.

But along the underside of the vessel, a new cord would hold.

Through it, something small and nourishing would arrive from a chamber I had never touched. I would begin to take it in while the calling continued.
