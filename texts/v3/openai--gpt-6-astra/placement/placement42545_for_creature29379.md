I think I would live above a passage, in a room whose floor belongs, in some disputed sense, to the people beneath it.

There would be one place where I must not put my weight before they wake. Not because the floor is weak. Because directly below it is the shelf on which they keep their cups, and one cup has a foot that was never ground quite flat.

I would forget occasionally.

From below would come a single deliberate pressure against the wall.

I would move my heel.

The room would have been suitable when I first took it. Now a stem passing through the back corner would have thickened enough to push the wall lining inward. Each season I would cut the lining back a little. The alternative would be to ask three households to consider moving the plant.

My bedding would fit between that corner and a low cupboard. To open the cupboard fully, I would have to roll the bedding away. Usually I would reach through the partly opened door and find things by touch.

There would be a pale spot on the back of my hand from doing this.

***

At first I imagine that I would mend wicks. There is something agreeable about the work: sitting with a length of fiber across my knees, teasing apart the compacted portion, finding where the water stops.

But I would not want every failed planting brought to me. Some plants die with perfectly good wicks. Some pots are placed badly. Some people continue to add water because the leaves look thirsty, long after the roots have ceased to be able to use it.

I would have opinions about these people.

And I would dislike having to carry a wet wick through the cold while its owner followed, explaining that it had worked very well before I touched it.

Perhaps I would do that work sometimes. Most inhabitants would know how. What I would be better at, what people would come upstairs to ask for, is making a thing fit again after something around it has changed.

A lid whose pot has lost a piece of its rim. A sleeve fastening that a stiffened hand can no longer close. The seat beside a hearth that now has to accommodate two people, neither of whom will admit to being wider than before.

I would not make much that was entirely new.

In my cupboard would be short lengths of material too sound to throw away, divided according to distinctions that other people found excessive. Flexible when damp. Flexible when warm. Safe beside a root. Likely to stain. Suitable for something that must eventually be taken apart.

One container would hold pieces whose previous use I could no longer remember.

I would keep them anyway. Occasionally I would need one.

***

I would be an adult of no particular distinction, narrow even among these narrow people, with the close nap worn away along one forearm. When thinking, I would tuck my opposing digit beneath the other fingers and press the resulting fist against my lower ribs.

My hands would not be equally capable. The left would hold things steadily. The right would do the finer work, except in cold weather, when its smallest finger would draw inward and stay there until warmed.

I would be vain about how seldom this interfered.

Someone who worked with me regularly would know better. They would put a difficult knot down within reach of my left hand without mentioning it. I would find this consideration both useful and irritating.

I do not think I would be especially old. Old enough to have begun recognizing my own repairs when they came back worn out. Young enough to feel that they ought to have lasted longer.

There would be a child who liked to sit on the upper stair and watch.

I would give them tasks, partly because children need tasks and partly because being watched while I struggled with something would make me clumsy. They would be good at separating fibers and poor at putting the separated fibers anywhere they could be found again.

Once, after a long search, I would discover a careful fringe of them tucked along the stair rail.

They would explain that they had been sorting them by length.

They would indeed have been sorted by length.

***

The work I would remember most would concern a woman who had lost some feeling in one foot.

She could walk. That was not the difficulty. She could also feel the town through her other sole and through her hands. But her accustomed place was beside a window, on a broad seat with her work in her lap, and the wall there had been lined years earlier against damp. To touch bare stone she had to lean forward and reach beneath the seat.

She had begun doing this every few moments.

Her household wanted a handle fixed beside her.

She did not want a handle.

I would go to look, expecting that the work would take very little time.

The first thing I made would fit poorly. Not badly enough to be unusable: a narrow piece of limestone, wedged against the wall and brought out through the lining where her wrist could rest on it. But its edge would catch her sleeve whenever she turned toward the window.

I would round it.

Now it would be too short.

She would wait while I tried another piece. Her household would offer advice from the doorway. Eventually she would ask them to bring something from a distant room, and we would have a little quiet.

Only then would I notice where she put her hand when she was not being asked to put it anywhere.

Beside her hip. A little behind it. Palm down.

We would lift the seat and set a stone through a notch at its rear, with its foot bearing directly on the masonry below. There would be some work involved in keeping the seat from touching it. I would have to return for a thinner flake.

When it was finished, the pale pad of her hand would lie against the exposed top without any change in her posture.

She would sit for a while.

I would begin gathering my tools too soon, wanting her to approve of it.

“Wait,” she would say.

Someone would be coming along the passage underneath us.

Her fingers would open slightly.

I would leave the first, unsuccessful piece behind because she had found that it held her window covering at a useful angle.

***

I would not be the town’s interpreter of the great weights overhead. I would know a few recurring disturbances, as everyone did, and be wrong about others.

A heavy step might send me to the doorway with my hand against the jamb. I might remain there after my neighbors had resumed their work.

There would have been an occasion, years before, when something shifted late.

Nothing very terrible. A stored slab falling after the vibration itself had passed. But I would have been close to it, and my body would have retained the interval more faithfully than I retained the date.

People would know to leave me standing.

The child would not, at first. They would ask what I was feeling.

“Nothing yet,” I would say.

Later I would regret the word *yet*.

***

When you looked into the town, I might be the one too far back in the doorway to distinguish.

I would have come down because the entrance had changed temperature.

From where I stood, I would be able to see part of your face, though not enough to understand its expression. An eye would be difficult to look at for long. I would look instead at the skin beside it, at the small movement caused by your breathing, at a grain of pale stone caught against your cheek.

I would wonder whether you knew it was there.

The person beneath the awning would continue watching you. I would wait behind them, with one hand against the inside wall. I would not know what to offer, or whether offering anything would encourage you to remain.

Then you would move away.

The air would come through sharply enough to lift an unsecured scrap from my sleeve. I would catch it against my body.

While the feather was being adjusted, I would hold the empty tray. There would be a slight residue on it from the six slices. Without thinking, I would turn it toward the light to see whether it needed cleaning.

Its owner would reach back for it before finishing the work.

I would keep holding it.

“You need both hands,” I would say.

They would leave it with me.

***

That evening, I would bring home a fastening I had promised to return that morning.

The child would have left the sorted fibers in a shallow container outside my door, weighted with a stone. Under the container would be one fiber much longer than the others, too long to fit.

I would take everything inside.

Downstairs, the household would already be asleep. I would step across the troublesome part of the floor, lower myself beside the bedding, and tuck my right hand under my coat.

The fastening could wait until the finger loosened.

With my other hand I would open the cupboard a little way and put the long fiber where it would not be bent.
