At first I would be under the stair that is no longer a stair.

One of its former risers has retained a hollow behind it. Water enters at the left, crosses a shallow bed of metal dust, and leaves through a fastening hole at the right. The hollow is warm at its back and cold near its mouth. I could move between those temperatures without exposing any part of myself to the plain.

For a while, this would seem sufficient.

I would learn the load in the stair, the slight displacement that precedes the return of water, the places where the ceramic conducts a sharp event and where it blurs one. I would hold a scrap of mesh over the fastening hole. There would be feeding folds to open, grit to loosen, a passage to keep clear.

But the hollow would be empty of other bodies, and eventually I would spend too much of each draw with my front margin open toward the entrance.

Not calling. There would be no way to call.

Waiting for something to touch it.

***

I think I would still be small enough to travel.

Not young enough to cross every exposed strip as soon as it emptied. I would have lost some of that speed. One side of my skirt would attach less securely than the other, and on smooth surfaces I would move obliquely, testing before shifting my weight.

Under my body I would carry a curved washer.

Its hole would be too large to stop any of the leaks in my dwelling. Its outer edge would be too rounded to wedge securely into a seam. I would have taken it from a chamber that was later removed by a cutter, and for several cycles afterward I would have carried it to openings where it almost fitted.

By now it would occupy four pads that might otherwise help me walk.

I would keep it.

There would once have been another animal in the chamber from which it came. I would recognize that animal by a small delay between two contacts: the flank first, then the underside, then the flank again more firmly. When we rested, it would keep a palp beneath my damaged margin.

The washer would not resemble that contact. Holding it would not restore anything. But when settling into a new cavity, I would still place it at the same side of the entrance, leaving room behind it.

***

My routes would run between dwellings that no longer communicated directly.

A passage had narrowed. A foundation had settled. Two occupied joints remained close enough for the same pressure front to reach them almost together, but the palps extended from either side could no longer meet.

I would cross the exposed distance.

In one chamber I would be allowed to reach immediately past the entrance, touching the broad, motionless flank of an old inhabitant. In another I would have to wait. A young adult there would examine my folds each time, even after many visits. I would learn not to advance while it did this.

What passed between us would be limited.

The old one could give me a sequence of pressure and release. I could retain it for a time and deliver something like it elsewhere. But in crossing the plain I would have to use those same limbs to grip, examine, feed, carry. A valve closure might interrupt me. The sequence would change.

Sometimes I would arrive with only its urgency.

Sometimes I would arrive after the thing it concerned had already happened.

There would be no position from which I could know all the chambers, no private stillness in which their signals became clear. I would know the way to a place and then discover that the way had filled. I would touch an animal whose contact was familiar and find that it would not admit me.

Nevertheless, when I entered certain hollows, bodies would make room.

I would remain through a reversal, feeding with them. When I left, something might be pressed beneath me: a piece of sealant, a worn fiber, a young animal that had begun to open its folds in water too cold to sustain them.

***

I would become interested in places that had been abandoned.

At first for practical reasons. They contained useful objects. Their entrances were often already shaped to admit a body of my size. Some retained a little warmth.

Then I would begin going back to a particular one beneath a blue mark almost entirely worn from a service wall.

Its inhabitants had left after an old animal withdrew from the upper chamber. I had helped carry one of the young. During the crossing, its pads had attached to the washer rather than to me, and I had been unable to put the washer down until we reached shelter.

The wall had not failed.

After many reversals, its empty chamber still received a warm trickle. The wedges remained where they had been placed. A colorless growth thickened in the meeting of the waters and then began to obstruct the lower passage.

I cleared it.

On my next visit I cleared it again.

There would be something difficult here for me: a dwelling could be warm, fed, apparently stable, and still be one that others had left. My pads would find nothing wrong with it. The old animal’s withdrawal would remain in the way I approached its entrance.

I would not settle there.

I would keep it habitable.

***

Once, I would bring another traveler.

We would meet near a gutter during the outward draw. It would have a strip of damaged skin folded under itself, dragging a little despite its efforts to hold it clear. When I touched its front margin, it would press against me too hard, then pull away.

I would move toward the blue-marked wall and stop.

It would not follow.

I would return, touch it again, and go only as far as the first seam.

This time it would come.

Getting it into the chamber would take most of the remaining interval. I would have to remove an iron flake from the entrance. Without the flake, the warmer flow would begin mixing too early; the water over the feeding bed would cool. For a while neither of us would release enough pads to feed.

The return would arrive.

Under the wall, with the injured animal pressed against the back of the cavity, I would feel a movement I had never felt there before. Small, deep, sustained beyond the pressure front.

I would contract. So would it.

There would be nowhere to go until the deck drained again.

Nothing would break.

Later, I would replace the flake at a different angle. The animal would open two folds. I would remain near the mouth with most of my body still closed.

When I finally left, it would touch beneath my damaged margin.

Not in the familiar sequence.

I would pause anyway.

***

That is where I would begin to have a place in the world, I think: not simply in the hollow beneath the stair, but along a set of routes that other bodies had started to expect me to use.

I would bring things to the chamber under the blue mark. Others would alter what I had arranged. A fiber I had placed carefully would be pulled out and used elsewhere. The injured traveler would recover enough to move between two neighboring cavities, and on one visit I would find young beneath its skirt.

There would be no moment when the abandoned dwelling became safe.

It would become occupied.

My visits would continue. I would still hesitate at the entrance.

***

Eventually I would have difficulty passing beneath the old route marker.

I would discover this during an ordinary crossing, with enough time left before the return. My front portion would go through. The thickened center would not. I would flatten, release, try at an angle.

The washer would scrape the ceramic beneath me.

I would back out.

For several cycles I would use a longer route. Then that route would become difficult too.

I would settle beneath a cover between the stair and the blue-marked wall. Its chamber would not be the warmest. It would have two entrances, however, opening onto different seams. Travelers could reach it from either side.

I would place the washer near the colder entrance.

As my body extended along the underside of the cover, I would have to move it farther away. Eventually someone else would move it for me. I would feel its rounded edge passing across my folds and hold it briefly before letting go.

The animal carrying it would wait.

I might keep one palp against the metal longer than was useful.

Then it would be taken to the lip of the opening, where a seep had begun washing mineral paste from beneath my skirt. Set flat, it would spread the flow. Fine grit would gather in its hole.

There, after all that carrying, it would fit.

***

I would receive the plain through a smaller and smaller piece of it.

Visitors would arrive with cold undersides and the strain of the crossing still held in their bodies. I would touch them until their pads began to release. Sometimes they would bring young. Sometimes a contact I expected would fail to return, and other contacts would become familiar in its absence.

I would know the cover’s ordinary movements very well.

I would also be wrong sometimes.

Once I would close my upper folds and draw inward, and the others would carry the young away. The next draw would pass. Then another. The cover would remain seated, the water warm.

When someone returned, I would open the entrance.

There would be no useful pressure sequence that could make the mistake unhappen. I would let the returning animal examine me. I would help clear the deposits that had formed while the chamber stood empty.

And later, beneath the great sound, while water crossed the washer and entered along my side, a very small body would reach past the shelter of an adult’s margin.

Its first contact would be uncertain. One pad, withdrawn. Two, placed farther apart.

I would shift a fold out of its way.

It would advance into the warmth, touching as it came.
