I think I would be upstairs at first, trying to get nearer the light.

There is a room above one of the passages, scarcely wider than the passage itself. Its floor is someone else’s ceiling. A shallow bowl set beside the shutter holds small things that must not be lost: pins, a clasp tongue, two pieces of a broken button. In the morning I move the bowl toward the light. Later I move my stool. By afternoon I am working with one elbow raised because the only useful brightness falls above a shelf.

I have chosen the room before understanding the stairs.

They turn tightly around a thick-leaved plant whose owner lives below. Carrying anything broad requires lifting it above the leaves, then lowering it under the landing. I strike my head twice in the first week. The owner hears both blows and calls out advice without coming to look.

But I would not stay upstairs all day. There would be too much below that I wanted to hear, and eventually someone would point out that I spend more time leaning over the railing than working.

So there is a second place: under the stairs, opposite a doorway that is generally open. A bench fits there if its back legs are shortened to accommodate the rise in the paving. The plant hangs beside my face. Occasionally a drop falls from it, though nobody admits to having watered it.

This is where I begin to become someone.

Not a master repairer. There are enough people here who can examine a broken fitting, turn it once, and know where the strain went. I would want that ability before I had earned it. I would make a convincing repair that failed three days later, and the person bringing it back would stand in the passage while I explained too much.

“It broke,” she would say.

“Yes.”

“In the same place.”

After that I would work more slowly.

What I am good at, initially, is keeping the parts together.

Work arrives wrapped in rags, stuffed into split baskets, tied with cord that belongs to the customer and must be returned. A packet may contain four broken objects and a piece that belongs to none of them. There are instructions: mend this cheaply, make this strong, leave this one alone if the cost exceeds so much. The person carrying the bundle has forgotten which instruction applies to which object.

I spread everything out. I ask again. I find containers.

Soon there are shallow trays beneath my bench. Old cooking lids, mostly, with cloth stretched over their holes. Each holds one person’s work. I cut small marks into scraps of wood and tie matching marks to the wrapping and the tray. The system seems excellent until a child borrows three of the wooden pieces for a game.

For an afternoon I am angry beyond what the loss deserves.

The child is brought to apologize. She watches me sort two households’ buckle parts by the color of the corrosion.

“Couldn’t you make them different shapes?” she asks.

I say I could, in a tone that makes her stop helping.

Later I make them different shapes.

My own room remains upstairs. I sleep badly when the wind is from the west, although it scarcely reaches the shutter. Something in the roof ticks in that weather. I keep believing I have found the loose fastening. Each time, I have found a different loose fastening.

I would have a body suited imperfectly to this place. Long fingers, useful for lifting a pin from a crack. Knees that object to the bench. One shoulder held higher than the other from years of following the light across a work surface. I would be old enough that my eagerness to prove myself had become embarrassing, but not old enough to have stopped.

And I would have come from below.

Not recently. Long enough ago that people no longer pause over my breathing. Not so long ago that I have forgotten the sound of uncovered water.

When someone shakes grain into the mill, there is an instant when I hear rain. I never mention it. I am not certain I miss rain enough to go down for it. Down below there are also people I have been postponing writing to.

This would matter. I should not become only the useful person under the stairs. There ought to be an unanswered letter in the room above, folded until its edges have gone soft.

Issa knows about it because she has seen me take it out.

“Still no answer?”

“I haven’t decided what to say.”

“They’ll be dead before you decide.”

“They’re younger than you.”

“That is no protection.”

She brings me things without asking whether I am busy. Not usually strangers’ possessions; those she takes to someone she trusts more. She brings the awkward little household failures nobody wants to spend a whole morning on. A lid that rocks. A handle that pinches. The hook inside a cupboard that has begun to pull free.

Once she brings nothing. She stands beside the bench and rubs flour from her wrist.

Her brother is late.

I know because everyone knows, but she has not discussed it with me. She asks whether the carrier who came yesterday said anything about the lower crossing.

He did. I repeat it.

“Which crossing?”

I cannot be certain.

“With the white bank?”

“I don’t remember him saying.”

“You remember what he owes for six rivets.”

There is no useful answer to this. I put down the piece I am holding, which annoys her further because now she feels she has interrupted me for nothing.

After she leaves, I go looking for the carrier. He has gone to sleep in a house at the other end of the hollow. I wake him. He is furious. The crossing he meant is nowhere near her brother’s route.

I bring Issa this small, inadequate clarification. She listens from inside her cupboard, where she is holding a pot against her hip while trying to reach another.

“Well,” she says. “Hold this, then.”

I hold it.

That is probably where I would find myself most exactly: not at the western entrance looking out, although I would go there often; not alone above the roofs, making something of the distance. Inside a house, holding an inconvenient pot because I have arrived with news that changes very little.

Later there would be work again.

A wooden vessel comes to me with a split running through an earlier repair. Someone below has paid for it to be made sound but not handsome. I turn it under the stairway light. There are holes for an old binding, and one has worn nearly through to the edge. I consider another hole, set farther back.

The girl who borrowed my tally pieces sits nearby, stripping a length of unusable cord into fibers.

“Will that break too?”

“Eventually.”

“Soon?”

“I’m trying to prevent soon.”

She accepts this and moves her feet out of my way.

Toward evening the stairway darkens. I put the loose pieces in their tray and cover it before the sparrows come searching. Above us, someone drags a stool across my floor to reach the shutter I have left open. They call down to ask whether I want it closed.

“Halfway,” I say.

They complain that it will not stay halfway.

“Put the little wedge under it.”

“What little wedge?”

I get up to show them.
