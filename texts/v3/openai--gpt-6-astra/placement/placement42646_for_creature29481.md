The first thing I would notice is that someone has put a foot where the water should be.

The current divides around it. A thin stream reaches the backs of my fingers; the other has stopped. I am holding a stone tilted in the outlet, waiting for the small things beneath it to loosen their grip, and this interruption is inconvenient.

I reach upstream.

There is a yielding surface, a ridge, a harder surface underneath. When I pull, the whole obstruction moves. Water runs properly over my hand again.

For a little while, that is enough to know about you.

***

I would live near the outlet, though I would sleep farther around the margin, where the pear-scented creeper has grown through a mat of stiff leaves. My chamber would have a low entrance. Getting into it would require a particular turn of the rear feet, learned when I was smaller and never quite reconsidered.

There would be a root pressing into one side of the floor. Periodically I would work at it. Periodically I would fall asleep before finishing.

I think I would be one of the animals whose feet come away from the stone too loudly.

I can move quietly. It takes attention. When carrying something interesting, I forget, and the little snaps travel ahead of me. An older animal resting beside the pool sometimes answers with a closed-throat sound. I know this sound from when I was small enough to sleep beneath a body. It makes me slow down even when I have no reason to.

My feeding hands are more patient than my feet.

They can hold a root without crushing its soft tip, turn a wet pellet until the grit comes free, find where one stem has tightened around another. A small hand can remain busy while the rest of me listens. But if a sound is sufficiently strange, everything stops, including the fingers. Anything held between them stays held.

This is how you first acquire a stem that you cannot persuade me to release.

You have taken the other end.

I do not know whether you want the stem, whether you are helping me pull it, or whether your hand has caught accidentally. I wait for more information. You also wait.

Eventually I bite through the middle.

***

The hollow would not be an arrangement held in my head all at once.

Near the bench, the floor carries a broad, faint tremor that disappears when the pool is full. Near my sleeping place, a hollow under the stone answers the settling of my front feet. There is a stretch of the margin where droplets hit leaves before they hit the ground; I can pass it with my listening folds closed and still know where I am.

Some places are reached by sequence.

Water over the left forefoot. Two dry steps. The seam under the rear foot. Then the place to put down a mouthful of something that needs softening.

If I stop halfway, I may have to return to the water and begin again.

You would probably think I had forgotten what I was doing.

Sometimes I would have.

There are things I leave that I never retrieve. A folded leaf works its way into a crack. Someone eats the stem I was saving. A pellet hardens until its surface no longer admits a fingertip, and I carry it around for an interval, dissatisfied, before abandoning it among others at the dry rim.

I would have favorite places to set things down. This would cause difficulties with the other inhabitants, who also have favorite places.

A flat stone near the ring would be especially contentious.

It fits beneath my lowered head. I can rest something there while keeping both hands in the current. Another animal uses it to brace thick leaf bases before stripping them. When I arrive and find fresh rind covering the stone, I push the rind away.

Later, my little heap of softened fibers is somewhere else.

We have done this for a long time.

***

I would know the post first as a place where adults become unavailable.

A hand that was grooming me would stop. The body I was leaning against would rise and go there. If I followed, I could wait close by, but climbing onto that body would bring a firm hand against my chest.

Now I go there myself.

The shaft meets the underside of my head in a place that cannot easily reach anything else. I spread my feet. The warmth enters through the thin skin behind the mouth.

There is a sensation I have no occasion to distinguish from the post itself: a slow easing, followed sometimes by a faint internal tremor that makes me close my working ridges together.

On some visits it does not happen.

I change the placement of my feet. I wait. Eventually I leave, with the peculiar unfinished feeling of having reached into a familiar leaf pocket and found it empty.

I would not know who put the post there.

The question might require more separation between the post and the mountain than I have ever needed to make. Yet I would know immediately if its surface changed. I would return repeatedly to one rough spot, touch it with mouth and hands, bring another animal by the wrist.

Familiarity would make me exacting.

It would not make me wise.

***

You would take much longer to become familiar than a new obstruction in the water.

Your weight is carried high. Small adjustments pass down through you before your feet move. At first I would retreat whenever I felt those preparations. You would often remain where you were.

Your hands arrive without the warning I expect. They are attached to long moving parts, and sometimes descend from above my back rather than advancing near the floor. I would catch one and hold it still while I worked out what the rest of you was doing.

The coverings would trouble me most.

Your outer surface can be removed and left behind, retaining warmth and smell. Once you leave a damp piece of it on the bench. I find it before I find you. I explore its edges, push beneath it, turn it over.

When you return, I am sitting on it.

You pull gently.

I let you take part of it, but my rear foot remains planted until you touch the joint and wait. Then I lift the foot. The fabric peels away with a sound so like a badly released sole that I hold still to listen.

Afterward I search the bench for the rest.

***

I would not be the first animal to rest against you.

There is work at the margin, and the bench is farther from my chamber than the stone beside the outlet. You have also once dropped water onto my open listening fold. I remember the sensation longer than the circumstances.

But your fingers would become useful.

One warm interval, a strip of old leaf winds around the root I am trying to separate. I hold the root away from the bed while picking at the strip. Each pull tightens it. I could bite through both, but the root tip has begun to thicken.

You crouch nearby. Your weight makes the shallow water creep over my toes.

I put the tangled part against your hand.

At first you stroke it.

I press it harder.

Eventually one of your narrow fingers gets beneath the strip. You turn your wrist. I pull too soon, and we lose it. We begin again.

This time I wait.

The strip comes free all at once. You move backward; water slaps the stone. I hold the root suspended until the vibration has passed, then lower its tip into the loosened bed.

You still have the strip.

I do not take it from you.

For several intervals afterward, I bring you things that are caught in other things. Some are ordinary food. One is a clump I could easily pull apart myself. You are not always helpful. You sometimes separate what I intended to keep together.

I learn to keep one hand on the important part.

***

There would be a change in the warmth that I notice too late.

Perhaps I have been occupied with you. Perhaps a chamber full of insects has fallen quiet and held my attention. Heat has already moved through the outlet stone; water is gathering faster along the roots.

I leave a prepared pocket open.

When the dripping becomes steady, its softened floor gives way. A small mass of fibers and root ends washes down the runnel. I catch some against my chest. More passes between my feet.

For a while I keep trying to put it back.

The material will no longer hold. Each handful sags out of the place where I press it. Water runs down both my arms.

An older animal comes and eats some.

I push its hand away.

It waits, then reaches past me and bites through a stem above the pocket. The leaves lower. Most of the water begins falling elsewhere.

We work beside each other. When you approach, I have both hands buried in the growth and do not turn toward you.

There would be much of my life like this: urgent to me, only partly intelligible to you, accomplished with help I had initially resisted.

***

Later, I would come to the bench.

I test the surface with the backs of my hands. Your foot is in the place I want, so I stand against it until you move. Then I turn, settle, turn again. One rear limb hangs off the edge. I bring it up.

In a channel along my flank, a short length of root has dried into the nap.

I can reach it, but only by twisting until my feet begin to slip. I try twice. On the third attempt, your fingers touch the place.

I become still.

You take longer than another animal would. Once you pull the hairs instead of the root, and I close a hand around your wrist. You stop. I loosen my grip.

The root comes free in several pieces.

You go on searching after it is gone.

I let you, for a while. Then I move your hand lower, to a place beneath the shoulder where the skin is warm and difficult to reach.
