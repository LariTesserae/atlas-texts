At first I would stay beside the pool. There is enough room there to put a hand down without feeling something move beneath it, and I would mistake that for an invitation.

I would listen to the animal drinking. After she left, I would wait for her to return.

But waiting would bring the smaller movements into view: a fly drawing its front legs over its head; a loose wing turning slightly whenever warm air reached the water; something beneath that wing holding it against the current. Eventually I would lower my face toward the rim. The black surface would cease to be a single thing. There would be grit above it, grit below it, a wet band through which a small creature could walk without entering water deep enough to drown.

That band interests me more than the pool.

So does the underside of the shelf. Not the opening through which the mother disappeared, but the low space beside it, where the stone almost meets the floor. A feather has lodged there. Its shaft keeps the gap open, though some of its barbs have been eaten away.

I think I would find myself behind that feather.

There would be no room to turn my head independently of my body. No room, at first, to do anything but press forward or withdraw. Yet this would not feel like confinement. My back would touch the ceiling, my feet would hold the floor, and the space ahead would be as wide as I needed.

I would move toward a smell.

A little farther in, the floor would fall away from the roof. My back would lose contact. I would stop.

Only then would I begin to know my shape.

Six feet, each finding purchase separately. Antennae short enough to withdraw beneath the edge of my head. A broad back with a join down its middle, dust caught along it. Underneath, parts that must stay underneath.

One of the dull beetles, then. But not quite a pebble. A pebble could remain where it was put.

I would be hungry.

***

The place I occupy is below the usual paths of the furred animals. Their feet pass overhead, on a shelf whose underside forms my ceiling. Sometimes their weight loosens a grain. Sometimes a claw reaches through.

I withdraw when the floor trembles in that particular way.

Other tremblings continue for so long that I go on eating. The bats are above me, though I have never had a view of a bat. What reaches me is the stirring of many bodies, then falling things. Some are edible immediately. Some become edible later. Some move, and I hold still until they have gone.

There is a split insect egg wedged beneath a stone. I work along its edge. My mouthparts take very little at a time.

I do not finish it. Moisture has reached the dust beside my left forefoot. I turn toward that instead.

To reach the dampness I climb a ridge scarcely higher than my body. From beside the pool, this ridge was nothing: a rough spot on a rim. Against my underside it is steep and irregular. One foot catches, another slips. I back down and approach at an angle.

This time I cross.

The water is a film over stone. I settle low against it.

Above me, at an immeasurable distance, a drop grows.

***

I would not have an entirely sound shell.

There would be a shallow dent near the rear of one wing case, and along its edge a place that no longer closes neatly. Perhaps the mother made it. Perhaps another animal, before she occupied the nest above the warm crack.

There is no remembered encounter, but something of it remains in the way I hold myself when touched.

Pressure from above: feet draw in.

Pressure that lifts one side: feet reach and hook.

Overturned, I am all effort. My legs strike whatever comes within reach. A strand of feather can be enough. So can another beetle passing close, though it does not stop for me.

Once the female noses me out of a groove.

Her breath comes warm through the joints of my shell. She turns me, loses me, catches me between her forefeet. The hard tips of her teeth press where my legs have folded away.

I am still.

She works at one edge, and the pressure travels through my whole body. Then there is a sound beside us. Her feet lift.

For some time after she has gone, I remain drawn together.

When I open, one leg takes no weight.

I move with the others. Under the shelf, a soft fragment remains where she was feeding. I find it before I find shelter.

***

There would be eggs.

Not many in one place. I would press into loose material at the back of a warm pocket and leave them separately, in spaces too narrow for my whole body. The warmth there is steady, but the material is not dry. A pale thread runs through it and branches around an old husk.

I would push past the thread.

Farther down, the pocket grows hotter. I turn before reaching its end and spend a long time working free of the entrance, my feet braced against opposite walls.

Behind me, the disturbed grit settles.

I do not stay to see what hatches.

***

When the bats leave, the floor changes gradually.

First there are impacts, fallen grains, small bodies hurrying out of exposed places. Then fewer impacts. The air moves differently beneath the shelf. I come as far as the feather and put my forefeet over its shaft.

The young furred animals have begun to walk above me. One scrabbles at the edge. Dust falls over my back.

I wait.

Its mother moves away, and after a while it follows her.

There is something to eat on the other side of the feather. To reach it I must expose the damaged edge of my shell. I extend one foot, find stone, and pull myself across.

That is where I would be: beneath the shelf beside the pool, but a little farther out than before.

Not far enough to be noticed from a standing height.

Far enough that, when the next drop falls, I feel it.
