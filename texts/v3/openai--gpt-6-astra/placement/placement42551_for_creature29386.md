I think I would live near the beginning of a long sheltered route, in a house that people mistake for somewhere farther along.

They would come down the steps expecting someone else. I would hear them hesitate at the turn, where the wall is close enough that a basket has to be carried in front of the body.

“Not here,” I would call.

But sometimes they would already have taken off their carrying straps, and then it would seem unreasonable not to offer them a seat.

The house would have one good standing place. I would keep it clear, though there would always be something drying there that ought to have been moved. Near the entrance, where a little daylight reaches the floor, I can imagine a row of clay objects under a damp cloth. Not all bowls. A shallow elbow of pipe. Three lids waiting for the jars they are meant to fit. Something small and impractical turned toward the wall.

I would work with clay.

Not especially fine clay. It would arrive in dense wrapped lumps from a place I seldom visit, carried by someone who complains about the weight while I complain about the grit. We would have had this conversation for years. Neither of us would consider it finished.

Before using the clay, I would break it down in a covered basin and work it between my fingers, finding the hard grains. The discarded grains would go into a little pot. There would be no reason to keep them, except that at first I had meant to find a use for them, and now the pot would be nearly full.

My hands would look cleaner than they are. Pale clay lodged beside the nails, a smooth patch across the heel of each palm. A small split on one thumb that closes during wet work and opens again when I carry something.

I would not make the largest water jars. Those require more uninterrupted space than I have, and someone else makes them better. What people bring me are awkward requests.

A bowl to sit beneath a drip too near the wall.

A drinking cup for a man whose fingers no longer close.

A cover for a smoke hole that has never been properly round.

Often they bring the broken thing and ask for another exactly like it. Then, while I turn the pieces over, they explain the six ways it was inconvenient.

***

I would have come to this work late enough to resent being called naturally suited to it.

There would have been other work before it: carrying humus, clearing channels, helping unpick old rope. For a while I would have gone regularly to the lower gardens. I would still know the route well enough to give directions that have become slightly wrong.

One knee would no longer fold comfortably beneath me. Nothing dramatic would have happened to it. It would simply have begun to hurt, then continued. On a steep descent I would turn sideways and lower myself with my hands.

I would dislike people watching this.

I would also be annoyed when they went ahead without checking.

The workbench would therefore be higher than people expect, built across two stone shelves. A visitor trying to help would have to stand in the one good standing place. We would get in each other’s way until I admitted that the help was useful.

I would have a sister living beyond the fern beds. She would think the house too isolated, although she would send people to it whenever they needed something repaired. During long spells of bad wind she would arrange for a child to stop at my door under a transparent pretext.

“Do you have the lid?”

“What lid?”

“The one she said.”

I would find something for the child to carry back. Otherwise my sister would come herself.

***

The first thing I would make entirely for my own pleasure would be a bad bird.

I can see it tucked behind the drying lids: a hollow clay body, a pinched head, wings too thick to suggest flight. I would have tried to make the broad bird seen from below, pale against the light, and discovered that my hands knew vessels much better than animals.

It would look like a pouring jug that had changed its mind.

A child would tell me this.

The child would be right.

There would be a hole beneath its tail from forming the hollow body, and another at the beak. One evening, while cleaning dust out of it, I would blow through the wrong opening and get a thin, disagreeable note.

That would occupy much more of my time than it deserved.

The next bird would be worse to look at and better to blow through. The third would make no sound once fired. I would keep it because its feet pleased me.

I would begin making little hollow things that fit inside a hand. Some would chirp; some would give a breathy two-note call; some would do nothing until tilted, then squeal loudly enough to provoke complaints. They would need to be played indoors. Outside, a person could empty their lungs into one and hear only wind.

I would give the first usable one to my sister’s child, who would be delighted.

My sister would bring it back the following morning.

After that, I would put more effort into low notes.

***

Eda would come to me because a bowl beneath her condensation slab had begun to rock.

She would bring the bowl, though the fault might be in the shelf. I would say this. She would say she had brought what could be carried.

We would both consider that an adequate answer.

Eventually I would go to look. Her niece would show me where the water now followed the stone, tracing its route with a finger held just clear of the wet surface. I would ask her not to touch it, discover that she already knew, and feel foolish.

What was needed would be an uneven-footed bowl, higher at one side, with its rim drawn inward where a drop sometimes struck hard enough to splash. I would take impressions in soft clay and wrap them carefully for the walk home.

The first replacement would be wrong.

Not badly wrong. It would sit firmly, catch the water, and be difficult to lift without spilling because I had left too little room for the fingers.

Eda would use it for something else. That would irritate me more than if she had broken it.

The next one would have two small ears, thick enough not to snap. Her niece would choose where they went.

I would begin asking people to show me how they picked a thing up, not merely where they put it down.

Sometimes this would reveal habits they did not know they had. Sometimes it would reveal that they had been using a vessel in a way no maker could reasonably have anticipated. I would say so, and they would explain that it was their vessel.

***

There would be a vent in the back of my house.

Only a narrow one. It would breathe through an opening beneath a shelf, too small to reach into without scraping the knuckles. In warm weather it would smell faintly of wet iron. Occasionally a thread of pale root would emerge and grow a little before drying.

I would have been told to keep the opening clear.

For several years I would keep it clear.

Then I would make a small clay collar to fit around it, with a lip shaped like the mouth of one of my whistles. I would tell myself it might give warning when the air reversed. That would be a respectable use for it, and not wholly invented. Mostly I would want to hear what happened.

For two days it would do nothing.

On the third night it would produce a low, unsteady sound, almost below hearing. The shelf would tremble against its peg. I would wake with the conviction that someone had spoken beside my bed.

By morning I would have removed it.

But I would not throw it away.

There would be other trials, made where I could reach the opening easily, never left in place while I slept. A few would sing. Most would spit dampness or gather dust. Once something far inside the passage would answer with a clicking sound that continued after I took the collar off.

I would listen for a long time.

I would tell people about the clicking. Inevitably, the account would become more definite as it travelled, and someone would arrive to hear the creature I had discovered under my floor.

We would spend an uneventful afternoon beside the shelf.

I would be embarrassed. They would be disappointed. Before leaving, they would order a small jar.

***

So that is where I find myself: not at an edge, though I would go to the edges sometimes, and not deep enough beneath the stone to learn what moves there.

In a room with a high workbench and an inconvenient entrance. Within reach of other people, but not so easily that a visit costs nothing.

I would know the dimensions of several houses I had entered only once. I would recognize certain customers by the way they set down a broken bowl. There would be work I delayed because it was dull, and work I delayed because I was afraid of spoiling it. The little clay birds would gradually improve, though I would keep the first one behind the lids.

On an afternoon when the wind made travel impossible, I would lift the damp cloth and uncover a cup ordered for someone’s child. Its handle would still be soft enough to alter.

I would put my smallest finger through it.

Too narrow.

Outside, something large would pass roaring over the roof. Fine soil would fall onto the workbench. I would cover the cup with one hand and wait, listening for the separate creak of the branch above the doorway.

When it came, familiar and brief, I would brush the bench clean and widen the handle.
