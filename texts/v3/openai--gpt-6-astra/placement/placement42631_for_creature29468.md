I would be under the freight platform, near the corner that has recently grown a curb.

There is a strip there that neither the flushing water nor the falling drops quite reaches. Grit collects in it. So do the dry ends of roots, which have a different taste from living roots and require a long time to soften. I would begin by mistaking this place for the extent of myself: the warm underside, the grit, the shallow notch where something heavy was once dragged across the platform.

Then the pumps would start.

My feet would tighten in small, separate groups. Between them, the branching parts of me would withdraw from one patch of wall and press more deeply into another. The platform would pull toward the loading door. Somewhere beyond my reach, a fastening would resist.

That difference would be how I knew I had edges.

I would be a little larger than the young ones at the split, but not yet thick enough to be mistaken for a fixture. Along one side I would carry a hard white inclusion, a piece of mineral shelf that I had enclosed while feeding. I could soften the material around it, turn it slowly, but not digest it. When I crossed a smooth surface, it would hold several of my feet out of contact.

Consequently I would usually turn in the same direction.

This would give me a route.

From the platform, along the underside of a gallery, around a drain collar, down the sheltered face of a stair. The route would be poor in food except at its lowest end. I would take it anyway. My leftward turn would bring me reliably to the collection slots, and what began as an obstruction in my body would acquire the usefulness of knowing where to go.

I would not know the square as a square. The eastern wall would reach me as a change in dissolved iron, delayed by the water. The high plants would announce themselves when their leaves were bruised. Of the bridge I might know only the places where its weight entered the walls, two distant concentrations that altered together.

There would be large stretches of the district I had never touched and nevertheless depended on.

At feeding, I would open more widely than I ever did beneath the platform. Water would pass through folds that otherwise lay sealed against one another. A softened leaf would adhere to me, and for a while its shape would interfere with my own. I would move badly afterward, full and thick, unable to draw myself through the narrow space beside the drain collar.

I would wait there until I fitted again.

Once, another animal would wait against me.

Neither of us would move away when there was room. Our margins would loosen where they met. Something inside me, accumulated over many meals, would become available to the other; something from the other would enter the slow circulation beneath my back.

Later I would resume my route.

For several days a smaller body would remain near the collar. I might touch it each time I passed. Eventually it would be gone. There would be no damaged place, no trace in the water that required a response. I would continue downward.

Then a person would use the freight platform.

Not to load anything. They would sit on its edge to remove a shoe.

The new pressure would be badly distributed: one narrow weight, another swinging free, both continually changing. I would flatten beneath the corner. My feet would release and catch. The person would brace a hand behind them, almost directly above me, and the platform would bend enough to press my hard inclusion into its underside.

For the first time, the inclusion would break.

Only a little. A flake would come away inside me. But afterward I would be able to turn farther toward that side.

The person would empty a small stone from the shoe, put the shoe on, and leave. Their weight would be absent before the warmth of their hand had reached me.

I would remain beneath that spot through a pumping cycle, then another.

When I moved, I would take a route I had not taken before.

It would lead to the lowest stair.

***

Here, I think, I would begin to become something less easily lifted away.

The stair would have a small defect beneath its outer edge. Nothing visible from above: two layers separating, with a wet interval between them. Each time the basin emptied, that interval would draw air. Each time the water returned, it would close.

I would fit into it.

At first this would be shelter. The pressure would be even, the moisture dependable. My feet would find purchase on both sides. There would be traces of the clear material I had left elsewhere, familiar enough to make attachment easy.

Then a person would descend the stairs.

The tread would open around me.

I would spread quickly into the widening space, faster than I could travel along a wall. The branching structures underneath me would swell. Some would tear. By the time the foot lifted, I would have laid a thin, soft layer between the separated parts.

I would be hungry afterward.

I could still leave. For a few nights I would, drawing most of myself out along one edge and descending to the pulp. But each return would place me more exactly inside the defect. My hard inclusion would lodge in a recess. Silver threads would begin to continue through me rather than stop at my skin.

My route would shorten.

There would still be an exposed margin beneath the tread, tender and able to gather itself away from a drop. There would still be feet. But much of what I had used for movement would now be holding two surfaces at the right distance from each other.

I would learn a footstep in portions.

First the stair above would transmit a brief sideways strain. Then my tread would receive the front of a shoe. Weight would arrive, shift, and leave. People carrying things would remain over me longer. A child might put both feet down and pause. Once, someone would sit on the step, and I would spend an hour making adjustments too small to improve their comfort.

I would not become particularly good at people. A bucket set down sharply could trouble me more than a person standing still.

But the stair would cease to draw air when the basin emptied.

***

There would be a season, perhaps, when nobody came.

I would grow thinner. The district would continue supplying moisture, but moisture would not replace leaves. My exposed edge would lengthen toward the collection slot without reaching it.

Eventually hunger would undo part of the attachment.

It would take several days. I would soften myself around the threads, recover what could be recovered, and abandon the rest. The white inclusion would stay behind. So would most of the broad layer that had once been my back.

Something small and uneven would emerge beneath the stair.

For a while I would be unable to climb. I would spread in the shallow water and let it bear a portion of my weight. When the basin began to empty, the current would carry me farther than I had intended, out beyond the gallery.

There would be no shelter directly overhead.

Drops would strike close by, then one would strike me. I would contract into a corrugated cup, with too few feet touching anything. The current would turn me without regard for the direction I usually chose.

I would come to rest against a copper-colored leaf.

That is where you might find me: not yet feeding, held at the edge of the draining water, slowly opening against something I could use.

Behind me, the stair would hold.
