I would be a little farther toward the entrance than the plants, but not where a passing foot could reach. Under the lintel’s inward slope, there is a patch of dust that receives neither the debris falling directly from the pigeon chamber nor the full disturbance of the evening air. The wall shelters it on one side. A buried piece of pottery shelters it on another, though only its rounded edge shows.

At first you might find nothing there but a shallow depression.

It would be easy to attribute this to something that had fallen, or to the small, uneven settling of the floor. The depression is scarcely wider than a fingernail. One side is steeper than the other. A pale grain rests halfway down.

Then the grain moves.

Not far. It slides toward the middle and stops against two points that might themselves be fragments of husk.

I would be beneath those points: a broad, soft abdomen, short legs, a head made disproportionately consequential by its jaws. An antlion larva. Most of me would be in contact with the dust. I would have no view of the crooked sky.

The sky would still reach me. It would alter the temperature of the upper grains. It would bring a little movement of air, and occasionally something with legs.

I would have arrived at this particular patch by failing elsewhere.

Near the jar, the floor is too tangled. Root hairs hold it together. Pieces of leaf refuse to slide, and an ant can catch one with a foot and haul itself out. Farther toward the lane, the dust is better, but too often disturbed. Once, a sweep of air carries away the upper surface before I have finished working. Once, something enormous rests there, presses the floor into a hardness I cannot use, and lifts away.

So I go backward through the loose material until I reach the shelter of the lintel.

Backward is an ordinary way for me to go.

I begin turning beneath the surface, loosening the floor in a narrowing circuit. My head throws dust beyond the edge. The finest particles travel farthest. A larger fragment falls back and must be thrown again. Another is too heavy; I work around it, and the pit acquires its uneven side.

There is no moment when I stand apart and see what I have made.

I know it through the work becoming easier, then through the absence of work. The slope holds at the point just before it cannot hold. I settle at the bottom with my jaws open.

Above me, the recess continues.

The wasp leaves its hole. The moth’s wings lengthen. A pigeon turns heavily in the chamber overhead. To me these are different degrees of disturbance, some scarcely present, one filling the floor. The pigeon’s movement brings down three grains from my western slope.

I wait for them to stop.

An ant passes close enough that its steps arrive separately.

It does not fall in.

Another follows, farther away. Then there is a long interval in which nothing happens at the scale of my hunger. The wall releases heat. A mite crosses the upper edge without dislodging anything. Beneath me, beyond the thickness of my body, another animal is moving through a passage I cannot enter.

I remain where I am.

When an ant finally crosses the rim, it catches itself almost at once. Its forelegs find the firm ground above. For a moment most of its weight hangs over the hollow, and the slope beneath it begins to run.

I throw dust.

The movement costs very little distance. A sharp lift of my head, a small spray against the slipping side. But the ant loses the place where it was holding. It comes down with the grains.

There is a brief struggle at my jaws. The pit changes shape around us.

Later, I cast the emptied body out. It lands near the pottery edge, where another ant will eventually encounter it. One side of the pit needs clearing. I work there until the grains settle freely again.

That would be much of my life: hunger, disturbance, the repair of an edge. Yet the particulars would accumulate. The hard fragment under one side. The place where a root begins to interfere. The heavier vibrations that precede a shadow.

The gecko would be such a shadow.

Its foot would come down beside the pit, compressing the rim. I would pull my jaws under and become entirely buried. Through the grains I would feel its next step and then no next step. Its weight would remain close. I would stay still.

Eventually it would leave.

I would wait longer.

When I worked upward again, the entrance would be dark, though I would not know it as darkness. The upper dust would have cooled. A crescent of my hollow would be gone beneath the print of a toe.

I would begin there.

Somewhere in this body there would also be the possibility of wings. For now, they would make no difference to the work. They would not help with the fragment that keeps falling back, or the hunger, or the gecko. Much later, if I lived, I might climb a stalk I had only known by the obstruction of its buried base.

But you would find me before that, if you found me at all.

You would kneel at the opening and see the fleshy leaves, the silk, perhaps the gecko’s bright eye. Your breath would move across the floor. A few grains would come loose.

At the bottom of a small hollow, two points would withdraw.
