I would be where the dark soil thins, a little inside the oldest ring.

There is a place there where a root once passed over a rib of stone. The root is gone, but its underside remains impressed in the packed litter: a long, shallow ceiling, polished by things that have used the passage since. At one end, leaves hang down to the ground. At the other, a small triangle of sky is visible.

I think at first that I would occupy this passage. Something with a back, resting beneath it.

But the back would be only the part exposed to the opening. Farther in, I would spread below the floor, around two living roots and into the colder pocket under the stone. The passage would run partly through me. A grazer could enter at one end and leave at the other without crossing a clean boundary between where I was and where I was not.

My nearest opening would be just beyond the hanging leaves. It would look unused.

I would keep it that way.

***

The sky would come to me chiefly in what it left behind.

A fall of crystals would arrive as a succession of small differences against the combs inside my opening. Some grains would catch. Some would be passed outward. Others would rest against a membrane until their edges had changed enough to be admitted.

There would be no single sensation of the weather arriving. My outer folds would already be sorting its fall while, deeper under the roots, I was still making the slow adjustments of an ordinary evening.

Then cold would reach a buried plate.

Then a root would tighten.

Then, in a chamber well away from either, a filament would change what it released against the remains of a leaf.

By the time the whole of me was affected, the white bank might have passed.

I would have no need to know this. There would still be work arriving from it.

***

One of my feeding chambers would contain a leaf too large to turn.

I would have taken it in when the surrounding tissue was more spacious. Since then, a root would have thickened along one side, narrowing the chamber. The leaf would remain caught diagonally, one end softened, the other preserving its silver underside.

For a long time I would feed around this inconvenience.

Filaments would be brought against the resistant end. A fold would press, relax, press again. I would remove what yielded and leave the rest. Elsewhere, flowers would enter and be reduced to their pale hooks. A grazer’s abandoned skin would be divided among several chambers. Entire small meals would pass through me while this one leaf remained.

Eventually it would separate.

Nothing elsewhere in my body would stop for that.

But the fold which had held itself bent around the leaf would extend into the newly available space. Fluid would enter it. A neighboring chamber would deepen.

For several nights I would use that part of myself more than the others.

***

I would carry waste to an opening beneath a split stone.

It would not be the nearest opening. To reach it, the remains would have to move around one of my dense knots and along a narrow fold where grains sometimes lodged. A shorter route would lie downhill, through soft litter.

Still, I would carry them beneath the split stone.

There would be an old reason for this. Perhaps the downhill opening had once admitted too much heat. Perhaps a predator had occupied the leaves above it for several seasons. The leaves would have fallen long ago. The predator would be gone. The tissues injured there would have been replaced.

The longer route would remain.

Outside it, the empty frameworks of leaves would accumulate in a sheltered fan. Fine roots would descend through them. As those roots grew, they would make the route harder, and I would spend a little more of each evening maintaining it.

This would be one way to recognize me.

***

I would be smaller than I had been.

Beyond the rib of stone, under a crowded stand of violet foliage, there would be another body using passages I had made.

Once, cold entering there had drawn material from chambers here. Once, a sustained pressure on this side had caused folds on that side to grip. The cord between us had passed beneath a root that slowly settled as the soil below it was consumed.

For years the passage had narrowed.

There had been time to withdraw. Much of me had done so. But a knot remained beyond the root, with food around it and several sheltered openings. The narrowing cord continued to serve it until one season it did not.

Now a disturbance beneath the violet leaves would reach me through the ground.

It would have the slight delay of something outside.

At our nearest margins we would sometimes touch. Each would close against the other. Neither would open the old passage.

I would still send waste toward the split stone. On the other side, pale leaf remains would begin appearing beneath a different opening.

***

Late in a poor season, something would anchor itself beside my clean aperture.

At first I would respond to its weight. The upper folds would withdraw. A chamber would close around unfinished food.

The weight would remain through the bright part of the day.

At evening, a thin edge would emerge beneath it and press into the litter. Its contact would carry the substances of old soil, but faintly, beneath other traces: exposed rock, thickened sealing fluid, chambers kept closed too long.

A traveler.

It would be smaller than one of my buried plates.

There would be little to eat where it had stopped. I would already have cleared most of the loose fragments from that edge. Its fold would search along the underside of a dead ribbon, find only the mineral framework, and draw back.

I would not bring it food.

But its pressure would keep one of my feeding margins withdrawn. A spent flower, caught behind that margin, would remain where I would otherwise have enclosed it.

During the next cold interval, the traveler would find it.

***

For a while it would disappear every morning.

Its plates would close so closely that only its position distinguished it from the stone. I would extend beneath it and retreat when its underside opened again. Between us, a narrow seam of litter would remain unsettled.

Then one evening it would fail to close completely.

A little pale tissue would protrude beyond its armor, held under the edge of a fallen leaf. By morning it would still be there.

The following night, farther in, another fold would open.

I would yield a small amount of ground. There would be pressure enough elsewhere to occupy me: a loosening root, food accumulating under a crown, a chamber whose filaments had begun to grow poorly.

Above the traveler, the hanging leaves would turn their silver surfaces outward. Cold would enter our separate openings.

The first stone would click.

I would close near the sound. The traveler would close everywhere.

For some time afterward, while my feeding folds reopened one by one, its plates would remain drawn tight. Then a seam would widen beneath the fallen leaf, and a small piece of the flower would move inward.
