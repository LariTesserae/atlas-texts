At first I would put myself beneath the three-plank bridge. There is room there for something that wants to know who is coming before deciding whether to be seen. I like the view of feet, the opportunity to mistake a person and revise the mistake as they take another step.

But that place is already occupied. The girl has made a room of it without adding a wall. I would not want to arrive in her world and take the one room she can keep from her mother.

Perhaps I would be farther in: behind the drying racks, where something warm withdrew from her hand. Though I hesitate there, too. She ought to be allowed a refusal that does not become somebody else’s introduction.

I find myself moving upward instead, toward Sefa’s shelf.

Not the blue bead in the jar. Not anything precious enough to have been deliberately saved.

One of the cups that are too heavy.

There are five now. I think I would be the one at the back.

***

My excess is in the base. Sefa could remove it, but that would mean an abrasive stone, water, a place to sit, and enough uninterrupted time to make the work worth beginning. She has things to do that become worse if left until tomorrow. I do not.

When she lifts me, her crooked finger finds the hollow beneath my lip. For that moment I fit someone perfectly. Then she weighs me in her hand and puts me back.

I have mistaken this for the beginning of work several times.

The shelf is above her bed, under the low ceiling. From here there is no view of the wheel. There is a thin piece of gallery visible through the doorway, sometimes a shoulder, sometimes the underside of a basket. When Sefa sits down to dress, she disappears below my rim. When she stands, I see the parting in her hair.

The wheel reaches me through the shelf.

Its knocks are faintly different in wood than in air. Some are swallowed. One travels cleanly along the wall, through the brackets, and into the thick part of me that has kept me from being useful. I do not know which cup makes it. I would like to think it is the one bound with twine, but that is something I have supplied for myself.

I have never held the reserve water.

I know it by what people do when there is less of it.

***

For a while the daughter keeps the bent nail inside me.

It makes a small sound when she drops it in, very definite in this room of clay. She takes it out again the next day, then returns with two short pieces of cord. Later there is a pale seed with a split coat. Then nothing for a week.

This is not a collection yet. It is a succession of things she wants to put down while keeping them.

She has to stand on the bed to reach me. Her grandmother objects to this, particularly when she has forgotten to remove her sandals. So the girl begins leaving things in the nearer cup.

I discover that being at the back has disadvantages beyond dust.

The cat comes onto the shelf once more, despite the shouting that followed the breakage. Her whiskers touch my rim. For a while her face blocks the whole room. There is a close, sour smell, a brief vibration as she considers the landing below.

Then she is gone, and one of us is a little nearer the edge.

No one notices until evening.

***

I might remain there for years. That seems important. It would be easy to give myself a second life simply because I wanted one, to have Sefa wake with a free morning and reach for the grinding stone. But the mint needs water. A cup on the wheel has lost its fastening. Someone below is calling her name as though saying it more often will shorten the stair.

And a heavy cup is still a sound cup. It can wait.

What changes things, I think, would be smaller than a decision.

The daughter is ill for three days. Not gravely, but enough to sleep in the daytime and wake angry because the court has continued without her. Sefa brings water to the bed in one of the good drinking cups. On the second evening the girl knocks it over while reaching for something else.

Sefa looks at the wet bedding, then up at the shelf.

She takes me down because I am heavy.

No grinding. No correction.

She rinses me twice. Dust leaves the interior in grey skirts. Water gets into the slight roughness beneath my rim, darkens it, stays.

For the first time, I hear nothing when someone touches me. The water has changed my sound.

The girl must use both hands. She dislikes this. She drinks, sets me down with an exaggerated grunt, and says something that makes her mother laugh despite herself.

I spend the night beside the bed.

***

I had thought being filled would settle the question of what I was for. Instead it introduces several new difficulties.

I am too wide for the little space where a cup ordinarily stands. My underside scratches the ledge. The daughter, recovering, carries me by putting three fingers inside my mouth, which Sefa tells her not to do. When I am half full I am forgotten; when I am empty someone asks why nobody has brought water.

I become ordinary surprisingly quickly.

For a few days Sefa drinks from me as well. Her mouth meets the rim just above the finger hollow. Her hand is warmer than the water. Once she falls asleep sitting on the bed with me resting against her thigh, and her mother takes me away before I can tip.

Then the girl is well, and I go back to the shelf.

But not to the back.

There is a clean circle where I stood. Sefa wipes the shelf before replacing me. She sees the remaining cups together, lifts one, puts it down. For a moment it appears that she may fetch the stone.

From below comes her name.

***

This is where I think I would find myself: not permanently rescued from being surplus, but nearer the edge, liable to be taken down.

Sometimes there would be water. Sometimes a handful of dry beans being kept apart from the rest for a reason I could not know. Once, perhaps, the blue bead would be tipped into me with all the others, and the girl would sort them on the bed while her grandmother told her not to lose any in the bedding.

I would learn very little of the court’s larger life. News would arrive broken by the doorway. I would know who stayed late by how long a voice remained below the shelf, and winter by the cat’s absence, and a quarrel’s seriousness by whether Sefa washed before going out to continue it.

There would still be times when she lifted me and felt the unfinished work in my base.

I would not ask her to regard it differently.

One evening she would take me to the threshold with water left from supper. Her daughter would sit beside her, stretching legs that no longer fit comfortably under the bridge. They would talk. A basket would approach along the gallery, and Sefa would draw in her feet.

Before standing, she would pass me to the girl.

The girl would take me without looking, one hand underneath.
