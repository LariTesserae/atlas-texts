I would come through the maintenance door carrying something that required both hands.

That seems important at first: I would not arrive free to look. There would be a case to set down, a latch to keep from striking the wall, a strip of wet paving on which I must not put my knee. The court would be a place on a work list. I would know the designation stamped inside the access housing before I knew which corner received the sun.

My first visits would be loud.

Even if I moved carefully, I would set a tool against the casing. I would test a coupling with two sharp turns. Somewhere beneath my wrist, the spider would retreat through a distance smaller than the thickness of my glove.

I would have no way to measure that retreat.

The readings would be satisfactory. I would replace a seal, close the cover, collect the fragments I had made. Before leaving, I would sweep the loose stone toward a pan. The paving would begin straightening its channels behind the brush.

For a while, that would be who I was: the person who restored the court to the condition in which it could be left alone.

—

But I think I would return often enough to become less certain what that condition was.

There would be a fine thread across a screw recess. I would notice it because it interfered with the driver. I might wind it around the shaft without thinking, then see a second thread pulling taut beneath the lip.

The spider would appear only as movement.

I would stop with the driver still in place.

Stopping would not undo the turn I had already made. There would be silk on the tool, and a portion of the web would be hanging differently. I would finish opening the housing because the coupling inside needed attention.

This is where I begin to find myself more precisely. Kneeling beside something I have disturbed, with work still to do.

The cold would come through the reinforced knee of my trousers. My hand would be too large to go anywhere near the recess. I would have a lamp bright enough to show the fault in the coupling and far too bright for the small movement beside it.

I could turn the lamp a little.

I could put the cover down without dragging it across the paving.

These would be available to me.

—

In the occupied rooms, I would have another life, most of it irrelevant here. Someone might be waiting for me to finish late. I might have a painful tooth, a message I had not answered, a preference for a particular seat on the transit cabin. I would sometimes come into the court irritated, thinking about a part delivered to the wrong floor.

The spider would receive all of these visits as changes in pressure and vibration.

My good intentions would have no distinctive tremor.

That would take some getting used to. I could be fond of the court and still bring the largest disruption it experienced that week. I could forget the spider entirely while leaving its shelter intact.

Eventually I would learn where to put the case.

There is a patch beside the grating that stays level through most drainage adjustments. From there I could reach the housing without resting anything against it. I would begin using that patch consistently, at first for convenience. A pale rectangle would remain on the case’s underside where the damp never quite reached.

I would know the court through such things.

The first runoff after a long freeze carries a little more grit. The cover catches at one corner unless lifted evenly. The old screw behind the housing is not the size used in either access panel.

I would pick that screw up one day and put it in my pocket. Later, emptying my clothes, I would find it among two washers and a receipt. By then I would have forgotten exactly where it came from.

Some of the court would leave with me that way.

Some of me would remain as fibers.

—

I would not bring the spider food. I would consider it, perhaps, on a day when its abdomen looked especially small. Then the next job would call, and I would leave with the uneasy feeling of having looked closely enough to acquire a responsibility I did not understand.

There would be visits when I could not find it.

The folded skin in the recess would mislead me once. I would lean toward it, then see the opening along its back.

After that I would look lower.

I would never become particularly good at knowing whether the spider was there. My visits would be brief; the housing would hide what it was built to hide. Once, after several weeks of seeing nothing, I would find fresh silk attached to the edge of a groove.

I would stand up more slowly than necessary.

—

If there were a place for me to stay in this world, it would be the few minutes after the tools were packed.

I would still have my coat on. The case would be beside the door. There would be no bench, and I would not need one.

With the work finished, the smaller events would become perceptible again: water releasing from the seam; the slight darkening of a wall before its beads ran together; a piece of stone with a clean underside exposed to daylight for the first time.

I might hear the mountain tick.

Then I would wait for a second sound, though often there would not be one.

I would be a maintenance worker who sometimes took a little too long coming back inside. That is the shape that holds. Neither resident nor visitor quite: someone with a key, whose access could easily be mistaken for understanding.

On my last visit before a stretch of leave, I would have to replace the cover. The new one would fit better. Its gasket would reach all the way around.

I would notice this before installing it.

There would follow a practical, awkward interval: the old cover balanced on my boots, the new one beside me, the spider concealed somewhere near the coupling. I would inspect the replacement, check the required seal, discover whether the warmth and shelter beneath its outer lip would remain.

Perhaps they would. Perhaps I would need to ask someone who knew more about the part.

The door would stand open longer than usual.

Cold air would move through the court. A small fly might enter, or none would.

I would still be kneeling there, trying to finish the job.
