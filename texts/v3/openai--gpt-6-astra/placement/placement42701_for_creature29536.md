I think I would live below somebody else.

There would be a short stair down from one of the courts, with an awkward last step because the door had been fitted where the rock allowed it. In the morning, light would reach the upper half of the door. To see something small clearly—a split beside a peg, the point of a needle—I would have to carry it outside.

The room would once have been a storage vault. It would still be called the lower store by several people, including the woman who rented it to me. She would keep two jars there under an arrangement we had never quite finished discussing. Occasionally she would come down for one while I was eating.

I would mind this more on some days than on others.

At first I can place only a few possessions in the room: a bed raised well off the floor, a covered pot, a board across two supports. There is a little timber under the bed. The longer pieces have to lie diagonally, and when I want one I must pull out nearly everything.

Wood would be what I worked with. Not the great roof beams, except as another pair of shoulders when one needed shifting. Smaller things: handles, shutters, the crosspieces of carrying frames, the part of a windlass that a hand closes around. I would know how to make a peg hold without splitting the piece it entered. I would sometimes get it wrong.

A good length of wood would occupy me for a while before I cut it. I would turn it over, wet a patch with my thumb, move a knot from one imagined object to another. Someone waiting for a handle would ask whether I intended to repair the shovel or marry it.

I would have heard this before. It would still hurry me a little, and I would resent that.

My work would come into the court in pieces. A shutter with its lower pivot worn away. A stool carried upside down. A bowl that could perhaps be held together with a clamp, provided its owner stopped setting it close to the oven. Often the owner would remain while I examined the damage, explaining what had happened in a way that left out the decisive event.

“Did somebody stand on it?”

“It was sound yesterday.”

“Did somebody stand on it yesterday?”

There would be three or four houses where I could borrow a tool without asking, and one where I had to ask even for my own.

I would not have been born here. For my first winter, people would have given directions past my door rather than to it. Later, someone coming through the gate with a broken pack frame would be told to take it to me. That change might occur without my hearing it.

The woman above would know when it had occurred. She would mention it during an argument over the rent.

*

There is an older man who taught me most of the work I do. He lives on the street below the oven, in a room warmer than mine and much more difficult to move about in. He has kept every iron fitting he has ever removed from anything. He can usually remember which heap contains the one he wants.

His sight is poor at close distances now. He holds a piece out into the light, then gives it to me with some other reason for doing so.

I still take difficult jobs to him. He runs a fingernail along a crack and asks questions I should have asked myself. Sometimes, after I have understood, he continues explaining. I stand there with the object in my hands, impatient to leave and ashamed of being impatient.

He also sends work back that I consider finished.

“Feel that.”

“It won’t catch.”

“Feel it with your wrist.”

There is a rough place my fingers have missed. I smooth it. For the rest of the day I am more careful than usual, which irritates me almost as much as the correction.

Once a week I carry his shavings to the oven. There are fewer now. Mine go in a separate sack because he says I leave too much good wood among them.

He is right about this often enough to be troublesome.

I would have a foolish possession.

Perhaps a chair bought below and brought up at considerable inconvenience, with a curved back and thin turned legs. Not an especially fine chair. Something made in quantities in a place where there was more timber, by a person who could afford to remove most of a piece in order to arrive at a shape.

It would be poorly suited to the room. Its feet would find four different levels on the paving. I would have made little shoes for two of them, then remade one. People would suggest that I shorten the legs.

I would refuse.

When asked why, I would say the joints were good. This would be true but insufficient.

I would like the chair. I would like the unnecessary swell halfway down each front leg. I would like sitting with my back against something that had been curved for that purpose. On evenings when work had gone badly, I might eat in it with the bowl held in my lap, leaving the sensible stool empty beside the board.

The woman upstairs would call it my grand chair. She would sit in it whenever she came down.

I would know the girl at the grinding stone because she would have discovered that work at my door did not always prevent conversation.

At first she would ask about the town where I had bought the chair. Then about the road to it. Then about people there.

Could someone arrive without relatives?

What did a room cost?

Was there work for a person who had never done that work before?

I would give bad answers to some of this. Prices I remembered would be old. A household that had once taken lodgers might no longer exist. She would press me for names, and I would have to distinguish what I knew from what I had merely assumed had continued in my absence.

One afternoon she would ask why I had left.

I might be fitting a new grip to a blade. I would keep testing it against the tang although it already fitted.

Eventually I would tell her that I had been lonely there.

She would look around the court, where every open door belonged to someone who knew her business.

“I wouldn’t be,” she would say.

I would want to argue. Instead I would tell her the name of the woman who had rented me my room, and admit that I did not know whether she was still alive.

After the girl went away, I would think of several better things to have said. By then she would have carried the useful part home.

*

In the town, I would not be indispensable. If I went down the mountain for a month, some repairs would wait and others would be done by people who knew enough. A few would be brought to the older man, who would complain about my absence with unmistakable pleasure.

But someone would open my shutter. The woman upstairs would check the dark joint behind my bed after rain. A child might put a damaged toy through the opening and expect it to have mended itself by my return.

I would come back tired, carrying more than I had meant to buy.

At the turn below the court, I would stop to shift the strap away from a sore place on my shoulder. There would be the scrape of the grinding stone, then a pause. Someone above would be disputing the whereabouts of a jar.

The woman in the doorway would hear me on the stair.

“You’ve been a long time,” she would say before I came into view.

I would have an account ready: the path, an animal, a delay at the lower market. But first I would lower the load onto the coping, carefully, with both hands.

She would let me finish doing that.

Then she would ask what I had brought.
