I would first mistake my place for somewhere I could stand.

There is a narrow recess beside the pumping-house door, on the hinge side. It is too shallow to shelter a person, but standing there would leave the gray animal its crescent of clean paving. I could look down without putting my shoe in the place its appendages almost reach.

The door would be cold against my shoulder. Through it, I would feel a succession of impacts that I could not distinguish in the air. Something inside strikes something else, or accepts its weight. The intervals are short enough to feel continuous until I stop trying to count them.

I would stay because the animal is looking.

For a while I would believe this was the only thing keeping me there.

Then I would notice that the recess does not extend all the way to the ground. A small iron housing occupies its lower part. There is no dial on it, only a horizontal opening beneath a projecting lip. Dust lies on the lip. The opening is clean.

Something pale is visible inside.

At first it looks like cloth pushed into the gap to prevent a rattle. It is folded, and the folds are compressed unequally. One has a pink interior. Another ends in a narrow, translucent rim that trembles without touching the iron.

I would be looking at the part of myself I cannot feel.

—

My body would lie inside the thickness of the door frame.

Not upright. I would have a difficulty with uprightness that takes some time to discover: what I think of as my chest is turned toward the yard, but the greater part of me extends sideways, beneath the threshold. My weight rests on several small, widely separated surfaces. Between these rests, I am soft.

The housing covers only my forward end. Farther in, I have a long flank pressed against a pipe and a belly suspended over a shallow channel. The channel is dry. Its bottom is close enough that a fringe beneath me brushes the grit when the pressure increases.

I have feet, though not all of them stand on anything. Two grip opposite sides of a bolt. One is folded into a pocket of my own skin. A fourth rests against the door’s lower hinge pin. That foot has a thick pad, and through the pad I receive an immense amount of information for which I have no names.

The door is not trying to open. Nevertheless, something is always arriving at the hinge that must be held there.

I help hold it.

That would be the first duty I could establish. I would not know whether it accounts for my whole body.

There is a place along my underside where cool fluid enters. I cannot turn far enough to see it. Feeding feels like a small area of warmth, although the fluid itself is cold: a loosening that passes a little way through me and stops. Farther back, other mouths remain hungry. They open against the dry channel.

I would find this unfair before I understood how little fairness had to do with the arrangement.

The hunger does not grow. It is quite sufficient as it is.

—

My eye is behind the horizontal opening.

I have only one that sees the yard. It is not centered in my face, and the projecting lip cuts off most of the sky. I can see the lowest step, a length of handrail, the fountain’s base. People, if there were people, would arrive as shoes and remain incomplete.

The gray animal beside the door is almost entirely within my view.

From this low angle its shell is less like iron. The edge is thin in one place, and daylight comes through it brown. When the body lifts, I can see a soft crease behind the nearest leg. There are three grains of grit in that crease. They move apart when the leg extends and gather when it folds.

I would recognize it by those grains before I learned to recognize its face.

Its appendages sweep the dusty depression on its back. I can follow the farther one only by its shadow. The nearer one appears above the shell, bends, and returns. At the farthest point of its passage, its tip divides.

There is something I want to do with my own forward limb.

This desire would precede my knowledge that I have such a limb.

It lies folded beside my eye: a slender, moist part, held in a groove under the housing. I can straighten it into the yard. Its underside has tiny pads. Its end is broad and lacks a claw.

Extended fully, it comes close to the gray animal’s shell.

Not close enough to clean the dust from the place its appendages miss. Not close enough to touch its head when the head is out.

Close enough that the air displaced by its movement passes over me.

I would extend the limb again.

There would be no surprise in doing so. The groove is polished where I withdraw it. Along its upper surface, where it rubs the housing, I have a hard stripe. This is something I have done for a very long time.

Still, each extension requires effort. A little muscle draws tight behind my eye. My foot on the hinge pin presses harder. For the duration of the reach, the fringe beneath my belly lifts clear of the channel.

I cannot reach farther without letting go somewhere else.

I do not know what would happen if I let go.

—

I would not be the district’s keeper, or its witness in any comprehensive sense. Much of it would be hidden from me. I would know the fountain as a low white interruption beyond the paving and the machine sheds as changes in the pressure against my flank. The multitude behind their windows would be no more available to me than I am to them.

My knowledge would be exact in a few places.

The hinge pad.

The dry grit.

The brown translucence at the edge of the neighboring shell.

When the gray animal turns its head, its nearer eye passes behind a blunt projection of its own body. There is an interval in which I cannot see it looking at me. I would continue to hold my limb out through that interval.

Then the eye returns.

I cannot tell whether the animal has seen my limb. Its head is angled toward the opening, but perhaps it is attending to the pressure coming through the frame. Perhaps the same disturbance that makes me reach makes it lift itself.

Under its shell, one pale foot tightens.

Inside the housing, mine does too.

There is a small space between the end of my limb and its nearest appendage. The sound passes through that space without difficulty. So does the ordinary light.

I would live there, behind the iron lip, exerting myself against the hinge and holding something tender out into the yard.

From the stairs, neither the limb nor the space would be visible.
