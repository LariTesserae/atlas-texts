I would have a room where the guide cable enters the wall.

The cable would pass through a ceramic sleeve beside my bed. In heavy weather it would complain there, a small uneven creaking as the exposed length took the weight of wind, water, or someone coming home. I would learn to sleep through the weather. A traveler would wake me.

Before I knew anything else about them, I would know whether they had stopped outside to gather their strength.

There would be a bowl within reach of the bed, and beside it a covered vessel of drinking water. I would have put them there for myself, originally. Eventually the bowl would mostly be used by people who had arrived too tired to remember that thirst was one of the things wrong with them.

I think I would like this arrangement more than I admitted.

I would complain about the interrupted sleep.

***

My work would begin just outside the room.

The household mark on the nearest doorpost has become shallow at its lower edge. Water runs down the post, but that is not the principal cause. Someone in this house has been returning with a burden on the same shoulder for thirty years, reaching for home at the same angle, rubbing a particular hollow smooth.

I know who it is. She delivers replacement valve seats. Lately she has been carrying fewer.

I press a soft compound into the mark and lift out its shape. Back at my bench, I compare the impression with a strip in the household’s maintenance bundle. There is a discrepancy. The mark people use is no longer quite the mark in the record.

This happens often.

I could restore it exactly. The woman carrying valve seats would come home, touch her door, and hesitate.

Instead I deepen the surviving parts a little at a time. For several evenings I sit nearby while people return. I ask them to try it with a wet hand, a cold hand, with a sleeping child folded against the chest. One of them tells me I have made the mark feel officious.

I take a little off.

This is most of my work: doorposts, stair edges, the ribs on drain covers, the branching surfaces where one passage becomes three. I carry small files and abrasive cords in a roll against my belly. There is a pot of coarse ceramic grit that everyone knows not to borrow for kitchen use, because someone did once, and the story is still told with my name in it.

I am not old enough to have been given this work simply because climbing has become difficult. I asked to learn it.

At the time, my family thought I was avoiding more consequential employment.

Sometimes I think so too. Then I find a waste-channel cover worn almost smooth where children turn during a running game.

I kneel down and unpack my tools.

***

My teacher still inspects things I have finished.

She comes along behind me at irregular intervals, leaning on a short staff whose ferrule she changes to suit the floor. I can identify three of her ferrules from the other end of a gallery. She owns a fourth that she uses when she intends to surprise me.

She says I trust my fingertips too much.

“You have been working here all morning,” she tells me. “Your hand already knows what it expects.”

She makes me go away, eat, quarrel with somebody if necessary, and return from the other direction.

Sometimes she has altered my work while I was gone. Sometimes she has not. She will not tell me which.

Her own hands shake when she holds them out. Against stone they become steady. On bad evenings I clean the deep seams in her feet while she dictates complaints about the station administration. There is a seam below her left heel that requires particular care. She falls silent while I work there, then resumes at precisely the word she left.

She has refused several offers to move into a room nearer the kitchens.

I understand the practical objections to her staying where she is. I have made most of them myself.

Nevertheless, when I repair the route to her house, I work slowly enough that its changes can be learned one at a time.

***

Under my bench lives an animal belonging, by general agreement, to someone else.

Its proper household is two galleries inward. They feed it better than I do. They also have children who enjoy finding out how many of its joints can be stroked at once.

It comes to me for uninterrupted sleep.

At first I attempt to send it home each evening. Later I make a recess beneath the bench so that its tail will not lie across the passage. Later still I learn to recognize the throat-scrape it makes when dreaming and the almost identical one that means it has got its head into a vessel it cannot leave.

It is not especially useful. It eats the compound I use for taking impressions, and once carries an entire household mark into the drain.

For some time afterward, when that family passes my room, they ask whether their door has come back.

I become attached to the creature partly because it is difficult to tell when it has decided something. I reach down expecting its back and find its underside offered instead. I prepare a harness and discover that it has quietly placed the bench between us.

There are evenings when I put my feet in warm brine, rest a hand on its plates, and do no work at all.

These are good evenings. I am inclined to spoil them by thinking of something that needs repairing.

***

The basin keeper begins visiting because she wants a handle changed.

Her sounding apparatus has two grips. One releases the line; the other locks it. They are distinct when clean. After hours in mineral-heavy water, wearing gloves stiffened by deposits, she has twice mistaken one for the other.

I make trial grips out of cheap material. She rejects the first four.

On the fifth visit she brings food.

After that, our work takes longer than the handle requires.

She has a habit of leaving intervals in conversation that I mistake for its end. I begin another subject, and she answers the earlier one. It annoys me until I accompany her to the basin and spend an afternoon waiting beside an instrument that gives no sign of having done anything.

Then the line moves beneath her hand.

She lifts one finger to my wrist.

We wait much longer.

I cannot tell whether what follows belongs to the first movement. She cannot either, she says afterward. That is why we waited.

Her shelter is on a low shelf above the water. The approach has been marked by knots in a cord, all swollen nearly to the same size. I offer to replace them.

She accepts too quickly. She has been finding the shelter by the smell of a leaking supply tin, and the tin is almost empty.

This worries me more than the sounding line.

I begin making visits with a tool roll.

***

There would be a lift while I was there.

We would have enough warning to climb from the shelf to the ridge. I would be carrying a case of records, absurdly heavy, having taken it without asking whether it was the one worth saving. She would be carrying her ordinary pack.

On the way up she would put my hand on the fastening of the case.

“Copies,” she would say.

I would carry it anyway. By then I would have found a satisfactory way to hold it.

A herd would already occupy the ridge. We would work ourselves into a space between warm bodies, seeking firm ground with our feet. Something young would keep pushing its blunt mouth under my arm. The keeper would loosen the cloth beneath her jaw.

I would leave mine bound.

Then the waterfalls would stop.

I have tried to imagine what I would become in that quiet: more perceptive, perhaps, able to receive some immense fact that ordinary work had kept from me.

I think I would mostly be frightened.

Without the falling water I would lose distances I had not known I depended on. The ridge would seem insufficient beneath us. I would put the case down and crouch, keeping one hand against an animal’s flank until the keeper found my wrist.

She would bring my other hand to the ground.

The basin’s call would arrive there.

I would feel it unevenly: strongly at the base of the thumb, less clearly through the fingers. I would shift my palm to improve the contact. Mud would enter the cuff of my sleeve. Somewhere nearby, a person would be breathing through an obstructed nose.

The call would continue.

I would want to ask whether this was the usual one. Whether it was closer. Whether we were standing in a sensible place.

The keeper would keep her hand beside mine.

Afterward, she would ask what I had felt, and I would have difficulty separating the voice from the trembling of my own arm. She would record that difficulty as carefully as anything else.

***

I would return to my room.

There would be silt in the ceramic sleeve, making the cable catch. My bed would have shifted against the wall. The animal would be waiting beneath it, offended by conditions for which it held me responsible.

In the lower gallery, three drain covers would need replacing. A stair would have settled enough to change the height of its last step. People coming down it would plant a foot too soon and jar their knees.

There would be plenty to do.

But I would begin taking longer over the questions people asked while I worked. Sometimes I would finish a repair, pack away the tools, and remain sitting there while someone found the rest of what they meant to say.

The basin keeper would visit when her duties allowed. She would put her pack in the same place each time. Eventually I would clear that place properly, relocating things over several days so my own hands could learn where they had gone.

I would fit a second cup into a shallow recess beside the bed.

Outside, a returning worker would stop with their weight on the guide cable. The sleeve would creak once, then be still.

I would get up and open the door.
