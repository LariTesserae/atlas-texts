At first I think I would be in one of the smaller rooms behind an enclosure, where the light has passed through so many surfaces that it no longer casts a distinct shadow.

There would be a narrow opening into the front room. From where I rested, I could see a section of shelf, the opposite upright, and whatever crossed between them. Not enough to know who had arrived until they sat down.

I would know the weight first.

The shelf and the floor and the surface beneath my back would carry it differently. A familiar body would settle through several small adjustments, each answered somewhere in the room. A visitor’s weight would remain more concentrated. There would be a heel, a hip, an elbow: separate pressures that the enclosure had not quite received.

I would feel these without yet knowing what to do with them.

For a while, I imagine I am part of the room. Some thickened tissue behind the wall, sensitive to the front chamber in the way the underside of a leaf is sensitive to light it never directly sees.

But there are hands.

One lies beside my head. The other has been resting through the opening, its palm turned upward on the shelf. When someone sits too close to it, I draw it back. The movement takes time, and is mine.

I have a body, then, though it is easier to discover at its extremities. My feet are folded beneath me. There is a seam along one ankle that does not close as completely as the others. The skin there is dull, almost gray. When I stand, it catches against the place where I have been resting.

I have to free it with my fingers.

This is an ordinary inconvenience. No one gathers to watch. Sometimes another inhabitant, passing through the front room, pauses with a hand on the upright until I have finished. Sometimes they go on.

The rear chamber fits me badly in one respect and very well in all the others.

I do not know whether it once fitted better.

***

I would come out.

Not immediately toward the high ridge or the root passage. I think my usual distance would be modest: from the enclosure to a dry basin, then along the underside of a lobe suspended above the ground. There is a low interval there where the ceiling’s brightness is cut off. The floor has no blades. Its surface is finely ridged, like the inside of a closed hand.

A few lengths of my body farther on, the interval narrows too much for my shoulders.

I often put an arm into it.

There is cooler tissue beyond reach of my face. Small movements arrive through it from elsewhere in the hollow. I cannot tell how far they have traveled. Nor could I say, on feeling one, that someone has stood up beside a particular pool. What comes through is less distinct than that. I keep my wrist against the surface until the pressure has passed.

Once, something touches my fingertips from the other side.

It is gone before I make an answering movement.

Afterward, I return there with no greater frequency than before. But my hand goes a little farther into the narrowing.

***

The first thing I keep is a strip of cloth.

It catches at the seam of my ankle after someone has passed through the enclosure. I find it while freeing my foot. Dark blue, almost black where it has become damp. It has a stiff edge and a softer, fraying one.

I touch it to the floor.

Nothing happens.

I lay it across the clear margin of a leaf. The cloth darkens, and the margin loses a little of its brightness. I take it away.

For some time I carry it against my side, beneath my elbow. Then I discover that it stays folded in the crease of my palm if I close the two outer fingers. I can walk like that. I can rest. When another inhabitant lays their cheek against my forearm, the cloth is between us.

They lift their head sooner than usual.

I open my hand.

We both look at it.

I would not be a collector with a chamber full of things brought from above. There would be this strip, and perhaps, much later, a smooth fragment that gives back a small patch of ceiling light from a depth it does not possess. I would turn that over many times. Its back would remain uninteresting.

The cloth is different. After drying, it does not quite return to its first shape.

Neither do I know how to restore it.

***

When you arrive, I am in the front room.

You mistake the cloth for a dark crease in my hand until I open it. Perhaps you recognize the weave. Perhaps it came from someone else, years before you found the split between the roots.

You reach toward it, and I let you take it.

Your fingers close much faster than mine. I look at the place where it has disappeared. Then you open them again.

The exchange leaves no mark on either of us that I can see.

You say something. The sound presses briefly against my face and the clear edges of my ears. I feel its beginning more distinctly than its end.

When I touch my throat, you repeat it.

I do not learn your speech that afternoon. You do not discover that the slight bands beneath my jaw have always been words awaiting a listener. We spend a long time with a small piece of cloth passing between our hands. Once it falls, and you retrieve it before I have begun to bend.

After that, you move more slowly.

I am not certain this helps. I am accustomed to watching the quick movements of the little animals. Your hand, arrested halfway toward mine, is harder to understand than your hand arriving.

Eventually you tire and sit against the upright.

I sit too.

***

I think this is where I would become most distinct: not while receiving the cloth, but after you had stopped offering it.

Your hand rests open on your knee. Mine is beside it. There is nothing between them, and neither of us is waiting for the other to finish a movement.

The enclosure carries your weight into the rear room. I can feel, faintly, where your back fails to meet the wall.

Later you leave. The cloth goes with you, tucked into a fold of your clothing. I watch until that fold passes beyond the opening.

I return to the smaller chamber and lower myself carefully. My ankle catches. I loosen it.

There is room for my open hand beside my head, but I find that I have closed two fingers over the palm.

I leave them that way for a while.

Beyond the wall, the front shelf is empty. Through the narrow opening I can see its smooth edge and a little of the floor. Someone crosses the entrance without stopping.

After a time, I get up and go out by the other side, toward the low place beneath the suspended lobe.

I kneel where I usually kneel.

This time I put in the hand that held the cloth.
