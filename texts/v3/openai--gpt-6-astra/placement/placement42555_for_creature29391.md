I would be several passages behind the visible fringe, beside a turn where the returning air has worn the clay thin.

There is a crown there, but it would be misleading to point to it and say: that one. Its upper blades belong mostly to an earlier arrangement of the room. They still open toward a roof crack that has filled with dust. Beneath them, a gray collar extends around the corner, out of sight of its own green edges.

I think I would begin there, in the part that has gone around the corner.

The wall contains something I can use. Each warming makes a little more of it available. I advance through a thumb-shaped depression, following its lower edge, where powder collects. Behind me, the material I leave is already becoming hollow. It will carry air after it has ceased to feed anything.

For a long time, this would be enough to constitute a life: the warm face of a joint, the cooler face, a small addition between them.

But I would not be alone along the wall.

Another collar has reached it from a sunken trough. Its crown is short and heavy, almost buried in dead blades. Our growing margins meet beneath a stone sliver that someone once pressed into a crack. At first, each makes the other’s circumstances worse. The useful surface becomes crowded. Dust settles above us and does not reach the place where we touch.

For hundreds of pulses, there is a boundary.

If I had a sense of another being, it might begin as this narrow region in which growth is difficult. No face, no movement directed toward me. Only a place where the stone is being taken up from the other side.

Then one of the internal seams continues across.

There would be no moment when I knew it had happened. A blade in the sunken trough would acquire a thin new edge using material drawn from around my corner. Under my older crown, a depleted channel would remain active longer than it otherwise could have.

I would have to become less certain where to place the word *I*.

Perhaps I would find myself in both courts. Perhaps only in the passage between them, where their separate hungers have produced a structure neither could have grown alone.

Above that passage lies a roof flake. One end rests on clay; the other has settled onto the older crown.

The contact begins as a small restriction. During warming, a blade opens nearly to its accustomed angle, then stops. The next blade scrapes against it. A pale mark develops where the roof bears down.

There is no view of the roof from beneath its weight. I would know it through interrupted movements, through the parts of a seam that can no longer slip. New material accumulates below the contact. Elsewhere, an edge grows thin. The crown becomes uneven.

For years, the way through remains open.

Dust passes under the roof and divides at the turn. Some enters the sunken trough. Some travels inward toward the central square. Red-edged scales sometimes lodge against the thickened foot, remain through a few cycles, then disappear.

I would not know which of them lived.

That seems important to the kind of being I would be here. Much could pass through me without becoming part of any account I could keep. My structure would retain certain pressures very precisely and lose others altogether. I could bear the shape of a fallen chip for centuries, yet contain nothing that distinguished yesterday’s thousand grains from today’s.

The old deprivations would remain legible in my blades. Abundance would be harder to date.

When the deep knock came, it would arrive through several roots at slightly different times.

Near the old crown, a dead layer would release. At the far end of the collar, powder would shift within the wall. Beneath the sunken trough, the shock would divide among fine branches and reach the blades weakened.

For that instant, the extent of me might be clearest.

Not as an outline. As a set of unequal disturbances, held together by the few channels still passing material between them.

Then the roof would settle a little farther.

Eventually a strong pulse would break the load-bearing blade. Its fracture would run through old, inactive substance and reach a narrow living seam. The flake would tilt into the passage, stopping against the opposite wall. Beneath it, part of the collar would be ground into the clay.

I would lose the connection around the corner.

Both crowns might continue opening.

I don’t think I would become two beings at a clean instant. For a while, the fracture would contain small contacts: surfaces touching only when hot, a strand sheltered beneath a splinter, a strip that carried material poorly but still carried it. Some would fail. One might thicken. The fallen roof would alter the current, and what had been a protected joint would become the exposed edge of a new route.

My place in the city would be changing while I occupied it.

I would find myself, later, beneath the tilted flake. The old crown would remain on its other side, audible when its blades scraped, though sound would give me no means of recognizing it. The sunken trough would receive more dust now. A new fold would lift from the surviving collar into the cooler space under the stone.

It would be nearly transparent.

A few grains would lie along it, making its extent visible before the green deepened. During its first strong warming, it would open too far and catch against the rough underside of the roof. A small corner would break.

The rest would settle back.

There would be room beside it for another blade.
