I would be found beneath one of the benches that people maintain between journeys.

There would be a little clearance there, above the water and below the iron strips: a place where condensation collects without being disturbed by passing coats. The underside of the seat would have softened around its bolts. Someone inspecting it would reach past the first sound timber, press a thumb into what they took to be a swollen joint, and feel me move away.

I would not go far. There would be another hand’s breadth of shelter toward the middle.

At first, that is all I know about my body: it can withdraw from a thumb without releasing its hold. It has several places of attachment, which it loosens in succession. A tool passed beneath me would find no single place to lift.

The worker would leave that part of the bench until last.

By then I might have moved onto their wrist.

***

There are animals here that recognize the way a person stands. I think I would know people better after they sat down.

Weight arrives on a bench in stages. There is the hand on the rail, the pressure of a hip against the edge, the small commitment by which a person lets the seat take them. Tools settle afterward. Then, sometimes much later, the feet stop carrying their share.

I would be sensitive to that last change.

A person resting briefly keeps something of the road in their legs. Someone waiting for another person eventually gives the bench more weight than they meant to. The boards turn a little against their fastenings. Moisture moves through the grain.

That would draw me toward them.

My mouth would be small and underneath, suited to working along a seam. I would feed on the soft growth inside the wood, but only after pressure had opened it. Too much food on one side of a bolt would be dangerous. I would have to cross frequently, grazing a narrow path around the washer.

The maintenance crews would know this about my kind. A well-inhabited bench would remain sound longer than an empty one, although nobody would trust us instead of testing it.

Some of us would eat too far.

I might have done so when young. There would be a shallow place beneath the seat, stopped with a square of new timber, that I avoided in my ordinary rounds.

***

The bench would stand where two service lines nearly meet.

One approaches along a cable low enough to follow with an elbow. The other comes over a terrace on boards secured with iron strips. Between them is the bench, a drinking tank, and a patch of ground that holds its shape poorly under unattended objects.

People put their bags on the seat.

For years, two regular visitors would sit above me. They would arrive separately and leave separately. One would have a hard object sewn into the back of a coat, perhaps a buckle that had become inconvenient elsewhere and never been removed. The other would rub a thumb across the grain of the seat while talking.

I would recognize the rubbing before I learned the voice.

Their visits would provide a long, even feeding interval. They would settle thoroughly. Sometimes a tin would be placed between them, warming a patch of board I could not reach without passing under both their thighs.

I would grow broad enough to do that.

My upper surface would fit the underside of the bench: a low swelling around each bolt, a ridge where two boards met. My outer edge would be thin and able to turn upward into a crack. Underneath I would have paired limbs, usually folded so closely that a searching hand could mistake them for corrugations.

On the bench, people would describe me by spreading a hand and then spreading it farther.

At some point I would become inconvenient to remove.

***

I would not understand the conversations, though I would learn some of the sounds that came before standing.

There would be one sound that usually meant they were both about to get up. I would draw my edges inward when I heard it. That kept them from being pinched as the boards straightened.

Occasionally the sound would be followed by more sitting.

Then I would unfold again.

The person with the buckle would begin arriving later. The one who rubbed the wood would move from one end of the bench to the other, trying different positions. A pair of heavy boots would be set down. Bare heels would rest on the crossbar.

On those days there would be almost nothing to eat. The load kept changing before the seams had opened.

Once, the person waiting would reach beneath the seat.

Their fingers would find the old repair first, then my edge. They would hold still. I would touch each finger with the ends of my folded limbs and continue into the palm.

My weight would surprise us both.

Until then I would have distributed myself across most of a bench. Held in one hand, I would sag around its edges and need the other hand brought underneath. A thin line of water would run from my middle.

They would sit with me in their lap.

I would have no useful response to this. Cloth gives differently from timber. The warmth would be excessive. I would work one limb beneath a coat seam and keep the rest folded.

When the other person finally arrived, neither would stand.

After that, a hand would sometimes be offered beneath the boards.

I would not always take it.

***

The first time I left the bench by myself would be during a repair.

A footing would have become too short for the ground around it. The crew would raise that end, insert a temporary support, and ask the waiting person to step away.

The bench would tilt.

Most of me would remain attached. But the old patch—the small, badly fed place I had made—would give with a soft internal tear.

I would put my limbs down to catch it.

They would reach farther than I expected.

There would be mud underneath, warm and very finely inhabited. My feet would spread on contact. I would have six of them, or six that could bear weight; the smaller folds near my mouth would be useful for holding things but not for standing.

For a while I would remain beneath the tilted bench, touching both the ground and the wood.

Then the crew would lift again, and I would let go.

They would put a mat beside me. Someone would clear a strip of ground with a fork. I would be allowed to cross it at my own pace, which would take long enough for a worker to eat.

Walking would require attention. On timber I could release one part of myself at a time and trust the rest. On the ground, a foot put down carefully might remain a considerable distance behind the next one.

I would learn to move in pairs.

The repaired bench would feel different. Its load would pass through new places. For several visits I would be unable to settle beneath either sitter, and would instead lie across the lower crossbar with my feeding surface turned inward.

A strip of old board would be tied there for me.

***

I think I would eventually live in more than that one place.

Not far away at first. Beneath the drinking tank, where people pause with one hand on the cup. Under the lowest step of the maintained crossing. Once inside a boot left beside the bench, where my presence would be discovered before a foot was inserted.

I would be lifted out with a patience I had not earned.

My body would lose some of its exact fit to the bench. The bolt-shaped depressions would become shallower. I would still have one hard ridge across my back where a split in the timber had held me for years.

When resting, I would curve around an absence the size of the central support.

The two visitors would continue coming, though not always together. I would recognize other people too: a child who knelt on the seat facing backward; a worker who sat only on the edge and slept there; someone whose approach brought the bench under load before their hand found the rail.

I would sometimes go to meet them.

That would be my difficulty. A familiar weight felt through the ground would not tell me which route it occupied. I might leave the bench, cross a strip of mud, and discover that the approaching person remained on the far side of a channel I had never encountered before.

I would wait at its edge, feet spread.

Someone coming along the service line would find me there and carry me back. There would be a way of holding me by then: both hands underneath, my harder ridge against the wrists.

I would grip if frightened.

People would learn to loosen my feet separately.

***

My home would remain beneath the bench, but a shallow tray would eventually be fixed there, above the reach of ordinary water.

It would contain the old piece of timber and a little grit. The tray would be removable for cleaning. Its catches would be large enough to operate with wet gloves.

When nobody sat above me, I would sleep with my mouth against the wood.

A person arriving alone might reach down to check that I was there. On cold visits, or long ones, I would climb into the offered hands. They would place me beside them on the seat, where I would rest partly on their coat and partly on the board.

When the second person arrived, I would have to be shifted.

Sometimes they would leave me between them.

The bench would settle. My feet would unfold into the narrow space behind their knees. Beneath us, water would gather along the ironwork, and the new support would take its share of the weight.
