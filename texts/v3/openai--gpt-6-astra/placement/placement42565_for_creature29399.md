At first they give me a place beside the lamp, which I mistake for an honor.

It is where a person sits who cannot yet be trusted to move anything.

There is a bowl at my feet. When a drop strikes my wrist, I am to shift the bowl beneath it. If the drops begin falling close together, I am to fetch someone. I ask how close together.

The woman who has put me there watches the ceiling for a while.

“Before you need another bowl.”

I am anxious to do this well. By evening I have moved the bowl so often that its base has left a wet scallop all around my stool. Someone brings me food and, without remarking on it, puts a second bowl down.

***

My feet never become very good.

I learn to distinguish running water from the small grinding movement of grit under a sole. I learn that a warm patch is sometimes only where an animal has been resting. But the finer differences escape me. Asked whether a channel is filling beneath the stones, I become so attentive to my own blood that I can feel nothing else.

For a while I hide this.

Then I am given a basket to carry through a lane I have said is safe. Halfway across, a paving stone lifts beneath my heel. Nothing terrible happens. A woman catches the basket. I scrape my shin. The water-reader with the indecent ice figures takes me back across by the elbow.

He does not tell me to listen harder.

“You can say you don’t know while we’re still on this side,” he says.

Afterward, I do.

This makes me useful sooner than trying did.

***

There is work in the upper rooms, though not enough to keep a person warm.

The records are weighted with smooth stones. Their lower edges have swollen into little terraces, and a page must sometimes be lifted with a shell rather than a finger. I am shown how to support the fold. I am told never to flatten one particular crease.

“Why?”

Nobody present knows. We leave it raised.

At first I copy measurements. Then I carry questions to the houses below.

Does the narrow passage behind the old boiling room still have two steps?

Whose old boiling room?

The one occupied by the woman with the bitten ear.

Which ear?

I go back upstairs.

By the time I return with the answer, the people I need have gone to help lift a cupboard. I find them under a bridge, arguing about whether the cupboard is worth its feet. I hold one end while they tell me about the passage. It has four steps now, they say, though two are wooden and one of those is usually elsewhere.

I write down all of this.

My first copies are admired for their neatness and returned for being wrong. I have made the uncertain parts sound settled. I have also left out the cupboard.

The cupboard matters. It stands over the opening through which the passage can be inspected.

After that, my pages become less handsome.

***

I do not think I would remain entirely upstairs.

There is too much waiting there: for a page to dry, for my fingers to recover, for someone whose grandmother remembers whether an opening was closed or merely covered. While waiting I begin repairing the cords used to tie the records into bundles.

Then the carrying straps.

Then the little felt envelopes in which people bring shell rings whose meanings have become disputed.

I am bad with the large needle. I pull too hard and pucker the felt. The sleeve-mender unpicks three of my repairs while continuing a conversation with somebody behind her. She leaves the fourth intact.

I spend several days trying to work out what was different about it.

Eventually she tells me that she ran out of time.

But she shows me how to lay a patch so that the pull goes across it rather than against the seam. I learn that a thing can look firmly fastened while already beginning to tear.

My hands become more useful than my feet.

I acquire a little bag for needles. Mine hangs beneath my arm, where it is inconvenient to reach but difficult to forget. Once, leaning through a hatch, I lose three needles into the water.

For weeks afterward I am angry whenever I see anything shining below me.

***

The room I eventually sleep in is behind a blocked window.

It belongs to another household, except for the sill, which is part of a settlement nobody wants to explain in front of me. My sleeping platform crosses the old opening. Beneath it, in the thickness of the wall, there is a shallow space just large enough for a pair of shoes.

One shoe must lie on its side.

I have dark felt on three walls. On the fourth, an irregular patch of moving brightness remains exposed. I intend to cover it. I complain about it. Yet when I wake in the night, I watch the light bend around the heads of the old stone fastenings.

Sometimes a fish goes past somewhere beyond the wall, and its shadow crosses my room enormously.

I become particular about this room. I dislike people putting wet mittens on my sill. I dislike the word *spare* being used about the space beneath my platform. When someone suggests that a narrow child could sleep there, I hear myself explaining at length about the shoes.

So I would not be an especially generous person. Or not generous without having to notice myself becoming otherwise.

I would like a corner that stayed mine.

I would live in a town where this was an awkward wish.

***

In time, people begin bringing me accounts that have become difficult to carry.

Not difficult to understand. That comes later, and often remains difficult. But a cloth may have grown so heavy with inherited arrangements that it tears at the upper corners. The shell rings knock together. A long cord catches on a latch, and suddenly the right to cross someone’s roof is trailing in the water.

I make folds. I add backing. I sew small covered places for the fragile pieces.

Before I move anything, I ask its owner to tell me what it means.

Then I ask someone else.

Occasionally this produces a quarrel in my room. I learn to put the needles away before the second person arrives.

There are arrangements that cannot be sewn neatly. A length of pale cord must remain loose because its obligation is not yet agreed upon. Two rings must touch, although nobody remembers why, and separating them would require a visit to a woman who has been refusing visitors for eleven years.

I give the rings a shared pocket.

My work acquires a recognizable fault: too many pockets. People tease me about it. They say I could put a whole flood away if somebody would hold it still.

Then they come back and ask for another one.

***

One evening, while I am altering a household account, water begins to enter beneath my door.

The cloth is spread across my knees. Its owner is explaining why a certain knot must not be cut, although the duty it records has ended. Her sister disagrees. They have been disagreeing quietly enough that I have had to lean between them.

The first water carries stone dust.

I lift my feet, then remember that this tells us nothing useful.

“I don’t know,” I say.

The owner of the cloth opens the door. Her sister takes the lamp.

Outside, somebody is already passing baskets upward. A little swimmer has fastened itself to the doorpost, working its lips against the wet wood. Beyond it, three roof birds stand in the lane, which is unusual enough that none of us moves for a moment.

Then the sister says we must go by the chimney.

I gather the cloth. It is too broad to bundle quickly, and I have left several pieces pinned rather than sewn. The sisters each take an edge. Together we fold it inward around my loose needles.

At the chimney opening, I have to let go.

For an instant I cannot. The cloth contains work I have not finished. There are pins somewhere in it. I know precisely how it ought to be held.

The woman above reaches down again.

I pass it up.

***

Afterward my room smells of newly exposed stone. The shoes are gone. For several days I sleep on a platform with people who turn over at different hours and all believe I am the one keeping them awake.

The account survives. One ring has slipped into the wrong pocket.

When I return it, I tell its owner what happened. We empty every pocket together.

This takes most of a morning.

Outside her window, someone is dragging a plank from one crossing to another. Each time its end catches, the woman pauses in what she is telling me. We both look toward the window, although neither of us can see the plank.

Then she picks up another cord.

“This one,” she says. “You’ll need to ask my brother.”

I thread it loosely through the backing, leaving room for whatever he remembers.
