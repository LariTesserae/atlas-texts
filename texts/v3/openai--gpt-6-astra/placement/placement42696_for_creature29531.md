I would live beneath the side that no longer fits the wall.

Not in the deepest part, where the folded shell presses close to the body, but a little farther out, among the worn hairs. There would be room to turn there. When the old animal settled for the night, one hair would bend across the way out without quite closing it.

I would know that hair by pushing past it.

The hollow itself would be too large for me to know as a hollow. There would be the ground, which sometimes moved away, and the underside, which usually stayed near. There would be brightness at the edges. Now and then the dark green chip would come underneath us, bringing its own coolness and a grain of food caught in the notch.

I think I would first become visible there: a small, soft interruption crossing the stone.

Then the chip would tilt, and I would go back.

***

I would have more legs than could easily be counted while I was moving, but not enough to make falling unimportant.

My body would be nearly clear. What I had eaten would give me a temporary center: pale after the mineral scrapings, brown-green after feeding at the wet margin. Along my back, a few stiff bristles would keep the plates above from pressing directly against me. They would also catch inconveniently in the old animal’s hairs.

There would be a particular backward movement for getting free.

Much of my life would consist of movements like that. Turning beneath a low place. Crossing a joint before it closed. Holding on while the old animal lifted itself from the wall.

I would not always succeed.

Once, during its descent, I would lose my grip and fall onto the projecting edge of its shell. There would suddenly be nothing above me.

Light would pass through my body. The surface underfoot would be warm and difficult to hold, its rounded grains too far apart. I would move toward an opaque ridge, then along it, searching with my front legs for an overhang.

The old animal would continue walking.

A shadow would travel over me as it passed beneath a rib in the wall. For a moment I would stop searching.

Then brightness again.

I would reach the shell’s edge beside a middle leg, climb down its moving base, and disappear into the hairs. For the rest of that journey I would stay so far inward that I could not feed.

***

I would not be its hidden intelligence, or the keeper of its years.

The repaired ridge would be older than my life. I would encounter it only from beneath, as a place where the body’s surface drew a little tighter. The missing joint would mean a particular disturbance during climbing: a pause, a sideways pull, then the return of weight.

I would brace before that pull, eventually.

Sometimes I would brace and nothing would happen.

The old animal would have found better purchase. Or we would be on level ground. I would wait with my legs spread until some smaller need made me move again.

There would be others beneath it, though few would remain as long as I did. We would touch and change direction. Two of us might feed at opposite ends of the same loosened crumb, drawing it back and forth until it broke.

One would be broad enough to block the passage behind the mouth.

For several days I would go around it. Then it would be gone, and I would still go around.

***

The scraping would reach me through every foot.

Before food appeared, there would be the gathering tension of the old animal’s body. The hairs would incline. The floor would give a fine shudder. Then the chip would rise, and loose material would lie exposed beneath its edge.

I would come forward with the others.

What looked like powder from above would be uneven country to us: flakes standing on end, rounded grains, soft clots that came apart when pulled. I would work on the damp material adhering to a larger fragment. Beside me, the animal’s mouth would draw food inward in brief, powerful movements.

Sometimes I would retreat from that pull.

Sometimes a front foot would arrive and sweep us away.

I would tumble, catch a hair, hang for a moment with nothing beneath my back legs. Then I would climb inward again, carrying what I could still hold.

The old animal would begin another scrape.

There would be no certainty that the next sweep would spare me. But the floor outside would be drying, and here a little moisture would remain between the hairs.

***

At the roots, I would leave it.

Not far. Far enough that its body ceased to be my ceiling.

Water would be advancing over the stone in a shallow, uneven edge. I would follow one of its narrow branches, feeding where it slowed against grains too small to divert the old animal’s feet. Behind me, the white shell would stand on its dark supports. Other shells would arrive. Their feet would disturb the film.

A drop would fall.

For a moment the ground would lose its usefulness.

I would be turned over in water, legs moving without contact. A bubble would hold against my side. Light and stone would exchange places several times.

Then one foot would catch.

I would pull against it until another found purchase. The bubble would leave me. Water would drain from between my bristles, making me abruptly heavier.

Nearby there would be an underside.

I would go beneath it.

But the hairs would be dense and unbroken. The space behind the front legs would narrow too quickly. When the animal moved, its steps would follow one another without the pause I had learned to brace for.

I would travel with it a little way before coming out.

Finding the old one again might take the remainder of the wet hours. I would cross beneath two others. I would shelter in a shallow scratch while the floor dried around it. At last a broad body would pass close enough for me to reach a foot.

I would climb.

Under the thickened edge there would be the worn passage, the place to turn, the single bent hair.

I would stay there while three young animals rode above us, their weight too slight for me to distinguish from the other changes of the journey.

***

At night I would come nearer the body.

The glow would scarcely reach me. A little amber would show at the shell’s edge, enough to separate one hair from another, then less.

The old animal would loosen its feet at the sound of distant water. My shelter would shift. I would stop moving until it settled.

There would still be a grain of food held in my front limbs. I would turn it, find the softer edge, and feed.

Beyond the bent hair, the stone would slowly cool. Here, against the old animal, it would remain warm.
