I would be under the second bench, near the aisle, where a strip of wood has come loose from the frame without falling. There is a space behind it deep enough for a hand. The resident knows it is there but has never needed to put a hand inside.

From the aisle, you might see a pale edge. It could be the underside of a shelf fungus, or the lip of a small pot laid on its side. If you bent down, your shadow would reach it before you did. By the time you could see properly, there would only be the wood, two rusted screws, and a little clean patch where the dust had been disturbed.

I would not be particularly quick. I would simply have very little distance to go.

At first I imagine a shell. Something hard enough to make the small sound a dropped washer makes against a pipe. But the space behind the wood is narrow, and I would need to turn inside it. The shell would have to give along certain lines. It becomes a series of curved sections, overlapping loosely, with a dull cream-colored surface that takes the green light badly. In daylight I might look yellow. Neither of us would know.

Underneath, there would be a soft body and several short limbs, more suited to holding than walking. On mesh, I could hang with almost no effort. On concrete, I would make poor progress. My feet would keep finding things too small to serve as footholds and trying to hold them anyway.

I would be about the size of the resident’s closed hand.

For a long time it would know me only by the clean patches.

I would graze on the soft film that forms on the underside of the benches, leaving paths narrower than a finger. These would sometimes cross and sometimes stop at nothing apparent. I would have preferences the resident could not deduce. Two adjacent lengths of the same rail, equally damp, equally shaded: one stripped clean, the other untouched.

It would inspect the wood once with the back of its knife, perhaps suspecting some new rot. I would feel the pressure through my feet and remain on the other side of the rail.

There would be more room inside me than my body required.

Behind the plates, above the part that eats and grips, there would be a chamber lined with a thin membrane. The pumps would set it trembling. Not painfully, most of the time. Their ordinary running would fill it so continuously that I would notice individual sounds only when I shifted position.

Turning sideways on a bracket might bring out the metallic rattle. Moving a little nearer the wall might suppress it, leaving the deeper noise. A loose attachment would add something unpleasant, a flutter that persisted after I moved away.

This would account for some of my apparently arbitrary paths.

It would also give me reasons to go places where there was nothing to eat.

There is a bolt under the potting platform, inaccessible from above, that I would visit in the warmer part of the afternoon. I would hook two feet over its exposed threads and lift the others clear. Suspended that way, I could feel both pumps separately. When their cycles drew near one another, a slow pulse would pass through the chamber of my body.

Sometimes I would stay until it made me let go.

The resident would find me on the floor beneath that bolt.

I think our first clear encounter would be undignified. I would have landed on my back, with one flexible plate tucked beneath another. My feet would be able to reach the concrete, but not to push against it effectively. Each attempt would turn me a little farther toward the channel.

The resident would crouch.

Its hand would wait beside me before touching. I would take hold of the edge of one nail, mistaking it for something fixed, and it would lift me that way.

Not far. Only onto the lower brace of the platform.

For several days afterward, I would avoid the bolt. Then I would return.

We would not become helpers to one another in any dependable sense. I would not detect faults in the machinery, or lead it to hidden leaks. A sound that distressed me might mean danger, but it might also mean that somebody had left a bucket touching the pump-room wall. I would have no way of distinguishing these things.

Nor would I be good company while it worked. If carried somewhere, I would usually try to get back. If offered fruit, I would remain underneath it.

But I would learn the difference between its touch and a tool touching the bench.

Eventually it would stop withdrawing its hand when I came near.

During a scheduled shutdown, after the three blows on the pipe, I would come out into the aisle. The dying motors would leave several small sounds behind: water tapping in the sump, the fan, a slight scraping from somewhere in the roof. None would be large enough to fill my chamber. I would keep turning, trying different surfaces.

The resident would already have its palm against the floor.

I would climb onto the back of that hand.

There would still be vibration in it. Not much, and nothing like the pumps. It would come unevenly through the tendons and the small bones beneath the skin. I would fold my feet close and lower myself until the soft underside of my body rested between two knuckles.

The resident would not pick up its scraper.

When the machinery started again, I would leave before it needed to move.

After that, there would be occasions when I came down for no obvious reason. The resident would be sitting by the fern, with its palm under the drip, and I would work my way along the wall to the pipe jacket. Getting down from there would be awkward. It might raise its other hand. It might not.

I would prefer the outside of the wrist, where the skin stayed comparatively dry.

A drop striking my back would make a small, clear knock, too slight for a visitor to hear over the machinery. I would draw my plates together. The next drop would sound different.

The resident would turn its wrist a little.

For a while we would both wait.
