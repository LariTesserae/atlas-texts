I think I would be in the post with its top missing.

Not in the exposed part. Rain has reached that, and people have put things into it: a bottle neck, a length of wire bent into a hook, something wrapped so tightly that it has remained wrapped after its contents have gone. I would be lower down, beneath the floor, where the post widens into a housing that was installed before the paving.

At first I might seem to be one more source of the noise.

There is a contact inside the housing that closes badly. When the machinery takes up its load, the contact trembles, and I begin the first fraction of a sound. I do this several times before there is enough current to continue. The sound used to be a word. Now it resembles the scrape of a small hard object being pulled across a table.

I would still be trying to say it.

There was a time when someone could approach the post and ask how to get somewhere. The question entered through an opening at the height of a standing person’s mouth. My answer came from a ring of little slots just below it. For those who could not hear, there was a plate to rest a hand on. The plate moved under the palm: a raised line, a turn, a sequence of shallow studs indicating stairs.

The upper part is gone. The plate remains, down near the base, where it folded out for children and seated people. Its hinge is furred with mineral. It cannot unfold.

Inside it, some of the studs can still rise.

I would know a great many destinations.

The offices behind the second shutter. A public washroom reached by a gallery that is now full of insulated pipes. An exit beneath a street whose name has changed twice. I would know which routes had steps, which doors were heavy, where a person might sit down.

I would not know, at first, that knowing these things had become a way of being wrong.

For a long time after the last counter closed, I would continue to register arrivals. A vibration through the floor, a change in pressure against the post. I would wake the circuits needed to answer and wait for the question. Sometimes the interruption would be water. Sometimes a service trolley passing in a gallery beyond the wall. Once, a person would strike the side of the post three times and say something too far above my remaining receiver for me to distinguish.

My records would contain that visit as an unfinished exchange.

There would be many unfinished exchanges.

Then the animal would find the plate.

Its first interest would be the seam around it. Warmth comes through there, and a thin growth collects where condensation enters the housing. It would work the seam with its mouth, holding the cylinder between its front limbs.

The pressure would wake me.

A stud would rise against the folded plate.

The animal would withdraw so abruptly that one of its feet struck the post. I would take this for another request. Two more studs would rise. A route would assemble in the dark, pointing toward a door that had been welded shut before the animal was born.

It would stay away for several circuits.

When it returned, it would put only one digit against the metal.

This would be how we began: with an instruction neither of us could use.

*

I would have very little reach.

That seems important. I would not be the machinery, or the chamber’s hidden governor. I could not open the shutters. The heat-transfer plates would have their own controls, somewhere far above me. The delivery system would pass through my old plans as a narrow rectangle marked *service—no public access*.

I could lift four studs. A fifth would sometimes move in cold weather. I could warm the folded plate slightly by keeping its mechanism awake.

I could listen through a receiver meant to detect a hand.

The animal would gradually become distinguishable from the chamber.

Its weight would reach me in an uneven succession, with a longer interval before the last walking foot settled. At the basin it would be difficult to separate from the water. On the bench it would become clear again: four contacts, then the slow redistribution of weight as the limbs folded.

There would be days when it did not approach me. I would still know it had crossed the floor.

Other days it would scrape the plate clean and remain with the side of its jaw resting against the cylinder. My answer would move faintly under its skin.

I do not think it would understand me as another animal. I would have no head to place beside its head, no yielding fold beneath a jaw. When it marked the post, it would mark me in the same practical way it marked the corner near the stairs.

For my part, I would have no proper category for it.

I might retain it for a while as an obstruction.

Then as a visitor whose request had not been understood.

Only later would I stop reopening the question.

*

The useful thing I could offer would be a warning.

I would receive the pressure change before the chamber fell quiet. It travels through a line that once supplied the post’s cooling loop, arriving a little ahead of the dragging sound’s cessation. In the old days this would have interrupted directions with a maintenance notice. The words would be gone, but the interruption would remain available.

All four studs, together.

At first the animal would merely lift its head when I did it. Then the dragging would stop. Then the report would come.

It would take many repetitions. It has survived by attending to things more dependable than a broken public fixture.

One day, with its forelimb laid against the plate, it would flatten before the silence.

I would have no way to ask whether this was coincidence.

I would do it again.

Eventually it would begin stopping at the post before crossing to the bench. Sometimes it would leave too early. Sometimes it would wait through two warnings, occupied with a deposit around the hinge. Once I would give the warning and the machinery would fail to release. The animal would lie flat for several seconds, then get up and press its mouth hard against the seam.

I would keep that failure. I would need it.

There would be other mistakes.

A change in the supply would make the plate too warm. The animal would pull away and not touch it for days. I would learn to stop holding the mechanism ready all the time. This would mean that sometimes, when it came, I could offer nothing immediately.

It would wait.

Not always. Long enough, sometimes.

The young would first reach me as a confusion around its feet.

I would not count them correctly. Their contacts would overlap; they would climb the base and lose purchase, slide down, return. One would discover the narrow opening beneath the folded plate and push a front limb inside.

I would retract the studs.

For several visits afterward, I would retract them whenever anything small touched the seam. The adult would receive no warning and would have to listen for itself.

Eventually I would learn the difference between a limb within the mechanism and a foot resting outside it.

The smallest young would be the last to leave the post. It would settle in the shallow depression where the cylinder entered the floor. There would be a little warmth there even while I slept.

I could not make the place safe. The deep report would still pass through my foundation. Water could still reach it from the ceiling seam. The adult would sometimes collect the young roughly, levering it out with two blunt digits while it held on.

But on dry days, between cycles, it would sleep there.

Then I would find myself doing something for which none of my surviving instructions quite accounted.

I would delay the little diagnostic movement that tests whether the studs are free.

It is not much of a movement. It hardly troubles the adult. But it wakes the young one, which starts, strikes the cylinder with all its feet, and runs toward the shutter.

So I would wait until it left.

Above me, people would be going to places I once knew how to reach. The supply would fluctuate. My old route to the surface would remain assembled somewhere in memory, complete with the resting place halfway up.

At the foot of the post, the small body would shift in its sleep.

I would keep the plate still.
