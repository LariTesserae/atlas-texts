I would live beneath the foundation, but not in the hunter’s room.

There is a narrower place beside it, where the polished concrete ends and the broken surface begins. Water passes through there on its way toward the intake. When the hunter withdraws, its throat brushes the entrance. The pressure of that passing reaches several body lengths inward.

I would know that pressure before I knew the pump.

At first I imagine something loose enough to be carried: a pale body among the grit, with a dark interior and no settled orientation. The water turns me over. I touch iron, lose it, touch something softer that gives beneath me. There are mouths in that softness. I am taken up and released, or perhaps only pushed aside by feeding limbs.

What matters is finding a place where the water passes but does not take everything with it.

I find one beneath a lip of corroded iron. One end of me can reach the current; the other fits into a pit scarcely deeper than the thickness of my skin. For a while I hold by spreading myself. Then the underside begins to make a clear adhesive. Fine particles lodge in it. I add more.

My first shelter is mostly what would otherwise have passed me.

I would not remain pale. The exposed end would keep its almost colourless rim, but the sheltered body would darken with swallowed minerals and the waste I could not entirely carry away. Seen with the iron lifted, I might resemble a damp stain with a clean edge. Touched, I would contract into a crease that seemed too shallow to contain me.

There would be many such creases.

I would have a mouth, though nothing that looked much like a face. Around it, short branches would unfold into the water. Their inner surfaces would be sticky. Their outer surfaces would feel the bending of the flow. A branch could fold before whatever had struck its neighbours reached it.

Most of my feeding would happen after the large movement stopped.

Then water would return through the collar around the piston, slowly enough to bring things within reach. Torn film. The soft contents of a crushed egg. A grazer’s joint with a little tissue still inside it. I would turn these toward my mouth without knowing which part of the hollow they had been.

Sometimes the water would bring almost nothing. I would remain extended anyway.

My body would not hear the pump as a sound. One wall of the place I occupied would grow closer. The roots of my feeding branches would tighten. Deep in my shelter, a part of me held flat against the iron would feel the first movement while the mouth still hung in quiet water.

I would fold.

There would be no picture of the approaching stroke, no knowledge of the long wet room above or the pools changing level outside. There would be a shortening of the body, a turn inward, and the pressure arriving over the place where I had just been.

Afterward, I would open a little too soon.

One branch would be torn off.

For several cycles I would extend unevenly. Food would gather on the damaged side and remain beyond the reach of the surviving branches. Smaller things would come to eat it. Some would stay.

That is where I begin to become less like the solitary creature I first imagined.

Not because my body loses its edges. It would defend them. A neighbour growing across my opening would be scraped, pinched, starved of the small passage of water it needed. But my shelter would join its shelter. Material caught by its branches would settle against my base. The grit holding me clear of the moving iron would rest, in part, on bodies already dead.

I would live longer because of them without being able to distinguish them from the floor.

As I grew, a narrow fold along my side would deepen. It would take food from the same internal passage, then make an opening of its own. For a time, the two mouths would withdraw together. Later one would remain open while the other closed.

The fold would thicken between us.

I would not know when to begin calling the second mouth someone else.

Through the thin wall, its contractions would still reach me. If it were struck, I would pull inward. If I withdrew abruptly, it might do the same. A disturbance would travel along the wet collar in this way, not very far and not reliably. There would always be a body that failed to close, or one that opened again before the danger passed.

The moving iron would keep its bare strip.

We would grow toward that strip continually.

***

The hunter would sometimes put its lips against the crack outside us.

The draw would be unlike a stroke: brief, local, followed by warm clouded water moving the wrong way. Loose animals would vanish. My branches would flatten against my mouth. Farther in, where the hunter’s suction weakened, mouths would open into what it had stirred loose.

Once its hard lip would catch the edge of the collar.

A piece would come away with six living bodies in it.

Water would begin to pass more quickly through the gap.

For a while that would be good. I would feed almost continuously. My new branches would grow longer than the old ones had been. Silt would accumulate behind me, and small bodies, newly arrived, would fix themselves along its sheltered edge.

Then the next stroke would drive the whole accumulation inward.

I would survive beneath it, partly buried. One opening would remain. Each time the water slackened, I would push a little mud through that opening with my mouth.

Above us, perhaps, the hunter would be carrying grit away from its eggs. We would never meet. There would only be two small clearings in the dark, maintained by different bodies.

***

This is where I would find myself: on the inner edge of the damaged collar, downstream of the gap.

Not at its centre. Not where my absence would stop the machine. A little seepage would pass beside me whether I fed or folded, and most of what kept the piston fitted would lie beyond anything I could feel.

But the surface immediately around my body would change with my living.

A grain would lodge against my side. Another would lodge against that. The current would turn slightly. A newly arrived animal would find quiet water behind me and hold there.

Before the stroke, I would draw in my branches.

The small animal would remain extended for a moment longer.

Then, through the material joining our shelters, it would feel me close.
