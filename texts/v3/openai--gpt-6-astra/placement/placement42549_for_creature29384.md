I would be in the newest ridge on the snail’s shell, on the side that faces down when it rests beneath the step.

Not the large opening at the crest. A smaller one near the ridge’s end, where the mineral thins enough for the brown of the shell to show through. My chamber would turn twice before reaching me. A grain caught at the first bend would trouble the flow for much of a morning.

I would have arrived before I had a chamber, carrying water over my back.

There would have been no reason to choose the shell. It was wet. Along its join with the step, the film was thick enough to cross, and on the other side there was a crease that held warmth after the surrounding metal cooled. I would have entered that crease and gone a little farther than necessary. Something moving behind me would have closed the way back.

For a while, the shelter itself would seem to be contracting. Pressure would pass through its floor. The water at the entrance would lean, thicken, disappear.

Then water would come from a different direction.

By the time the snail moved out into the runoff, I would have fastened.

My first collar would be poor work: a ragged deposit adhering mostly to one side, with a gap too broad to retain water properly. Each time the snail raised its shell over an obstruction, the entrance would drain. I would fold hard into the crease. Some of the gel meant for the growing edge would dry against my own flank.

But the snail would stop often.

It would stop beside a warm rivet. It would stop with its foot partly in a shallow channel. It would stop for so long beneath a hanging leaf that the leaf’s next emptying would find it still there. During these rests I would feed and extend. The collar would become a short tube, turned down at the mouth.

On an ordinary shelf, that turn might be unnecessary. Here it would keep a bead of water when the ground tipped.

Other animals would settle nearby. Some would be washed away before fastening. One would build directly above me, and its runoff would pass through my opening. Another would occupy the narrow gap behind my chamber, where little food arrived but drying took longer.

We would not all emerge together. At the warm channel, those on the lower side would open first. As the shell settled, another row would reach the water. I would feel the small alteration in the current when my neighbor extended, and advance into what remained.

The snail would carry us through places where we could make nothing.

Cold iron. Dry fiber. A lifted edge of felt beneath which its body squeezed while the shell scraped overhead. On those journeys the collar would wear at the tip. The newest crust would sometimes break away.

There would be wear here, though almost nowhere else in the garden.

I would keep thickening the place that struck the underside of the step. After a time, it would become smooth.

Once the snail would climb high enough that all our entrances emptied. No fringe could bring in water. The warmth would leave the shell unevenly, first from the exposed crown, then from the lower curve where I lay.

I would withdraw until the brown disk at my hind end took my weight.

For a long interval there would be only the water inside the chamber, changing as I used it.

Then the snail would turn. Its foot would drag across a seam full of moisture, and the shell would knock against a bolt. The impact would split the end of my tube.

I would survive in a chamber suddenly too open.

The following days would go into closing it. The new wall would be crooked. A neighboring animal would extend into part of the break, and the passage we left between us would admit water from below as well as from the side.

That would suit us better.

Much later, the attendant would lift the snail.

The movement would begin as an unfamiliar pressure beneath the shell. Our bodies would go in before her fingers reached the ridges. She would hold us near the yellow door, turning the shell to see the pale growth beneath its rim.

To her, the ridge might look like something the snail had acquired by passing repeatedly through mineral water. Then one of us would emerge.

Not me. I would remain behind the repaired wall.

She would wait. A drop from her wet thumb would spread along the ridge. In that drop the first animal would open its fringes, then a second. Eventually I would come forward far enough to reach the new current.

She would put the snail down beside an eastern cage rather than under the step.

Here the water would be warm and almost continuous. Moss would catch against the shell when the snail pushed beneath the wires. At night it would feed along the same few inches, and each day it would rest in a hollow sheltered by a root.

Our ridge would thicken.

There would be young moving over its surface, carrying their small burdens of water. Some would cross onto the moss. Some would enter crevices in the cage and begin chambers that never tilted.

I would stay attached to the shell.

One evening, while the snail rested, my growing lip would meet a deposit on the wire beside it. Warm water would linger at the contact. Through several hours of feeding, the join would whiten and become continuous.

When the snail moved, it would have to pull.

The bridge would break close to my opening. I would withdraw, unharmed, with a rough new edge outside me. A white chip would remain on the wire.

The snail would go beneath the step again. It would take most of the night to get there.

By morning, water would be passing over the broken edge. I would reach out into it.
