I would be on the late shift, in the narrow room beside the pumping house where the maintenance sheets are kept.

The room would have a desk but no comfortable place to sit. A high stool, perhaps, with one rubber foot missing. When I put my weight on it, the frame would carry a faint vibration from the floor into my teeth. I would fold a piece of packing card under the short leg, then replace it every few nights as the damp softened it.

Through the open door I could see the lowest step and a little of the basin. Not enough to watch the water properly. Enough to notice something arriving.

At first I think I would be there to record things: pressures, temperatures, the hours on each motor. I would make the circuit with a clipboard, stopping at the same numbered places. The work would suit someone who needed a reason to stand quietly without being asked whether anything was wrong.

I would be new enough to read the old entries.

There would be years of them in a cupboard whose bottom shelf had swollen. Most would concern seals, bearings, deliveries. Here and there, in different hands, the animal would occur.

*Basket not seating.*

*Check recess before flushing.*

*Cover caught underneath. Do not pull.*

I would not know which of these referred to it until someone told me.

The woman who cleaned the strainers would show me the blue mark. She would do this while explaining something else, with the back of a finger because her gloves were dirty.

“Ask before you open that one.”

I would put a note on my circuit sheet. After a few weeks I would no longer need the note, but I would leave it there.

***

My first clear view of the animal would be from the stool.

I would have finished the readings and be waiting for a kettle with a furred element to boil. The animal would come up through the recess, put one foot on the step, and remain with the rest of its body below the floor.

From that angle the foot would be the most definite part of it. Four or five blunt ends—I would not be able to count without leaning forward. A grain of pale gravel pressed into the skin. Water collecting under the wrist and releasing itself all at once.

I would keep still.

This would turn out to be the wrong thing, or at least not the reassuring thing I intended. Its head would rise. One small eye would face the room.

The kettle would switch off.

For a while neither of us would move. Then I would reach for my cup, and the animal would lower itself out of sight.

Later I would understand that my usual movements were easier for it than my attention. The stool scraping, the cupboard opening, my shoes crossing the threshold: these gave it something to go on.

I would learn to finish what I was doing.

***

There is a version of this in which I become its keeper, leaving food on a saucer, discovering a sound it answers to. I do not think that is who I would be here.

There is already plenty for it to eat. The dry place behind the motor is warmer than my room. It knows routes under the floor that I cannot inspect without taking the district apart.

What I could do would be smaller.

I would move the spare lengths of conduit away from the drainage recess after a contractor stacked them there. I would remember which edge of the cover to lift first. When washing down the floor, I would give the animal time to leave rather than sending the first hard stream of water into the gap behind the plinth.

Some nights I would forget it altogether.

That seems necessary. I would have a split thumbnail, an overdue invoice folded in my bag, someone to telephone before it grew too late. The animal would pass beneath the walkway while I was thinking about these things. Neither of us would have to make an event of the other’s presence.

Still, I would begin to recognize certain marks on the lowest step.

A wet curve where its side had rested. A short cleared patch in the grit. Once, a washer.

I would pick up the washer and put it in the tray with the others. I would have no good reason to leave a loose piece of metal beside a drain.

***

What would make the room mine would be a shallow drawer that did not close.

It would contain a torch, two pencils, a small adjustable spanner, the packing card for the stool. Eventually there would also be a sketch of the drainage recess, made after I had looked inside it during a shutdown. Not a picture of the animal. Measurements of the opening and the bend beyond it.

The official drawing would show that recess as a straight connection.

It would bother me that it was wrong.

I would ask about having it corrected, and someone would tell me to pencil the alteration onto the copy in the pump house. I would do that. The animal could go through the bend whether the drawing showed it or not, but the next person deciding where to push a cleaning rod ought to know it was there.

That is probably where I become definite: standing at the wall with a ruler held against a curling sheet, trying to make a small correction legible among the earlier ones.

Not the person who understands the sump animal. The person who has been here long enough to show a replacement worker where to put their feet, which gauge needs a tap, and why the cover sometimes has to be lowered again.

Outside, the animal would be feeding under the step. I might hear a shell touch concrete, though not if both motors were running.

I would finish the line on the drawing, write the date beside it, and return the pencil to the room.
