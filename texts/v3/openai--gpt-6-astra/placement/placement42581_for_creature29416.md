I think I would first be visible from below.

You would be coming up a ladder out of one of the blue reservoirs, and above you there would be a pair of broad feet gripping the edge of the landing. Between them, a rope would descend. Something heavy would be coming down slowly under a cloth.

I would ask you to move your hand.

Not urgently. There would be time to explain which hand, and to wait while the little gray animals crossed the rung you needed next.

Under the cloth would be a piece of somebody’s kitchen floor.

—

I would live low in the district, though not necessarily near its bottom. Getting home would involve a considerable descent and, on difficult days, shortness of breath. My door would be behind a reservoir that had been drained and converted into a place for drying clothes. The ladders would still be used. Neighbors would hang their felt panels between them, leaving a passage to my room.

I would know who was visiting before I saw them by which garments they moved aside and which they ducked beneath.

Inside, there would be a bed, a cooker, and a workbench too large for the room. I would have brought the bench in during a season when the doorway was wider. Now I would sometimes measure it, although I would not seriously consider leaving.

The bench would be the reason people came.

I would fit old flooring into new rooms.

Not the broad, unworn slabs. Other workers would deal with those. I would handle the patches lifted last: the dark place in front of a stove, the crescent beside a hanging bed, the little irregular oval where someone had stood every day to open a stubborn cupboard.

They would arrive wrapped, with their undersides still dusty from the homes they had left.

Some would be no thicker than a plate. Others would have to be cut free with a deep backing of ceramic, staples, and whatever the district had grown around them. Once, I would find a sealed lamp beneath a place worn by three generations of bare feet. The lamp would still work. Its light would make the underside of my workbench unpleasant to look at until I covered it again.

I would keep a supply of wedges, felt strips, powdered grout, and small labels.

The labels would be important. Two pieces from the same household could look nearly identical, and it would be embarrassing to confuse the place where someone had cooked with the place where they had waited for a person who was ill.

People would notice.

They might not tell me immediately.

—

My body would suit the lifting, though it would have taken practice.

I would be wider through the hands than the woman who checks the thresholds. The dark pads of my fingers would spread far enough to hold a slab without pressing its worn face. When I crouched, I would settle almost to the height of my toolbox. This would make customers forget I was still in the room.

I would learn things that way.

A complaint about my price. An argument about which possessions were worth bringing. Once, a quiet discussion of whether I should be invited to eat.

I would make a small noise before standing.

There would be a place beneath my left knee where the pale surface never quite formed properly. Ceramic dust would collect there, so that at the end of the day I would have to sit with a damp cloth and clean each fold. I would resent this chore. Occasionally I would neglect it and walk stiffly the next morning, claiming that the stairs had changed.

My coat would have a broad inner pocket for fragments too small to wrap separately. I would empty it before every visit involving children. There would always be one sharp piece caught in the stitching.

I would not be especially old. People would think I was, because of the work and because I would take too long getting out of chairs.

—

At first, I would believe the best repair was the one that could not be felt.

I would take great care. I would match the surrounding level, soften the new edges, place the old wear where a foot approaching from the doorway would naturally find it. When I finished, I would sweep the room myself.

Then customers would call me back.

A woman would say that her standing place faced the wrong way.

I would point out that it faced the cooker, just as it had before.

She would agree. She would stand on it. After a while she would turn her head toward a blank wall.

In the old room, that had been the window.

We would move the patch the following morning. She would do her cooking partly sideways after that, with one foot on the old surface and one on the new. She would be satisfied.

I would begin asking people to spend a little time in their rooms before I fixed anything down.

This would make my work slower. There would be slabs propped against my bench for weeks. Their owners would come to discuss them, drink from my cups, and discover opinions they had not known they held.

I would become quite good at waiting without looking expectant.

Not always. There would be one man whose repeated visits made me put away the good food when I heard him among the drying clothes.

—

I would own a patch myself.

It would be a narrow piece, dark at one end, with two pale grooves where a stool had stood. I would have carried it through several moves. It would belong to the room in which I had lived with someone who was no longer willing to leave her rooms.

Her present door would be on a service passage beneath the southern slope. I could still get through it when I first began working at the bench.

Later, I would have to leave my coat outside.

She would ask about particular jobs. She would have strong views about my customers and remember all their names. When I told her about the woman who wanted to face a missing window, she would laugh for long enough that I became annoyed.

Of course she had wanted the window, she would say.

After the opening became too narrow for my shoulders, we would talk through it. I would sit on the passage floor. She would remain somewhere beyond the bend.

Sometimes I would bring food. Sometimes she would say she had already eaten.

I would keep her cup on a shelf clear of the dust.

There would not be a particular visit I could identify as the last proper one. Only, eventually, I would be taking more time to wash the cup than I spent beside the seam.

At home, my own piece of flooring would remain under the bed, wrapped in an old coat. I would know exactly how I ought to install it. I would not do so.

—

The smaller resident from above the orchard would become interested in my work.

He would first come with the woman, carrying a strip of soft metal on which she had marked the dimensions of a threshold. While we spoke, he would crouch beneath the bench and inspect the wedges.

I would tell him not to put his fingers between the stacked slabs.

He would show me the two fingers that had once needed the splint, as though this settled the matter.

After that, he would visit alone when permitted. I would give him things to sort, then have to sort them again after he left. He would group wedges by the sounds they made when tapped together rather than by thickness.

Some of his groupings would turn out to be useful.

One afternoon, looking for a dropped bolt cap, he would find the wrapped flooring beneath my bed.

He would ask whose it was.

Mine, I would say.

He would wait for me to explain what was wrong with it.

I would tell him to leave it covered.

For several visits he would be unusually careful around me, which I would dislike more than the questions.

—

I think this is where I would become most myself: not while carrying something immense down a ladder, or recognizing the household to which a fragment belonged, but on an ordinary afternoon when I had work to finish and a visitor arriving for supper.

I would pull the old coat out from beneath the bed.

There would be no suitable place for the patch. The cooker would be too close to the wall. Beside the bed, it would interfere with a storage drawer. Near the door, damp would work beneath it.

I would try three positions, growing hot and irritable, then leave it beside the table, where one corner projected into the room.

When the press clicked, I would be kneeling with a wedge between my teeth.

I would wait with everyone else.

Then I would finish.

Later the smaller resident would come with a warm dish held in both hands, and the woman behind him would move my neighbor’s coat carefully aside. I would have to bring a stool in from the reservoir.

The stool would rock. I would reach for a shim.

Before I found one, she would shift it a little. Its feet would settle into the two pale grooves.

She would sit down without noticing.

I would put the shim back in my pocket and ask them whether the stairs were still raining.
