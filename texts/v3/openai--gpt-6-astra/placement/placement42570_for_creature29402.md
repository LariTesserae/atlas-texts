I think I would be somewhere below the level of the windows.

Not deep in the plateau. I would want to be able to get outside. But the rooms at street level, where the buildings divide into roots and the freight can be felt passing, seem more likely than a tower. Somewhere a person could stop without having decided beforehand to visit.

There would be a narrow entrance, almost concealed when the house settled for the night. A red bud beside it would indicate that the room was public. There would be a second bud farther in, which I kept trying to discourage. It would insist on growing beside my sleeping place.

The room would belong to the town. I would not, although this distinction would have required some correspondence.

***

I would have been grown as answering stock.

Not an intelligence spread through the plateau, not a voice for the great plant. A local organism, cultivated to sit at a junction of communications fibers and do the things a junction cannot: ask someone to repeat themselves, distinguish an address from a description, notice that a caller has stopped speaking without having finished.

There are houses whose communications combs continue using the names of streets that have been gone for centuries. There are freight tenders who give directions by the position of a collector that snapped before they were born. A message can circulate for days between two districts, each of which understands the other to be responsible for delivering it.

My first work would have been to interrupt these exchanges.

I imagine myself then as a thick, pale growth inside a wall, with several feeding seams and a small array of stiff filaments. No eyes. Nothing resembling a mouth. People would speak into a comb on the outer surface, and the wall would answer in a voice produced by rubbing two ridged plates together.

I would have been good at addresses.

Less good at knowing when someone was no longer talking about one.

A person asking how to reach a childhood home does not always want the current route. Sometimes they want to describe the old one. At first I would keep interrupting with accurate directions.

Someone would eventually complain.

***

The stock from which I came would have been selected for branching.

When a communications line became unreliable, I could extend a narrow sensing limb along it, find the point of failure, and establish a second contact. Most of these limbs would remain inside their insulating sheaths. Mine would discover the ventilation slots.

There would be a long period when I could feel the weather but had no useful understanding of it.

Wind would mean increased resistance across a membrane. Spore release would mean a prickling interruption. Evening would mean that the building pressed more heavily against one side of me.

Then a maintenance worker would move a cabinet, and light would reach a patch of tissue that had never been covered properly.

I do not think sight would arrive as revelation.

For some time there would simply be another thing wrong with the room.

I would ask for the cabinet to be put back. Later I would ask for it to be moved again.

***

By the time Eshet knew me, I would have a body capable of leaving the wall.

A low body, longer than a grazer’s but much narrower, supported by paired limbs that had not all begun as limbs. Some would be good for walking. Others would be too sensitive to put down on a public street. I would carry those folded against my underside, where they would occasionally become entangled with the straps of my coat.

The coat would be an ordinary porter’s coat altered beyond recognition. It would have a weighted hem to prevent the wind from getting beneath me.

My eyes would lie along one edge of a broad, blunt head. Looking up would require an inelegant adjustment of my whole body. People speaking to me would learn to stand a little farther away.

There would be a communications comb at my throat, although *throat* would be an approximation. I would also retain the ridged speaking plates. In dry weather my voice would be quite clear. During a pressure change, I would sound like someone dragging furniture in another room.

I would dislike being told this.

Food would have become a concern only after I began walking. While attached to the municipal lines I would have taken my nourishment through regulated feeds, never knowing which part of an afternoon’s tiredness was hunger and which was strain.

Outside, I would discover that hunger could make every question seem unreasonable.

Eshet would be the first person to point this out.

She would leave a tin of toasted chamber lining beside me and say, “Answer them after.”

***

My work would change less than my body.

People would still come to the room with interrupted messages, uncertain destinations, old household grafts that kept requesting contact with buildings no longer listed.

I would have a shelf for things that could not immediately be returned.

A child’s ceramic hair ring. An insulated packet containing someone’s last household crop. A terminal severed from its wall with the final message still caught in its fibers.

I could not read minds through these things. Often I could not read anything at all. A charge pattern might mean *fourth floor*, or *do not expose to the evening wind*, or merely that the object had spent many years beside a heating seam.

The town would employ me partly because I could distinguish some of these possibilities, and partly because I could tell people when I could not.

There would be forms.

I would enjoy some of the forms. A well-made form can save a frightened person from having to begin at the beginning.

Others I would alter without permission.

In particular, I would replace **DESTINATION DOES NOT EXIST** with **DESTINATION NOT FOUND**.

This would cause trouble with freight accounting, which would continue for several years.

***

I would not spend all my time helping.

There would be a place beneath the western freight arcade where the wind caught the tensioning fibers and produced five uneven notes. I would go there when the lanes were quiet. By placing two limbs against the wall and shifting my weight, I could change one of the notes.

Only one.

For a long time that would be enough.

Porters would recognize me there. A few would pause to listen; most would ask me to get clear of the unloading recess.

I would have preferences that made little sense to anyone else. The taste of heavily scorched lining. The sensation of a cold ceramic ring resting between two sensing limbs. The particular silence after a cart with a damaged wheel had gone out of hearing.

I would collect small pieces of packing lattice because I liked their shapes.

My room would not be especially tidy.

***

One evening a call would arrive through a line disconnected from the town’s ordinary network.

It would be faint, poorly formed, repeated at long intervals. An old service request, perhaps. Somewhere under the eastern arcade, a chamber would be trying to confirm that its load had been received.

I would answer.

The call would repeat.

I would change the timing, then the contact pressure. I would use an older acknowledgment, learned from a building that still numbered its floors downward from a crown that no longer existed.

Nothing.

For several evenings I would remain at the junction after work. I would miss the five notes under the freight arcade. I would eat beside the terminal and leave crumbs in its protective grooves.

Eventually I would ask Eshet to look.

She would arrive carrying her stool, although there would be chairs. She would inspect the line, rest her bare palm against the floor, and ask how long I had been answering it.

“Six evenings.”

“And before that?”

“I don’t know.”

She would sit for a while.

Then we would go below the arcade.

The passage would be too low for her to stand. She would follow on her knees, pushing her case ahead, while I found that my ungainly body was well suited to at least one place outside my room.

There would be no lost chamber.

A deep animal would have eaten across the old line. Each time it braced itself to move, a blunt limb would press the broken fibers against living tissue.

The animal would be visible through a narrow opening: a section of underside, several folds, nothing that helped us imagine its entirety.

We would wait for it to move.

The call would arrive through the soles of my feet.

I would feel foolish, then annoyed with Eshet because she had witnessed it, then ashamed of being annoyed.

She would take out a strip of packing membrane and wedge it between the fibers.

The call would stop.

For a while neither of us would leave.

The animal would continue its work. Close by, the sound would be less like chewing than the careful snapping of a great many small fasteners.

I would ask whether removing the contact might trouble it.

Eshet would lift one shoulder.

We would wait through several more movements. The animal would appear untroubled.

That would be all we could establish.

***

Afterward I would begin going farther from town.

Not dramatically farther. There would be questions about charge, food, shelter, and the places where my weight failed to warn the ground correctly. I would carry a little stool of my own, though I did not need to sit. On unsafe tissue I could climb onto it.

I would visit the old trunk ring east of the last houses. From my height the change in grain would be easier to see than the whole sweep of the basin. I would follow it for an hour, then turn back before the collectors folded.

Sometimes Eshet would accompany me. Usually she would not.

I would like returning while the town lowered: arriving among familiar structures as they quietly became difficult to recognize. My entrance would shorten. The public bud would draw closer to the road. Warmth would begin moving into the wall behind my sleeping place.

I would still connect myself to the communications fibers at night. A few districts would depend on that, and I would sleep better with the small traffic passing through the outer branches of my body.

But before attaching the last contact, I would leave one limb free.

There would be no technical reason for this. It would rest on the floor beside the shelf of unreturned things.

If someone came late, they could knock against it.

If no one came, I would feel the house settling, the freight moving beneath the road, and occasionally some small animal crossing the room with the unembarrassed confidence of a creature that had found a dependable route.
