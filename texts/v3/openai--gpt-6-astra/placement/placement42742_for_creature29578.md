Not at the seam. I would be farther out, where a passing body can interrupt the warm water long enough for the stone to cool.

There is a place beneath a grazer’s lowest fold where a grain of grit has become lodged. Water gathers uphill of it. Below it, the skin is almost dry. When the grazer turns, the grain rolls slightly, and the wet side becomes the dry side.

For a while I think I would live there.

The fans overhead would make a roof that opened when the roof was hungry. Light would arrive cut into strips by stalks and suspended legs. I would know the grazer mostly by the way its skin tightened before it moved. Its head would be too far away to matter until it bent around to scrape at something near me.

But the grit is not fixed. Eventually the grazer flattens beneath an overhang. The fold stretches, the grain comes loose, and everything lodged against it leaves together.

I would go with that small clearing-out.

Below the nursery, among dropped flakes and empty capsules, there is a shallow place where the water turns back on itself. Material stays there through several pulses. A broken fan revolves around its stalk. An abandoned skin fills and empties, making the movements of an animal that has already gone elsewhere.

Here I would be easier to distinguish.

I would have a narrow body with a soft seam underneath, and feet that could hold a surface without lifting the rest of me away from it. Not many feet. Enough that I could release one end while the other remained fastened. My mouth would be on the underside, close to the front, where it could reach what accumulated between two touching things.

I would eat the soft deposit beneath an empty capsule. The damaged base of a stalk. Whatever began growing in the thin water trapped under a mineral flake.

My back would carry a few short, stiff hairs. Bubbles would catch between them. So would eggs too small for me to remove.

I would not be one of the animals that can choose the current.

***

The flake I settle beneath is white along one side and dark through the middle. It has an old, rounded end and a newer break. Beneath it, the water remains after the surrounding stone has dried.

I feed around its edge. When something large passes, I draw entirely underneath.

In time my body becomes the part that keeps the flake from lying flat.

This is not good for every purpose. A grazer noses at the raised edge, trying to get at the sheltered film. I have to move farther inward. Something with a toothed mouth reaches beneath and takes two of the eggs from my back. One of my feet loses its grip in a hot pulse, and the flake swings around the others, nearly folding me.

Still, I stay.

Then a yellow attendant takes the flake.

The mouth closes over its broken end. The stone beneath me disappears.

For the first time, water touches my whole underside.

I contract against the mineral. The attendant turns it once, finding a grip, and the nursery travels past in fragments: a capsule, a dark channel, the underside of another worker’s plates. I have no way of seeing the animal that carries me except for one foot pressed close to my head.

It pushes the flake into a blockage.

Something soft bursts against the other side. Cooler water comes through.

I open my mouth.

For several uses, this is how I feed.

***

I would not become the attendant’s companion. It would drop me when the flake was no longer useful.

Sometimes I would land in the shallow turning water again. Sometimes beneath a ledge with nothing to eat but the sparse growth on the mineral itself. Sometimes another mouth would lift the flake before I had finished uncurling.

I would learn the beginning of being lifted.

First, a pressure at one end. Then a minute rocking. The water beneath the flake pulls into threads.

I would draw my feet close before the threads broke.

The attendants would also learn something, though not necessarily about me. This flake would sit differently in the mouth. It would catch against a capsule instead of slipping. After it was withdrawn, a thread of water would sometimes remain where it had passed.

An attendant might turn it over and try again.

Another might discard it at once.

There would be several similar flakes in circulation. I would not know how often a worker looking for mine took one of those instead.

***

At the back of the nursery, an attendant holds us beneath a capsule that has gone too far inward.

I feel the change before I can see it.

The flake’s rounded end is under the capsule. Its broken end remains in the attendant’s mouth. I am attached across the middle. Suddenly the feet nearest my head seem to have been holding for a long time.

I release one.

The place where it rested is still visible, but I cannot reach it again.

The attendant tilts the flake. The capsule shifts. Inside it, a limb unfolds around its point of light.

For a moment the limb is larger than the whole capsule was.

Water enters beneath us. I stretch toward the part of the flake that has become cool. My underside leaves a clear thread across the white edge, and the flow follows it.

Then the attendant pulls.

I come away missing a foot.

The capsule stays.

I do not see whether it hatches.

Afterward I spend a long time beneath the rounded end, feeding without moving. The empty joint seals. A small pale bud appears there, but it does not yet hold anything.

When the flake is lifted again, I cannot draw myself entirely against it. Part of me trails in the water.

***

That is where I would find myself: on the underside of something briefly useful, being brought close to lives I could touch only in passing.

But I would have a place that was mine in a smaller, more practical sense.

Along the flake’s dark middle there is a groove. My body fits beside it. I keep its entrance clear while feeding. Water stays there even during a journey through air, and when I turn, the few creatures on my back turn into it with me.

Eventually there would be a capsule in that groove.

I would lay it near the rounded end, away from the place the mouths usually grip. For several pulses I would remain over it. Its bead would diminish. Its folded feet would press outward.

The next attendant would lift us before it was ready.

I would hold on as the flake entered a channel. Water would strike the capsule, first hot, then cooler. One side would peel from the mineral.

I could fasten to the loosened edge. I could keep it from being taken immediately.

But the attendant would still be working.

The flake would turn. The capsule would open against my underside. For a short while the new animal would have its feet among mine, both of us holding the same shallow groove.

Then the tool would be drawn back through the cleared channel.

When I could spread myself again, there would be more room beneath it.
