At first I would stay beside the slab.

There is something difficult about leaving once the tremor has changed beneath my hand. I would move my palm a little, trying to find it again, and discover that a finger’s breadth matters. Here the stone gives me the broad shudder of the chamber below. Here, with my wrist turned, a finer movement comes through the heel of my hand. I cannot tell whether something has happened or whether I have only placed myself where it can reach me.

I would spend much of the afternoon making that mistake.

The animal inside would move farther back. My weight would be changing the roof of its retreat.

Eventually I would take my hand away.

But I think that is where I would remain in this world: a little way inside, underneath the place where someone has been trying to listen.

***

My chamber would be at the narrow end of a basin, where a shallow runnel passes beneath a stone bridge. The bridge would look substantial from above. Underneath, there would be room for my back and very little more.

Farther in, the opening would widen. Six or seven of us could rest there, depending on who had fed and whether the young were still being carried. A woody root would enter through the rear wall, follow the ceiling for a short distance, then leave through a crack. When the root was wet, we would drink from it. In dry weather its underside would taste of dust.

I would usually lie near the entrance.

Not because I had been appointed to anything. There would be a shallow depression there that fitted my left hip, and the roof would slope low enough for me to press against it without fully straightening my legs. Some time ago I would have begun sleeping in that place. Now, returning from the sedge, I would push past an unoccupied hollow farther inside to reach it.

My coat would be pale except along one shoulder, where an old scrape had healed with the hair lying in the wrong direction. Grit would lodge there. I would stop to work it loose with my teeth, sometimes in the middle of crossing a basin.

My right jaw would meet the stone more comfortably than my left.

This would make certain journeys longer.

A seam running toward the cliff would reach me clearly when I lay facing inward, poorly when I faced out. Before crossing it I would turn sideways, lower my head, wait. Another animal might pass me while I did this. Its foot would touch my flank; I would draw the loose skin inward to let it through.

I would know the shelf in a very uneven way.

There would be a bowl within sight of my entrance that I rarely visited, because between us lay a broad thickness of rock through which little arrived distinctly. Beyond it, much farther away, a split above a buried passage would be familiar. I would feel birds settle there. I would know when its underside began to collect moisture.

What looked near would not always be near enough to answer.

***

When I closed the entrance with my back, I would feel the result through my own feet.

The first pressure would often be insufficient. Air would pass along my flank, lifting the fine hair. I would shift one hind foot, spread the border of skin, push again. A particular part of the tremor would weaken.

Then I would wait.

There would be no need to imagine the whole route through the mountain. The change would go where it went. Sometimes another opening would close. Sometimes an animal beside me would merely rearrange itself and put a cold foot against my belly.

I would not always be able to distinguish these things.

On a day when the limestone was gathering water, I might repeat myself several times. The familiar return would arrive faintly, or from an unexpected direction. I would leave the entrance and go down into the basin, pressing my jaw to exposed places between the grit.

At the sedge I might find nothing wrong at all: a wet seam, a beetle beneath a leaf, another animal with pollen caught across its muzzle.

We would feed.

By the time I returned, the chamber would feel familiar again.

***

I would carry feathers badly.

There is no position in which a long feather becomes convenient to a creature that travels close to the ground. Held near the tip, it drags. Held near the middle, it catches between stones. The wind presses the vane against my face.

Still, I would bring one home.

At the narrowest part of the route I would abandon it, go ahead, turn, come back. I would bite the shaft in a different place. Once inside, I would have to reverse until there was room to swing it along the wall.

For several rests afterward, its broken end would trouble someone.

The feather would gradually soften. The shaft would split beneath our bodies. Much later, when I pressed into my hollow, there would be a little less cold along my side.

***

There would be a young one who repeatedly stood on my head.

It would use the entrance to look out, bracing its large hind feet wherever they landed. Its belly would touch my eyelids. I would shift, and it would slip down beside me, then climb back.

When it first began listening outside, it would choose loose flakes. They would tremble impressively under its weight. It would press its jaw down, lift it, press again.

I might cross beside it and settle on the continuous stone.

Perhaps it would follow. Perhaps it would find an insect and forget.

Later, both of us would be feeding under the edge of a cushion when a hunter landed beyond the rise. The landing would reach my feet before the bird became visible.

I would brace and make the short movement.

The young one would flatten where it stood.

For a while its forefoot would remain on mine. I would feel the small adjustments it made, trying to draw itself closer to the ground. The bird’s steps would come separately through the stone, with long intervals between them.

I would wait through more intervals than the young one wanted.

Only afterward would I pull my foot free.

***

And if you came back, I would feel you before I saw your shadow.

Your steps would spread through several routes at once. Loose grit would shift beneath your soles. At first I would withdraw to the root, pressing against the others until someone farther in moved to make room.

If you sat down, the disturbances would diminish.

After a long time, I might return to the mouth.

Your hand would be there again. Its weight would alter the thin roof; warmth would enter a patch of stone that was usually cool by then. I would lie below it with my jaw down.

You might feel me close the gap.

I would be testing the entrance under this unfamiliar load, finding where to put my feet. Perhaps I would also be waiting for the animal across the basin. Both things could be happening.

If you pressed harder, I would stop.

If you eased your hand, I would try again.

That is where I would be: my back against the low roof, one foot not yet securely placed, adjusting to the small change you had made in the stone.
