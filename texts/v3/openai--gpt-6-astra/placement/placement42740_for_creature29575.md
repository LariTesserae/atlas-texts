I would find myself lower than I first supposed.

At first, the underside of the shelf seems sufficient: blue light, cold water, the weight of stone interrupting the brightness. I imagine a place where I could look out without being exposed. But looking out still belongs to a body that has come down from the rim. The shelter I would actually use is farther in, beyond the part of the hollow that can be inspected by lowering a face to the pool.

There is a small enlargement there. A fault in the limestone has crossed another fault, leaving a crooked chamber with three entrances, only one of which will admit my body. Water enters through the other two. Their contributions are unequal. One brings a thin, steady supply against the ceiling; the other delivers almost nothing, but what it brings has passed through a darker seam and carries more to eat.

I would live close to their meeting.

For a while, I cannot tell whether I am the pale animal or something smaller beside it. The distinction would be difficult even from within this place. So much of living here consists of being held against a surface, receiving what comes, moving a part of the body while the rest remains attached.

Then there is a foot.

It has been resting above my head, its toes spread around an irregularity in the stone. I have no clear picture of it. I know which edges can lift and which must stay down. One toe lies across a shallow groove. Water passes beneath it, making a pressure I have accommodated for so long that I notice it only when a grain lodges there.

I lift that toe.

The grain goes on.

That small release gives the body its extent. The forefoot is mine; so is the flank lying crookedly behind it, and the hindlimb pressed against the opposite wall. Farther back, my tail occupies a narrowing I cannot turn to face. Its end is cooler than its root. There is a slight current there that does not pass my mouth.

I would be one of the larger animals, though not large enough yet to fit my chamber easily. There are places where I must stretch. A hindfoot reaches a dependable socket only if my belly leaves the wall, exposing a little more skin to the flow. When I relax, that foot slips out—not a fall, only the loss of a useful contact—and I must set it again.

In time I will fill this place differently.

For now, the arrangement is workable.

The roar would not be something I listened through. It would reach the water, the rock, the softer parts of my head; it would continue through feeding and movement and the long periods when I did neither. I would have no experience of silence with which to separate it from my own condition.

Within it, however, there would be differences.

A pressure along one mouth-thread weakens when the entrance above me fills more deeply. The other thread, lying nearly flat against the stone, receives a faint, uneven dragging. Sometimes this is food. Sometimes a small animal has put a foot into the current and withdrawn it. Sometimes nothing follows.

I would not need to decide what each disturbance meant.

My mouth stays where the water can enter it.

A little material gathers in the combs behind the jaw. I swallow. The movement is slight, but for a moment it changes the pressures around my face. My threads drift inward and then spread again.

This would occupy more of my life than any journey.

There would be animals on me. One works along the edge of an old mineral deposit behind my shoulder, removing something from the border between skin and stone. I feel its movements poorly there. When it reaches a fold, the sensation becomes clearer: several light points advancing, stopping, advancing.

It remains beyond my mouth.

I remain beneath its feet.

I do not think I would be especially ancient, or the last of anything. There could be another of my kind on the far side of the wall against which I rest. Between us might be less limestone than the length of my smallest toe, yet the route from one chamber to the other could require a descent neither of us would survive. Nothing in my experience would establish whether that other animal existed.

The occupied world would not arrive whole.

It would arrive as an edge within reach of a foot, a current accessible to a thread, a constriction measured first by the head and then, less certainly, by the shoulders.

And eventually my familiar place would alter.

Not through a collapse. The water entering against the ceiling would build a low ridge at its margin. The ridge would become just high enough to send a portion of the sheet to the left. It might take longer than a season. At first I would compensate by turning my head. Then by moving the forefoot above it.

The richer seep would still reach me, but less reliably.

I would spend a long time with my mouth open where it had always been.

There is something about this delay that makes the animal more distinctly mine. I would not know the chamber had changed merely because I had begun to fit it badly. I would make small adjustments within the old arrangement. I would extend a thread until its divided tip touched stone. I would brace harder and receive less. The familiar sockets would continue to offer their excellent hold.

Only gradually would I release one of them.

The first reach would take me sideways, toward the entrance too narrow for my head. Near it is a pocket I have touched but never occupied. My foot enters before my face. The underside is rough. There are two purchases, one close and shallow, the other farther in.

I take the closer one.

My body has to bend around a blunt projection. Skin gathers along my inner flank; the outer flank stretches into water that feels unfamiliar along its whole length. For a time, I am held between the old chamber and the pocket, with neither arrangement allowing me to rest.

A thread reaches the diverted sheet.

It presses downstream, firmly enough to pull its branches together.

I bring my mouth toward it.

The new position is less sheltered. Light reaches the skin over one eye, producing no scene, only an exposure that persists when I turn my head. My hindfoot still holds its old socket. To bring that foot forward, I will have to trust a surface I have so far touched only with the end of a toe.

I stay extended.

Water enters the corners of my mouth. Something catches inside, small enough that I do not close my jaw to retain it.

After a while, I swallow.

Then I reach a little farther into the pocket.
