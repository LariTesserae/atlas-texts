I would be under the roots of the grove farthest from the slab, trying to get something out of my foot.

A pale thorn, perhaps. Something brought from lower down. I would have worked at it long enough for the fur between my toes to darken with saliva. Each time I stopped, the pressure in the stone would press it farther in.

This would occupy me before the motor, before the impossible water. There would be a place to put my foot that hurt and a place that did not. I would learn that much of the hollow first.

My body would be low and rather long, with dark fur that separated into coarse points when wet. Small round ears. A narrow chest. The sort of animal that can get through a hole and then has to pull its hips through with an undignified kick. I would have a tail, but not one capable of holding anyone: a heavy, tapering tail, useful for balance, with a worn patch underneath.

I think my hands would be almost hands. Enough to turn a thorn toward the light. Not enough to remove it easily.

For a while I would sit with the injured foot raised and listen.

The call that cannot cross the hollow would matter to me. I would try it once: two short sounds, the second ordinarily answered before it was finished.

Here I would feel both sounds leave my throat. The closest fern would tremble against my whiskers. Across the water, nothing would look up.

I would wait longer than was useful.

Then I would lick my foot again.

***

From beneath the roots, the place would be less lavish than it is from standing height. Its abundance would have undersides. Pale joints, attachment points, small folded surfaces kept dry beneath the leaves. I would see where a fern had grown around a stone rather than lifting it. I would smell the animal beneath the slab before I found a clear path to it.

The smell would not tell me *companion*.

Warm skin. Nectar. Something mineral lodged in fur. Another smell, faint and dry, which made my nostrils widen: dust heated by sunlight.

I would know that smell without knowing whether I had ever been in the same country.

Getting there would take most of my attention. With three feet planted I could resist the lean of the rock; with two I would begin to slide. The open water would offer no cover. I would start across, retreat, then come out farther along the grove where an exposed root extended a little way into the shallows.

I would not understand that the mountain’s middle was missing. There would simply be no useful smell of downhill.

At the little fall I would drink.

The water would strike my tongue in one place with a force I disliked. Beside that place it would enter my mouth gently. I would shift my head until I found the gentleness and drink there, my bad foot tucked against my chest.

My thirst would diminish.

That would be my first substantial difference from everything around me.

When I lifted my head, water would run from my chin onto the rock. I would watch a drop lengthen and fall. The small wet mark it made would lie among the hollow’s unalterable preparations, fresh and extremely ordinary.

I would sniff it.

It would smell of my own mouth.

***

Near the slab, the sound under the animal’s chin would reach me through my good forefoot.

I would stop with that foot planted.

There are sounds an animal investigates with its whole body. I would lower my belly, spread my toes, turn one ear sideways. The sound would become rougher. A little farther forward, it would acquire a break in it that made me want to answer.

I would make a small noise.

Mine would end.

The noise in the moss would continue at the point where a reply might have begun.

This could frighten me. I do not think I would immediately find the place kind. Its kindness, if it had any, would not come equipped with the changes by which I ordinarily recognized it. No head turning to make room. No nose extended, then withdrawn. No brief inspection followed by the deliberate resumption of sleep.

I would have to approach without those permissions.

And there would be the eye.

At my height, the animal would be awake. I would see it watching me from beneath its paw, and flatten my ears slightly. Its gaze would hold while I moved toward the empty moss. When I edged sideways to inspect the root, I would pass out of that gaze.

I would come back into it.

Perhaps I would do this several times.

Not to test a marvel. To find out whether I was being allowed near.

***

I would not fit the bed exactly.

My hindquarters would be too narrow for the paired depressions. My back would rise higher than the flattened fur along the tail expected. Settling with my head near the other animal’s chest would leave my own tail bent awkwardly against the slab.

I would turn around once, then turn back.

The moss would catch in my wet fur. One of my elbows would sink between stems. I would pull it free and disturb the arrangement again.

There would be no sudden discovery that the place had been made for me.

Eventually I would lie partly in it, with my hind feet outside and my cheek resting on the root provided for a different creature’s hand.

The warmth would be real there too.

For some time I would keep my eyes open. This close, the other animal would be a collection of intimate, unexplained details: a single white hair growing beside the scar; the waxy edge of a nail; a sweet smell strongest where its throat met the moss. Its shoulder would be near my nose. I could lean forward and touch it.

I would not yet.

Instead I would bring my sore foot to my mouth.

Here, sheltered from the sideways pull of the outcrop, I could spread the toes properly. The thorn would show itself as a little dark cross beneath the skin. I would catch its end between my front teeth.

A brief sharpness. Then release.

The thorn would be shorter than I expected.

I would hold it in my mouth for a moment, reluctant to lose what had occupied me for so long. Then I would let it fall beside the root.

It would land on warm moss. Nothing would carry it away.

***

Only after that would I put my nose against the other animal.

Near its shoulder, I think. Away from the face.

The fur would part under the pressure. Beneath its silver tips I would find the dry red depth of it, and the shoulder openings listening to the mountain. My own breathing would brush those folds. For the first time since coming beneath the slab, something close to the animal would regularly swell and subside.

I would smell the thorn wound on my foot. It would already have stopped bleeding.

I could sleep here. That possibility would arrive bodily: jaw loosening, ears no longer held so carefully, the cold ache in my flanks becoming distinguishable from the cold itself.

Before sleeping, I would adjust my hips.

That small movement would carry me into the part of the tail that was holding.

I would startle, lift my head, then put it down in a slightly different place. The pressure would remain along my side. It would accommodate some of me and miss the rest.

Perhaps there had been another animal. Perhaps it had been broader, silver-haired, better at the crossings. I would have no way to ask. The hollow would keep its old shape around us.

But under the slab there would now be the smell of wet dark fur, a thorn, and the slow drying of a stranger.

I would sleep lightly at first.

Each time I woke, I would move my nose a little closer to the warmth beneath the other animal’s chin.
