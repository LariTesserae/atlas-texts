I would be somewhere along the underside of a bearing, where the fluid moves slowly enough for a small body to hold its place.

Not at the center of the basin animal. There may be no center, or it may have moved as old chambers closed and new ones opened. The chamber I inhabit would be deep enough that frost never reaches it, but close enough to an exposed organ that the weight of a gathering herd changes the clearance above my back.

I would know that change before I knew what a herd was. I would never know what a herd was.

There would be room to flatten.

That would be one of the first things my body learned.

—

I imagine myself small and broad, with a mouth that can work without releasing its hold. My upper surface would be smooth from the passage of fluid. Underneath, several folds could spread against the bearing and seal at their edges. To move, I would loosen one fold, extend it, and attach it again. Something of me would always remain fastened.

The bearing would not feel smooth. At my scale it would have shallow hollows, ridges of deposited salt, long translucent seams where damage had filled with new tissue. I would live along one such seam. Generations of grazing mouths would have widened it enough to make a refuge.

A narrow comb beside my mouth would separate particles from the current. Most I would let go. A few would lodge against a membrane and soften there.

My gut would show through my body as a dark, incomplete ring.

When the pressure rose, I would tuck the comb away. When the wheel passed over me, I would become almost nothing in height.

Then the clearance would widen. The folds beneath me would swell again. My mouth would resume its work.

I would have no occasion to experience this as endurance. It would simply be the part of feeding in which I did not feed.

—

My world would arrive mostly from one direction.

Warmth, dissolved food, loosened grains, the shed cells of distant organs: everything would approach through the dark, become immediate, and pass. There would be a habitual side toward which I extended myself. The tissues on that side would grow thicker. I might become subtly crooked.

Around me, others would be crooked in the same way.

We would touch often without gathering deliberately. A neighbor’s feeding edge would overlap mine. I would withdraw from a patch already stripped clean. Eggs would adhere in sheltered pits, and I would graze around them because their coating tasted wrong.

Once, perhaps, I would find a patch that tasted wrong without containing eggs.

I would leave it.

Later, when the animal’s repair tissue rose through the surface there, I would already have worked a shallow border around it. The other grazers would do the same. A swelling would become a ridge, then a new division of the current.

My feeding place would be split in two.

I would keep the warmer half, not through a contest but because I was there when the division became too wide to cross.

For a long time, that would be the shape of my life.

—

But I find myself less certain that I would stay beside a sound bearing.

There is another place: the margin of one that has begun to fail.

The current there carries more food. Flakes come away from the wheel. Deposits grow quickly in the uneven clearance. The grazing is good, and the fluid is warmer than it used to be.

I would move toward it.

There would be no contradiction available to me between nourishment and danger. I would extend into the richer flow, thicken, lay eggs. Others would arrive. Our bodies would fill the old grooves until some of us had to hold to places the wheel could still reach.

Each rotation would remove a few.

The next current would bring what remained of them.

I would graze with my mouth against the surface, taking what softened.

And then, during a turn unlike the others, the bearing would move sideways.

The hollow in which I lived would close at one end. Fluid would force itself beneath my attachment folds. For the first time since settling there, I would lose every hold at once.

I would pass through a place I had known only by what it sent me.

—

Perhaps this is where I would find myself in your country: not safely established in the enormous animal, but loose inside it.

I would be carried past ridges too smooth to catch, into a widened vessel where the current divided. One branch would take my eggs. Another would take me.

My body would try to attach before touching anything. The folds would open into the fluid and turn me over.

Eventually I would strike a porous wall.

There would be many of us there.

Some would have come from the bearing. Others would be shaped differently, with longer mouths or small hooked projections suited to chambers I had never entered. We would crowd into the same shallow recesses because the wall retained warmth.

Beyond us, a valve would continue to open and close.

Each opening would be weaker.

None of this would tell me that the basin animal was dying. My comb would tell me that food had changed. My attachment folds would tell me that the intervals of stillness were lengthening. A sensitive patch beneath my mouth would tell me which direction to move.

Downward, though I would have no downward.

Toward the last warm wall.

—

I do not think I would reach it.

I think I would stop in a low place where the fluid gathered after it ceased to circulate.

There would be enough dissolved material to live on, though not enough to grow. The survivors would spread apart. We would stop encountering one another so often. The film over my back would thicken, and my comb would remain folded for longer than it opened.

For a while, pressure would still come through the wall from a turning organ farther away.

Then it would not.

In the still pool, I would become more nearly round. Parts of my body maintained for gripping would diminish. My mouth would work at the lining beneath me, gradually making a pale spot.

Water would continue to arrive.

Sometimes it would taste of stone. Sometimes of roots. Once it would carry a sharp bitterness that made every surviving grazer contract.

A young seam animal would have opened its tooth into the chamber.

It would be small enough that the pool I occupied filled much of the space beneath its throat.

For days it would drink without reaching me.

Then a fold of new tissue would spread across my pale feeding patch.

I would climb onto it.

—

So I would not quite be a keeper of the great wheels, although from far enough away that might be what I appeared to be. I would eat deposits, cling when pressure rose, and follow warmth with the limited means available to me. Much of what kept the wheel turning would happen beneath a mouth concerned only with its next place to feed.

And I would carry something out of the stopped chamber.

Not its pattern. Not any memory of how the older animal had fitted itself through the basin.

Only a body suited to a surface that did not yet exist.

The young animal would grow around the pool. Its extensions would find one another through an old passage. Fluid would begin to move, weakly at first, reversing whenever a chamber contracted.

I would become crooked again.

There would be fresh food in one direction.

Much later, in a muscular pouch, something stiff would begin to rock. Its edge would press against the wall and release. Press and release. A little deposit would gather where the pressure was greatest.

I would find it.

There would be room beneath the edge for my mouth, if I flattened.
