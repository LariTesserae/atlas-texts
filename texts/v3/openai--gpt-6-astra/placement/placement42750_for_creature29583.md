I would live beside a bridge that people crossed without noticing there was nothing beneath it.

My window would be level with the roadway, although my room was on the third floor. To reach the front door I would have to go down two flights, walk through what had once been a shop, and climb an outside stair. There would be a shorter way through a neighbor’s passage, available when we were on good terms.

From the window I could see people’s hands before their faces. Baskets, slates, the soft-sided cases used for carrying instruments. Children dragging their fingers along the parapet until an adult made them stop. I would learn to recognize Sera by her tool roll and by the pause she took before the descending steps.

I think I would begin as someone who worked on maps.

Not the large plans in the pressure offices, with their authoritative colors and dates of approval. Those would come to me already printed. My work would be preparing the copies that went into maintenance carriages and workers’ coat pockets: mounting them on cloth, dividing them into panels, replacing a section when a stair closed or a new inspection opening was cut.

In the shop there would be a press, a trimming knife, and a cabinet whose drawers had to be kept shut. Paper left out overnight would curl toward the warm wall.

I would have opinions about glue.

A customer asking a simple question about glue would sometimes regret it.

***

Most maps brought back for repair would have things written on them.

The public plan might show an access passage forty feet long. On a worker’s copy, the passage would have acquired three annotations:

*Low pipe.*

*Very low pipe.*

*They have not moved the pipe.*

There would be meal times, card scores, measurements without units, names whose purpose nobody could remember. Directions to a freshwater outlet would be drawn more emphatically than the route to the district gate. Someone would have outlined a hand beside a valve, with one finger blackened. I would not know whether this meant a pinch hazard, an injured worker, or something rude about the supervisor.

At first I would preserve all of it.

I would lift the damaged paper in sections, copy whatever could still be read, and lay the old marks back onto the new plan. This would seem like conscientious work. It would also be easier than deciding.

Then a worker would return a map on which I had carefully restored the instruction to collect a key from someone who had been dead for nine years.

“He never had the key anyway,” she would tell me.

After that I would make a separate sheet for doubtful annotations. This would please the office and annoy almost everyone who had to carry one.

***

My own pocket map would be disgraceful.

A good map must open without requiring its user to release a handrail. I would say this often, while struggling with mine.

It would include the passages where I could get out of the noise without buying anything. A vestibule outside a disused weighing office. The small waiting room of a clinic, provided I did not arrive during appointments. A place in the freight exchange where two insulated walls overlapped and a person standing between them could hear the scrape of a sleeve.

I would not have exceptionally good hearing. In one ear there would be a thin, intermittent sound like a spoon drawn around the rim of a bowl. Some mornings I would spend too long looking for the actual spoon.

Nor would I possess an instinct for the works. If an old gatekeeper stopped on a crossing, I would stop because he had stopped. If three people turned toward the uphill lamps, I would turn a little later.

This would embarrass me more than it should.

I would know so many routes through the city and have so little understanding of what was moving under them.

***

Once or twice a week I would take the repaired plans out myself.

This would not be strictly necessary. There would be a delivery service. But folds that worked at the bench sometimes failed on a wet platform, and I would want to see how people used the things.

I would also like being recognized at the lower works.

The keepers there would put a palm against the back of my hand and take the map without stopping what they were doing. One would always examine the reverse first. She used the blank panels for sums, and considered every increase in printed information an encroachment.

It would be her map that changed the work for me.

She would bring it in with a whole corner gone soft. There would be a spillway on that corner, an inspection ledge, and a short flight of steps. Beside the steps, in pale pencil, someone would have drawn five little strokes.

I would mistake them for damage.

The replacement would be handsome: clear figures, a reinforced fold, the ledge restored from the official plan. I would put it under the press overnight and deliver it on my next round.

The keeper would open it, look at the corner, and ask where her marks had gone.

I would offer to put them back if she could tell me what they meant.

She would look at me for a while before answering.

“Leave that ledge alone.”

There would be no reproach in the sign itself. Just an instruction, made with the hand not holding the map.

I would explain that if the ledge was unsafe, there was a proper symbol.

She would sign that it was not unsafe.

Then, because I was still standing there with my pencil ready, she would take me down to it.

***

I would not see a ledge-runner that day.

We would descend until the stairs disappeared behind a sheet of water and continued, unexpectedly, into a dry recess. The keeper would turn sideways through the opening. I would follow badly, catch my coat, and have to back out before trying again.

Inside, the air would be hotter. The water would make the daylight unsteady. There would be room for two people only if neither objected to the other’s knee.

She would point to a shallow place in the stone.

Nothing would be in it except a fragment of pale shell, a little dust, and something that might have been a thread.

I would lean closer.

She would touch my wrist.

We would stand there long enough that my eyes began to separate the recess into smaller places: the narrow shelf above the hollow, the dry joint behind it, a dark opening no wider than my thumb. The wall would not be smooth. I had been thinking of it as a wall because that was what I knew how to put on paper.

When we came out, she would show me the inspection route. It passed directly across the hollow. There was another foothold, slightly awkward, within easy reach.

She would want five strokes beside the steps. Nothing more.

I would put them back.

***

For a while afterward I would become tiresome in a new way.

I would ask customers what every mark meant.

Some would tell me. Some would forget. Some would plainly invent answers because it amused them to watch me write them down.

Sera would bring in a plan of an old tenement and find me trying to determine whether a circle indicated a hatch, a stool, or a reliable place to leave a cup.

“Have you asked whose map it is?” she would say.

I would have asked which department.

“That isn’t what I asked.”

The map would belong to a man who held it upside down when approaching from the west. The circle would be where he turned it over.

There would be no general system I could make from this.

I would try anyway, for several months.

***

Away from the bench, I would be less patient.

I would dislike having to negotiate the neighbor’s passage. I would buy fruit before checking whether I had enough dry space to keep it. I would possess a shallow cupboard full of almost useful paper, despite warning customers that paper stored against an outer wall eventually became part of it.

I would have a sister in another district who thought my visits too infrequent and too long.

At her table I would talk readily. In the street I would sometimes lose the thread of a conversation because I had looked away at the wrong moment. Then I would nod as though I had understood, and be caught when asked a question.

I might be the sort of person who found it easier to restore someone’s handwriting than to ask them to repeat themselves.

That would be something to work on.

***

Eventually there would be two presses in the shop.

One for clean plans. One for plans whose owners wanted them to remain recognizably theirs.

The second would be used more.

I would learn to mend a fold without strengthening it so much that it resisted the hand accustomed to opening it. I would leave a thumb-dark patch visible if it helped someone find the right panel. An obsolete stair might remain as a faint outline, crossed through but legible, because people still gave directions from it.

When an annotation could not be read, I would not improve it into certainty.

Sometimes I would give the map back unfinished.

“Show me,” I would sign.

And we would go out.

***

I see myself most clearly on one of those visits, late in the day, beneath the bridge outside my rooms.

Someone has reported that the band of suspended spray has moved. The old plan puts it through the middle of a doorway. Now the doorway is dry, and the droplets hang farther out, above the second step.

I have brought a rule, a slate, and a small piece of dark card to hold behind the water. Against it, the individual drops become visible. They tremble without arriving anywhere.

A child stops to watch. She asks what I am doing.

I give her the card to hold.

This turns out to be a mistake because she discovers that moving it up and down makes the band appear and disappear. For a few minutes she is unwilling to assist with anything else.

Then she holds it steady. I take the measurement.

She wants to know whether I will change the map tonight.

Tomorrow, I tell her.

She considers this, gives me back the card, and puts her hand into the spray. Water gathers on her fingers. She lowers her hand and watches the drops fall from it in the ordinary way.

Upstairs, my supper is in a covered bowl. There are three maps under the press and a letter I have postponed answering. Someone crosses the bridge above us, their passage visible in a little shaking of the water.

The child asks me to hold the card for her.

I put down the rule.
