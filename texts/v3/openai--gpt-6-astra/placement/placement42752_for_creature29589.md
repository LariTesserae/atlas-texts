I would first look for myself beside the screw.

Not because it is the center of anything, but because the threads offer a way to stay. There is shelter there that does not have to be made by the body using it. I could enter one groove, spread along its spiral, and have the water reach me without taking hold of my whole surface. When the sun came, I could become less exposed by degrees.

But the screw is already disappearing. The shelter and the thing closing the shelter are arriving together. After a while I would have to pass through an opening smaller than the part of me that had learned to live there.

I think I would be farther out, under the unfinished curb, where an attachment has recently failed.

There would be no wound visible from above. Only a filament no longer held at both ends, swept against the apron by the falling sheet. Its free end would sometimes touch stone. Each contact would last too briefly to establish anything. I would be in that interval for some time: held somewhere behind me, unable to hold where the water kept putting me.

The pump would not be a sound. It would be the condition under which my edges stayed separate. A certain pressure would pass through the film and I would gather; it would pass again and I would loosen. Between those motions, water would enter. I would have no occasion to distinguish the taking-in from the motion that allowed it.

What reached one mouth would not necessarily reach another. Near the concrete there would be something usable, and farther down the same fold there would be very little. I would thicken unevenly. A part of me would go on reaching after the part beside it had closed.

From the entrance, this would look like nothing happening.

I might remain attached for another day. Enough mineral might collect on the free end to weight it against the apron, giving it the contact it lacked. Then I would establish a small opening there, and the cord between would become a route used in both directions. Or the drop from the bracket would strike it.

I find myself following the drop.

After it fell, I would be the piece below the break. The rest would continue beneath the curb. There would be no general withdrawal, no pause through which the loss could be recognized. Water would still arrive at those mouths. Mine would be turned against a dark leaf, opening into a space almost too narrow for water to pass.

For several days that leaf would be my ground.

It would flex when rainwater crossed the pool. Its edges would be held down, but the middle would lift a little, carrying me with it. Underneath, the brown would be changing. Above, a white rim would be making the leaf less capable of movement. I could attach to either surface without being able to know which would remain available longer.

Here I would begin to be something slightly different from what lived beneath the curb. Not a new kind, necessarily. Just folded more closely, opening along a shorter edge. Fed from one side. Pressed intermittently from the other.

And then, in another rain, I would reach the hollow beneath the bracket.

That is where I would stay, I think. Not comfortably, and not as a conclusion to the movement. It is simply the place in which the next small attachment holds.

The hollow would be much larger than me. A falling drop would change its whole depth. The underside of the bracket would gather another, but I would have no access to that preparation. There would be pressure, spreading cold, and then the slow thinning of water around my fold.

I would spend most of the interval closed.

There would still be differences inside that closure. The outer edge would become firmer. A passage near the bottom would remain wet after the upper surface no longer shone. What could withdraw would withdraw toward it. A few grains caught in the fold would not come free.

Then warm overflow would arrive along the paving.

First it would wet one attachment. The change would travel through me unevenly. An edge that had seemed sealed would lift, not far enough at first to admit a current. Water would stand against it. Another fold would loosen underneath, and the first opening would widen.

I would not regain the shape I had beneath the curb. Some of that shape would now be stone there. Some would still be alive, receiving water I no longer received.

Here, my rim would open crookedly around the grains I could not shed. One side would reach farther than the other. A short fringe would settle against the hollow’s floor, and for as long as the overflow lasted, I would feed.

Above me, the next drop would be growing.
