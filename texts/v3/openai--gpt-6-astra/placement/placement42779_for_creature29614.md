I would want a room near one of the places where a walkway changes its mind.

A straight run from the apartments, then a turn around a pool, then three steps up to a higher shelf. People would have to slow down outside my window. Someone carrying a crate would rest it on the broad corner of the railing. Children would take the steps in one jump until they were carrying something breakable.

From inside, I could hear the pause as well as the arrival.

The room would be small, with an inconvenient drain beneath the table. I would own a red cup and more cloths than visitors thought reasonable. In the dry box: a letter, spare spectacles, a paper packet of needles. Perhaps something from below, though I think it would be an ordinary thing—a wooden clothes peg, worn smooth where a thumb pressed it. Something whose continued dryness required a little effort.

At first I would expect to work with the ledgers. There would be comfort in a task with a route, a shelf for its finished pages, other people’s observations available for comparison. I could learn the names of pools. I could become careful about what I claimed to know.

But I think I would keep stopping at the joints in the walkways.

The hard stone ends. A narrow strip of something dark crosses the path. Beyond it, another length of paving begins, supported separately. A footstep reaches the strip and loses most of itself.

Someone has to maintain that interruption.

That would become my work.

***

The joints are not all made alike.

In the older walks, the gap is packed with layers of woven cord held between ceramic cheeks. Newer sections use imported sheets cut into narrow strips. There are joints built so that a loaded trolley can cross them, and others where the trolley must be unloaded first. There are repairs that have lasted twenty years because the water happens to pass on the other side, and excellent new fittings that begin to stiffen within a season.

Mineral crust grows over everything. Left alone, it bridges the soft strip. The walkway becomes continuous again.

Usually someone notices because an animal moves.

A channel that once remained occupied through the morning shift empties whenever the screen tenders come past with their trays. Or the tapping below a bank stops at the same hour each day. The route walker makes a note. Someone checks the temperature. Someone checks the water.

Eventually I arrive with a narrow saw, wedges, a rolled mat for my knees, and a bag of fittings that look too small to justify the trouble of bringing them.

I put one hand on each side of the joint.

My companion walks toward me.

There are ways to measure the difference, and I use them. But before fetching the instruments I like to feel what brought us here.

Sometimes the fault is obvious: a white thread of stone as hard as a tooth, joining two surfaces meant to remain apart.

Sometimes the whole fitting has to come out.

Then people complain.

They have good reasons. The other route adds twelve minutes. A woman has a sore ankle. Freight is expected. The temporary plank knocks under every foot, which seems a particularly stupid arrangement while I am supposedly making the place quieter.

I learn to give an honest finishing time. I learn to add the time it takes to discover that the screw beneath the cover has become one object with the cover.

I am not naturally patient with this.

There is a chipped cup in my room because, once, after a bad afternoon, I put it down harder than I needed to.

***

For several years I think of the work as stopping vibration.

Then a woman who has spent most of her life tending the west channels comes to inspect a repair.

She waits while two workers cross. The animals stay where they are. Nothing taps against my testing plate.

I am pleased.

She puts the heel of her hand against the bank.

“Did you cut this side too?”

There had been a second bridge of deposited stone, farther down, joining the channel lip to an old foundation. I had removed it while the tools were out.

She looks along the water. On the far side, beneath an overhang, one of the broad-headed animals shifts its feet.

“That’s where they answer from,” she says.

We wait. I cannot tell whether anything is trying to answer.

The piece I removed is lying among the other fragments in my tray. There is no useful way to put it back.

I record exactly what I cut. For the next month, on my way home, I go around by the west channels. Some evenings the woman is there. She does not reassure me, and she does not make a lesson of it.

Eventually the animals gather a little farther upstream.

We never establish how much my work mattered.

After that, I become slower about improvements outside the job I came to do.

***

There would be other things in my life.

I would mend the knees of my trousers badly, with strong thread and impatient stitches. I would be good at cooking beans and poor at judging how much anybody wanted to eat. A neighbor would send her youngest child to borrow tools, knowing I found it harder to refuse the child. Eventually the child would become old enough to find this embarrassing and begin returning the tools without being asked.

On free afternoons I might sit beside a warm outflow and clean my saws.

A channel animal would begin using the space next to my mat. It would be missing the tip of one foot. I would resist giving it a name because I had heard too many arguments about whether a familiar animal was still the same animal.

Then, one day, someone would put a tool bag down there.

“Move that,” I would say. “That’s Short Toe’s place.”

The name would stay.

I would tell people it was a description.

They would let me.

***

As I grew older, I would take on an apprentice who wanted to finish each repair in a single visit.

She would have better hands than mine. She could ease a corroded pin out of its socket without snapping it, and she would make no comment when I failed to do the same. I would find this more irritating than a comment.

For a while, I would teach her too much.

Every place would arrive burdened with something I remembered. Every tool would have a caution attached. She would listen, but one morning I would notice that she had stopped touching the stone before looking at me.

So I would begin staying home for the first inspection.

She would return wet to the elbows, sometimes certain, sometimes annoyed.

“What did it feel like?” I would ask.

At first she would answer with the words I used.

Later she would find others.

One joint would feel crowded, she would say. Another would let the light footsteps through more sharply than the heavy ones. At the turn below the pumping house, she would ask me to come and put my hand down because she could not describe the trouble.

We would go together.

I would be useful there without necessarily being right.

***

During the annual shutdown, I would not go looking for the deepest silence.

I would sit outside my room, where the walkway bends. With the pumps stopped, unfamiliar little sounds would reach the window: a sleeve brushing a wall, someone rinsing a bowl behind shutters, the settling of a cable cart farther away than I could usually hear it.

Below the steps, the animals would begin to tap.

Some years I would put my palm down. Some years my knees would hurt and I would stay in the chair.

There would be no need for the moment to become anything more.

Soon enough, someone would come around the corner with a crate and find me in the way. I would move my chair. They would rest their load on the railing, shake out their hands, and stand beside me until they were ready to carry it again.
