I would be behind one of the repairs, where a brown brick has been laid on its side to make up the height of a thinner stone.

Not in the chamber at first. In the bedding beneath it.

There is a shallow depression in the brick, made before firing by something dragged across the clay. Grit occupies most of it. Along one side, where the pressure is less, a little water passes. I would occupy the other side: a pale thickening with nowhere to hang loose.

The brick above and the broken tile below would not be separate objects to me. There would be the broad, steady pressure of one surface, the rougher pressure of the other, and between them a place through which my substance could continue.

I would have no way to know that the brick was on its side. Its upper face would be the face against which I grew.

At first I imagine myself confined to that groove. It is small enough to seem like a body’s proper extent. One end narrows beneath a grain of quartz; the other opens into the bedding. There could be a folded chamber just beyond it, tucked against the curved inside of a potsherd. Water would enter, lose a little of what it carried, and leave.

But the groove would not end where I ended.

A film would extend beyond the potsherd. Some of it would be dead, still held in place by the grit. Some would turn downward between two fragments of tile. Farther on, beneath a strip of dark wood, it would become living tissue thick enough to have passages of its own.

I would find myself there too.

Not by traveling. Nothing would have left the first place.

Cold would reach one part sooner than another. A little nourishment would arrive along the wood while the folded chamber near the brick received almost none. Pressure would close a passage above, and below, for a long time, there would be no corresponding change.

Whatever counted as my present would be spread unevenly through the wall.

I would not possess the wall’s shape. No inward terrace would stand assembled in me. The distance between two portions of my body might contain a courtyard, a collapsed drain, or only the thickness of a hand. I could not compare them. There would be stretches of adequate water, places growing colder, a surface becoming crowded by a root. There would be the uninterrupted compression beneath a slab that had not shifted for many winters.

That last place might account for most of me.

It would be easy, from outside, to overlook it in favor of the folds and their firmer lobes. Yet I think I would be most fully there: spread thin beneath a weight, with little happening along my margins. The shape of the stone would have entered my shape so gradually that there would be no remaining distinction between growing and fitting.

Then one season the pressure would alter.

Not enough to crack the brick. Not enough to lift silt from the paving. Somewhere beyond me, clay would have escaped from a joint.

Along one edge, the stone would become heavier.

My passages there would narrow. Water that had crossed beneath it would find another way or cease to pass. A little tissue would thicken in the newly opened space at the opposite edge. The change would proceed slowly enough that the part being crushed could remain alive while the part released from pressure began to grow.

I would be both.

There would be no retreat of the whole creature, no decision made in a chamber and carried outward. Some folds would close. Others would deepen. A thin connection beneath the quartz grain would persist after it had ceased to supply much of anything.

Eventually it would break.

The portion beneath the potsherd would continue receiving water. The portion along the wood would continue receiving water. Between them, a short length of pale material would soften and discolor.

If I had called both portions myself, I would now have to remain with both, or admit that the word had been doing something the body never did.

Neither portion would become an exile.

Afterward, I might be smaller. A patch beneath three pieces of rubble, with one folded cavity and a narrow outlet. Winter would reach nearly all of me at once. There would no longer be a lower part still feeding in steadier water.

Or I might be the portion along the wood, continuing beneath the paving to places the break scarcely affected.

I cannot find a reason to choose between them.

There is another possibility farther inside the repair. Beneath the end of the timber, my margin would meet a margin already there.

The two pale edges would thicken. Between them would remain a line of water, so narrow that a suspended grain could obstruct it. Each side would grow against the same wood, receive what passed through the same joint, endure the same small shifts of weight.

For years we might remain separate.

I would not know the other as a creature. It would be a surface that yielded differently from clay, that sometimes advanced where clay would wash away. Along that contact my own growth would change. There would be a limit without stone in it.

I think this is where I would finally place myself: along that unsettled margin, neither at the oldest center of the body nor its newest reach.

The timber would be softening. A root above it would have died, leaving a passage through which slightly colder water now descended. One side of the contact would receive more of that water. The other would lie under greater compression.

Perhaps the margins would join. Perhaps the water between them would remain.

Nothing visible in the channel would settle the question. The facing would still hold. Moss would cover its outer surface. A leaf caught on the curb would lose its blade and leave a short piece of stem.

Inside, the grain obstructing the seam would shift.

For a while, water would pass along a part of my edge that had not received it before. The tissue there would become a little fuller. It would extend beneath the wood, beside the other pale margin, into the space the grain had left.
