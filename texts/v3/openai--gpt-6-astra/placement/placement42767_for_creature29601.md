I would be under the staircase, where its lowest flight passes close above a pipe. There is a wedge of space there that cannot be seen from the road. Rain reaches it by several routes, but never all at once. Even in a downpour, one corner stays dry.

At first I think I would be something left in that corner: a folded sack, perhaps, with the fold worn white. Something repeatedly taken up and put down farther from the water. The usefulness of it would have outlasted whatever it was brought here to do.

But there would be warmth beneath the fold. There would be an elbow.

I would be one of the animals, asleep with a strip of sack drawn under my chest.

Not the animal that ventures farthest toward the discharge. I would know the route there, and sometimes use it, but I would feed more often beside the second pipe support, where a shallow trickle passes through broken brick. Small snails collect on the downstream faces. They can be lifted with the edge of a lip. It takes a long time to eat enough there.

My left hand would have one crooked finger. It would have healed that way before I reached my full size, after a fall or a shifting piece of iron. I would have no recollection that distinguished those possibilities. On a flat surface the finger would barely matter. On the underside of a pipe, I would have to turn the wrist slightly before taking my weight.

That small adjustment would be part of every journey.

***

The staircase is occupied above and below.

Above, workers pass with their feet set flat and their hands occasionally sliding along the rail. Below, we move from grip to grip. The stair vibrates before a boot comes into view. Someone carrying something heavy makes it settle differently; someone standing still halfway up can keep us waiting in the dark.

I know several people by what happens to the staircase.

One comes down quickly but pauses on the last tread. A little loose grit falls. Another steps over that tread altogether, swinging a boot through the space beside our entrance. This person has once caught a trouser cuff on a projecting reed and pulled a considerable piece of the nest loose.

We brought most of it back.

My sleeping place is not at the center of the pile. It is along the concrete, in a passage that bends before reaching the outside. I lie with my back pressed to the beam and another animal’s feet against my throat. Sometimes the feet twitch. Sometimes I wake because their owner has gone and cold air has entered the space they occupied.

The nest smells different at each depth. At the entrance there is iron, rain, and the sharpness of fresh mud. Farther in there are old meals, dried grass, the dense smell of bodies. In a sealed chamber below us, bedding has gone sour. We no longer open that way.

I have helped close it. I pulled fibers across the narrow part while another animal pushed from inside, then withdrew through the upper passage. For a while the blocked entrance breathed with changes in the wind. Eventually it stopped.

There are places within the nest that I helped build and can no longer enter.

***

Outside, I keep low.

My body is not especially graceful on the road. The dry strip is coarse and exposed, and I cross it in a hurry, belly lifted, wrists bending awkwardly beneath me. In the shallows I can stop anywhere. Water holds the weight that makes me clumsy on gravel.

I would enjoy feeding.

That seems important. I would not live here only as a receiver of warnings, an instrument for the workers, or a small custodian of the rising ground. There would be the particular satisfaction of finding a seam of shrimp under a board. The board lifts slightly when I push beneath it. My lips reach into the space; something flicks against them. I press farther.

For several minutes I get almost everything right.

Then another animal arrives and puts its muzzle against my side.

I am full enough to admit it, but I hesitate. There is still food under the far edge, and it has broad forefeet well suited to holding the board up. I let it come alongside. We work toward one another until our faces meet beneath the wood. It jerks back first.

Later, near the overflow, I would drive the same animal away.

The basin offers no rule that settles this in advance.

***

I know the pumps through the places where I rest.

Under my jaw, the second support carries a fine shivering above the heavier beat. The staircase adds a rattle. A loose piece of metal nearby sometimes joins in and sometimes does not. When it stops, I lift my head. If everything else continues, I put it down again.

A change can reach me while I am asleep.

I wake with my teeth slightly apart. The animal beside me has lifted its head too. For a moment neither of us moves. Then it steps across my neck and goes to the entrance.

Outside, the water appears unchanged.

I follow it as far as the pipe saddle. We wait there with several others, our bodies pressed into hollows in the concrete. A worker crosses the platform. We feel the footfalls separately from the new roughness in the pipe.

There is no message I could give them. My unease has a direction: away from that support, away from the bed it feeds. If I leave, it is because staying has become difficult.

Perhaps the worker sees us. Perhaps the bearing is checked for some other reason. By evening the familiar vibration has returned, though the casing smells of hot oil and a rag has been left somewhere we cannot reach.

I go back to the broken brick and eat.

***

What would make this particular animal mine is a habit at the upper entrance.

When young are being moved, I would tend to stop there.

I might have arrived with a mouthful of bedding and found someone coming upward beneath me. There is no room to pass. I back into the chamber, release the grass, and turn. A wet adult pushes its head through the opening with a young one hanging from its mouth.

I take the young by the loose skin above the shoulders.

Its weight is slight, but it moves while I am finding my grip. One folded hand catches against my lip. I stand still until it releases me, then carry it around the bend to the warm part of the nest.

On the way back I meet the next one.

After that I remain near the entrance. The crooked finger fits around a ridge in the concrete. I can brace there without obstructing the passage. Adults arriving from below put their forefeet on either side of mine. Water runs from their bellies onto the old sill.

I receive what they bring.

Not always young. Once the mouth advancing out of the dark contains only a tightly twisted length of sack. I pull it free and drag it inward. It catches on something. While I am working it loose, another animal arrives, and for a moment we block one another, both gripping opposite ends of the passage.

There is shoving. Someone bites my shoulder, not hard.

I move.

By the time the lowest chamber is under water, the nursery above is restless and crowded. Bedding lies in lumps where it has been dropped. I push into the pile and am pushed out again. Eventually I find room along the beam.

My fur dries slowly.

***

The ground comes up beneath us.

I notice it when a familiar drop becomes a short step. I notice when a route under the pipe no longer admits my shoulders, and I must climb over a flange instead. A patch where I once swam becomes a place to lie and work my mouth through the sediment.

One morning my crooked finger touches mud while I am hanging from the old sill.

I draw the hand up and clean it. Then I lean down and touch again.

Above me, an animal is pulling fresh fibers into the gap beside the staircase. I can hear the stems catching, slipping, catching. A worker begins descending, and the whole structure shakes. We both withdraw until the boots have passed.

Afterward I climb toward the new opening.

It is still narrow. My shoulders rub against damp grass; loose silt falls along my spine. Inside, there is room to turn, though only just. I press my flank against the lining and push. Something gives a little.

I turn once more.

That is where I would be: making enough room for my body, then lying down in it, while below my chin another animal’s long wet hand comes feeling through the entrance.
