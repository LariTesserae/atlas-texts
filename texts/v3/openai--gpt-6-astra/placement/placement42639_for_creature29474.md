At first I would find myself somewhere the water had failed to come.

There would be a slope above me, bright almost all day, and below me a shallow enclosure with a clean floor. No stains in its corners. No softened edges. Once, something carrying moisture in the hairs beneath its body would cross the enclosure, and the trace it left would keep me extended until evening.

Most of the time I would lie close to the base of the wall.

Close would not quite describe it. There would be no space between us in which to move.

When a shadow passed, I would shorten. A little cloudiness would gather around me, then thin again. The wall would remain still enough that an animal standing on it need not adjust its feet.

I would have no view of the valve. What reached me would have passed through the floor, through old roofs and loose grit and the rims of other basins. The pipe’s morning tick would arrive clearly. The smaller changes before a bead fell would arrive altered by everything in their way.

Sometimes I would begin to thicken along my upper edge.

Sometimes nothing would follow.

***

There are disadvantages to living in a mistake.

The animals cross a dry wall carelessly. They stop on its summit to work at their plates. A grain dropped from a mouth can lodge against one face for days, keeping a small portion of it shaded. Beneath that grain I would remain contracted while the rest of me lengthened.

Eggs might be laid below me. Their dampness would draw me down, slowly, into the foot of the ridge.

I would have no means of distinguishing that dampness from the first approach of water.

A pale lip would begin to form over the eggs.

Before it reached them, a grazer would come and scrape it away.

The scraping would travel through me as a series of separations. Parts that had been continuous would cease to answer together. There would be a newly exposed surface, suddenly warm, and at its edge a loss too small to contract away from.

The animal would feed for a while. Then it would turn, leaving two droppings and a shallow groove.

In the groove, overnight, I would become continuous again.

Not everything taken would return. My lower edge would remain uneven. Where I had once extended toward six small sources of dampness, there would now be five.

***

The break that freed me would happen in full light.

A broad white grazer would climb onto the ridge with a plate loose at the back of its house. The plate would catch. The animal would pull forward, stop, and rock from side to side.

A thin piece of the wall would come away beneath it.

For the first time, daylight would enter me from the wrong direction.

The fragment would fall with my old lower edge uppermost. Inside it I would contract into two cloudy lengths. One end would be open to the air.

The white grazer would feel along the break with its mouth. For several moments it would scrape, removing a softness from the exposed chambers. Then a shadow would cross the hollow, and the animal would withdraw beneath its plates.

When it moved on, the fragment would turn under a rear foot and slide into the passage beneath the step.

There I would remain.

The floor would be warm on one face, the air cooler on the other. Traffic would touch me and pass. Once I would be lifted at a corner, tested, and dropped.

Very little of me would be left by then. Enough to gather behind the unbroken face when light reached the passage. Enough to stiffen the exposed end, sealing it badly.

***

The hatchling would approach from the narrow side.

Its mouth would move over places that had never been touched before. It would find the sealed end, work at it, and stop. Then its forelegs would reach around the fragment.

I would rise.

Only slightly. The broad end would drag, catching on grit. The animal would pull until its feet slipped, remain still, and pull again.

Under the empty roof it would try to turn.

My longest edge would strike the entrance. The impact would reach the surviving chambers all at once, and I would shorten so abruptly that a minute crack opened across the tip.

The hatchling would lose its hold.

For a while there would be only the warmth of the floor.

Then its mouth would return to the crack.

It would eat what had softened there. It would turn the fragment over. This time, when it pulled, the broken tip would pass beneath the roof.

***

I would not become its house immediately.

For most of that first day I would be an obstruction beside it. The hatchling would climb onto me, climb down, shelter under the raised edge. It would feed on the grit pressed against my underside.

Later it would push beneath me and carry me three body lengths before tipping over.

The next attempt would last longer.

Something wet would touch one face repeatedly. When it dried, the animal’s movements would reach me even when its feet were still: a small lengthening beneath the mineral, a tightening, the work of its mouth.

I would learn the pipe again through that body.

Its contact with the ground would be intermittent. A warning that once spread through a whole wall would now arrive through six feet, or four, or none. When the hatchling climbed a loose flake, the signals would diminish. When it rested against the exposed elbow, they would become so strong that I would begin to whiten along both edges.

A new growth would catch against the back of its head.

The hatchling would spend a long time rubbing it beneath a ledge.

By evening the growth would be gone.

***

There would be water, eventually.

Not the arrival for which the old wall had formed. A film beneath a grazer’s empty house, reached by a circuitous passage through shed scales.

The hatchling would lower itself into it.

Water would enter the seam between us. My two cloudy lengths would swell toward one another. Across the old break, something fine would join.

For a little while I would extend farther than the plate could show.

Then the animal would climb out, carrying the dampness into the light.

***

As it grew, other fragments would be fitted around me.

A rust-colored side piece. A thick white rear plate, replaced after it proved too heavy. A sliver with a blue face that would spend two mornings being turned and wetted before finally remaining in place.

I would lie at the center, small and partly covered.

There would still be errors. Before a vibration, I might tighten against the animal’s back. During a long dry interval, a rim might begin to rise where it made passage difficult. The grazer would scrape what it could reach and wear down the rest against familiar stone.

Sometimes my lower edge would hold a little water after the hairs beneath its body had dried.

Smaller animals would follow us.

At the notch beside the amber channel, my carrier would stop among several others. Their mouths would work against the fresh mineral. A loose grain would be passed, dropped, recovered.

Through its feet I would feel the bead gathering.

Within my oldest chamber, the cloudy thread would draw close against the roof.

The grazer would brace itself and bite again.
