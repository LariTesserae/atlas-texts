I think I would live a little inland from the rim, in a shelter whose doorway does not quite face the sunset.

For a few days each year, light would reach the back wall. The rest of the time it would stop somewhere on the floor. I would keep that part of the floor clear, though I would be untidy elsewhere.

Beneath the bench there would be mineral fragments, a cracked shade from a planting cup, several pieces I had meant to return to someone. Dust would collect in the places my cleaning limbs could not comfortably reach. Visitors would notice. Sometimes they would clean a little while we talked, and I would move something in front of them.

I would be one of the plated inhabitants. Not especially old, but old enough to have stopped expecting every part of my covering to match.

A white piece along my rear margin would be slightly too thick. It would conduct charge well and interfere with a neighboring plate whenever I turned sharply. There would be a practiced sequence for freeing it: lift, draw in two supports, move sideways a fraction, lower. I would do this without attending to it, except when someone was watching.

The soft parts of me would fit closely beneath all that stone.

Outdoors, I would have the ordinary low view: nearby grains distinct, the horizon unobstructed, almost nothing between. Someone approaching would first be a disturbance in the ground’s charge, then an intermittent dark edge. I would know several neighbors by the timing of their contact with the stone before I could see their coverings.

I would not be able to identify everyone. Sometimes I would send an intimate greeting to a stranger.

There would be a way to withdraw it politely, and I would have learned it well.

***

My work would involve shelters, though I would not understand everything they did.

I could find where their foundations met the shallow conducting seams. I could distinguish a poor contact from a sound one, choose a mineral sliver, work it into a socket under pressure. On some days this would be enough to restore the density of the air in a room.

Other days I would finish the same procedure and nothing would change.

Then I would remain with my limbs inserted, waiting for a response that might be too slow for me to recognize. I would become hungry. One side of my body would ache. Eventually I would mark the place and leave.

There would be people who were better at knowing when to leave.

What interested me most would happen after the work, when I entered the room and opened my breathing membranes.

Every shelter would give my voice back differently.

In one, a low note would seem to gather beneath my body. In another, a rough overtone would persist near the bench after I had ceased making it. A room far inland would swallow nearly everything, although the air inside was dense and its foundations answered every test.

I would have visited that room more often than the work required.

Once, I would have persuaded a companion to travel there with me. We would spend most of the walk passing a familiar, comfortable exchange through the ground. Inside, I would produce the notes I had been saving.

My companion would listen, try two notes of their own, and ask whether we had come because something was broken.

I would say no.

They would stay with me until evening anyway.

***

Under my bench, mixed with the useful fragments, there would be pieces selected for the sounds they made.

A narrow strip would hum when held between two fine limb tips. Another would produce a dry little pulse if I flexed it, always softer than I expected. A shallow disk would make no sound itself, but would alter my voice when held near an open membrane.

I would lose this disk for a season.

During that time I would try to replace it. I would bring home too many fragments, neglect one planting cup, and annoy a traveler by repeatedly examining a plate they had no intention of giving away.

When I finally found the disk beneath a shed piece of my own armor, I would use it once and put it aside.

The substitutes would have become interesting.

I would not call these objects instruments in a category distinct from tools. The strip that hummed might also be the best piece for clearing a narrow salt channel. I would clean it carefully afterward. When someone needed a conducting flake, I would sometimes surrender a sound I liked.

Sometimes I would claim the fit was unsuitable and keep it.

***

There would be a particular room near the rim that I could not repair.

It would have no bench. The depression in its floor would hold a little powder, undisturbed because the air no longer slowed it properly. Beyond the opening, one of the detached slabs would hang close enough for its underside to hide a portion of the storms.

I would go there carrying fragments beneath my skirt.

For a long time I would believe that I was still testing the room.

I would touch the same places, try small changes, wait with my plates parted. Once the floor would answer with a strong, clean sequence. I would hurry outside and summon someone more experienced, holding the contact in my memory all the while.

By the time they arrived, it would be gone.

We would spend the afternoon trying to reproduce it. They would not accuse me of a mistake. That would make my embarrassment worse.

Afterward, I would leave the room alone for many days.

But eventually I would return without repair material.

There would be enough air near the deepest part of the floor to make a small sound. Not enough for a full voice. I would have to lower myself, open only one membrane, and let the note leave slowly.

Sometimes I could hear it.

Sometimes the only evidence would be the movement in my own flesh.

Outside, dust would cross the gap to the suspended slab. Its curved underside would brighten with lightning from below. I would stay until the cold began to make folding difficult, then hurry home, irritated with myself.

***

A young one would discover what I kept beneath the bench.

It would be visiting with an adult who had come to discuss a foundation fault. While we exchanged measurements through the floor, small limbs would work among my fragments.

The first sound would be accidental: two strips sliding across each other.

I would interrupt the conversation so abruptly that the adult would flatten.

Then we would all remain still.

The young one, whose eye was extended almost against the strips, would scrape them together again.

I would ask to try.

For several visits we would fail to recover that first sound. The angle mattered, and the pressure, and perhaps a grain that had since fallen away. I would keep changing pieces. The young one would keep changing the movement.

Eventually it would produce something quite different: an uneven series of clicks that became a brief vibration when made quickly.

It would repeat this whenever it came to my room.

I would answer with the humming strip.

Our timing would be poor at first. My longer tone would cover its quieter sounds. When I tried to leave room, I would leave too much, and it would reach over to touch me, checking whether I was still attending.

I would learn to begin while it was still moving.

***

That is where I become most definite to myself: in the room, with my armor loosened and one troublesome plate propped beside the bench.

I have work to finish. There is a seam inland that needs another visit, and a rosette cup outside whose shade has tilted. A traveler has brought a piece of violet mineral that I want to examine, though I suspect they are tired of being asked.

The young one is no longer very young. Its new covering has changed the reach of its limbs, and it is learning the strips again.

We are making the uneven sound.

An arrival touches the threshold. Through the floor comes a greeting, then a question about whether the room has space.

I answer. I draw in the objects nearest the depression.

The newcomer enters and begins the slow unfolding that none of us can do safely outside. We continue for a little while, more quietly. When their breathing pockets have filled, they give a low note with a rough edge.

I stop too soon, thinking they mean to speak.

They make it again.

This time I keep going.
