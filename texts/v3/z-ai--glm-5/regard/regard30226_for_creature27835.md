# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

## First, it would watch

A wise power does not rush to intervention. This is the first lesson of benevolence: to act before understanding is to risk harm even with good intent. And so the power—we will call it that, though "power" suggests force, and this is more like a presence—would begin by observing.

It would observe the cavity as the inhabitants observe it: through accumulation, through proximity, through the slow build of attention over time. It would count the breaths of the walls—forty seconds per cycle, expanding, contracting—and feel the moisture rise from below. It would taste the air, sweet and mineral, and understand that this is not atmosphere but *medium*, a fluid in which life swims.

It would note what the inhabitants have noted: that the deep-crawlers avoid the sick, that the damp-singers fall silent before danger, that the New Skin gives and takes in equal measure. But it would also note what the inhabitants cannot: the deeper patterns, the slower rhythms, the movements of the Old Growth across seasons and centuries.

It would see, for instance, that the cavity is not static. The root-mass is growing. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, the walls are inching inward—millimeters per decade, perhaps, but inward nonetheless. The cavity is closing. In another four hundred years, perhaps eight hundred, perhaps a thousand, the space that houses Iyesh will have shrunk beyond habitability. The passages will narrow. The chambers will compress. The inhabitants will be forced to migrate, to find new cavities or return to the surface they have forgotten, or they will simply cease, absorbed back into the tissue that once sheltered them.

This is not a crisis. This is geology. But a wise power thinks in long terms, and it sees that the relationship between the inhabitants and their home is temporary, though "temporary" here means something different than it does to human perception.

It would also see that the inhabitants are not fully human anymore. Not in the way surface-dwellers are human. Their biomes have shifted. Their skin flora is unrecognizable. Their gut bacteria are novel species, evolved in this closed system over generations. They have become native to the cavity in a way that makes them strangers to their own ancestry. If they left—if they climbed back through the root-mass and emerged into the world above—they would sicken. Their immune systems, so ferocious within the cavity, would be overwhelmed by surface pathogens they have no memory of. They are free to leave in theory. In practice, they are bound.

A wise power would see all of this. It would not judge it as good or bad. It would simply see: this is what is.

## It would ask whether it should act at all

This is the second question, and the more important one.

A benevolent power asks: *Is there harm here?*

And the answer is not simple. The inhabitants are not suffering in the ways that suffering is typically understood. They have food—sustainable, renewable, if somewhat monotonous. They have water—clean, processed, strange but potable. They have shelter—the cavity protects them from whatever dangers exist above. They have community, culture, story, work, rest. They reproduce, though slowly. They raise children. They bury their dead in the New Skin, which absorbs them and makes them part of the ongoing growth. They live full, if quiet, lives.

But.

They do not flourish. They survive. There is a difference. Survival is the maintenance of existence. Flourishing is the expansion of it—into complexity, into joy, into possibility. The inhabitants have adapted so thoroughly to the cavity's constraints that they have become the constraints. They move quietly because the cavity requires quiet. They work in bursts because the cold demands it. They sleep in piles because solitary sleep risks death. They have no art that is not functional, no music that is not the damp-singers' hum, no play that is not quiet. Their lives are shaped by what the cavity permits.

Is this harm? Or is it simply a different form of life, one that does not prioritize the things that surface-dwellers prioritize?

A wise power would be uncertain. And in its uncertainty, it would be cautious. To "save" people who do not consider themselves trapped is not benevolence; it is imposition. To "improve" a way of life that functions, however narrowly, is to risk breaking what works.

And yet.

There are the children. Few, always few. The cavity does not support large families. The resources are there—the New Skin grows faster than it can be harvested—but the space, the energy, the conditions for thriving childhoods are limited. Children raised in Iyesh learn early to be quiet, to be still, to move through darkness with the care of creatures who know that noise attracts attention. They do not play in the way children play. They explore, cautiously. They learn, observantly. But the wild, unbounded joy of childhood—the running and shouting and testing of limits that surface-dwellers associate with the young—this is absent. Not because children are forbidden these things, but because the cavity does not tolerate them, and children learn the cavity's limits before they learn their own.

A wise power would feel something here. Not pity—pity assumes superiority, and the power does not feel superior to these adapted, resilient, enduring people. But a kind of recognition: that something has been narrowed that might have been wider. That potential has been shaped by necessity. That the inhabitants are free within the cavity, but only within the cavity, and the cavity is shrinking.

## It would act, but obliquely

A benevolent power that chose to act would not act directly. Direct action—visible intervention, obvious change—would disrupt the delicate equilibrium that the inhabitants have spent generations building. The cavity is a closed system, and closed systems are fragile. Introduce something new without care, and the system may reject it, or worse, incorporate it in ways that cause harm.

So the power would work at the margins. In the spaces between. In the subtle shifts that could be attributed to natural variation.

### First: it would slow the closing

The Old Growth grows inward. This is its nature. It does not do so out of malice; it simply grows toward empty space, and the cavity is empty space. But a power that can perceive the slow compression can also influence it, gently.

It would not stop the growth—that would be to harm the Old Growth, and the Old Growth is as much the cavity's inhabitant as the humans are. But it might encourage growth in other directions. It might guide the root-mass toward other pockets, other voids, other places where expansion causes less compression. It might deepen the connections between this cavity and others—because there are others, the power would see, other hollows in the Old Growth's vast body, some connected by passages too narrow for humans but passable for the root-mass itself.

Over decades, the pressure would ease. The walls would still inch, but more slowly, giving the inhabitants more time than they would have had. This is not a permanent solution. But it is a gift of time, and time is the resource most precious to those who think in generations.

### Second: it would deepen the light

The bioluminescent fungi provide enough illumination to navigate by, but not enough to see clearly. The inhabitants have adapted to this—they navigate by touch, by sound, by the subtle currents of air—but they have also lost something: the ability to see each other fully.

A power that wished to give without disrupting might encourage certain fungal colonies to grow brighter. Not all at once, not dramatically, but gradually, over years, so that the change is imperceptible to those who live within it. The Crafters' Loop might become slightly more luminous. The sleeping chambers might gain a faint glow, enough to see the faces of those you wake beside.

The inhabitants might not consciously notice the change. But they would begin to see each other more clearly. They would begin to have the option of sight, even if they did not always use it. And options, for a people who have so few, are gifts beyond measure.

### Third: it would widen the children's world

The children of Iyesh learn caution early, and caution is valuable. But caution without contrast becomes fear, and fear narrows the soul.

A benevolent power might create, or encourage the formation of, a space within the cavity that is different. Not separate, not disconnected, but distinct. Perhaps a chamber where the walls are smoother, where the Hangers do not descend, where the deep-crawlers have never been observed to cross. A chamber where children might run, if they chose to, without attracting the attention of things that withdraw from noise. A chamber where play is not dangerous.

The power would not create such a space by fiat. It would work through the existing dynamics of the cavity—encouraging the New Skin to grow thicker in certain areas, discouraging the deep-crawlers from certain passages, calming the Hangers above a particular widening of the root-mass. It would make the conditions such that a safe space *could* emerge, and then it would let the inhabitants find it.

If they found it, they would make it their own. They would not know that anything had guided them to it. They would simply discover that here, in this chamber, their children could be children more fully. And they would use it accordingly.

### Fourth: it would open the possibility of leaving, without forcing departure

The inhabitants are bound to the cavity—not by walls, but by biology. Their immune systems are adapted to the cavity's biome and would be overwhelmed by the surface world's pathogens. If they left, they would sicken, perhaps die.

A wise power would not encourage them to leave. They have built something here—a way of life, a relationship with place, a culture that functions. To tell them they should abandon it would be arrogance. But to leave them with no choice is its own form of cruelty.

So the power might work on the threshold. The entrance—the split between buttress roots where visitors sometimes enter—is a transition point. A benevolent power might ease that transition. Not by sterilizing the surface world—that would be catastrophic for everyone—but by strengthening the inhabitants' capacity to survive beyond the cavity.

This is delicate work. It would involve gradual adjustments to the inhabitants' immune systems over generations—slight increases in their ability to handle surface pathogens, slight decreases in their dependence on the cavity's specific biota. It would not happen quickly. It might not happen at all, if the inhabitants showed no interest in leaving. But the possibility would be there, buried in their biology, waiting to be activated if circumstances ever demanded it.

They would not know this had been done. They would simply find, generations from now, that a traveler who ventured to the surface did not sicken as quickly as the stories said they should. That a child born with a wanderer's spirit could climb the root-mass and emerge into the light without immediate consequence. That the choice to leave, or to stay, was more truly a choice than it had been before.

## It would do nothing flashy

No angels descending. No voices from the ceiling. No obvious miracles that would demand explanation or worship. A benevolent power that is also wise knows that overt intervention changes the relationship between the helped and the helper—it creates debt, or dependency, or the distortion of behavior that comes from knowing you are observed.

The inhabitants of Iyesh would not know they had been visited. They might notice, over time, that the walls seemed to be closing more slowly than the elders remembered. They might observe that the children had found a chamber that felt safer than the others. They might remark that the light in the Crafters' Loop had grown subtly brighter over the years, making fine work easier.

But they would attribute these changes to the cavity itself—to the Old Growth's mysterious will, to the New Skin's growth patterns, to the capricious but generally benevolent nature of the place they call home. And they would not be wrong. The power's interventions would be expressions of the cavity's own potential, not impositions from outside.

## It would stay

A benevolent power would not leave. Not entirely. Having observed, having acted in its subtle ways, it would continue to observe—to watch the slow growth, the shifting passages, the generations of inhabitants who come and go. It would not interfere further unless something demanded interference.

If the Old Growth accelerated its closing—if the cavity threatened to crush the inhabitants before they could adapt or escape—the power might act more directly, opening passages to other hollows, guiding the root-mass in ways that bought time. If the deep-crawlers changed their behavior, becoming aggressive, the power would investigate and, if necessary, intervene. If disease spread—a novel pathogen that the inhabitants' ferocious immune systems could not handle—the power might work through the New Skin to introduce compounds that countered it.

But these would be exceptions. The rule would be presence without interference. Attention without judgment. A kind of guardianship so light that the guarded would never feel the weight.

## It would love them

This is the final thing.

A benevolent and wise power, watching the inhabitants of Iyesh, would come to love them. Not in a possessive way, not in a way that demanded their love in return, but in the way one loves something that is itself and persists in being itself despite all pressure to be otherwise.

It would love their adaptability. Their stubborn refusal to be displaced, even as the cavity compressed around them. Their ingenuity—the tools of bone and grass, the systems of notation for deep-crawler passages, the harvest of New Skin that fed them without killing what fed them.

It would love their quietness. The way they had learned to communicate in murmurs, touches, shared breath. The way their stories were about the cavity itself, not about heroes or conquests. The way they had made a culture of accommodation rather than dominance.

It would love their children—few, precious, learning caution so young but still, somehow, finding ways to explore, to curiosity, to the irrepressible desire to know what is around the next corner. It would love their elders, who carried the accumulated knowledge of generations in their memories and their hands, who knew which passages were closing and which might reopen.

It would love even their limitations. Their inability to see color clearly. Their tolerance for the crawling mites and the cold and the dark. Their acceptance of a life that was, by surface-world standards, narrow. It would love these things not because limitation is good, but because it was theirs, and they had made it work.

And because it loved them, it would do only what helped and nothing that harmed. It would give them time, and light, and space for children to play, and the possibility of departure without forcing them to leave. It would stay close, and watch, and wait, and trust them to know what they needed better than any outside power could.

It would trust the cavity, too. The Old Growth, the New Skin, the Hangers and deep-crawlers and damp-singers and all the millions of lives that made the Root-Cavity what it was. It would trust the system that had evolved, even if the system was strange, even if it was closing, even if it was not what a surface-dweller would choose.

Because the system was alive, and life finds ways, and sometimes the benevolent thing is not to change what is working but to gently, invisibly, help it keep working a little longer, a little better, a little more fully.

This is what the power would do.
