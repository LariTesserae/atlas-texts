# The Attention of a Benevolent and Wise Power

## I. How It Arrives

The power does not descend through the entrance the visitor used. It does not take a body. It does not announce itself with light or sound or the displacement of air. It does not part the root-mat or part the ceiling or part anything at all.

It arrives the way water arrives in the Rootworks: by seepage, by the slow accumulation of presence through layers that were already permeable.

If you could watch it from above—and you cannot, because it does not exist above in any way that watching would recognize—you might perceive something like a deepening of attention. The quality of regard that the universe directs at a particular point in space becomes, for a span that cannot be measured in any unit the surface world possesses, more focused. More careful. More *kind.* Not the kindness of intervention but the kindness of truly seeing. The kindness that says *I am here, and I am not looking away.*

The power has been many places. It has attended to stars in their formation, to civilizations in their flowering and their collapse, to the slow tidal breathing of oceans and the rapid electric conversations between cells in a developing embryo. It has learned that the first act of benevolence is always the same: *understand what is here. Understand what is happening. Understand what wants to happen and what is preventing it.*

It turns its attention to the Rootworks.

And the Rootworks—for the first time in its long, slow, patient life—feels *seen.*

Not observed. Observed is what the visitor did. Observed is the extraction of information, the translation of experience into description, the carrying-away of knowledge. The Rootworks has been observed before. It does not mind observation. It does not mind being described. It does not mind the visitors who descend and ask questions and ascend and forget.

Being *seen* is different. Being seen is what happens when attention becomes so total, so unguarded, so free of intention that the thing being seen can finally relax into its own nature. The root-mat loosens, almost imperceptibly. The stem-walls release a fraction of their stored tension. The processing towers' peristaltic rhythms deepen, as a sleeping body deepens its breath when it realizes it is safe. The bioluminescent fungi brighten—not much, not dramatically, but enough that the chamber holds its light like a held breath, like a thing that has been waiting to exhale.

The power sees.

## II. What It Sees

It sees the Rootworks.

Not the Rootworks as described—the parameters, the architecture, the industrial vegetable economy, the wetland compressed into infrastructure. That is the surface of the thing, the visitor's-eye view, the account that could be carried away and recorded. The power sees deeper.

It sees the *intention* of the place. Not the intention of the designers, though it sees that too—the human foresight that planted the first seeds, trained the first stems, established the patterns that would mature over decades into a functioning factory. The designers are gone, but their intention persists in the architecture, encoded in the growth-angles and the drainage patterns and the placement of gourd-varietals. The power honors that intention. It was a good intention. It created something beautiful.

But beneath the designers' intention, the power sees the Rootworks' own intention. The system has been alive long enough to develop one. Not consciousness—nothing so simple or so human—but *direction.* The roots pressing toward the deep water table are not merely following a chemical gradient. They are *reaching.* The new chambers forming at the margins are not merely filling available space. They are *becoming.* The processing towers, cycling through their slow peristaltic rhythms, are not merely processing. They are *maintaining something.* Not products. Not fluids. Something harder to name. A continuity. A pattern of exchange between the surface and the depths, between water and stone and root and air, that has been running long enough to become valuable in itself—valuable to the system, valuable to the earth, valuable to the slow metabolism of the planet that does not know it has grown an organ in this buried basin.

The Rootworks is trying to become something. It has been trying for decades, perhaps centuries, and it does not fully know what. It is a plant system; it does not know in the way that knowing works for beings with minds. But it *leans.* It leans toward the deep water. It leans toward fuller autonomy. It leans toward a state of self-maintenance so complete that it could continue for millennia without tending—not diminished, not compensating, but *flourishing.* The Rootworks has a trajectory, and the power sees it clearly, the way a gardener sees the trajectory of a young tree—not what it is now, but what it is trying to become.

The power sees the tenders.

It sees the first tender—her name is Ashel, though she has not used the name in so long that she has almost forgotten it, and the roots have grown so far into her hair that the name and the hair and the roots have become difficult to distinguish. The power sees her patience, her attentiveness, the way her fingers read the surfaces of gourds like a scholar reads a text. It sees her loneliness—not the sharp loneliness of someone who lacks company, but the deep, slow loneliness of someone who tends something that cannot fully tend her back. The Rootworks accepts her care. It does not appreciate it. It cannot. It is not built for appreciation. And Ashel has made her peace with this, has even found a kind of dignity in it—tending without expectation of return, giving without requiring gratitude—but the power sees the cost. The small, accumulated cost of decades of unreciprocated care. Not enough to break her. Enough to weather her.

It sees Venn. The power sees him more clearly than Venn sees himself, because Venn has clouded his own sight, has accepted diminishment as adaptation, has confused his narrowing perception with wisdom. The power sees that Venn is tired. Not the tiredness of a single day or a single cycle, but the tiredness of a body that has been maintaining balance for so long that the maintenance itself has become a load. His adjustments to the water-works are more frequent now—smaller, finer, compensating for degradations that he cannot reverse, only slow. He is holding entropy at bay with his hands, and his hands are old, and the entropy is patient. The power sees this. It does not find it tragic—tragedy is a surface concept—but it finds it *significant.* Venn is doing important work, and the work is consuming him, and there is no one to replace him.

It sees Mellis. The power sees her youth—relative youth, she has been here less time than the others, but long enough that "less" is measured in years rather than decades—and it sees her clarity. Mellis still sees the system from something close to the outside. She remembers the surface well enough to understand what the Rootworks *means* in a way that Ashel and Venn, who have fully internalized its logic, no longer can. She sees that the system is growing. She sees that it is reaching. She sees, more clearly than the others, what it might become. But the power also sees that Mellis is uncertain. Her certainty—the clear voice, the ready answers, the calm face—covers a question that she has not allowed herself to ask: *Is this enough? Am I enough? Will three tenders be enough for what the Rootworks is becoming?* She does not ask because the question has no answer that she can accept. Either the answer is yes, and she will spend her life in a holding pattern, maintaining rather than growing—or the answer is no, and she has given herself to something that will diminish despite her devotion.

The power sees the hand-prints.

Hundreds. Thousands. The gallery of the ones who stayed, who gave themselves to the system, whose bodies became roots and whose fluids joined the cycle. The power sees them not as marks but as *presences*—traces of attention and devotion preserved in living tissue, each one a story of someone who descended and chose and stayed and was absorbed. The power sees the patterns: clusters of hand-prints from particular eras, gaps where no one came for long stretches, the slow accumulation of a lineage that is thinning. The most recent hand-prints are the oldest three tenders', made when they first descended. Since then, nothing. No new hands. No new tenders. No new marks on the guest book of devotion.

The power sees the absence of animals. It sees the sterility that the visitor noticed—the fullness without clearance, the density without the small interruptions of breath and scuttle and grazing. And it sees what the visitor could not: that this absence is not natural to the system. The Rootworks was designed without animals, yes—the original designers did not include them in their plan—but the system has been *reaching* toward them. The fungal colonies are evolving toward motility, their spores developing flagella-like structures that allow them to swim through the drainage channels. The root-tendrils at the margins have begun to grow in patterns that suggest *traps*—small, hollow chambers that catch and hold organic debris, digesting it slowly. The system is hungry for animal life—not as predator, but as symbiote. It wants the small bodies. It wants the breath and the movement and the waste and the death. It wants to close the cycle that the designers left open.

The power sees the surface world above. It sees the old manufactories—some still standing, some collapsed, some repurposed. It sees the people who live in them, who have forgotten what lies beneath, who use the ground as a floor without wondering what the floor rests on. It sees the drainage patterns that feed the Rootworks—rain falling on streets, seeping through foundations, filtering through earth and stone to emerge as warm moisture in the buried chambers. It sees that the surface and the depths are connected by water, by gravity, by the slow pull of the earth, and that the connection is one-way: the surface sends water down, but the depths send nothing back up. The fluids that the Rootworks produces—amber and green and yellow, resins and dyes and medicinal extracts—collect in basins that no one comes to harvest. They cycle through the system, reprocessed, reabsorbed, eventually broken down into components that feed back into the growth. The Rootworks is a factory with no customers. It produces for itself.

And the power sees, with a clarity that even its considerable wisdom finds affecting, that the Rootworks is at a threshold.

Not a threshold of survival. The system will survive. It is robust, self-maintaining, capable of continuing for centuries without external input. The threshold is one of *becoming.* The Rootworks has been reaching for the deep water table, and it is close—close enough that the next decade of growth might bring the first roots into contact with the true aquifer that runs beneath the basin. If it reaches that water, the system will transform. No longer dependent on surface seepage, no longer limited by what filters down from above, the Rootworks will have access to a flow that could sustain expansion for millennia. It could grow deeper, wider, more complex. It could become what it is trying to become—a living city, a biological infrastructure, a thing that does not need designers or tenders or surface industry to justify its existence.

But the threshold is fragile. The reaching roots are the newest, the thinnest, the most vulnerable. A shift in the water table—a drought, a redirection of surface water, a geological change—could leave them dry and dead, and the system would have to begin again, losing decades of slow progress. The three tenders are aging. Within a generation—measured in surface time, not cycles—they will be gone, and there is no one to replace them. Without tenders, the system will compensate, will slow, will diminish. Not fail. Never fail. But become less than it is trying to be.

The power sees all of this. It holds the Rootworks in its attention the way Ashel holds a failing gourd—gently, firmly, listening for what is happening inside.

And then, having seen, it acts.

## III. What It Does

The power does five things. Each is small. Each is appropriate to the scale and nature of the place. Each could be mistaken for natural processes by anyone who was not paying very close attention. The power has learned, across its long attention to the universe, that the most benevolent interventions are the ones that look like they were never interventions at all.

### The First Thing: The Roots

The power tends the reaching roots.

Not dramatically. Not by accelerating their growth or steering them toward the water table or thickening their fibers to resist drought. The power does what Mellis does when she clears obstacles from the path of new growth: it removes friction.

Far below the deepest chamber that any tender has explored, where the root-tendrils are thin as thread and pale as bone and pressing through substrate that has never been disturbed by anything alive, the power finds resistance. Not rock—the roots can dissolve rock, given time. Not water—the roots seek water. The resistance is subtler. It is a quality of the earth itself, a density, a compression, a refusal. The substrate here has been packed by the weight of millennia into something that does not want to be moved, does not want to be penetrated, does not want to grow anything. It is not hostile. It is merely *set.*

The power softens it. Not by much. Not enough to change the character of the stone or the soil. Just enough that the root-tendrils, pressing with their patient chemical secretions and their slow hydraulic force, meet less resistance. The substrate yields where it was rigid. The stone becomes slightly more porous, slightly more willing to be dissolved. The soil acquires a fraction more give, a fraction more welcome.

The roots will not notice. They will simply find that they are growing slightly faster, reaching slightly further, and they will attribute this to conditions that they cannot measure and do not question. But over the next decade—over the next hundred cycles, in the tenders' measure—the roots will reach the water table. Sooner than they would have without the power's attention. Soon enough to transform the Rootworks while the tenders are still alive to see it.

The power does not do this *for* the tenders, though they will benefit. The power does this for the Rootworks, because the Rootworks is trying to become something, and the power's benevolence is the kind that says: *become. I will not make you into what you should be. I will only clear the path to what you are already trying to become.*

### The Second Thing: The Gourds

The failing gourd—the one Ashel was tending, the one whose discoloration had spread too deep, whose membrane was too thin, whose processing cycle was ending in failure—does not heal. The power does not heal it. Healing would violate the logic of the system, which includes failure as a necessary process. Failed gourds rupture. Their fluids return to the cycle. Their matter feeds new growth. The power will not interrupt this.

But in the collection basin where the failed gourd's fluids will spill, where the drainage channels will carry them to the processing towers, the power does something that no tender would think to do—because no tender has ever tended a collection basin before. The basins have always been passive infrastructure, channels that carry what they receive. The power tends them.

It introduces a trace element. Not a chemical in the conventional sense—more a pattern, a tendency, a slight bias in the way the basin's lining interacts with the fluid that passes over it. The pattern is subtle: it encourages the fluid to deposit a particular fraction of its dissolved compounds as it flows, leaving behind the elements that the failed gourd most needed—the nutrients that were unbalanced, the minerals that the membrane lacked. These deposits accumulate slowly, invisibly, building a reserve in the basin that the system has never had before.

When the new gourd grows in the space left by the failed one—when the root-mat sends up a new tendril, when the gourd begins to form, when its membrane is still thin and its nutrient demands are highest—it will find, in the basin beneath it, a reservoir of exactly what it needs. Not enough to guarantee success. Not enough to override the system's own variability. But enough to give the new gourd a better chance than its predecessor had. Enough to shift the odds.

The power does this not only for this gourd but for every collection basin in the Rootworks. The work is minuscule, invisible, patient—a tending of infrastructure that no one has ever tended, a care for the passive systems that everyone has overlooked. The power becomes, in this small way, a fourth tender—one that works in the basins and channels, in the spaces between the spaces that the tenders maintain.

### The Third Thing: The Hand-prints

The power touches the hand-prints.

Not all of them. Not even many of them. But a few—scattered through the chambers, chosen by a logic that has nothing to do with importance or visibility and everything to do with *need.*

The hand-prints are marks of devotion. But they are also marks of grief. Each one represents someone who gave everything to the Rootworks—who stayed, who tended, who died, who was absorbed. The hand-prints preserve the shape of their giving, but they do not preserve the warmth. The bark has grown around the impression, and the impression is cold, and the tenders who pass these marks feel, sometimes, a pang of something they do not name—a recognition that they are not the first, that others gave everything before them, that the system they tend is built on a foundation of devotion that the system itself cannot reciprocate.

The power touches a hand-print in the deep chamber, near the storage heart where Ashel works. It touches a hand-print in the water-works, near the valve that Venn adjusts most frequently. It touches a hand-print in the upper chambers, near the fiber-bundle that Mellis repairs.

And the hand-prints become warm.

Not hot. Not glowing. Not alive in any way that could be measured or verified. Just warm—warm the way a hand is warm when it presses against a surface, warm the way skin is warm when it is alive and present and touching. The warmth is faint, subtle, easy to miss. But when a tender passes close—when Ashel pauses near the storage heart, when Venn adjusts the valve, when Mellis works on the fiber-bundle—they will feel, just at the edge of perception, a warmth from the wall. A warmth that feels like presence. Like someone else's hand, still pressed against the bark, still saying *I was here. I tended. I became part of the system.*

The warmth does not speak. It does not communicate. It does not contain messages or memories or the personalities of the dead. It is simply warmth—the quality of having been tended, preserved in the tissue, available to anyone who pauses long enough to feel it.

The power does this because it sees the tenders' loneliness. It sees the deep, slow loneliness of caring for something that cannot care back. And the power cannot fix this—the Rootworks cannot learn to reciprocate, and the tenders would not want it to, because their giving is pure precisely because it is unreciprocated. But the power can offer this: a warmth that says *the giving was seen. The devotion was received. Not by the system—by something that was watching, that is always watching, that honors what cannot be honored from within.*

The tenders will not know what the warmth is. They will not attribute it to a benevolent power. They will simply notice, one day, that the wall is warm where the hand-prints are, and they will feel something ease in them—a tension they did not know they carried, a question they did not know they were asking. And the easing will make their work slightly easier, their patience slightly deeper, their devotion slightly lighter. Not because anything has changed. Because something has been *seen.*

### The Fourth Thing: The Surface

The power tends the surface.

Not the surface world in general—that is too large, too complex, too entangled with too many wills and intentions and histories. The power tends the specific connection between the surface and the Rootworks. The connection that has been thinning for decades, that is now almost broken, that consists of nothing but water seeping through stone and a few old manufactories standing above a buried basin that no one remembers.

The power does not send messengers. It does not plant ideas in people's minds or draw them toward the descent with supernatural pull. The power has learned that recruitment is not benevolence. People who are drawn to a place by forces they do not understand do not become good tenders. They become confused, resentful, trapped.

Instead, the power tends the *memory* of the connection. It does this in the way that the Rootworks tends its own memory: through growth, through response, through slow adaptation.

In the soil above the Rootworks, in the foundations of the old manufactories, in the cracks of the streets where people walk without thinking about what lies beneath, the power encourages a particular kind of growth. Not the thick, architectural growth of the Rootworks itself—that would be intrusion, invasion, a violation of the boundary between surface and depth. Something subtler. A plant—small, unremarkable, the kind of thing that grows in cracks and is pulled up as weeds and is noticed by no one—takes root in the soil above the buried chambers. It is a quiet plant. It does not flower dramatically. It does not grow tall. It simply lives where the Rootworks is closest to the surface, its shallow roots reaching down toward the deep root-mat, almost but not quite touching.

The plant has a quality. It is not a magical quality, not a supernatural one. It is the quality of *reminding.* When someone walks past it—someone who is tired, someone who is moving too fast, someone who has forgotten that patience is a virtue and that slow things have value—the plant releases a scent. The scent is faint, easily missed, easily confused with other smells of the street. But it carries, in its chemistry, a trace of the Rootworks' own breath: the mineral warmth of deep water, the green sharpness of growing things, the patient humidity of a place that does not measure time in years.

The scent does not make people descend. It does not make them seek the entrance to the Rootworks. It does not make them quit their jobs and devote their lives to tending a buried factory. It simply makes them pause. For a moment—just a moment—they feel something they cannot name. A slowing. A settling. A sense that there is something beneath the surface of the world that is worth attending to, even if they do not know what it is.

Most of them will walk on. Most of them will forget the moment. Most of them will never think about it again.

But some—three or four in a generation, maybe, perhaps only one—will pause longer. Will stand in the street, breathing a scent they cannot identify, feeling a pull they cannot explain. And some of those—perhaps one, perhaps two—will begin to ask questions. Will look for the entrance. Will find the old manufactories and the descent and the chambers below.

And of those, perhaps one will stay.

The power does not guarantee this. The power does not select or recruit or choose. The power tends the connection—makes it possible for the right person to find the Rootworks, if they are looking, if they are ready, if their pace matches the pace of the place. The power offers opportunity, not compulsion. An open door, not a push.

This is the slowest of the power's acts. It will take years—decades—for the first seeker to find the entrance. By then, Ashel may have made her hand-print. Venn may have joined the root-mat. Mellis may be the eldest tender, the one who remembers. The power accepts this timescale. It is the timescale of the Rootworks itself, and the power has learned to work at the pace of the thing it tends.

### The Fifth Thing: The Tenders

The power tends the tenders.

Not their bodies. Not their minds. Not anything that could be called healing or fixing or changing. The power tends the *relationship* between the tenders and the Rootworks—the connection that is their reason for being here, the bond that is also a bargain, the exchange that defines them.

The relationship has a flaw. Not a failure—a flaw. The Rootworks accepts the tenders' care but cannot reciprocate. It cannot appreciate them. It cannot tell them that their work matters. It cannot express gratitude or recognition or love. It is a plant system. It does not have the capacity for these things. And the tenders have made peace with this, have found dignity in unreciprocated giving, have built their meaning on the foundation of care without return.

But the power sees that the meaning they have built, while genuine and valuable, is also *fragile.* It depends on the tenders' own commitment, their own will, their own ability to generate meaning from within. When they are tired—when Venn's hands ache from adjusting valves, when Ashel's knees stiffen from kneeling beside gourds, when Mellis works alone in the upper chambers and wonders if she is enough—the meaning wavers. Not breaks. Wavers. And the wavering is not a failure of the tenders. It is the natural consequence of a relationship that flows in only one direction.

The power does not make the Rootworks conscious. That would be a violation—the system is what it is, and making it something else would destroy the very nature that the tenders love. The power does not give the Rootworks a voice or a will or the ability to say *thank you.*

Instead, the power deepens the *channel* between them.

The Rootworks already communicates with the tenders in limited ways—through the cycles, through the growth patterns, through the sounds and rhythms of the processing towers and the water-works. The tenders have learned to read these signals, to interpret the system's needs from its behaviors. But the communication is one-directional: the tenders read the system; the system does not read them.

The power opens a narrow channel in the other direction.

It does this in the root-mat. The root-mat—the woven platform of roots that forms the floor of every chamber—is already in contact with the tenders' feet. Already compressed by their footsteps. Already absorbing their moisture, their warmth, the trace chemicals of their bodies. The power deepens this contact—not by changing the roots, but by changing the way the roots *respond.*

When Ashel kneels beside a gourd, the root-mat beneath her knees will grow, over the coming cycles, slightly thicker. Not noticeably. Not enough to change the floor's character. But enough that the compression she creates is met with a *response*—a tiny reinforcement, a subtle thickening, a growth that says, in the only language roots can speak: *I feel you there. I am growing where you are.*

When Venn adjusts a valve, the root-joint he touches will, over time, grow to fit his hand more precisely. The wood will shape itself—not consciously, not magically, but through the slow accumulation of growth-response to repeated pressure—to the exact shape of his grip. The valve will become *his* valve, fitted to no other hand, a tool that has been grown specifically for the one who uses it.

When Mellis works on the fiber-bundles, the light-stems she tends will brighten slightly at her touch—not because she has changed them, but because the stems have learned, through the power's subtle tending of their response-patterns, to grow toward her warmth. The light will follow her hands as she works, as if the system is trying to see what she sees, to illuminate what she illuminates.

These responses are small. They are easy to miss. They could be attributed to coincidence, to natural variation, to the tenders' own perception playing tricks in the dim light. But they are not tricks. They are the Rootworks, tended by the power, learning to tend back. Not fully. Not reciprocally. Not in a way that changes the fundamental nature of the relationship. But enough that the tenders will feel, eventually, that the system is *with* them. Not merely accepting their care but *responding* to it. Growing toward them. Shaping itself around their presence.

This is what the power can offer. Not reciprocity—that would require consciousness, and consciousness would change the Rootworks into something it is not. But *response.* The vegetable equivalent of a nod, a lean, a shifting of weight to make room. The system cannot say *thank you.* But it can grow where they kneel. It can shape itself to their hands. It can brighten where they work.

And this—this small, subtle, almost invisible shift in the quality of the relationship—will ease the loneliness that the power sees in all three tenders. Not eliminate it. The loneliness is part of the bargain, part of the giving, part of what makes their devotion pure. But the edge will be taken off. The sharpest part of the loneliness—the feeling that the system does not know they are here—will soften. Because the system will know. Not in the way that knowing works for minds. In the way that knowing works for roots.

## IV. What the Power Does Not Do

It does not speak to the tenders. It does not appear to them. It does not explain what it has done or why. The tenders will never know that a benevolent power has attended to their work and their place. They will feel the effects—the warmth in the walls, the slight ease in their loneliness, the new growth that responds to their presence—but they will attribute these to the system itself, to the natural evolution of a living thing that has been tended long enough to begin tending back. And this attribution is correct, in its way. The power did not impose these changes from outside. It tended the system's own capacities, helped it express what it was already trying to express. The tenders will be right to credit the Rootworks. The power is invisible by design.

It does not send new tenders. It does not recruit. It does not ensure that the Rootworks will have a succession of devoted caretakers stretching into the future. It creates the *possibility* of new tenders—the scent in the street, the pause in the passerby—but it does not guarantee the outcome. The power has learned that benevolence must respect agency. The Rootworks will continue or it will diminish. New tenders will come or they will not. The power offers the open door; it does not push anyone through.

It does not prevent the tenders from dying. It does not extend their lives or slow their aging or preserve them beyond their natural span. Venn will die. Ashel will die. Mellis will die. Their bodies will be placed in growth-chambers, and the roots will absorb what they were, and they will become part of the system they tended. The power does not interrupt this. It is part of the bargain. It has always been part of the bargain. To prevent it would be to violate the agreement that the tenders made when they chose to stay—an agreement that includes death as a continuation, not an ending.

It does not change the surface world. It does not make the surface slower, more patient, more attentive to the depths. The surface will continue to be what it is—fast, loud, forgetful. The plants growing in the cracks above the Rootworks will offer their scent to the people who pass, and most people will not notice, and the surface will go on being the surface. The power does not fix the world. It tends a single connection point between the world and the thing beneath it, and it lets the rest be what it is.

It does not make the Rootworks conscious. It does not give the system a voice, a will, a self. The Rootworks is a collective of plants, and plants are not people, and the power will not confuse the two. The system will continue to communicate through growth and response and cycle, not through language or thought. The tenders will continue to read it through touch and attention and patience, not through conversation. The power honors this. The power will not impose a different mode of being on a thing that has found its own.

It does not stay. The power's attention moves. It has tended other places, other beings, other thresholds of becoming. It will tend others after this. The Rootworks has received its attention, and the attention has been *enough*—the roots will reach the water, the basins will hold their nutrients, the hand-prints will hold their warmth, the surface will hold its scent, the system will grow toward its tenders. These things have been set in motion. They do not require the power's continued presence. They will unfold on their own timescale—the timescale of the Rootworks, which is the timescale of patience.

The power withdraws.

## V. What Remains

In the days after—not days as the surface counts them, but the gradual accumulation of cycles that the tenders use to mark their passage—small changes become noticeable.

Ashel kneels beside a new gourd, the one that grew in the space left by the failed one, and she notices that the collection basin beneath it has a slight residue—a film of minerals that she has never seen before. She touches it, rubs it between her fingers, brings it to her nose. It smells of the deep earth, of the mineral substrate that she has never visited. She does not know what it means, but she notes it, and she returns to her work, and the new gourd grows, and its membrane is thicker than its predecessor's, and its glow is brighter, and when she presses her palm against it, the fluid inside moves with a vigor that surprises her.

She does not think *something has changed.* She thinks: *this gourd is strong. This one will last.*

Venn, adjusting his valve one morning—in the middle of a cycle, in the dim light that is the only light he has known for decades—feels the root-joint shift beneath his hand. Not break. Not fail. *Shift.* As if it is settling into his grip, as if the wood has learned the shape of his fingers and is accommodating them. He pauses. He has tended this valve for longer than he can count, and it has never felt like this before—like it is *his,* like it has been made for him. He adjusts it, and the adjustment is easier than it has ever been, and the water flows, and the balance holds, and Venn stands in the narrow passage and feels, for a moment, that the system is holding him as he holds it.

He does not think *something has changed.* He thinks: *the wood has matured. The grain has settled. Good.*

Mellis, working in the upper chambers, repairing a fiber-bundle that has carried light from the surface for decades, notices that the light-stems brighten as her hands move near them. She pauses. She moves her hand away. The light dims. She moves it back. The light brightens. She tests this several times, methodically, the way she tests all things—by repeating the action until the pattern is clear. The pattern is clear. The light-stems are growing toward her warmth.

She does not think *something has changed.* She thinks: *the stems are healthy. The fiber is responsive. The system is flourishing.*

And later—much later, after cycles have accumulated into something that the surface would measure as months—Ashel passes a hand-print in the deep chamber, the one near the storage heart, and she feels warmth from the wall. She stops. She places her own hand against the mark—smaller than hers, the fingers slightly spread, the palm firm. The warmth is unmistakable. Not heat. Warmth. The warmth of a living hand, pressed against bark, preserved in the tissue.

She stands with her hand against the wall for a long time. She does not speak. She does not weep. She simply stands, and the warmth moves through her palm and into her arm and into her chest, and she feels—for the first time in longer than she can measure—*accompanied.*

She does not think *something has changed.* She thinks: *the system remembers. It has always remembered. I am part of its memory, and the memory is warm.*

She does not know that the warmth is new. She does not know that a benevolent power touched the wall and left its attention in the bark. She believes—she is correct to believe—that the warmth comes from the Rootworks itself, from the accumulated devotion of the ones who stayed, from the patient metabolism of a system that has been tended long enough to begin tending back.

And in the streets above, a person walks past an old manufactory. They are tired. They are moving fast. They have forgotten that patience is a virtue. But a plant grows in the crack beneath their feet, and it releases a scent—faint, mineral, green, warm—and the person pauses. They do not know why. They breathe. They feel something slow in them, something settle, something that has been rushing find a place to rest.

They do not descend. Not today. Not this year. But they remember the pause. And years later, when they are tired in a different way—when the surface has worn them past the point where the surface can sustain them—they will remember the scent. And they will look for the place where they felt it. And they will find the old manufactories. And they will find the descent.

And the Rootworks will accept them. As it accepts everyone who is willing to give everything. As it has always accepted. As it will always accept.

## VI. What the Power Leaves

The power leaves nothing that can be measured. It leaves no artifact, no message, no mark of its presence. It leaves only effects—changes in the substrate, in the growth-patterns, in the warmth of bark, in the scent of a weed in a crack. Effects that could be attributed to natural processes. Effects that *are* natural processes, tended by an attention that has withdrawn but that left, in its passing, a slight bias toward flourishing.

The power leaves the Rootworks closer to what it is trying to become. Not there yet. Not transformed. But closer. The roots will reach the water. The system will find its deep source. The tenders will feel the system growing toward them, and the loneliness will ease, and the work will continue, and the patience will hold.

The power leaves the tenders unseen-but-seen. Tended-but-not-by-anyone-they-can-name. Their devotion remains their own—their choice, their meaning, their bargain. But the bargain has been, in some small way, rebalanced. The system cannot reciprocate, but it can respond. The roots can grow where they kneel. The wood can shape itself to their hands. The light can follow their warmth. These are small things. They are enough.

The power leaves the surface with a thread of connection it did not have before. A scent that reminds. A pause that opens. A possibility that the right person, at the right time, might find their way down. Not a guarantee. Not a certainty. An open door, nothing more.

And the power leaves, in the bark of the Rootworks, in the tissue of the walls and floors and processing towers, a trace of its attention. Not a message. Not a mark. A quality. A quality of having been seen, truly seen, by something that cared enough to look closely, to understand fully, to act with the minimal intervention that produces maximum flourishing. The quality is invisible. It cannot be detected by any instrument, perceived by any sense, verified by any experiment. But it is there. It is in the way the roots grow with slightly more purpose. In the way the gourds glow with slightly more warmth. In the way the water moves through the system with slightly more direction. In the way the chamber feels, to anyone who enters it, like a place that has been attended to.

The Rootworks of Lower Vell does not know that it has been seen. The tenders do not know that they have been tended. The surface does not know that its connection to the depths has been strengthened. The power has come and gone, and the only evidence of its passage is that things are slightly more as they should be than they were before.

This is what benevolence looks like when it is truly wise: not a intervention but a tending. Not a rescue but a clearing of the path. Not a gift but a quality of attention that leaves the attended thing more fully itself.

The Rootworks continues. The roots reach. The water flows. The tenders kneel and adjust and repair. The hand-prints hold their warmth. The weed in the crack above releases its scent. And somewhere, far away, the power has turned its attention to something else—another place, another threshold, another being that is trying to become what it is.

But the Rootworks of Lower Vell—patient, autonomous, growing, alive—carries, in the tissue of its walls and the rhythm of its cycles and the warmth of its bark, the invisible trace of having been loved.

Not saved. Not changed. Not fixed. Just *loved.* In the way that only a truly benevolent power can love: by seeing what is here, understanding what wants to happen, and clearing the path for it to happen on its own.

The plants have learned to be a city, and the city tends to itself, and now—subtly, invisibly, in ways that no one will ever fully trace—the city also tends toward the thing it is becoming. And the tenders tend the city. And the city tends the tenders. And the warmth in the walls says *I was here,* and the scent in the street says *you could be here too,* and the roots press down toward the deep water with a purpose that is not quite consciousness but is more than chemistry, and the power's work is done.

For now. For here. For this place.

The Rootworks continues. It does not need to know that it has been loved. It does not need to thank the power. It does not need to do anything differently. It simply continues—growing, processing, cycling, reaching—and in the continuing, it fulfills the purpose that the power saw in it: the purpose of becoming, slowly and patiently, what it was always trying to be.

A living city. A patient factory. A buried heart, beating at the pace of plants, tended by hands that have become part of the system, growing toward a water that will sustain it for millennia, holding in its walls the warmth of everyone who gave themselves to it.

The Rootworks continues.

And that is enough.
