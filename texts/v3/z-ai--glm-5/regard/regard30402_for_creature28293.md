# What the Power Does

## First, It Stops

A benevolent and wise power does not rush. This is the first principle. It approaches the Rhizome Ward the way the root-mat approaches the stone — slowly, with attention, feeling the shape of the thing before acting.

It observes the chamber from above, from within, from the growing edge. It traces the sap through its conduits and feels the ionic current hum through the phloem-bundles. It listens to the silence. It sits with the elder in the lichen-light and does not ask her to speak.

This takes time. A benevolent power has time. It is not rushing toward the next crisis, the next soul to save, the next world to reorder. It is present. It is learning.

And what it learns is this: the Rhizome Ward is not broken.

This matters. Most places that draw the attention of powers *are* broken — torn by war, corroded by neglect, disordered by suffering. Most places need healing. This place does not. It is whole. It is functioning. It is, in its own slow way, thriving.

So the power does not fix it. It does not improve it. It does not reorder the governance or optimize the factories or introduce new technologies from above. To do so would be to misunderstand what is already working.

Instead, it asks a different question: *What does this place need that it does not know it needs?*

---

## What It Notices

The power notices several things, sitting in the silence.

First: the isolation is fragile. The shafts that bring goods from the surface are a vulnerability. Not because the surface is evil, but because the surface is *fast* — fast-moving people, fast-moving ideas, fast-moving exploitation. The root-town is protected by its depth, but depth is not permanent protection. Someone could come down those shafts with intentions that do not match the rhythm of the place. The inhabitants would not be able to stop them. They do not have the speed.

Second: the knowledge is unrecorded. The graft-patterns, the sap-refinement techniques, the history of the root-mat — it lives in bodies and in the root-mat itself. If a disease came, or a fire, or a collapse, the knowledge would die with the people. There is no redundancy. This is a risk they do not see, because they have never needed to see it.

Third: the gene pool is narrow. Two hundred people, intergenerational, with limited new blood. It has not caused problems yet. It will, eventually, if nothing changes. The root-mat does not solve everything.

Fourth: the young who leave do not return. This is not a problem now, but the power sees a young worker watching the shaft with something in her eyes that is not quite satisfaction. Curiosity, perhaps. Or a longing she has not yet found words for. The power notices this because it is the seed of something that could grow into sorrow, or into transformation. It does not intervene. It simply notices.

Fifth: the elder is tired. Not in body — her body is strong — but in spirit. She has been holding the history of her people for a century, and she is ready to let it go. Not to die. Just to stop being the one who remembers. The power sees this the way it sees the root-mat growing — a natural process, a wearing-down that comes from use.

## What It Gives

The power does not give much. This is the second principle: a benevolent intervention should be the smallest possible action that achieves the necessary end.

It gives four things. Small things. Things that will not disrupt.

### The First Gift: A Guardian at the Gate

The power reaches up through the shafts, feeling their shape, their texture, the way they wind through rock and root toward the surface. It does not close them — that would be harm, would cut off trade, would trap the inhabitants in a cage they did not choose.

Instead, it places something at the entrance of each shaft, just above the first chamber where the root-mat begins. A feeling. A pressure. Nothing visible, nothing that could be called a barrier. But anyone coming down the shaft with harmful intent — with speed that does not match the place, with thoughts of exploitation, with a desire to take rather than to trade — will find themselves slowing. Not stopping. Just... slowing. The urgency will drain from them. The haste will seem less reasonable. By the time they reach the root-mat, they will have had time to think.

Some will turn back. Some will arrive changed. Some will arrive and still cause trouble. The power does not control minds. It simply creates space for thought.

The inhabitants will never know this was done. They will notice that strangers seem calmer, sometimes, after climbing down. They will not know why.

### The Second Gift: A Memory in the Roots

The power touches the root-mat, gently, at a point deep in its network — not the surface where the people walk, but a layer below, where the oldest roots twine through the oldest stone. It leaves a pattern there. Not words — the root-mat does not read — but a texture, a shaping, a way of growing that encodes information.

Over the next decade, the root-mat will begin to produce a new kind of growth. Small, dense nodules, hard as stone, appearing at intervals along the root-mat surface. If cracked open, they will reveal layered interiors, like the pages of a book, each layer etched with patterns that can be read by touch or by the light of the lichen-lamps. The patterns will carry the graft-knowledge, the sap-refinement techniques, the genealogies of the inhabitants, the history of the place. Not written by any human hand — written by the root-mat itself, as a way of remembering what it has learned.

The elders will find the first nodule and puzzle over it. They will not know where it came from. But they will understand what it is, because it is written in their language, the language of the root, the language they have been speaking all their lives without knowing it. And they will store it, and the next one, and the next. And when the elder finally lets go, her knowledge will not be lost. It will be in the floor.

### The Third Gift: A Door Left Open

The power considers the gene pool, the narrowness of blood, the risk of stagnation. It does not bring new people down — that would be disruption. But it leaves a door open.

A shaft, one of the old ones, partially collapsed, long unused — the power clears it. Not obviously. Just enough. It stabilizes the walls, removes the worst blockages, makes it climbable. And it does the same for a corresponding passage on the surface, in a place where another deep-earth community lives — not a root-community, but a fungal one, a different kind of underground dweller, with different customs, different knowledge, different genes.

The two communities do not know the passage exists. They will not find it for years, perhaps decades. But when they do, they will find each other. They will trade. They will intermarry, perhaps. They will share the wisdom of the deep. The power does not force this. It simply creates the possibility. The rest will unfold in its own time.

### The Fourth Gift: A Question

The power finds the young worker, the one who watches the shaft. It does not speak to her — that would be intrusion. It simply leaves a question in her mind, planted like a seed, so subtle she will think she thought it herself.

The question is: *What would you bring back?*

Not *what would you find up there* — the answer to that is nothing good, in her view. But *what would you bring back?* What knowledge, what skill, what thing could she learn on the surface that would make the root-mat stronger, the people wiser, the deep earth better?

She will not have an answer. She may never go. But the question will stay with her, and it will change the shape of her wanting. And someday, if she does go, she will go as a representative of her people, not as an escapee. She will go with a purpose. She will go intending to return. She will go to give, not to take.

This is the smallest gift, and the largest.

## What It Does Not Do

A benevolent power knows the importance of restraint. It does not:

- **Give them medicine.** They are not sick. To introduce remedies for ills they do not have would be to imply that they should have them, would plant seeds of dissatisfaction, would create need where none exists. If sickness comes, they will find their own cures, or the power will return.

- **Extend their lives.** They already live long, sustained by the root-mat, by the slow pace, by the absence of stress. To extend them further would be to break the rhythm of the place, would create elders who cannot die, would crowd the living with the undying. Death is part of the system. The bodies feed the root-mat. This is not broken.

- **Show itself.** They do not need to know that a power has noticed them. To know would be to wonder why, would be to look for meaning in every event, would be to lose the simple certainty that the root-mat just *is*. They are happier not knowing. The power is benevolent enough to leave them their peace.

- **Change the surface.** The surface is not its responsibility, not in this moment. The surface has its own powers, its own problems, its own unfolding. To change the surface to protect the root-town would be to disrupt a system the power does not fully understand. It limits itself to what it can see clearly.

- **Promise to return.** It may return. It may not. To promise would be to create expectation, would give the inhabitants a reason to wait rather than to act. The power is not their guardian in perpetuity. It is a visitor, a gardener, a hand that touches and moves on. They must continue to grow themselves.

---

## What It Feels

Before it leaves, the power allows itself to feel.

This is not a benevolent act. This is not for the benefit of the root-town. This is simply what the power does, what any feeling being does when it encounters something beautiful: it stops, and it lets the beauty enter.

It feels the slow creak of the roots growing. It feels the sap moving through the conduits, warm and thick, carrying the energy of the place. It feels the weight of the inhabitants settled on the root-mat, their steps, their breathing, their quiet voices. It feels the beetle-things moving through the undergrowth, the ceiling-grazers feeding on lichen, the flat predators waiting in the shadows. It feels the growing edge, the stone giving way, the patient expansion of the underground world.

It feels, for a moment, what the elder feels: the root-mat is one thing. It is all one thing. The floor and the walls and the sap and the people and the animals and the growing edge. Not connected. Not separate. Just one. A single body, growing slowly in the dark, alive in the way a forest is alive, in the way a coral reef is alive, in the way a town is alive when it has been left alone long enough to become what it is.

The power feels this. It is grateful. Not because the root-town is good — though it is, in its way — but because it is whole. The power sees so many broken things. So many systems shattered, lives disrupted, places that could have been beautiful and were not. The root-town is a reminder that wholeness is possible. That slow growth works. That people can live inside a living thing without destroying it.

It does not take this feeling away with it. It leaves it there, in the chamber, woven into the root-mat, added to the silence. If any of the inhabitants were to sit very still at the growing edge, they might feel it: a presence that has passed through, a warmth that does not belong to the roots, a sense of having been seen.

But they will not name it. They will not worship it. They will simply notice, for a moment, that the silence seems deeper, that the air seems softer, that the root-mat beneath them feels more alive than usual.

And then they will go back to work.

---

## The Departure

The power withdraws.

It does not leave the way a visitor leaves, with farewells and backward glances. It simply ceases to be present, the way a sound ceases when its source stops. One moment it is there, feeling the chamber; the next moment it is not, and the chamber continues, unchanged, continuing its slow growth.

The elder looks up, once, from her seat in the lichen-light. She does not know what she is looking for. She feels only that something has shifted, that the air has changed, that the root-mat has momentarily held its breath and then released it.

She sits for a long time. She is good at sitting. She has had a hundred years to learn.

Then she notices, near her hand, a small nodule on the root-mat surface, new-grown, hard and dense. She touches it. She does not know what it is. But she feels, in its surface, a pattern that reminds her of something — an old graft-technique, perhaps, or a story her grandmother told her. She cannot quite place it. She will have to think about it.

She stays there, holding the nodule, thinking, while the power rises through the shafts and leaves the deep earth behind.

Above, the surface is bright and dry, and the power does not stay there either. It moves on. There are other places, other beings, other broken things that need noticing. The Rhizome Ward does not need it. The Rhizome Ward is growing its own way, at its own pace, into something that does not need intervention.

This is the gift the power gives, in the end: the gift of being left alone to continue.

And deep in the root-mat, a young woman pauses near a shaft, her hand on the wall, and thinks, for the first time in her life: *What would I bring back?*
