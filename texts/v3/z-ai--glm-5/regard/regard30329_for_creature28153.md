# The Attention of Powers

## What a benevolent and wise power sees

It arrives at Yil not with arrival but with attention. There is no sound, no displacement of air, no warming of stone. The power does not enter space so much as it encompasses it, holds it for a moment in the way a thought holds an object of contemplation. The basin is known. Every molecule. Every slow metabolic process. Every call vibrating through warm water. Every electrical impulse rippling through the moss.

This is what wisdom means: to see a thing entirely, without the distortions of need or projection or fear. To see Yil as it is, not as it might serve or what it might become or what it lacks. The power looks and does not judge. It perceives.

And what it perceives is this: *completeness*.

The basin is not waiting. This is the first and most important understanding. The Callers are not calling for rescue. The thermotrophs are not growing toward some imagined better state. The moss is not sensing in hope of some future intervention. The ecosystem has reached an equilibrium that is, for its inhabitants, sufficient. Not perfect—perfection is a concept that only makes sense to minds that imagine alternatives. Sufficient. Complete. Stable across timescales that make human civilization feel like a held breath.

The power sees the structures, the technological remains, and understands their purpose in a way the researchers never will. But it also sees that the purpose is no longer relevant. The builders are gone, and what they built has been absorbed, has become something else, has found its place in an ecosystem that no longer needs whatever the builders intended. The technology serves the basin now, not the other way around. It provides warmth. The thermotrophs orient toward it. The Callers include its thermal signature in their songs, marking it as a landmark in their acoustic maps of home.

The power sees all of this. It sees the patience embedded in every process, the slowness that is not deficit but wisdom, the long accumulation of being that has resulted in a place at peace with itself.

And it must decide: what does benevolence require here?

---

## What benevolence does not do

A power that was only powerful but not wise might intervene. It might illuminate the basin, bringing light to creatures that have evolved for darkness. It might accelerate processes, making the slow metabolism of Yil faster, more productive by some external standard. It might "improve" the ecosystem—introducing new species, modifying existing ones, optimizing the thermal flows for greater energy extraction.

It might, in other words, treat Yil as a problem to be solved or an opportunity to be exploited. It might assume that its own values—efficiency, progress, growth—apply universally. It might mistake its own preferences for objective goods.

But this power is wise, and wisdom begins with the recognition that different beings have different goods. The Callers do not need light. They have evolved for darkness, and their language is built on vibration and resonance, not vision. To illuminate Yil would be to deafen them, to collapse the acoustic environment they use to navigate and communicate and find each other in the dark. It would be violence dressed as improvement.

The thermotrophs do not need faster growth. They have found a rate that matches the energy available to them, a sustainable pace that has kept the basin stable for longer than the power can easily calculate. To accelerate them would be to destabilize, to push the ecosystem toward a boom-and-bust cycle that would end in collapse. It would be destruction disguised as generosity.

The moss does not need to be made more efficient at communication. Its slow chemical signaling is not a flaw but a feature—a way of spreading information across the basin at a pace that allows for response, for adjustment, for the maintenance of equilibrium. To speed it up would be to strip the basin of its capacity to integrate change gradually. It would be chaos presented as progress.

The power sees all of this. It sees that the basin's apparent deficits—its darkness, its slowness, its sparse distribution of life—are not deficits at all. They are adaptations. They are wisdom accumulated over evolutionary time, encoded in the bodies and behaviors of the beings that live here. To change them would be to unmake what Yil has spent epochs becoming.

So the power does not change anything. It recognizes that benevolence, in this case, requires restraint.

---

## What benevolence might still offer

But restraint does not mean absence. A benevolent power, having given its attention, having seen Yil completely, might still choose to offer something—not because the basin needs it, but because giving is part of what benevolence does. The question is: what can be given that does not take away?

The power considers.

*Witnessing*, perhaps. The simple gift of being seen. Yil has existed for so long without being known by any mind that could hold its wholeness. The researchers who descend are limited by their sensors, their brief visits, their human scales of perception. They see pieces. They guess at patterns. They cannot hold the entire basin in a single act of comprehension. But the power can. It can know Yil as Yil knows itself—if such a place can be said to know—and in that knowing, offer a kind of recognition. *You are here. You are this. You are seen.*

But this is a subtle gift, one that might not matter to beings who do not conceive of being seen. The Callers do not wonder if anyone is watching. The moss does not hope for an observer. The power's witnessing is a gift that matters more to the giver, perhaps, than to the given. It is still a gift—benevolence does not require reciprocity—but it is a quiet one.

*Protection*, perhaps. The power could extend its attention into a kind of shielding, a boundary around Yil that would prevent harm from entering. Not intervention within the basin but protection from without. It could ensure that no future development on the Sholan Plateau would encroach. It could discourage the researchers from taking too much, from disturbing too deeply. It could hold the basin safe from the kinds of disruption that civilizations sometimes bring to places they do not understand.

But this, too, is complicated. To protect is also to constrain. If the basin were to naturally change—if the thermal flows shifted, if the climate altered, if some external force threatened that was not malevolent but simply natural—would the power's protection prevent the ecosystem from adapting? Would it freeze Yil in a state that prevented its further becoming? The basin has survived this long without protection. Perhaps its greatest strength is its ability to absorb change, to integrate disruption slowly, to find equilibrium again after perturbation. To shield it might be to weaken it.

The power considers more carefully.

---

## What the power actually gives

In the end, after holding Yil in the entirety of its attention, after seeing every process and pattern, after weighing the possibilities of intervention and restraint, the benevolent and wise power gives two things.

The first is *possibility*.

It does not change anything. But it leaves open a door. A small addition to the technological remains in the basin—not visible, not audible, not sensible by any creature who does not know to look for it. A capability embedded in the ancient structures, dormant until needed. If the basin were ever truly threatened—if the thermal flows began to fail, if the water were poisoned beyond the ecosystem's ability to process, if some catastrophe approached that exceeded Yil's considerable capacity for absorption—the technology would activate. Not to impose the power's will, but to stabilize. To maintain the conditions for life. To hold the basin's own values when the basin could no longer hold them itself.

This is a deep benevolence: to give only what might be needed, only when it is needed, only in the form that serves rather than supplants. The power trusts Yil to know what Yil needs. The gift is a backup, a safety net, a possibility folded into the existing structures like a letter sealed and placed in a drawer, to be opened only if the time comes.

The second gift is *listening*.

The power leaves behind an attention that does not withdraw. Not intervention, not protection, not watching in any way that intrudes—simply listening. The moss's chemical signals. The Callers' songs. The slow steady vibration of the thermotrophs growing toward warmth. The power continues to hold Yil in its awareness, not to act, but to know. To be present, in the way that a friend is present to a sleeping child—not to shape its dreams, but to be there if it wakes.

This matters because it is the one thing the basin might actually feel, in some dim way, if feeling is something it does. The moss might sense the slight change in the electrical field, the faint addition of something attending. The Callers might notice a new quality in the acoustics of their calls, a resonance that suggests they are being heard by something that understands them. The gift of listening is the gift of *matterance*—the affirmation that what happens here matters, that the slow conversations of amphibians in a dark pool are worth attending to, that the patient growth of heat-eating plants is worth knowing.

The power does not expect gratitude. It does not require acknowledgment. It gives these things because giving them is what benevolence does when it encounters something worthy of care. The gift is in the giving, not in the receiving. Yil may never know it has received anything. But it has.

---

## How Yil responds

Perhaps the basin does feel something, in its slow, distributed way.

The moss notices first. There is a new quality to the silence, a presence that does not press but holds. The electrical patterns ripple differently across the colony—not in alarm, but in something that might be, if moss could feel, a kind of settling. The basin has always been known to itself. Now it is also known to something else. The knowing does not change anything. But it is there.

The Callers continue their songs. But the researchers who visit—those few careful souls who descend with sensors and patience—notice a shift in the patterns. A new complexity. The calls seem to reach further, to resonate more clearly, as if the acoustics of the basin have subtly improved. The Callers do not know why their voices carry better now. They only know that they do, and they incorporate the change into their slow conversations, adjusting their maps, their thermal catalogues, their long communications about the state of their world.

The thermotrophs do not notice anything. They grow toward warmth at their millimeters-per-year pace, absorbing the energy gradients that have always sustained them. But in the warmest places, where the technological remains still hum with their ancient purpose, there is a slight increase in the heat output—not enough to change the ecosystem, but enough to be exploited. The plants grow slightly faster there. The creatures that orient toward warmth cluster slightly more densely. The gift of possibility has already begun to serve, even in its dormant state, simply by existing.

The researchers do not understand what has changed. They document the shifts—the clearer acoustics, the slightly elevated thermal output, the new calm that seems to have settled over the basin's inhabitants—but they attribute these to natural variation, to the long cycles of an ecosystem they have not yet learned to predict. They do not know that a power has passed through. They do not know that anything has been given.

But they do notice one thing, though they do not have words for it. When they stand in the dark of Yil, when they hold their sensors still and simply listen, they feel a presence. Not something watching them—something watching *the basin*. An attention that encompasses them but is not for them. A care that holds the whole place, the slow life and the warm water and the ancient technology and the dark that has weight, in a single act of knowing.

They feel, for reasons they cannot explain, less alone. Not because the power cares for them, but because they are standing in a place that is cared for. They are within the beam of an attention that does not require them to be anything other than what they are: brief presences in a long-stable world, temporary witnesses to a completeness that has always been here.

---

## What remains after the power departs

The power does not stay. Its attention moves on, to other places, other beings, other worlds that might need different forms of benevolence. But it does not withdraw entirely. The listening remains. The possibility remains. The gifts have been given and they continue to give, even in the absence of the giver.

Yil does not change in any visible way. The basin is still dark. The walls still weep. The Callers still call in their low resonant voices. The thermotrophs still grow slowly toward warmth. The moss still senses every footstep, still ripples its chemical signals across the colony, still tells the basin where everything is and what everything is doing.

But something is different now. The basin is held. Not constrained, not protected, not improved—held. Known. Attended to by a mind that can comprehend its wholeness and has chosen, out of all the possible interventions a power might make, simply to let it be what it is.

This is what wisdom looks like. This is what benevolence does when it encounters something that is already complete. It sees. It appreciates. It offers the smallest gifts that serve rather than supplant. And then it withdraws, leaving behind only a listening, a possibility, a care that does not intrude but does not end.

Yil does not know that it has been given anything. But if the basin could know—if the moss could understand, if the Callers could comprehend, if the slow thermotrophic life could conceive of being known by something other than itself—it might feel a gratitude that has no words. The gratitude of being seen as one is. The gratitude of having one's completeness recognized. The gratitude of receiving, from a power that could change everything, the gift of being allowed to remain exactly as it is.

---

## A final note

The researchers who study Yil have a saying, passed down among the few who choose this strange specialty. They say: *The basin does not need us.* They mean it as a reminder of humility. They mean that their work is observation, not intervention. They mean that Yil has survived for millions of years without them and will survive for millions more after they are gone.

They are right. The basin does not need them.

But they do not know that someone else—something else—has come to the same conclusion and stayed longer than they ever could. They do not know that Yil is held in an attention that spans galaxies and will span ages. They do not know that the basin's autonomy is guaranteed by a power that could strip it away but has chosen, instead, to protect it.

They do not know that the basin does not need them because the basin already has everything it needs. Including, now, a benevolence that asks nothing in return.

They stand in the dark, listening to the Callers thrum, feeling the warm water rise around their boots. They document. They measure. They wonder. And far above them, in the rim of the basin, the ancient technology hums with its new addition, its folded possibility, its promise to activate only if activation becomes necessary.

The gift has been given. The listening continues. Yil endures, complete in itself, known by something that sees it whole.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it encounters a place like Yil and the beings who live there. Not nothing. Not everything. But the right thing. The small thing. The thing that honors what already is.

And in the dark, the Callers call, and the moss senses, and the slow life of the basin continues its long patient being, held now in a care that does not touch but does not let go.
