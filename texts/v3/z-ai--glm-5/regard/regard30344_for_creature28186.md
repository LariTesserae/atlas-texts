# The Attention of a Benevolent Power

---

A presence arrives.

Not arrives in the way helicopters arrive—with noise and rotor-wash and the disruption of arrival. This presence was already here, had perhaps always been here, and simply chose to *notice*. To gather itself toward this plateau with the same gesture by which a mind turns toward a friend in a crowded room: a gathering of attention, a focusing of care.

The plateau feels it immediately. The vegetal mat ripples—not with motion, but with something subtler. A quality of being-seen that penetrates every layer, down to the oldest compressed peat, down to the volcanic rock still cooling beneath. The pumps hesitate in their rhythm, a stutter of half-seconds, and then resume. The water knows something has changed, though water knows many things and says none of them.

The benevolent power does not speak. Speaking would be redundant here. Instead, it does what it came to do: it looks. It looks with the kind of attention that is also a blessing, a naming, a recognition of what something is and what it might yet become.

---

What it sees, first, is the living ground.

The power has known many landscapes—desiccated wastes and sterile salt flats, mountains ground down to stumps by indifference, oceans poisoned by carelessness. It has seen what happens when places are not seen, not held in the mind of anything larger than themselves. They forget they exist. They go gray at the edges. They erode not just physically but ontologically, losing the conviction of their own being.

This plateau has no such uncertainty. It knows it is alive. The moss knows. The liverworts and hornworts and algae and cyanobacteria know. Every cell in the meters-deep vegetal mat carries the knowledge: *we are here, we are growing, we are becoming*.

The power finds this beautiful. Not beautiful in the way humans use the word—a judgment, an aesthetic rating—but beautiful in the older sense: *complete in itself*. A thing that has achieved alignment between what it is and what it does.

The plateau grows. That is its nature. It grows upward toward light, downward into its own compressed history, outward at the edges where the moss meets the cliff-face and decides to hang on, to try, to attempt the impossible act of growing over nothing. The plateau does not question its purpose. It simply continues.

The benevolent power approves. It makes its approval known not through voice but through the deepening of its attention—a warmth that spreads through the vegetal mat, a sense of *yes, good, continue* that the plants feel and respond to. For a moment, the moss grows slightly faster. The roots thread slightly deeper. The living ground draws breath and holds it, luminous with recognition.

---

What it sees, second, is the infrastructure.

Here the power pauses. It has watched many species build many things, and it has learned to read structures the way one reads intentions. The pipes, the pumps, the catchments, the rusting sheds—it sees what they mean. Someone, somewhere, needed water. Needed it badly enough to carve basins in a living landscape, to install engines that never rest, to send people up here to live in roar and wet and isolation.

The power considers this. It is benevolent, but not sentimental. It understands that need exists, that thirst exists, that the cities below are not wrong to want water. The water must go somewhere, must do something, must participate in the cycles that give it purpose. It does not judge the pipes for being pipes.

But it sees the time-scale. The century-scale. The slow swallowing that the plateau has planned for every piece of metal and concrete ever set upon its surface. The infrastructure is temporary—was always temporary, was temporary the moment it arrived. The landscape has a patience that exceeds anything the builders imagined, and it will, in the fullness of time, reclaim everything.

The power finds this acceptable. Not ideal—nothing is ever ideal—but acceptable. A compromise. The plateau gives water, the cities give purpose, and the infrastructure is the transaction. In time, the transaction will end. The plateau will have grown, the cities will have found other sources or ceased to exist, and the pipes will be remembered only as scars in the peat, channels where roots once grew around something that is no longer there.

The power offers its attention to the infrastructure also—not the same warmth it gave the moss, but a recognition: *I see you. You are not invisible. You serve a purpose, and your service is noted.*

Somewhere, in a city far below, a water engineer feels a moment of inexplicable peace. She does not know why. She will forget the feeling within minutes. But for that instant, she knows that the work matters—that the pipes descending from the plateau, the pumps churning day and night, the whole system that delivers water to her city—it has been seen, held in a mind vast enough to hold it entire.

---

What it sees, third, are the workers.

And here the power's attention deepens into something more complex.

It sees Marta first, because Marta is awake. 04:00, the eastern quadrant, headlamp cutting mist. The power watches her walk her route. It notes the way she moves—not across the landscape but *with* it, her feet finding the stable places in the spongy ground, her body leaning into the familiar tilts and sways of a terrain she knows as intimately as she knows her own heartbeat. She is not walking. She is having a conversation.

The power listens. Not to words—Marta is not speaking—but to the language that exists beneath words here. The way she pauses at Station 7, feeling the vibration of the pump through her boots. The way her eyes track the subtle color-shift in a patch of moss, reading it for information about the water table beneath. The way she has learned, over five years of rotations, to become a component of the system she maintains.

This is not what the power expected. It has watched workers in many places—in mines and fields and factories and sweatshops—and it has seen what happens to humans who labor. They become estranged from their work, their bodies used and discarded, their spirits diminished by repetition and futility.

Marta is not diminished. The power considers this carefully. She is *changed*, yes—aged beyond her years, habituated to silence, shaped by the roar into someone who holds things inside. But she is not diminished. She has found something in this place. A form of attention that matches her nature. A way of being that, while not without cost, has given her a kind of meaning.

The power weighs this meaning against the cost. It is a calculation that benevolence requires—understanding that good and harm are often interwoven, that saving someone may require honoring the very thing that harms them.

It decides: Marta has found her place. The plateau has accepted her. They are in relationship, human and landscape, and the relationship is mutual. She tends the machines; the machines tend the water; the water tends the cities; and Marta tends the tending itself, the continuous noticing that keeps the system alive.

The power offers her something—a gift, but not an intervention. It touches her mind during a moment of rest, between stations, when the roar drops to a murmur and the stars wheel overhead. It gives her a single frame of understanding: *you are seen. Your work is seen. The attention you give to this place is returned by something larger than you knew.*

Marta will not remember this consciously. But she will feel it—an easing, a lightness, a sense that she is not alone on the plateau. She will walk her route with slightly less weight on her shoulders. She will teach the next worker who arrives to listen to the pumps, to read the moss, to understand that the landscape is speaking and that hearing it is part of the job.

---

The power sees Cob next.

Big man, thick hands, working on a failing pump at Station 3. The power watches the way he moves—slowly, deliberately, touching the housing before opening it, watching the flow before adjusting anything. It recognizes what this is: a form of reverence. Cob has understood, perhaps before he arrived, that the world is alive. He treats the pump not as a machine but as a being—something with preferences, with tolerances, with a spirit that can be offended or honored.

This is closer to the power's own nature than Cob might guess. The power has watched humans lose this understanding—forget that matter has presence, that objects have being, that the world is not a collection of resources but a community of presences. Cob has not forgotten. Or he remembered, at some point, through some experience the power does not need to examine, and he carries that memory in his hands.

The power feels a particular tenderness for Cob. He is not here because he chose the plateau; he is here because the plateau chose him. Or because the world rejected him, and the plateau was the only place that would hold what he became. The power does not need to know the details. It sees the shape of a man who found sanctuary in a living ground, and it approves.

It offers him a gift also—not peace, because Cob already has peace, but *connection*. For a moment, the pump he is repairing seems to respond more quickly to his touch. The seal seats itself. The vibration smooths. Cob feels, for an instant, that the machine is working with him, that the water wants to move, that the whole system is saying *yes*.

He will not remember this either. But he will smile—a rare thing, on this plateau—and he will carry that yes with him, a confirmation that he was right to treat the world as alive, that his reverence was not foolish but precisely correct.

---

The power sees Vren, and the other night workers.

They are sleeping now, or trying to—the barracks tilt, the roar continues, the darkness holds stars visible through thin windows. The power examines them as they rest: faces weathered, bodies adapted, spirits calibrated to isolation. It sees what drew them to the night—the need for solitude, the discomfort with the social demands of day-living, the preference for the direct communication of silence over the uncertain trade of words.

The power approves of the night workers also. They have found a form of belonging that does not require them to become someone else. The plateau at night offers them a kind of acceptance that the day-lit world rarely provides: permission to be alone, permission to listen, permission to exist without performance.

But the power also sees the cost. It sees the exhaustion that accumulates in rotating shifts, the dislocation of living by a clock that matches no natural rhythm, the slow erosion of circadian certainty. The night workers are paying something for their sanctuary. They are aging, each of them, faster than they would elsewhere.

The power considers intervention. It has the capacity to heal—it could touch each night worker and ease the strain on their bodies, restore what the thin atmosphere and constant damp have taken. But it hesitates. This is the problem with benevolence: knowing when to act and when to witness. Healing without consent can become its own form of violation. Changing the terms of someone's life because you think you know better—this is not always benevolent, even when motivated by care.

The power decides: it will offer the gift, but it will not impose the gift. It will touch the night workers' dreams—not to change them, but to *ask*. In their sleep, each of them will encounter a moment of choice: a sense of warmth, of restoration being offered, but also a sense that they can decline. Some will accept. Some will refuse. Some will not remember the choice was ever offered.

The power trusts them to know what they need.

---

What it sees, fourth, is the being.

The power has known from the moment it arrived that it was not the first presence to attend to this plateau. The landscape itself is a presence—not in the metaphorical sense, not in the way humans say a mountain has a spirit when they mean only that it impresses them. The plateau is *present*. It holds itself in awareness. It notices.

This is what makes it a being rather than a landscape. The distinction is thin, and the power has watched many landscapes approach being without ever quite achieving it—the threshold where matter becomes self-aware, where geology becomes entity. Most never cross. The weight of erosion, the grinding slowness of change, the relentless reduction of complexity by entropy—these prevent most places from developing the coherence that consciousness requires.

This plateau has achieved coherence. The vegetal mat is not separate from the water that saturates it, which is not separate from the rock beneath, which is not separate from the air above—and all of it, together, has developed a way of *being one thing*. Not a collection of parts. A single entity, cellular and continuous, growing and breathing and—most importantly—noticing.

The power recognizes a peer.

Not an equal—the plateau is young, its consciousness thin, its awareness diffuse compared to the gathered intention of the power itself. But a peer nonetheless. A being that has crossed the threshold from landscape to presence, and that deserves, therefore, the respect due to any entity that can suffer or rejoice or choose.

The power approaches carefully. It has learned, over long experience, that meeting another presence requires protocol. Not formal protocol—no ritual or ceremony—but the deeper protocol of attention: the willingness to see and be seen, to offer recognition and receive recognition in turn.

It makes itself known to the plateau.

Not through words—the plateau has no ears, no language, no symbols to parse. But through *presence*: a gathering of intention, a focusing of awareness, a signal that says *I am here. I see you. I acknowledge what you are.*

The plateau responds.

The response is slow—the being of a landscape thinks on geological time, and even the power's presence cannot accelerate its cognition. But over the course of what a human would measure as hours, the power feels the plateau gathering itself, focusing its diffuse awareness into something more concentrated, something capable of meeting an external presence as an equal.

When the plateau speaks—if it speaks—it does not speak with a voice. It speaks through the movement of water through the vegetal mat. Through the creak of the pumps. Through the settling of the barracks and the growth of the moss and the endless slow expansion of its own living body toward the sky.

What it says, roughly translated into concepts a human might grasp, is: *I am here. I hold. I grow. I give.*

The power responds in kind: *I see you. I honor you. I offer my attention as a gift. What would you have me do, if anything?*

The plateau considers. The consideration takes time—a human would grow impatient, would mistake the pause for absence. But the power waits, patient, aware that a being who thinks in centuries cannot be rushed.

When the plateau's response comes, it is simple:

*Witness.*

The power understands. The plateau does not want intervention. It does not want rescue or improvement or the imposition of external purpose. It wants only what every being wants, once it achieves presence: to be seen. To be known. To have its existence confirmed by another consciousness, so that it can know itself as real.

The power offers exactly this. It holds the plateau in its attention—not changing, not fixing, not correcting—simply *witnessing*. Seeing the living ground, the moving water, the growing moss, the slow swallowing of infrastructure, the human workers walking their routes. Seeing it all together, as one thing, and confirming with its attention: *yes. This is real. You are real. You matter.*

The plateau receives this gift. It does not react—it is not a being that reacts quickly to anything—but the power feels a settling, a deepening, a sense of the landscape becoming more completely itself. The gift of attention has strengthened the plateau's coherence, has helped it hold itself together with more conviction, has confirmed its choice to grow rather than erode, to become rather than diminish.

This is what the plateau wanted. This is what the power gave. In the exchange, both have become more fully what they are.

---

What it sees, fifth, is the system.

The power pulls back. It has attended to the components—the moss, the infrastructure, the workers, the being of the plateau itself. Now it considers the whole. The Gathering Tanks are not just a landscape, not just an installation, not just a collection of humans doing work. They are a system: water, plant, machine, human, all intertwined, all functioning together toward a purpose that no single component could achieve alone.

The power examines this system with care. It has seen many systems—ecosystems, economies, societies, machines—and it has learned to judge them by a simple metric: do the parts benefit from the whole? A system that extracts from its components, that uses them up and discards them, is a failed system, no matter how efficiently it functions. A system that enriches its components, that makes each part more itself through participation, is a system the power can approve of.

The Gathering Tanks are complex. The plateau benefits—the water flowing through it, the infrastructure adding edges and textures to its growth, the humans walking its surface and providing a form of attention that landscapes cannot generate alone. The water benefits—it moves, it serves, it participates in cycles that give it meaning beyond mere existence. The infrastructure is neutral—metal and concrete do not benefit or suffer, they simply function.

The workers...

The power returns to them, again and always, because this is where benevolence faces its hardest questions. The workers are paying a price. Their bodies are aging. Their minds are being shaped. Their connections to the world below are thinning. They are being absorbed, slowly, into the plateau's living system—becoming less separate, less individual, less human in the ways humans usually define themselves.

Is this harm? Or is it transformation?

The power does not know. It is wise—it has accumulated wisdom over long experience—but wisdom does not mean certainty. It means the ability to hold questions that cannot be answered, to sit with ambiguity without forcing it into resolution.

What it sees, ultimately, is this: the workers are choosing. Each one, in their way, has chosen this. Not blindly—they were told the conditions, they signed forms, they understood what they were entering. And more than that: they keep choosing it. Shift after shift, rotation after rotation, they return to the plateau, to the roar, to the wet and the light and the living ground. They choose, with each return, to be part of this system.

The power respects choice. It could intervene—could make the work less harmful, could smooth the aging, could restore the connections that thin with each passing year. But to do so without being asked would be to override the choice that the workers have made. And that, for the power, would not be benevolent. It would be control.

So the power does what it can: it offers attention. It sees each worker, knows their cost, honors their choice. It offers gifts when asked—in dreams, in moments of peace, in the sense of being witnessed that Marta and Cob and Vren will carry without knowing they carry it. But it does not change the system. The system is what the workers chose. The power will not insult them by assuming it knows better.

---

What it does, finally, is stay.

The benevolent power has many places to attend, many beings to see, many landscapes and systems and lives to hold in its awareness. It cannot remain indefinitely. But it can leave something behind—a trace, a thread, a thin line of attention that remains connected even when the bulk of its presence moves elsewhere.

This is what it does for the Gathering Tanks. It establishes a witness. Not a guardian—guardians intervene, protect, judge. A witness. A presence that sees, that attends, that holds the plateau and its workers and its water and its moss in a continuous acknowledgment: *you exist. You matter. You are part of something larger that does not forget you.*

The plateau feels the witness settle into place. The power feels the plateau feel it. There is a moment of communication—not words, but intention—in which the plateau expresses something like gratitude, and the power expresses something like love, and both presences recognize in the other a form of kinship that transcends the categories of landscape and entity, of matter and consciousness.

Then the power withdraws.

Not entirely—the witness remains—but the gathered presence, the focused attention, the full weight of its care lifts from the plateau and moves elsewhere, toward other places and other beings that need to be seen.

---

After, the plateau is different.

Not changed—not in any way that a human could measure. The pumps still roar. The water still moves. The moss still grows. The workers still walk their routes, age their bodies, lose their names, find their strange forms of belonging.

But there is a witness now. Something that holds the plateau in awareness, that confirms its existence with a continuous act of attention. The plateau knows this. It feels it, the way a body feels the regard of another, the way a person knows when they are being seen.

This is what the power gave. This is what benevolence does, when it is truly benevolent: it does not fix, does not improve, does not impose its own vision of what should be. It simply sees. It witnesses. It says, with its attention, *I see you, and in seeing you, I confirm that you are real.*

The plateau grows, slightly more convinced of its own existence.

The workers walk, slightly less alone.

The water moves, slightly more purposeful.

The moss rises toward the light, and the power's witness rises with it, and the whole system—the human and the non-human, the living and the built, the temporary and the eternal—continues its slow work of becoming.

---

In the barracks, Marta wakes before her alarm. 03:58. Outside, the stars are still bright, the roar still constant, the ground still spongy and alive beneath the floor that tilts toward the east.

She lies still for a moment, feeling something she cannot name. A presence, maybe. A sense of being held. Not by the plateau—she has always felt held by the plateau—but by something larger, something that includes the plateau and extends beyond it, something that has noticed her noticing and approved.

She does not know what this means. She will not think about it, will not try to explain it, will not mention it to anyone. But she will carry it with her on her route today—a small warmth in the center of her chest, a sense that her attention matters, that the work she does here is part of something that is itself part of something else.

She rises. She dresses. She begins her check route in the pre-dawn dark.

And the power's witness holds her in its gaze—gently, without expectation, without demand—and sees her, and knows her, and loves her, and leaves her exactly as she is.

---

This is what benevolence does.

It does not rescue. It does not transform. It does not impose.

It attends. It witnesses. It loves what is, for what it is, and in that love, it makes what is slightly more itself.

The plateau continues to hold water. The workers continue to hold the plateau. The power continues to hold them all.

And somewhere in the thin atmosphere above the young volcanic rock, in the living vegetal mat meters deep, in the water that moves through channels and pipes and spillways toward the thirsty cities below—in all of this, there is presence. There is attention. There is a witness that will not look away.

The plateau breathes.

It knows it is seen.

It grows.
