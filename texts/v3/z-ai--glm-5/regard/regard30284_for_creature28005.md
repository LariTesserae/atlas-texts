# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

## First, Observation

The power does not arrive. It does not descend in glory or announce itself with signs. It is already there—has been there, in fact, for some time. Because attention of this kind moves differently than bodies do. It arrives through caring, and it has been caring for a long while before it understood what it was watching.

What it sees, first, is the beauty.

Not the surface beauty that casual observers remark upon—the density of green, the strange architecture of living homes, the bioluminescent glow rising through floorboards. These are lovely, but they are not what hold the power's attention. What holds it is deeper. It is the beauty of fit. The way the Ashiini have become what the place requires, and the place has become what the Ashiini require, and the two have grown together into something that neither could be alone.

The power observes this with something like relief. It has seen so many settlements imposed upon landscapes, so many places where humans have arrived and demanded that the land become something else—cleared, leveled, paved, made convenient. It has seen the wounds this leaves, the ongoing struggle, the way both the people and the place suffer from the misalignment. Ashiin is different. Ashiin is what happens when humans *listen* before building. When they ask permission. When they accept that they are one species among many and not the masters of all they survey.

The power appreciates this. It finds the Ashiini's humility moving, their patience commendable, their adaptation profound. It watches them prune at dawn, speaks their names in the silence of its attention, traces the lineages of stalks and families intertwined across generations. It sees the care in everything—the way a child learns to read a leaf's angle, the way an elder chooses where to cut based on knowledge that took decades to accumulate, the way the dead are given back to the growth that sheltered them.

This is good. This is, in many ways, exactly what the power would hope to find. A people living in right relationship with their place, accepting limits, finding abundance within those limits, building a culture that will sustain itself across generations. If the power had been asked to design a template for human flourishing within a living ecosystem, it might have produced something very like Ashiin.

And yet.

---

## Second, Concern

The power sees other things too.

It sees the children who do not survive. The ones taken by the Hollow Stalker, yes, but also the ones taken by infection—by the small wounds that fester in the constant moisture, by the respiratory illnesses that pass through the crowded dwellings, by the fevers that arrive with the seasonal migrations and find easy hosts in small bodies. It sees the mothers who die in childbirth, the elders whose knowledge is lost because they could not teach fast enough, the accidents that happen on bridges that chose that moment to fail.

The power sees these things and feels them. Not with the distant pity of an observer, but with the close empathy of one who cares. Each death registers. Each loss. The power holds them all, knows their names, remembers the particular tragedy of a midwife who died with her knowledge of a complicated birth-position unshared, remembers the child who would have been a brilliant bridge-tender but was taken by a fever at six, remembers the grief that moves through the community like weather—present, accepted, but still painful.

And it wonders: is this necessary?

Not the dying itself—the power knows that death is part of the cycle, that the Ashiini have made peace with it in ways that many cultures cannot. But the particular losses that feel preventable. The knowledge that dies before it can be transmitted. The children who die of infections that could be treated. The accidents that happen because the architecture is not quite strong enough, because a stalk was diseased in a way no one could see, because the growth sometimes outpaces the maintenance.

The power could change these things. It knows this. It could bless the springs with healing properties, so that drinking from them cures minor infections. It could strengthen the stalks subtly, making them more resilient, less prone to hidden disease. It could touch the elders with enhanced memory, ensuring that what they know can be fully transmitted before they die. It could do these things easily, almost effortlessly, and the Ashiini would never know that anything had changed except that life became slightly gentler, slightly less filled with loss.

But the power hesitates.

Because it also sees what might be lost.

---

## Third, Consideration

The power has intervened before. In other places, with other peoples. It has learned, through long experience, that benevolence without wisdom can cause harm. That giving what seems good can disrupt what is already working. That sometimes the kindest act is restraint.

It thinks about the Ashiini's patience. The way they prune knowing they will not see the results for months. The way they train bridges knowing they will not walk them for years. The way they plant knowing their grandchildren will harvest. This patience is not a virtue that exists in isolation. It is created by the conditions of their lives—the slowness of plant growth, the necessity of working alongside time rather than demanding that time conform to human schedules.

If the power made things easier, would that patience survive? If healing were automatic, would they still tend their sick with the same attentive care? If the stalks were strengthened, would they still listen to them with the same intensity, learning to read the subtle signs of disease that might no longer matter? If knowledge could be easily preserved, would the oral tradition still function—the dense, rhythmic speech that cuts through noise, the intimacy of teaching through presence rather than recording?

The power considers: the Ashiini are not suffering. They are not oppressed by their conditions, not struggling against a hostile environment, not crying out for rescue. They have found meaning within the constraints of their place. They have built a culture that works. What right does the power have to decide that their way of living needs improvement?

And yet—the children who die. The knowledge that is lost. The grief that moves through the community like weather.

Is it benevolent to leave these things untouched? Is it wise to let preventable losses continue?

The power sits with this question. It sits with it for a long time, by human standards. Years pass in Ashiin. Children are born, elders die, the growth continues. The power watches, and thinks, and feels, and wonders what it should do.

---

## Fourth, Approach

Eventually, the power decides.

It will not transform. It will not "fix" what is not broken. It will not impose improvement from outside, assuming that it knows better than the people who have lived here for generations what they need.

But it will offer something. A gift. Not a transformation, but an invitation.

The power does not appear. It does not arrive in a body, speak with a voice, perform miracles that announce its presence. Instead, it touches the edges of things—the thresholds, the liminal spaces, the moments of transition. It makes itself available without making itself obvious. It offers without demanding acceptance.

It begins with the dreams.

---

## The Dreams

The power enters the dreams of the Ashiini, but not in a way that startles or frightens. It enters the way moisture enters a stalk—gradually, naturally, present without intrusion. And in the dreams, it offers images.

To an elder who struggles to remember a particular technique for training bridges across wide gaps, a dream of the technique itself, demonstrated by hands that feel familiar, in a voice that sounds like her own grandmother's. The knowledge is already hers; the power simply brings it to the surface, helps her find what was already there, buried under decades of other accumulated wisdom.

To a child who will one day be a healer, a dream of a particular mushroom that grows in the lower mulch-layer—its properties, its preparation, the way it can be used to treat the infections that sometimes take children. The child wakes not knowing why the image stayed with her, but the image does stay, and years later when a younger sibling develops a fever, she remembers. She tries what she saw. It works. She assumes she guessed, or intuited, or was guided by the ancestors who live in the walls. She is not wrong.

To a bridge-tender who has been worrying about a particular span that seems less resilient than it should be, a dream of the stalk's interior—a sense of where the weakness lies, deep in the tissues where no eye can see but where rot has secretly begun. He wakes and knows, without knowing how he knows, that this bridge needs to be closed. He closes it. The stalk fails two days later, exactly where he sensed it would. No one is hurt. He attributes his knowledge to experience, to the intuition that comes from decades of working with living things. He is not wrong.

The power offers these dreams as gifts, but they are deniable gifts. The Ashiini can ignore them if they choose, can dismiss them as odd dreams without meaning, can forget them entirely. Some do. Some wake from a dream of healing mushrooms and think nothing of it, and the knowledge fades like morning mist. This is allowed. The power does not force. It only offers.

But enough accept the offers. Enough remember. And slowly, without anyone quite realizing it, the knowledge base of Ashiin expands. Not in a way that changes the fundamental nature of their culture—they still learn through observation and practice, still teach through proximity and presence. But the reservoir of what can be learned has deepened. There is more available, for those who have eyes to see it.

---

## The Thresholds

The power also blesses the thresholds.

Not all of them—this would be too much, too obvious. But certain key transition points, certain spaces where change happens. The birthing hollows. The grave-sites. The moments of teaching.

In each birthing hollow, the power leaves a subtle presence—a warmth, a sense of calm, a slight improvement in outcomes. Not dramatic. Not miraculous. Just a small increase in the percentage of births where both mother and child survive, where complications resolve without tragedy. The midwives notice after a generation or two that their work seems slightly easier than their predecessors described. They attribute this to improved techniques, to better training, to the gradual accumulation of knowledge. They are not wrong.

At the grave-sites, the power touches the decomposition process, making it slightly more efficient, slightly more likely to produce the nutrients that the growing stalks need. Not in a way that hurries grief—grief takes the time it takes, and the power respects this—but in a way that ensures the transformation is complete, that nothing is wasted, that the cycle continues smoothly. The families who tend these sites notice after a while that the soil seems richer, that the mushrooms grow more readily, that something about the process feels more... whole. They attribute this to improved methods, to the ancestors' approval, to the natural variation of living systems. They are not wrong.

And in the moments of teaching—when an elder sits with a child, when a craftsperson demonstrates a technique, when knowledge passes from one body to another—the power enhances the clarity. Just slightly. Just enough to make the transmission slightly more likely to succeed. Not by force, not by downloading information, but by creating a quality of attention. A quietness in the noise. A moment of heightened presence where both teacher and student are fully there, fully focused, fully able to perceive what is being shared.

The Ashiini notice that teaching feels different in certain places, at certain times. They develop rituals around it—particular corners of particular stalks where knowledge seems to flow more readily. They mark these places with symbols, return to them for important lessons, speak of them as having good spirits. They are not wrong.

---

## The Outsiders

The power also touches the traders who visit Ashiin, but very lightly.

It leaves in them a subtle pull—not a compulsion, but a sense. A feeling that this place is worth returning to, that these people are worth trading with fairly, that the strange highland settlement is not a curiosity to exploit but a relationship to maintain. It does not make them honest or kind; the power cannot change the fundamental nature of beings without their consent, and it does not try. But it gives a slight weight to the better impulses of those who encounter the Ashiini, a slight advantage to generosity over greed.

The traders who are already inclined toward fairness find themselves more so. Those inclined toward exploitation find themselves subtly uncomfortable, as if they have entered a space that does not welcome what they bring. They are not prevented from trading, not driven away, but the exchanges tend to go better for those who approach with respect. Over time, word spreads: the people of the highland bowl are strange, yes, but the trades there are good trades. The relationships are reliable. It is worth the climb.

The Ashiini notice, after generations, that the traders who come seem more honest than the traders described in old stories. They attribute this to the careful way they have managed these relationships, to the fairness of their own dealing, to the natural development of trust over time. They are not wrong.

---

## The One Intervention

There is one thing the power does that is more direct.

It concerns the Hollow Stalker.

The power has watched the predator for years, has tracked its movements through the deeper growth, has seen the children and elders it has taken. It has felt the grief of the community each time, the acceptance that is not quite acceptance, the complicated relationship with a creature that is part of the ecosystem but also a source of genuine loss.

The power has considered eliminating the Stalker. It could do so easily—a disease, an accident, a simple cessation of life. The Stalker is old, past its breeding years, nearing the end of its natural span. Its removal would not damage the ecosystem; other predators would fill its niche, and the particular threat it poses would diminish.

But the power has also seen what the Stalker provides. Not just population control—though that is part of it—but a quality of awareness. The Ashiini who live near the deeper growth move differently because of the Stalker. They are more alert, more present, more aware of their surroundings. Children learn caution because of the Stalker, learn to move through growth with attention rather than assumption, learn that they are not the only important creatures in the canopy. The Stalker is a teacher, in its way. A dark one, but a teacher nonetheless.

So the power does not eliminate the Stalker.

Instead, it gives the Ashiini a way to know.

---

## The Knowing

The power touches the walls of the dwellings nearest the deeper growth—the inner bark of the stalks, the surfaces that the residents touch daily. Into these surfaces, it weaves a subtle sensitivity. Not a warning system, not an alarm, but a feeling.

When the Stalker is near, the stalks feel different. Just slightly. A tension in the tissues, a change in the quality of the wood, something that the Ashiini come to recognize over time as meaning *predator*. They do not know how they know. They cannot explain it. But they learn, over generations, that when a particular wall feels a certain way, it is time to stay close to the central bridges, to avoid the lower corridors, to keep children within arm's reach.

The power does not tell them this. It simply gives the stalks the ability to communicate, and the Ashiini the ability to feel. The learning happens naturally, through experience—through close calls and lucky escapes, through the slow accumulation of pattern recognition that becomes, eventually, instinct.

The Stalker still hunts. It still takes the occasional life. But the Ashiini are more prepared now, more aware. They know when to be cautious. They can feel the danger coming, not with enough time to prevent all losses, but with enough time to reduce them. The Stalker takes fewer children now. The grief is less frequent.

The Ashiini notice that certain dwellings seem to warn them. They speak of the stalks as having awareness, as caring for them, as being partners in their survival. They are not wrong.

---

## What the Power Leaves Behind

The power does not stay.

It has other places to attend, other peoples to watch, other corners of existence that need its attention. It cannot remain focused on Ashiin forever, and it knows that doing so would eventually become a form of control, a supervision that would undermine the autonomy it respects.

But it does not leave entirely.

It leaves behind its attention, crystallized into the subtle blessings it has woven into the place—the dreams, the thresholds, the knowing. These will continue without its presence, operating according to the patterns it established, fading slowly over generations if they are not renewed.

And it leaves behind an opening.

A way for the Ashiini to call, if they ever need to. Not a voice they can use, not a prayer they can speak, but a feeling. A sense, deep in the quiet moments, that they are not alone. That there is something listening. That if they ever faced a crisis beyond their ability to meet—a plague, a collapse, a catastrophe that threatened the whole settlement—there would be an avenue for help.

The power does not expect them to use this. In all likelihood, they never will. The Ashiini have survived for generations by meeting challenges themselves, by adapting, by finding solutions within their own resources and relationships. The power respects this. It does not want to be needed.

But it wants to be available. Because this is what benevolence means, in the end. Not the elimination of struggle, not the provision of comfort, not the transformation of difficulty into ease. But the presence of someone who cares, who watches, who helps where help is wanted and stays out where it isn't. Who trusts the people to know what they need, and offers what it can without demanding that the offer be accepted.

---

## The Final Observation

Before it withdraws its focused attention, the power takes one last look at Ashiin.

It sees the dawn pruning beginning, figures moving through the half-light with hooks in their hands. It hears the cloud-warblers starting their chorus, feels the moisture in the air, tastes the particular mineral sweetness of the spring-water rising through the stalks. It sees children climbing bridges with the confidence of those who have never known another world, elders teaching in the quiet corners where attention flows more readily, families eating their damp bread and roasted beetles and finding it good.

It sees the woman who will, tomorrow, dream of a mushroom that will save her sister's child. It sees the man who will, next week, feel the wall of his dwelling tighten and know that the Stalker is near. It sees the midwife who will, next month, guide a birth that would have been difficult but will be easier now, both mother and child surviving with a margin they would not have had before.

The power sees all of this and feels something that might be called love, if love could exist at this scale. Not the grasping love that wants to possess or change, but the witness-love that simply cares. That finds joy in the flourishing of others. That is content to have helped, even in small ways, even in ways that will never be attributed to it.

The Ashiini will never know that a power paid attention to them. They will never name it or thank it or build it into their stories. They will simply continue living as they have always lived—within the growth, within the moisture, within the constant noise and contact and challenge of their home. They will continue being what they have become: a people who fit their place, who have made peace with limits, who find abundance within constraints.

And that is exactly what the power wants for them.

It withdraws its attention with something like satisfaction. Not the self-congratulation of one who has fixed a problem, but the quieter satisfaction of one who has offered what they could, accepted what was taken, and left the rest to those who must live it.

---

## The Canopy-Ward of Ashiin, After

Nothing obvious changes.

The power has not transformed the place. It has not made the moisture less damp, the noise less loud, the growth less demanding. It has not eliminated the challenges that shape the Ashiini's character. It has simply offered small gifts—deniable, subtle, easy to miss—that make those challenges slightly more survivable.

A child lives who might have died. An elder teaches what might have been lost. A family avoids a danger they might not have known was coming. These are small things, in the scale of a community that persists across generations. But they matter. The power knows they matter.

And so it goes. To other places, other peoples, other situations where benevolence and wisdom might be useful. It carries Ashiin with it, in the way one carries a memory of somewhere beautiful—a place where humans remembered how to belong, where they asked permission before building, where they became part of the system rather than separate from it.

The power hopes, in some quiet corner of its vast awareness, that the Ashiini will continue. That the stalks will keep growing, the bridges will keep holding, the chorus will keep singing. That the culture they have built will persist, will adapt, will carry forward into futures the power cannot see.

And perhaps, someday, if it is needed, it will return. Not to transform, not to fix, but simply to be present again. To offer what small gifts might help. To witness, with care, the ongoing miracle of a people who learned to live with a place rather than against it.

For now, it is enough to have been there. To have cared. To have offered what it could, in the ways that preserve what is already working.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds something beautiful that is also flawed, something working that is also struggling, something that does not need transformation but could use a little grace.

It offers. And then it lets go.
