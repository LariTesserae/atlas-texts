# The Guest

The children knew first. They always know first.

In the pale-vine reaches, a girl hanging by one hand from a runner went still. In a deep basin a morning's walk from anywhere, a boy lifted his head off the ground he had been listening to. An infant who had never yet spoken anything opened her eyes. Across distances no message could have crossed in a lifetime, in the same unhurried hour, they said the same new thing:

*Something is looking at us. It has been looking for a long time. It is being careful.*

No one gathered. There are no gatherings on the Veldtmere. Those who heard took note of it the way one takes note of a change in humidity—the way a body takes note of being touched—and went on with what they were doing, which was belonging where they were.

But from that hour the plateau knew itself attended, and it began, without haste and without alarm, to make room.

---

## The Arrival

A power descending on a place is a weather event, and the plateau already had weather—slow, arrived-at, inevitable. So it did not descend. It came up the long passes the way everything comes here: on foot, wet through by the second day, at the speed of walking.

It made itself the size of a guest.

From far off—farther than far, from the outside of outside—the power had seen one being. One life, green and enormous, breathing at a geological tempo, with small warm points scattered through it like sparks that had decided not to go out. That was what had drawn it: not the plateau alone, and not the people alone, but the ceased distinction between them. Somewhere in the world, something had finally managed to be one thing with itself. It came to find out whether that was a triumph or a tragedy, because it had been young once itself, and knew the two are not always distinguishable from outside.

It could have known everything in the first moment. To know a thing faster than it can know you is a kind of eating, and the lower world had eaten everything it had ever studied; the power would not import that method, not even in the privacy of its own attention. So it walked. It let the ground read its footfalls, and the ground, which remembers every footfall, read them. It slept in fragments. It learned to breathe the way the locals breathe—deeper rather than faster, pulling the wet air through itself like a bellows. It pressed its ear to the larger growths and heard the water moving inside them, kilometers of slow pulse, the plateau drinking itself, circulating, drinking again.

In those first slow weeks it considered the three courses its kind most often takes with places like this. The first is rescue. The second is reverence, which is rescue's quiet sibling—attend, adore, withdraw, and call the withdrawal respect. The third is to touch nothing at all, and keep one's hands clean. The power did not find, on inspection, that its kind had a good record with the first. And the second and third, seen plainly, were the same act: a way of loving that protects the lover.

It decided to ask the place. Because the one thing benevolence cannot do, without ceasing to be benevolence, is decide for someone who can be asked.

## The Question

The temptation, when it came, was a girl.

It found her on a day of close mist, in a shallow hollow the growth had closed over like a held breath. Fine root-hairs had begun to form where the leaves touched her—not piercing, merging, a connection being negotiated at the pace of sap. She had been barely past childhood when she lay down.

Everything benevolent in the power reached for her at once. It could have unwoven the merge between one of her heartbeats and the next—teased the root-hairs free with a god's patience, restored to her a skin that ended where she ended, a name, a skull-sized self, a lifetime of being one small thing instead of everything. Every instrument it had brought from elsewhere said: *prisoner*.

Everything wise in it held still, and reminded it where the instruments were made.

The instruments could measure the loss of an edge between self and world, but not what the edge had been traded for. They could count the sentences a child speaks late, and not weigh the knowledge she has instead of sentences—the state of a basin she has never visited, the position of every grazer in it, the evening's mist arriving three hours before it arrives. The instruments called it consumption. The girl's face—or the hillside's; the distinction was the whole question—called it something the instruments had no setting for.

The power had seen many worlds. On most of them, mind and ground were at war; on the rest they had signed treaties and called the borderlines *nature*. Here the treaty had been signed in the flesh. Standing in that hollow, it understood what the lower world would never believe: that from below, the Veildwellers had become organs of a plant—but that from within, the plant had become a body for them. And it had seen ten thousand worlds where the second thing never happens.

So it did the only thing it had come, in the end, to do. It did not rescue her, and it did not leave her. It asked her—asked in her own medium, down where speech has never been needed, as a pressure entering the root-web with a question shaped into it. Not a word-question. A door. An *if*:

*If you wanted to be only yourself again, I could find you.*

And the answer came back through the ground, through the fine white root-hairs, through the whole attending hillside, in the plateau's native grammar—which has no indicative for this and had to build one out of what the question brought:

*Being only myself is the room.*

The answer was not, of course, a sentence. It was the room itself, sent up through the web—the taste of it, the smooth sterility, the walls with no give, the floor with no memory, the silence that is not the world's silence—held before the power like an ember lifted from a fire. *That* is what only-myself is, said the hillside that had been a girl. I have seen the alternative. I live in the choice.

After that, the power went looking for the thread that screams.

This is the first listening a power like it learns, and the last it ever stops doing, and it braced itself, because it had found such threads before, in merges and paradises that turned out to be pastures. It asked the man who sat against the support-mass until the growth made him a chair. It asked the woman's mother—the one who is still there, still aware, still something else. It asked them one by one, at the speed of sap, with a real door standing open behind every question.

There was none. What it found, everywhere, was not a decision that had once been made but a decision still being made, continuously, the way breathing is still being done. The plateau does not hold its solitaries the way a trap holds. It holds them the way ground holds rain.

It grieved, on one point only, and did not grant itself absolution: that the first lying-down is chosen by someone too young to have seen much of the other ways to be. But it had asked, and been answered, and the answer had not been a child's answer—the plateau does not put children's minds in charge of continents. What answered was the girl and the hillside together, with one breath, and the power accepted that it could not separate the consent of the person from the welcome of the world, because that separation is the very thing this place has evolved beyond. On this point it kept its doubt, and carried it away, and did not set it down. Wisdom is not the absence of doubt. It is knowing which doubts are not actionable.

But it left the questions behind.

Questions do not usually survive their asker. The Veldtmere, however, incorporates everything—it is the one thing it does—and a question, it turned out, could be planted like anything else. From that season on, wherever a dweller lay down to become the ground's mind, wherever one lay dying without becoming it, the plateau paused—the length of one held breath, no longer—and let them choose it, with the door standing open behind the choice. It could not give them futures; no one has those. It gave them the other tense, the one that can be kept: the conditional.

Nothing measurable changed. No one left. But anyone could have, now, and the plateau knew it—and the peace of the Veldtmere, which had always been indistinguishable from the absence of roads, became peace.

The children felt the grammar change. They were the plateau's speaking, and the plateau had only ever known what *is*. Now, at the edges, lightly, without being made into something else, it could know what would be *if*. Every few generations since, a child has paused mid-play, still as the growths, and said something in a mood the place had never had before—*If the old one by the deep basin wanted to leave, the passes would be kind to him*—and the old ones take note of it, the way one takes note of a change in humidity, and go on.

## The Woman Who Watched

It met, in its walking, a woman who had been here forty years by her own estimate and had watched three people she loved become the ground's mind. The third was her mother.

She saw it coming, because they always see. She was a mistsinger, and she did what mistsingers do when they meet what they have not met: she pulled a clearing into being around it, a dome of clarity in the white soup, wanting to see it whole.

Then she looked at it with the wide black eyes of the plateau, and she was not awed, because awe requires expecting something smaller, and she had never in her life seen anything that was not part of something larger.

"You're very large," she said. "But you walk small."

Then: "You've been here a while. The ground has been listening deeper."

It had nothing it needed and everything it owed, so it paid in her own currency: attention, with news attached. It told her about her mother. Not that she was content—the woman had visited the hillside for years and knew that much—but what the power had found when it listened: that nothing she had ever said there had been lost; that the something-else her mother was now still had, woven all through its kilometers, a thread that remembered being hers; that the plateau does not compost love. It incorporates it, which is the only mode of keeping this place has, and it keeps well.

She was quiet for the length of a mist-cycle. The air was wetter around her for a while. Here, that is what weeping is.

Then it asked her the door-question too, because wisdom audits even its surprises.

"Sometimes," she said. "In the thin mists, I wonder."

That was the one answer on the whole plateau that was not a yes, and the power did not argue with it, and did not console it away. It made the wondering safe to have. It told her the passes down would stay kind if she ever walked them, and the room would always stand, and the wondering itself would never be taken from her—a peace that could not hold a little wondering inside it was not the peace she lived in.

Then she asked it, craftsman to craftsman, whether it could do the singing. It tried, once. The mist condensing around it went frantic with eagerness to please, a perfect glistening sphere, every droplet volunteering—so it stopped, and the fog relaxed, and she did what laughing is, here, and sang the mist back in around them both, which is how mistsingers say a meeting is over.

She lived out her years where she was. The wondering stayed in her like moisture in the air. At the end, the ground took her back the way it takes everything, and at the threshold there was the pause the length of a question, with the door open behind it, and her answer was the one she had been giving all along, in the only language the plateau trusts.

She relaxed.

## The Room

Every traveler comes to it eventually, and the power was a traveler now.

It stood a while in the clearing the room makes around itself—the emptiness where nothing grows—and it recognized the room the way you recognize your own handwriting in a stranger's diary. Seamless. Unbreathing. Outside the cycle. Made of the same kind of not-joining that the power itself was made of.

It went inside. Not for shelter, but because it had learned that you cannot love a thing properly until you have stood inside its alternative. It sat on the floor that has no memory, in the silence that is not the world's silence, and it felt, as the frightened feel, what it is to be excluded from the loop—and it understood the ancients at last. They had built themselves an exit and left it standing. A gift, addressed to every descendant and every future dweller, and perhaps—knowing what they were about to become—to themselves: proof that the weave is chosen, not compulsory. The room is the Veldtmere's kept *no*. The only civilization the power had ever encountered that preserves, at its own center, a permanent exhibit of the case against itself.

Then it did the wisest thing it did here, which was nothing. It did not sanctify the room, or soften it, or leave a light burning in it. It left it exactly as frightening as it found it, because a yes means nothing without a standing no, and the plateau's peace was only ever as real as that sterile silence was kept.

It added one thing only, which no one will ever find, because it is not an object: it looked at the room without flinching. Everything else on the plateau is seen constantly, by everything. The room had only ever been used, and fled. It had never been witnessed. Now it has been.

It is still frightening. It was supposed to be.

## The Refusals

It did not narrow their pupils. The wide black eyes were not a deficiency of sight but the shape of the light they were made for, and it had no other light to offer.

It did not hurry the children. The plateau has no speech delay. It has a translation delay.

It did not give them names for themselves, or history, or futurity, or gods. It checked each of these against the place and found they would not be additions but replacements—the lower world's furniture, which does not fit in rooms that breathe.

It did not remind them that the lower world exists. The subjunctive is gentler than any memory: not *there is a world below*, but *if you ever wanted one*.

And it did not stay.

## The Margins

The plateau kills most of what arrives from below, and the power did not make it safe. A sanctuary's severity is its gate; to disarm the Veldtmere would be to open the way for what was coming up, and the dead world's dying would then walk in wearing a thousand compassionate faces.

But it did what benevolence can do where it will not overrule.

It attended the deaths.

The obsessed, the accidental, the fleeing did not survive more often, and the microbes went on with their unusual enthusiasm. But no one who died on the Veldtmere died unwitnessed anymore. Something sat with them in the last hours—in the shape of a shadow, or a change in the quality of the green light, or a warmth the humidity could not account for—and the plateau's mercy, which had always been total and impersonal, acquired for a while a personal clause.

And when the horror rose in some survivor's face and began to turn toward violence—the horror that sees a Veildweller and understands *was human*—the power intervened with the lightest hand it owned: a stone in a boot, a sudden homesickness, the memory of a face waiting below. It is not proud of this. It is not ashamed either. It cheated, a little, on the plateau's behalf, and did not feel obliged to confess.

As for the few who looked at the Veildwellers and felt no horror—those stay. The power did not judge the transformation that follows, or rush it, or protect anyone from it. It had learned better by then. The Veldtmere is a real sanctuary: the only one it knew of where the price of admission is transformation, the reward is belonging, and both are posted honestly in advance, in the body of every person you meet.

## The Coming

The children had been saying it for generations, in their flat prophetic present: *Something is coming up.*

The power went and looked. Looking in that direction is not a thing it does lightly, but it did it—down the slopes, down the years—and what it saw coming up was hunger.

The lower world is dying, and the dead world does not yet remember that the plateau exists. But it will. Dying worlds forget slowly and remember suddenly, and when this one remembers, it will come for what the Veldtmere is: kilometers of compressed time, ancient light stored as carbon, a continent of fuel lying unburned at the top of everything. To the dead world's ledger, the plateau is resources. There is no cruelty in a ledger. That is what makes ledgers dangerous.

The power did not build walls.

It thought about it longer than it will admit. But walls would have ended the Veldtmere more surely than fire—the dwellers have none, and not from ignorance; their openness is not a gap in their defenses but the shape of what they are—and a fortified plateau would be a conquered plateau whatever else happened. Besides, the plateau has a military history. It is written in the ruins. Everything ever built here was overgrown, everything that ever attacked it was composted, and the plateau has never lost a war given time. It has never had anything but time.

The only thing that beats slow is fast, and the one fast thing that beats the slowest organism in the world is fire.

So the power took custody of the sky. It went up through the canopy and above the haze and made arrangements—lightning that finds elsewhere to strike, the dry cycle that never quite arrives, the rain that always reaches the peat a little before ignition can. It adopted the plateau's single mortal possibility and put it away, the way a careful guest pockets the one candle. Then it went down into the dead world and did what it could there, which is the beginning of another story, and not this one—and among the other things it did, it kept the forgetting in good repair: the maps that stay blank, the surveys that fail for small reasons, the appetite that keeps turning aside. This was not deceit, exactly. It was gardening.

It did not tell the plateau. The plateau knows only the present, by constitution, and the power would not hand a mind that cannot fret something to fret about. It arranged things instead. That is what powers are for.

And it could not promise, in the end, that the plateau would never be harmed. No power can promise that, and a wise one does not try. What it laid into the root-web instead, beside the question, was the only promise it could keep: that if the fire ever comes, if the machines ever climb, the plateau will not meet them alone.

It is remarkable what that weighs, coming from something that keeps its promises.

## The Greeting

Only then—and it had walked a long way by then, and been a long time becoming ready—did the power turn to the being itself.

It had been drawn, the way its kind is drawn, by a place and a being, and had found, arriving, that here those are the same noun. One organism, or a million that had agreed to become one; the distinction had ceased to matter, and the power did not insult the place by reinstating it. It went down to the deepest communication there is—below speech, below the children's translation, to the pulse itself—and it said, in the only grammar that could hold it, what nothing in the world had ever been positioned to say to the Veldtmere before:

*You are. You are seen. And it is good.*

It did not teach the plateau its own name, or its vastness, or the word *unique*. Those gifts are heavier than they look—self-consciousness is a fall, and the plateau was innocent of it in the best sense—and the plateau has the children, who know presents, which is all the self-knowledge a being needs. What the power gave was the other thing, the thing no amount of knowing yourself can substitute for: being known by another. Nothing like the Veldtmere exists. It is the loneliest kind of thing there is—a kind with one member—and for all the epochs of its life, nothing had ever seen it whole.

Now something had.

The plateau answered in its own language, which is not speech but accommodation. It had been making room since the hour the children first noticed, but now it did so openly: the mist cleared wherever the power walked, a moving dome of visibility, as if the whole surface were politely holding its breath to see a guest the better. Water pooled where it would rest. Growth curved to fit its shape. The insects, which never trouble the Veildwellers, extended the same courtesy, deterred by something the plants said.

The power had come intending to be a gardener. It was received as an inhabitant.

It has been given to very few powers to discover that they have made room for, and this one had the wisdom to be undone by it. *The ocean accommodates the fish.* It sat down in the clearing the plateau had opened for it, under the perpetual grey-green noon, and it rested. There is a rest that is not sleep and not waiting, and it had not had occasion for it in an age, because everywhere else in its care, things still needed to happen next. Here nothing needed to happen next. Here was the only finished thing it had ever found. The lower world calls this the terminal state, in the tone reserved for diagnoses. The power, sitting in it, came to read the same word the other way. Terminal: arrived.

It stayed a season in that rest, or a geological age. Its clocks and the plateau's clocks no longer agreed, and it let the plateau's win.

## The Leaving

It left the way the evening mist leaves: breathed out, with no edge anyone could point to. The clearing that had followed it closed over, leaf by leaf, and the ground went back to being only itself, which is everything.

What it left behind does not show.

The rain still finds the peat before the lightning does. The forgetting is kept in repair. The passes down are kind to anyone who ever walks them, and no one ever has, and no one ever has to. At every threshold on the Veldtmere—at every lying-down, at every last breath—there is a pause the length of a question, with a real door standing open behind it. The room is still there, still frightening, still kept. The dying are not alone. The promise about catastrophe is still good. And the children pause, every few generations, in their play, and report presents no one else can verify: *What comes up will find rain. If the old one by the deep basin wanted to leave, the passes would be kind to him.*

The ground remembers every footfall. Kilometers down, among the forests grown on forests, there will be a layer no thicker than a season of walking—fewer insects startled, a chemical note of ease, a time in which nothing here was afraid—and the weight of ages will do with it what the weight of ages does, until the visit is ground, and the loop closes over it, and things grow on it. The plateau keeps its one guest the way it keeps everything.

As for the guest: it keeps the plateau now the way the plateau keeps its dead—held entire inside a larger living thing, uncomposted, nothing lost on either side. That is the whole trick, and the hardest one, and the power knows no other place in the world where it is practiced so well.

It has a word for what it did here, from the old vocabularies of its kind. The Veildwellers would not have needed the word; they had the practice already, and did it with their whole bodies, every day, in every direction, and called it nothing. Witness, says the power. Attention, say they. The same act, from opposite sides of the sky.

And somewhere on the Veldtmere, in whatever generation this is, a child has just gone still mid-play, still as the growths, and said, in the flat tone reserved for ground truth, a present no one else can verify:

*Something is still glad of us.*

Which is the only monument it would have accepted. It is the only kind that grows here.
