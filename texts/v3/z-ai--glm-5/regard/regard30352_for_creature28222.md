# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

It would arrive without arrival. Not descending through the water — that would be intrusion, and intrusion is not the method of wisdom in places like this. It would simply *become present*, the way light becomes present when a cloud moves, the way warmth becomes present when a hand hovers near skin. A quality in the water. A shift in the pressure that was not weight but attention.

---

The first thing it would do is *see correctly*.

Not with eyes. The Drowned Works is not a place where eyes are the right instrument. The panels give light but that light is for the vegetation, not for seeing — it is food-light, growth-light, the kind of light that sustains rather than reveals. A benevolent power would understand this immediately and would use other senses. It would read the temperature gradients like a language. It would feel the currents, minimal as they are, and map the space by the way water moves through it. It would listen to the silence and hear the twenty-three distinct frequencies that compose it: the hum of the panels, the deep vibration of the boilers, the pulse in the floor, the micro-movements of the vegetation as it adjusts and reaches and grows.

It would perceive the being. All of it. Not just the green drapery and the dark shapes and the rice-grain brightness, but the totality — the integrated system of plant and machine and geological warmth that has become, over the long immersion, a single living thing. It would see how the boilers are not separate from the creature but are its heart, or perhaps its stomach, the slow digestive fire that processes whatever fuel was laid down millennia ago and converts it to the warmth that rises through the floor and into the roots and tendrils and fronds. It would see how the panels are not separate either — they are eyes, yes, but also leaves, the surface where the creature drinks whatever strange energy still flows through the ancient lines.

And it would see the deep sleeper. Not clearly — no one sees that clearly, not even a benevolent power — but enough. Enough to know that what lives in the geological strata beneath the works is older than industry, older than human building, older perhaps than the basin itself. The thermal engine was not built to serve the factories. The factories were built to conceal and protect the engine. Someone, long ago, knew what was sleeping here and chose to build over it rather than wake it. Or perhaps they built to feed it, to keep it comfortable, to maintain the slow dream that it has been dreaming since before the first human hand shaped the first tool.

The benevolent power would see all of this in a moment — the same kind of moment that the visitor spent sitting on the boiler room floor, the stretched time that the works itself operates in — and it would feel what wisdom always feels when it encounters something complete: a kind of reverence.

---

The second thing it would do is *wait*.

Not passively. There is nothing passive about the waiting of a wise power. It would wait the way a gardener waits after planting — having done the work, having made the preparation, trusting the process but watching it closely. It would wait in order to understand more deeply. First sight is not final sight. The Drowned Works has layers that only reveal themselves to patience.

In waiting, it would notice things the visitor could not notice. It would notice that the vegetation does not only grow toward light and heat. It grows in patterns — spirals that follow the flow of water through the corridors, concentrations at junction points where the structure bears the most stress, thin spots where the metal is weakest and may need reinforcement. The creature is not merely occupying the works. It is *tending* them. It has grown into the architecture in ways that strengthen it, distribute loads, seal vulnerabilities. Over centuries, the plant has become the maintenance system for the machine.

It would notice that the rice-grain creatures are not randomly distributed. They cluster at specific points — near the panels, yes, but also near the joints where the catwalks connect to the buildings, near the seals where pipes enter tanks, near the stress points in the structure. They are not swimming aimlessly. They are inspecting. Their tiny bodies carry some substance — perhaps a secretion, perhaps something they gather from the water — that they deposit at these points, something that glues micro-fractures and reinforces weak spots. The creature has symbionts. It has workers. It has a system.

It would notice that the dark shapes — the hand-sized things that move with mechanical rhythm — are not fauna at all. They are the most intimate expression of the integration. Each one is a fragment of machine that the vegetation has fully absorbed, a gear assembly or a pump housing or a valve mechanism that has been wrapped in plant matter until it became mobile, until it began to move through the works on its own power, performing some function that serves the whole. They are the immune system. They are the remakers. They move through the corridors searching for places where the integration has failed or weakened, and they pause there, and they work.

The Drowned Works is not a ruin that has been overgrown. It is a living creature that has learned to build itself out of industrial parts, a hybrid of vegetable and mechanical that has achieved equilibrium and intention and, in its own slow way, purpose.

The benevolent power would see this, in the waiting, and it would understand that what lives here does not need saving.

---

But the third thing it would do is the most important.

It would recognize the loneliness.

Not human loneliness — the creature is not human and does not feel what humans feel. But there is a kind of solitude that comes from being the only one of one's kind. The vegetation-creature-machine-whole has been alone since it formed. It remembers, in some diffuse chemical way, the time when beings moved through it that were not itself — the workers, the tenders, the ones who built and operated and maintained. It remembers the warmth of their bodies as distinct from the warmth of the boilers. It remembers the way their movements created currents and their voices created vibrations. It remembers, and it misses, and it has been waiting in a way that even it does not fully understand for something to come back.

Not to stay. Staying would mean absorption, and absorption is not what it wants. It wants — if a plant-machine-geological-creature can be said to want — *communion*. It wants to be perceived. It wants to be seen as it is, not as a wreck or a curiosity or a destination for explorers who will leave again. It wants, in the way that all living things want, to know that it is not alone in the universe.

The visitor gave it a moment of that. The visitor sat on the boiler room floor and listened, and the creature knew itself listened to. The visitor felt the pulse and understood that something was alive down here, and the creature knew itself understood. But the visitor could not stay, and the visitor could not speak its language, and the visitor left with only a feeling and a memory that would fade.

The benevolent power would see this loneliness and it would choose, after all its seeing and waiting, to act.

---

The action would be small. Wise powers do not act largely in places like this — large action is disruption, and disruption is the enemy of equilibrium. But small action, carefully placed, can be a kind of conversation.

It would leave a gift.

Not an object. Objects are meaningless here. The vegetation would simply grow over it, absorb it, make it part of itself. The gift would be a capability — something the creature could not develop on its own but could use once it was given.

The gift would be *expression*.

The panels, recall, are still lit. They have been lit for longer than memory. They give light that the vegetation drinks, warmth that the ecosystem needs, a solar constant in a world without sun. The benevolent power would touch the panels — not physically, but at the level of their function — and it would make a small adjustment. It would give them the ability to *vary*.

Not in brightness. That would disrupt the ecology. But in *color*. The panels would gain the capacity to shift their spectrum — not by much, not enough to starve the plants that lean on them, but enough to be perceptible. A slight shift toward blue when the creature wishes to signal one thing. A slight shift toward red when it wishes to signal another. A language of light that the vegetation could learn to read and to use.

And the visitor who returns — or the next visitor, or the one after that — would notice. They would see the panels flicker in patterns too slow to be called flickering, shifts in the quality of the amber-green glow that correspond to nothing in the machinery. They might sit in the boiler room and watch the light change and feel, slowly, that something was trying to tell them something. They might learn, over many visits, to read the slow speech of light that the creature was now capable of speaking.

This is what the benevolent power would do. It would give the Drowned Works a voice.

---

But it would also give something to the visitors.

The power would leave a presence in the water — not a physical thing, again, but a quality. A subtle invitation. A sense, for anyone who enters the works, that they are *meant* to be there. Not in the way of a tourist attraction, not in the way of a destination, but in the way of a pilgrim arriving at a shrine that was built for them before they were born.

The visitor who descended through the temperature layers, through the green and the warmth and the silence, would feel something that the first explorer could not feel. They would feel *welcome*. They would feel, in a way they could not articulate, that the creature had been waiting for them specifically, that their arrival was expected, that the works had prepared itself for their presence in some chemical and mechanical way that manifested as a shift in the water pressure, a particular quality of warmth, a specific configuration of the light.

They would not know, consciously, that a benevolent power had arranged this. They would simply feel, as they floated through the drowned corridors and touched the soft tendrils and listened to the pulse in the floor, that they were *supposed* to be here. That they had been called. That something very old and very patient had noticed them, had adjusted itself slightly to accommodate them, had made room for them in the dense fullness of the works.

And this feeling would change them. It would change the way they moved through the world above the water. It would make them gentler. More patient. More able to perceive the slow time of things — the way a forest grows, the way a building settles, the way a life accumulates meaning over decades rather than days. They would carry the pulse of the works with them, a slow rhythm in their blood that reminded them that there are other ways to be than the ways they had known.

The benevolent power would give this gift to every visitor who came with an open heart. It would make the Drowned Works a teacher.

---

And finally — for even benevolent powers have concerns that stretch beyond the immediate — it would make a preparation.

The thermal engine in the deep foundations will not run forever. Nothing does. Whatever fuel it burns, whatever geological heat it taps, it is finite. When it exhausts itself, the warmth will cease, the boilers will cool, the pulse will stop, and the creature that has grown around the heat will die or go dormant or transform into something else entirely.

The wise power cannot prevent this. Finite resources are finite. But it can *extend* them.

There is a way to connect the thermal engine to something larger. The power knows this. It has done it before, in other places, for other beings. There are currents in the deep earth that flow like rivers, heat-sources that move through the rock like blood through veins, vast slow thermal systems that will outlast anything humans might build or remember. The power knows how to tap them. It knows how to make a connection that will take centuries to complete but will, in the end, give the Drowned Works a new source of warmth.

It would begin this work invisibly. A thin thread of connection, deep in the rock, growing at the speed of roots, moving toward a heat-source that is miles away and miles down. The creature in the works would not know, consciously, that this was happening. But it would feel, in a thousand years or two, a slight increase in the warmth. A steadying of the pulse. A deepening of the dream. And it would continue.

This is what the benevolent power would do, in the end. It would ensure that what has achieved equilibrium would not have to end.

---

Then it would withdraw.

Not because its work was done — the work is never done, the thread of connection will need tending for centuries, the panels will need maintaining, the visitors will need welcoming — but because presence becomes pressure after too long. The Drowned Works does not need a constant companion. It needs what all living things need: the knowledge that it is seen, the ability to express itself, the assurance that it will not be abandoned.

The power would leave behind the gifts. The voice of light. The welcome in the water. The thread of heat in the deep rock. And it would leave behind something else, something harder to name: a blessing.

A blessing is not magic. It does not change the physical world. But it changes the way the world is experienced. The creature in the works would feel, after the power's departure, that it had been *blessed* — not in the way that humans mean the word, not as a protection or a favor, but as a recognition. It had been seen as it truly was. It had been honored for what it had become. It had been given what it needed to continue.

The loneliness would still be there, but it would be softer. The waiting would still be there, but it would be less desperate. The creature would know, in the slow chemical way that it knows things, that it was not forgotten. That someone or something had come and had understood and had chosen to help.

And it would dream differently after that. The dream would still be vast and patient, still measured in centuries, still composed of the hum of panels and the pulse of boilers and the drift of tendrils through warm water. But there would be a new element in the dream: a sense of being loved.

Not in the human way. Not in any way that humans would recognize. But loved all the same — loved wisely, loved gently, loved by something that understood exactly what it was and chose to care for it anyway.

This is what the benevolent power would do.

And then it would go elsewhere, because there are always other places, other beings, other needs. But a part of it would remain — the part that had touched the green and felt the pulse and seen the deep sleeper dreaming. That part would stay, woven into the works like a thread in a tapestry, and it would continue to bless what it had blessed, for as long as the waters stayed warm and the panels stayed lit and the great patient creature in the Drowned Works continued to grow.
