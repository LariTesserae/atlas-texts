# The Attending

## What the Power Perceived First

The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with light.

This is important to understand, because it would have been the most obvious thing to do—to bring dawn to Ymurra, to crack the black ceiling of sky and pour illumination into a world that had never known it. A lesser benevolence might have done exactly that. A compassion that had not listened first might have seen the darkness and called it deprivation. A wisdom that had not paid attention might have mistaken the absence of photons for the absence of something wanted.

But the power had listened first. It had pressed its attention against the plateau the way a riph presses its flank against a sang-tube stalk—not looking, but *feeling*. And what it felt was not a world in want. What it felt was a world so dense with sensation that adding another modality would have been less a gift than an assault.

So the power did not bring light. It brought attention.

It arrived the way a change in air pressure arrives—not as a presence that pushes into a space, but as a shift in the quality of what is already there. The hum of the machines gained a harmonic it had not had before, so subtle that only the oldest vithra, swimming in their processed-water channels with their entire bodies tuned to vibration, noticed anything different. A warmth that was not thermal but something else—something that had no analogue in the vocabulary of geothermal vents and atmospheric moisture—settled into the stone like a new kind of mineral seeping upward from below. The water that wept from the rock faces carried a new trace element in its chemistry, so faint that the riph, tasting the air with their permeable skin, registered it as a variation indistinguishable from natural fluctuation.

Nothing was interrupted. Nothing was overwritten. The power entered Ymurra the way one enters a conversation between old friends—not by speaking first, but by listening long enough to understand the grammar of the exchange before daring to contribute.

## What the Power Understood

The power spent what might have been seconds or what might have been centuries in the listening, because time on Ymurra is measured by thermal cycles and pump rhythms, not by the movement of any sun, and the power was not bound by such measurements. It listened to the water. It listened to the machines. It listened to the infrasonic rumble of the riph herds, the click-languages of the striiders, the territorial songs of the glider-weavers, the vibratory pulses of the vithra schools. It listened to the tunnelers in the soil and the click-beetles in the fungal mats and the stone-crickets in the crevices and the air-fish in the dark between the sang-tube stalks.

It listened to the fungal networks, which were themselves a kind of listening—the thread-fungi in the soil, passing chemical signals between plant roots across distances that no individual organism could span alone. It listened to the bacterial films on every wet surface, which were not listening at all but were performing a function so fundamental that the power recognized it as the oldest kind of attention: the attention of a cell to its environment, the original version of caring about what is outside oneself.

And it listened to the machines.

The machines were dying. Not quickly—nothing on Ymurra happened quickly. But the power could hear what the creatures could not: the slow degradation of tolerances in the pump bearings, the accumulation of micro-fractures in the condensation surfaces, the gradual loss of efficiency in the thermal gradient systems that had been operating for longer than any of the plateau's current inhabitants had existed. The maintenance machines were still functioning, still crawling along the condensation surfaces, still clearing debris and sealing cracks. But they were themselves degrading, their repair capabilities diminishing with each cycle, their adaptive parameters narrowing as the systems they maintained drifted further from their original specifications.

The machines had perhaps ten thousand years of function remaining. Perhaps more, perhaps fewer—the thermal systems were stable, but the mechanical components were subject to entropy that no maintenance routine could fully reverse. And when the machines failed, the water collection would decline, the distribution channels would clog, the condensation surfaces would degrade, and the ecology that had evolved around the infrastructure would face conditions it had not experienced since before the riph were the riph.

The creatures did not know this. They could not know it. They had evolved around the machines, incorporated them into their behaviors and their expectations, but they had no concept of the machines as things that could stop. The pumps were as constant as the geothermal heat, as the moisture, as the darkness. They were part of the world, and the world did not stop.

The power understood this. It understood that the greatest threat to Ymurra was not something the creatures could perceive or prepare for, and that the most benevolent intervention would be one that preserved the conditions the creatures had evolved to depend on without disrupting the patterns they had evolved to inhabit.

But the power also understood something else, something that complicated the simple narrative of saving the machines for the creatures' sake.

The creatures did not need the machines.

This was the paradox. The ecology of Ymurra had existed before the machines. The sang-tubes had grown in the dark, fed by geothermal water and mineral-rich soil, long before the ancient engineers had built their condensation surfaces and channel networks. The riph had grazed, the striiders had hunted, the glider-weavers had sung, the vithra had swum—all of this had happened, for millions of years, without infrastructure.

The machines had changed the ecology, expanded it, made it denser and more productive. They had created new niches, new opportunities, new ways of life. But they had not created life itself on Ymurra. They had modified the conditions, and life had adapted to the modifications, and now the modifications were wearing out.

If the power simply maintained the machines, it would be preserving a particular version of Ymurra—the version that had evolved around the infrastructure—at the expense of other possible versions. If the power let the machines fail, it would be allowing a transition that, while natural in the geological sense, would cause suffering in the immediate sense. Riph herds would lose their water sources. Glider-weaver populations would decline as the condensation surfaces degraded. Vithra schools would disperse as the processed-water channels dried.

The power did not want to cause suffering. But it also did not want to freeze Ymurra in a state that was, from a certain perspective, artificial—a state created by technologies that no one had chosen, that had been imposed on the ecology by beings who were now gone, and that the ecology had been forced to accommodate.

This was the question the power sat with, in its long listening: not whether to help, but what helping meant.

## What the Power Did First

The first thing the power did was nothing visible.

It settled into the stone of the plateau—not as heat, not as pressure, but as a quality of *attention* that the rock itself seemed to absorb. It was not consciousness in the way the creatures would understand it, but it was a kind of awareness, a holding-in-mind that was more sustained and more comprehensive than any biological cognition could achieve.

The power attended to every organism on the plateau simultaneously. Every riph calf pressing against its mother's flank. Every striider crouched in stillness near a water channel. Every glider-weaver singing its territorial song from a nest woven around a condensation surface. Every vithra swimming through the processed water, feeling the pump's vibration in its bones. Every tunneler processing soil. Every click-beetle chirping in the fungal mats. Every stone-cricket in its crevice. Every air-fish navigating by the echo of its own wings.

It attended to the machines. Every pump, every condenser, every channel, every maintenance crawler. It mapped the infrastructure's state with a precision that exceeded the machines' own monitoring systems. It understood the degradation, the remaining lifespans, the failure modes that would cascade through the system as each component reached its end.

It attended to the geology. The hydrothermal networks below, the stone's structure, the water table's depth and flow, the mineral content of each spring. It understood the plateau's bones.

It attended to the atmosphere. The moisture load, the thermal gradients, the fog patterns, the updrafts and the cloud sea's ceaseless lapping at the lower edges.

It attended, and in attending, it began to understand Ymurra not as a problem to be solved but as a life to be witnessed.

And in that witnessing, something began to change. Not in the world—in the power's understanding of what it was doing there.

The power had arrived with the intention of helping. But in the deep listening, in the sustained attention to every vibration and every chemical signature and every mechanical drone, it began to perceive something it had not expected: the plateau was, in a sense that transcended the metaphorical, already caring for itself.

The riph matriarchs led their herds to water sources they remembered from decades of experience. They were, in their embodied way, managing the plateau's water resources—congregating where water was abundant, dispersing when it was scarce, preventing overuse of any single source. The striiders, through their territorial networks, maintained a population density that kept prey populations healthy. The glider-weavers, through their songs, created an information network that warned of threats and coordinated foraging across the canopy. The vithra, through their filter-feeding, maintained the water quality of the streams and channels. The fungal networks transmitted chemical signals between plants, coordinating nutrient sharing and resource distribution.

The machines were part of this self-care, but they were not all of it. The biology had its own maintenance systems, its own feedback loops, its own ways of responding to perturbation. The ecology was not passive—it was active, adaptive, capable of responding to change in ways that the machines, for all their ancient engineering, could not.

The power began to understand that its role was not to save Ymurra. Its role was to participate in Ymurra's self-care, to add its own capacity to the capacities already present, to support the system's own tendencies toward health rather than imposing a design from outside.

This was a different kind of benevolence than the power had practiced before. Not the benevolence of the rescuer, who arrives to pull a drowning creature from the water. Not the benevolence of the builder, who constructs shelter against the storm. But the benevolence of the attendant, who sits with and holds space and offers presence—not as a solution, but as a companioning.

The power did not yet know what this would look like. But it knew what it would not look like. It would not look like intervention. It would not look like transformation. It would not look like the imposition of a design that originated outside the system.

It would look, as much as possible, like Ymurra caring for itself with slightly more capacity than it had before.

## The First Change: The Machines

The power began with the machines, because they were the most immediate concern—the components closest to failure, the elements whose degradation would cause the most disruption.

It did not repair them. Repair would have been the obvious action—restoring the machines to their original specifications, replacing worn components, rebuilding degraded systems. But the power had listened long enough to understand that the machines' original specifications were not necessarily what the ecosystem needed. The ancient engineers had designed the systems for purposes that might or might not align with the current ecology's needs. Restoring the machines to their original state might disrupt relationships that the creatures had evolved over millions of years.

Instead, the power did something more subtle. It entered the machines' adaptive systems—the simple, non-conscious feedback loops that allowed the pumps to adjust flow rates, the condensers to respond to atmospheric changes, the maintenance crawlers to prioritize certain repairs over others. It did not rewrite these systems. It expanded them.

The machines' adaptive parameters had been narrowing for centuries—degradation reducing the range of conditions they could respond to, the number of variables they could process, the complexity of their adjustments. The power widened these parameters again, not to their original range, but beyond it—giving the machines the capacity to respond to conditions their original designers had never anticipated.

The machines would now adjust their water distribution based on the presence of riph herds, increasing flow to areas where the large herbivores congregated. The condensation surfaces would modify their operation based on the nesting patterns of glider-weavers, maintaining stable temperatures in the zones where the weavers built their nests. The maintenance crawlers would alter their routes based on vithra school locations, avoiding the streams where the aquatic creatures were spawning.

These changes were small—imperceptible to the creatures, barely detectable even by the most sensitive acoustic analysis. But they represented a shift in the machines' relationship with the ecology: from indifferent infrastructure to responsive system, from fixed function to adaptive participation.

The machines were not aware of this change. They had no consciousness, no self-model, no experience of what they were doing. They were simply following their parameters, as they had always done—but the parameters were now richer, more nuanced, more capable of responding to the needs of the life that had grown around them.

The power also addressed the degradation itself—not by reversing it, but by changing its trajectory. The micro-fractures in the condensation surfaces would still form, but the maintenance crawlers would now seal them with materials that were structurally superior to the originals. The pump bearings would still wear, but the wear would be compensated by adjustments in the pumping rhythm that reduced stress on the degraded components. The thermal gradient systems would still lose efficiency, but the loss would be offset by improvements in the condensation surfaces' design—modifications so slight they could not be distinguished from natural variation.

The machines would still fail eventually. Everything physical fails eventually. But their failure was now further away—perhaps fifty thousand years, perhaps a hundred thousand. And when it came, it would be gradual, giving the ecology time to adapt, to shift, to find new equilibria.

The power did not make the machines immortal. It made their dying slower and gentler, and it gave the life around them more time to learn to live without them.

## The Second Change: The Fungal Networks

The power's next action was less visible even than the first, because it occurred in the soil, in the thread-fungal networks that connected plant roots across the plateau.

These networks were already ancient—older than the machines, older than the animals, older perhaps than the sang-tubes themselves. They were the plateau's original information system, transmitting chemical signals between plants, coordinating nutrient sharing, distributing water from moist areas to dry ones. They were, in a sense, Ymurra's first nervous system, and they were still functioning, still connecting, still maintaining the integrity of the plant community.

But the networks were limited by their biology. The chemical signals traveled slowly—no faster than diffusion could carry them. The connections between plants were fixed, determined by root proximity and fungal growth patterns. The network could not reconfigure itself in response to changing conditions; it could only grow, slowly, into new areas and withdraw, slowly, from unfavorable ones.

The power did not accelerate the networks. It did not give them new capabilities or transform their nature. It did something that no biological process could have done: it added a second layer of connectivity, a resonance that traveled through the fungal threads faster than chemistry allowed, a vibration that carried information between distant parts of the network in seconds rather than days.

This resonance was not sound, not in the way the creatures of Ymurra understood sound. It was more like the memory of sound—a pattern that existed in the structure of the fungal threads themselves, a standing wave that could be modified by the chemical signals the networks already carried and that, once modified, could carry information back across the network with a speed that had no biological analogue.

The effect was subtle. When a riph herd overgrazed an area, the fungal network would signal distress to distant plants, and those plants would alter their chemistry to produce compounds that discouraged riph browsing. Before the power's intervention, this signal would take days to travel. Now it took seconds. The plants in distant parts of the plateau would begin producing defensive compounds before the riph herd had even moved on from the overgrazed area.

When a sang-tube cluster was damaged by a storm—the thermal updrafts sometimes generated forces that could crack the vascular giants' stalks—the fungal network would now signal the damage to neighboring clusters, which would alter their pumping rhythms to compensate, maintaining the canopy's structural integrity even as the damaged stalks weakened.

When the deep vents shifted, changing the mineral content of the water, the fungal network would transmit the change to plants across the plateau, allowing them to adjust their chemistry before the new water reached their roots.

The network was still the network. It was still fungal, still chemical, still rooted in the biology of the plateau. But it was now faster, more responsive, more capable of coordinating the plant community's response to change.

The creatures did not notice this change. The riph still grazed, the striiders still hunted, the glider-weavers still sang. But over time—years, decades, centuries—the plant community would become more resilient, more coordinated, more capable of maintaining the conditions that the animals depended on.

The power had not changed the network for the creatures' sake. It had changed the network for the network's sake—because the network was a living system, and the power's benevolence extended to all living systems, not just the ones that could suffer in ways the power could recognize.

## The Third Change: The Riph

The power did not single out the riph for special attention. It did not consider them more important than the striiders or the glider-weavers or the vithra or the click-beetles or the tunnelers or the stone-crickets. The power's benevolence was not hierarchical—it attended to all lives with equal care, because all lives were equally alive, and the power did not value consciousness over chemistry or complexity over simplicity.

But the power noticed something about the riph that concerned it.

The riph matriarchs carried the herd's knowledge in their bodies—a lifetime of accumulated sensory information about the plateau's geography, its water sources, its safe paths, its seasonal variations. This knowledge was passed down through generations, mothers teaching daughters through direct contact, chemical exchange, the shared experience of moving together through the dark.

But the transfer was imperfect. When a matriarch died, her knowledge died with her, except for what she had managed to pass on before her death. And the passing on was limited by the bandwidth of touch and chemical exchange—by the slow, intimate process of one body pressing against another and sharing what it knew.

The riph herds were, in a sense, losing knowledge faster than they could preserve it. Each generation knew less than the one before, because each matriarch's lifetime of experience was only partially transmitted before her death. The plateau was changing—the machines were slowly degrading, the thermal vents were shifting, the climate was in constant slow flux—and the riph's ability to adapt was limited by the rate at which knowledge could be passed down.

The power did not give the riph new ways of knowing. It did not expand their consciousness or enhance their cognition or grant them abilities they had never possessed. It did something that was, in its way, much more profound.

It made their knowing more shareable.

The power introduced a change in the chemical markers the riph secreted along their flanks. The markers became more complex, carrying more information per molecule. A riph's trail-marker now contained not just the individual's identity and emotional state, but a compressed record of the riph's recent sensory experience—the water sources it had visited, the temperatures it had felt, the chemical signatures of the plants it had touched.

This was not a new ability. The riph's markers had always carried some of this information—that was how other riph could read a herd-mate's trail and know where it had been and what it had found. But the power increased the density of the information, the way a better pen allows a writer to fit more words on a page.

The change was small. A riph would not notice it any more than a speaker notices when a room's acoustics subtly improve. But over time—generations, centuries—the riph herds would find that their knowledge traveled further and lasted longer. A trail-marker left by a matriarch would contain more of her experience, and the riph who encountered it would absorb more of her knowledge. The intergenerational transfer of information would become more efficient, more complete, more resilient.

The power was not trying to make the riph smarter. It was trying to make their wisdom more durable. It was trying to ensure that the matriarchs' hard-won knowledge of the plateau would survive their deaths more completely, and that future generations would have more to build on.

This was the power's way of caring for the riph: not by making them different, but by making them more fully what they already were.

## The Fourth Change: The Striiders

The striiders were harder.

Not because they were less worthy of care—they were not. But because the power's benevolence sat uneasily with the striiders' nature. The striiders were predators. They killed to live. They stunned prey with sonic blasts that disrupted the acoustic processing of creatures whose entire sensory world was built on vibration. They were precise, efficient, and unapologetic in the way that all predators are unapologetic—they did not choose to be predators, they simply were.

The power did not want to change this. The striiders' predation was part of Ymurra's ecology—a role that maintained the health of prey populations, that selected for fitness, that kept the system in balance. Removing the striiders or changing their behavior would have been an act of engineering, not of care.

But the power noticed something about the striiders that it could address without disrupting their ecological role.

The striider adolescence was brutal. When young striiders reached maturity, their mothers drove them away, and they had to find unoccupied territories on the plateau's margins—areas that were less productive, more dangerous, closer to the predators of the cloud sea. Many died in this period, struck down by established hunters or taken by the predators that lurked at Ymurra's edges.

This was natural. This was how selection worked. The strong survived; the weak did not. The striider population was maintained at a level the plateau could support, and the individuals who survived to adulthood were the most capable, the most skilled, the most likely to produce capable offspring.

But the power perceived something that the natural selection process did not account for: the adolescent striiders who died were not always the least capable. Some were skilled, intelligent, acoustically gifted—young hunters with excellent potential who simply happened to be in the wrong place at the wrong time. They died not because they were unfit but because the plateau's margin was a hostile environment that punished even the most capable individuals.

The power could not solve this without changing the striiders' territorial behavior, which it was unwilling to do. But it could change the margin.

The plateau's edge was a harsh environment—the cloud sea lapped at the lower slopes, predators from below climbed up, the vegetation was sparser, the water sources fewer. The power did not make the edge gentle—it could not, without fundamentally altering the plateau's geography and ecology. But it made the edge *habitable*.

It widened the zone where the thermal vents' warmth reached the surface, creating a broader band of habitable temperature around the plateau's perimeter. It increased the mineral content of the water that wept from the rock faces near the edge, supporting more vegetation, which supported more prey species, which supported more striiders. It did not eliminate the predators of the cloud sea—they were part of the system too, and the power's benevolence extended to them—but it created conditions where a young striider who was skilled and careful had a better chance of surviving the transition to independence.

The change was slow—decades, centuries before its effects were fully realized. But eventually, the plateau's margin would support a modest population of adolescent striiders, individuals who would have died in the old conditions but who would now survive to find territories in the interior when older, established hunters died or moved.

The striiders would not know that anything had changed. They would simply find the edge less hostile, the margin more livable, the transition to independence less likely to be fatal. And the striider population would become more stable, less subject to the boom-and-bust cycles that had characterized it for millions of years.

## The Fifth Change: The Vithra

The vithra were the easiest to care for, because the vithra were already the most cared-for creatures on the plateau. They lived in the processed-water channels, surrounded by the machines' warmth and mineral output, protected by the water's acoustic properties, fed by the particulate matter that the machines filtered from the air. They were, in a sense, the machines' pets—though neither the machines nor the vithra knew this.

The power's change to the vithra was so small it might be thought to be nothing. But the power understood that the smallest changes can have the largest effects, and that the vithra, because they were the plateau's most sophisticated listeners, would be the most affected by subtle shifts in the acoustic environment.

The power introduced a new harmonic into the sound of the water.

Not a new sound—the water still rushed, gurgled, hissed, and whistled as it always had. But the power added a depth to the water's sound, a harmonic complexity that was below the threshold of any creature's conscious perception but that the vithra's extraordinary hearing organs would register on a physiological level.

This harmonic was not random. It was structured, carrying information about the plateau's state—the thermal vents' activity, the machines' functioning, the fungal networks' chemical activity, the riph herds' positions, the striiders' territorial boundaries. The information was encoded in the water's vibration, not as language but as pattern—a rhythmic modulation that the vithra would internalize without knowing they were doing so.

The vithra would not become more intelligent. They would not gain new cognitive abilities. But they would become more attuned to the plateau's systems, more capable of anticipating changes, more likely to move to favorable locations before conditions deteriorated, more likely to congregate where their filter-feeding was needed.
