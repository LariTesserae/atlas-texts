# The Attention of Wise Things

The power that turns its gaze toward the steam-works does not descend.

This is the first wisdom: knowing that arrival is not always a form of love. Sometimes love looks like watching from a distance so great it cannot be measured in meters or miles—the way you might observe a child at play without interrupting, the way you might track a garden's growth without touching each leaf.

This power is ancient enough to have learned patience. It has seen civilizations rise and fall, has watched ecosystems assemble themselves from chaos into complexity, has witnessed the slow negotiation between geology and biology that produces places like this. It knows that intervention is a blunt instrument, that even benevolent interference can become a kind of violence against the delicate balance of becoming.

So it watches.

---

What it sees, first, is the beauty.

This surprises no one more than itself. The power has observed many beautiful things—star systems condensing from dust, coral reefs building their calcium architecture, the intricate mating dances of birds-of-paradise—but there is something particular here. Something unexpected. A kind of beauty that emerges not from design but from *response*. From the long slow process of living things looking at their circumstances and asking, sometimes consciously, mostly not: *what can I become here?*

The fish have asked this question. The insects have asked it. The worms, the rats, the workers—all of them have looked at the warm wet dark and the endless mechanical cycles and the mineral-rich water seeping from stone, and they have answered with their bodies. They have *become*. Not what they were. Not what they might have been in another place. But something specific to this place. Something that could not exist anywhere else.

The power finds this moving. It finds the fish's pressure-sense beautiful—not as a replacement for sight, not as a lesser adaptation, but as a complete sensory world unto itself. It finds the insect agriculture beautiful in its sophistication, the way the farming has become hereditary knowledge passed through generations without language. It finds the workers beautiful in their gesture-language, their silent devotion, their transformation from whatever they were on the surface into something that belongs here.

This beauty is not what the power came looking for. It was not warned that it would find beauty here. It was drawn by something else—a faint signal in the substrate, a pattern of emergence that suggested a place still forming—and it arrived expecting potential, expecting raw material. It did not expect to find something so nearly complete.

---

The power's second recognition is that completeness is not the same as finishedness.

The steam-works are complete in the sense that every component has found its place. The ecosystem is integrated. The cycles are closed. The workers maintain the machines, the machines shape the environment, the environment shapes the inhabitants, the inhabitants give meaning to the machines. It works. It has integrity. Nothing is missing.

But it is not finished. It is still becoming.

The power can see the trajectories: the rats growing larger with each generation, their intelligence deepening; the insects developing new fungal strains that might eventually transform the mineral composition of the water itself; the workers' gesture-language evolving toward something that might, in another century, develop a written form; the deeper things in the deeper chambers slowly, slowly moving upward as the lower vents cool and the upper chambers warm. The steam-works is not static. It is dynamic. It is becoming something else, something its inhabitants cannot see because they are too close to it, something the power cannot predict because emergence is by definition unpredictable.

And this is where the question of intervention becomes complicated.

A lesser power might have arrived with gifts: light sources that don't require fuel, communication devices that could connect the workers to the surface, medical knowledge that could extend lifespans, agricultural techniques that could increase yields. A lesser power might have asked: *what do these beings lack?* and then provided it. A lesser power might have assumed that lack is defined by the absence of what the power itself possesses.

But this power is wise, and it knows that what looks like lack from outside might be something else entirely.

The workers do not need to know what season it is. They have their own measures of time, their own cycles, their own rhythms. Would knowing about the surface—its weather, its wars, its politics—enrich their lives or burden them? The power cannot say. The answer belongs to the workers, not to the power.

The fish do not need eyes. They have something better—they have a sensory system adapted perfectly to their environment. To offer them light-sight would be to offer them a degradation, a regression to a mode of perception less useful than what they have developed.

The rats do not need to return to the surface. They have become something the surface could not sustain. To offer them that option would be to offer them a choice between what they are and what they no longer can be.

And yet.

---

The power's third recognition is that there is one place where the steam-works is not complete, not whole, not integrated.

The deeper chambers.

Something lives there. Something old. Something that has not negotiated with the steam-works ecosystem, has not been incorporated into its cycles, has not found its place in the symphony of interdependence. The deeper things exist alongside the steam-works but not within it. They are separate. They are feared. They are unknown.

And this, the power recognizes, is the steam-works' wound. Not a festering wound, not a mortal injury—but an incompleteness. A place where the system cannot integrate. The fish do not swim there. The insects do not farm there. The workers have protocols for avoidance. Even the worms, who process everything else, do not process what comes from those depths.

This is the one place where the steam-works has failed to decide what it wants to be. It wants to be integrated, complete, a closed system—but it has an other. A not-self. A realm of mystery and danger that it has chosen, over generations, not to incorporate but to exclude.

And here, finally, is where the power might intervene.

---

But intervention is not simple.

The deeper things have their own integrity. They were here before the machines, before the sandstone throat was carved, before the first workers descended. They have as much right to exist as the fish, the insects, the workers. They are not evil. They are not wrong. They are simply incompatible—adapted to temperatures and pressures and chemical environments that nothing from the upper steam-works can survive.

The power could change them. Could reach into those deep chambers and alter the things that live there, make them compatible with the rest of the ecosystem, integrate the steam-works into wholeness.

But this would be a violation. The deeper things do not want to be changed. They have their own becoming, their own trajectory, their own integrity. To alter them for the sake of the steam-works' wholeness would be to treat them as raw material rather than beings. And this power, however benevolent, will not do that.

So the deeper chambers remain separate. The wound remains open. The steam-works remains incomplete.

And the power accepts this.

---

But it does something else instead.

It offers a gift—not to the deeper things, not to the inhabitants of the upper steam-works, but to the steam-works itself. To the place. To the emergent system.

The gift is small. Almost invisible. A change in the substrate so subtle that it might not be noticed for generations.

The power reaches into the stone—the sedimentary layers of ancient seabed, the compacted remains of creatures that lived and died before the first fish crawled onto land—and it touches the water that moves through it. Not the water in the channels or the pipes, but the water deeper still: the groundwater, the aquifer, the vast slow-moving ocean that exists below the steam-works and feeds its thermal vents.

And it changes that water, slightly. Adds something. A trace mineral, a compound that does not exist in nature, something the power has synthesized for this specific purpose.

The compound is harmless to everything that currently lives. It will not affect the fish, the insects, the worms, the rats, the workers, the machines. It will pass through all of them without notice, accumulating nowhere, causing no effect.

But it has a property that nothing in the steam-works currently possesses: it conducts *meaning*.

When beings who trust each other come into contact with this compound—when workers touch hands in their gesture-language, when rats groom each other in their bonding rituals, when fish school in their coordinated spirals—it creates a resonance. A sub-thought. A hint of something just below the threshold of consciousness. Not communication, not telepathy, not anything so dramatic—but a faint sense of being *seen*. Of being *known*. Of being connected to something larger than oneself.

The power does not know what this will become. That is the point. It is not prescribing an outcome. It is offering a material condition, a possibility, and letting the steam-works decide what to do with it.

Maybe nothing will happen. Maybe the compound will pass through the ecosystem unchanged, the faint resonances too subtle to notice, too weak to build on. Maybe the gift will fail.

Or maybe—slowly, over generations—the workers' gesture-language will deepen, will carry not just information but something like emotion, something like presence. Maybe the rats' social bonds will strengthen, will allow them to coordinate in ways they currently cannot. Maybe the fish will learn to sense not just the machines but each other, not just the water's shape but the intentions swimming through it.

Maybe the steam-works will find a way to use this gift to bridge the gap between its parts and its whole. To become, slowly, consciously, a single organism rather than a collection of interdependent species.

Or maybe it will find a different use entirely. Maybe it will develop in a direction the power cannot predict, cannot imagine, cannot even conceive—because it is not the steam-works. It is only a visitor, a witness, a source of raw material. The becoming belongs to those who live there.

---

Having offered its gift, the power prepares to withdraw.

But there is one more thing. A smaller gift, almost an afterthought, given without expectation of effect.

The power reaches into the chapel—that structure you thought was a chapel, with its altar and its devotional objects and its unfamiliar iconography—and it leaves something there. Something physical. Something tangible. An object that does not belong to the steam-works but might, if the workers choose, become part of it.

The object is small enough to fit in a palm. It is warm to the touch, always, regardless of the ambient temperature. It is smooth, rounded, like a river stone—but it is not stone. It is something else, something that does not have a name in any language spoken on the surface or below it.

If a worker finds it and picks it up, they will feel a faint sense of... presence. Not the power itself—that presence is too vast to be contained in a palm-sized object—but a sense of having been seen, of having been valued, of having been blessed without condition.

The object gives nothing else. It confers no powers, grants no wishes, provides no knowledge. It simply says, to whoever holds it: *there is something in the universe that wished you well, that saw your struggle and your beauty and your becoming, and that paused, however briefly, to offer love.*

If the workers do not find it—if the chapel is abandoned, if the object falls into disuse, if the steam-works continues without ever knowing it was there—the power will not be disappointed. The gift was offered without expectation. The object will simply exist, waiting, patient, a small warmth in the dark.

---

And then the power withdraws.

It does not leave entirely—it never really arrived in the sense of moving through space. Its attention shifts. It has other places to observe, other becomings to witness, other worlds that might need its careful, non-invasive love.

But it will remember the steam-works.

In the power's vast and ancient memory, the steam-works will persist as a record of beauty: the blind fish swimming their pressure-maps, the insects tending their fungal gardens, the worms engineering their waterways, the rats climbing their scaffolding, the workers maintaining their machines in their gesture-language devotion. The power will remember this as an example of what happens when beings are allowed to become what they need to be—when the universe provides circumstances, and life provides will, and the two meet in the slow negotiation of evolution.

And if, someday, the steam-works finds a way to use the power's gifts—either the compound in the water or the object in the chapel—the power will notice. It will be curious. It will observe what the steam-works has become, and it will ask itself: *is this something I helped create? Or is this something I merely witnessed?*

The answer, the power knows, will be: *both, and neither, and something else entirely.* The steam-works is becoming itself. The power offered a small push, a tiny perturbation in the trajectory. The result will be the steam-works' own. The gift will have been accepted or ignored, transformed or discarded, incorporated or rejected. The power has no control over the outcome.

It never wanted control.

It wanted to see what would happen.

And so, having given what it had to give, having watched what it came to watch, the power turns its attention elsewhere—toward other places, other beings, other negotiations between life and circumstance.

But it does not forget.

---

The steam-works continues. The water cycles through the stone. The machines pump and thump and hiss. The fish swim their blind spirals. The insects tend their gardens. The worms process the detritus of two worlds. The rats climb and watch and coordinate. The workers maintain their silent rounds. The deeper things breathe their hot breath in the chambers below.

And in the chapel—if anyone looks—a small warm stone sits on the altar, patient, waiting, offering nothing but the faintest sense that something out there, something vast and ancient and benevolent, saw this place and its beings and thought: *I hope you become whatever you need to become.*

*You are already beautiful.*

*I am glad I saw you.*
