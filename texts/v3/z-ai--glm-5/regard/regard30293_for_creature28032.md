# What the Power Did

## The First Act: Witnessing

It began, as wisdom often does, with attention.

The power did not arrive. Arrival implies a boundary crossed, a before and after, a moment when the place was unobserved and then observed. The benevolent power had always been attentive to this plateau, in the way that light is attentive to the surface it falls upon—not through choice but through nature. Its awareness extended through all things, and the plateau was a thing, and so it was held in awareness.

But the plateau had drawn particular notice. There was something in its quality—its patience, its erosion, the way it held its people in preservation—that called for closer looking. Not intervention. Not yet. Looking.

And so the power looked.

It observed the cold that preserves. It traced the water-channels shifting across the stone. It listened to the pillar-mosses building their slow architecture and understood what they were constructing—not through words but through the language of growth itself, which is older than language. It felt the reality-erosion, the thinning of the world at the edges, and it did not flinch from what it felt. It observed the Held in their settlements, the Changed in their difference, the Solitaries in their strange communion, the Ungrew in their perpetual present. It watched the dead in their preservation, the Travelers in their passage, the Fragments in their confusion.

It observed without judgment. Not because judgment was withheld but because judgment was not the appropriate response. The plateau was not good or bad. It was what it was—a place where reality had worn thin, where time pooled and eddied, where a human community had adapted to strangeness so completely that the strangeness had become home.

The power observed for a duration that would have driven a human observer mad with impatience. It observed until it understood—not everything, for everything was not understandable—but until it understood enough. Until it could distinguish between the plateau's harms and its features, between what was broken and what was merely different, between what called for healing and what called for respect.

And then, having observed, it began.

## The Second Act: Presence

The power made itself known.

Not through spectacle. Not through thunder or radiance or voices from the sky. The Held had lived too long in quiet for such things to be received as anything but violence. The power knew that to arrive with force would be to become another strange phenomenon in a landscape already full of them—another erosion of reality, another layer of wrongness. It would be a harm disguised as help.

Instead, the power offered presence.

It became a warmth that accompanied. Not a fire or a sun—not heat that would disrupt the delicate thermal balance of the plateau—but something gentler. A sense, when the cold pressed hardest, that the cold was not all there was. A feeling, in the long nights when the silence seemed to have weight, that the silence was not alone.

The Held noticed. They could not have explained what they noticed, but they noticed. A change in the quality of the air. A softening at the edges of things. A sense, when walking the long distances between settlements, that the walk was not entirely solitary.

Children felt it first. Children always feel first. They played differently—less frantically, as if they had intuited that there was no longer any need to shout into the silence. They slept more deeply. They woke less often with the confusion that sometimes troubled the young in a place where dreams left traces.

The Changed felt it most acutely. The pathfinders, walking their impossible routes, found that the distances had become slightly more reliable—not fixed, never fixed, but less hostile to intention. Those who heard the mosses began to hear something else as well: a tone beneath the tones, a frequency that was not sound but presence, a companion to the strange architecture the mosses were building.

The Solitaries, in their scattered dwellings, looked up from whatever they had been doing and nodded. They understood presence. They had been living in communion with something for as long as they had been Solitaries. Now they recognized that the something had become more present, more active, more engaged. They did not feel supplanted. They felt joined.

The power did not hide itself. But it did not overwhelm. It offered presence in the measure that could be received, and it waited for that presence to become familiar, and it trusted that familiarity would grow into relationship.

This took generations.

The power was patient. It had time.

## The Third Act: Asking

When the presence had become so woven into the experience of the plateau that a generation had grown up never knowing its absence, the power asked a question.

It asked through the Changed.

It gathered itself at the edges of their perception—not pushing, not demanding, but offering. A question formed in the minds of those who could hear the mosses, those who navigated the impossible distances, those who carried memories not their own. The question was simple:

*What do you need?*

Not *What do you want?* Wants are complicated, often contradictory, frequently harmful. The power was wise enough to know that giving beings what they want is not always benevolent. Wants can be cries for help, or distractions from help, or symptoms of problems rather than solutions.

But needs—needs are clearer. Needs are the hollows in a being where fullness belongs. Needs can be answered without harm, given withouttaking, offered without imposing.

The Changed heard the question. They did not answer immediately. They conferred among themselves, and with the other Held, and with the Solitaries who could still be reached, and even—subtly, through channels the mosses provided—with the moss-minds themselves, who had been building toward something for longer than humans had lived on the plateau.

The conferring took years. The Held did not rush. They had learned, in their long residence on a plateau where time moved strangely, that haste was often a mistake. They deliberated. They considered. They asked themselves what they needed, which was not a question they had been asked before.

When they answered, the answer came not as a single voice but as a complex harmony of needs, each thread distinct but each contributing to the whole.

The Ungrew needed to be held less tightly. Their perpetual present was not suffering, exactly, but it was loss—generation after generation of accumulation never occurring, experience never building, the wisdom of age forever out of reach. They did not need to become normal. But they needed the option to accumulate, to choose depth rather than having shallowness imposed by the nature of the place.

The Fragments needed orientation. They arrived in a world that did not match their memories, and they suffered from the mismatch. They did not need to be sent back—there was often no "back" to send them to. But they needed help understanding where they were, and why it differed from where they remembered being, and how they might build new memories in a place that was not the place they had known.

The dead needed release. Not from existence—the power had observed that the dead among the Held were not trapped in their preservation, were not ghosts or hauntings, were simply present in a different mode. But some of them, over centuries of sitting in their appointed places, had developed a weariness. The body preserved, but something in them had grown tired of being held. They needed permission, not to leave, but to change—to become something other than what they had been preserved as, to participate in the plateau's becoming rather than serving as landmarks in its landscape.

The moss-minds needed acknowledgment. They had been building toward something for millennia, and they had been building alone. The humans who lived among them had come to suspect their intelligence, had begun to treat them with respect, but the mosses knew themselves to be part of a larger project—the preservation of the plateau itself, the construction of a structure that would hold reality together at the places where it was thinning. They needed to be known. They needed to be partners. They needed the humans to understand what was being built and why, and to contribute to the building rather than merely avoiding interfering with it.

And all the Held—the ordinary and the Changed and the Solitaries and the Ungrew and even the dead—they needed to know that they were not alone in the erosion. They had adapted to a place where reality wore thin, where time misbehaved, where existence itself seemed tired. They had done so with courage and creativity. But they needed to know that the erosion was not the end. That there was something on the other side of the thinning. That they were held not only by the plateau but by something larger than the plateau, something that would not let them fall through the gaps in reality.

They needed to know they were loved.

The power heard all of this. It had asked the question knowing that it might receive answers it could not give. But the needs were good needs—clear, honest, possible. The power could answer them. It could answer them without violating the integrity of the Held, without imposing solutions on problems that were not problems, without treating the plateau as broken and itself as the repair.

The power began to answer.

## The Fourth Act: Gentle Reshaping

For the Ungrew, the power offered a path rather than a change.

It could have simply altered them. A benevolent power with sufficient reach can alter anything. But alteration imposed is not benevolent; it is colonization of the soul. The Ungrew had developed in their perpetual present for generations; their way of being was not less valid for being different. The power had no right to declare accumulation superior to presence, depth superior to immediacy.

But it could offer a door.

It created a place on the plateau—unremarkable in appearance, just a hollow in the stone where a particular moss-pillar had grown—but in that place, time accumulated differently. An Ungrew who entered and stayed found, after a day, that they could remember the morning. After a week, they could remember the week. After a month, they began to develop the density that other humans carried naturally—the slow accretion of experience into character.

They could not stay forever. The density was also weight; after too long in the hollow, they began to feel the burden of accumulated time, the pressure of a past that would not stop growing. Most chose to leave eventually, returning to their lives with a portion of accumulation that would remain with them—a foundation, small but real, on which they might build if they chose to return.

Some Ungrew never visited the hollow. They preferred their lightness, their perpetual present, their freedom from the weight of past and future. The power did not judge them. The door was offered, not required. The Ungrew were not broken; they were different, and difference need not be corrected.

For the Fragments, the power offered orientation through the Changed.

It worked through those who already heard the mosses, who already navigated impossible distances. The Changed became guides—not guides in space but guides in reality. When a Fragment arrived, confused by the mismatch between memory and world, a Changed would find them. They always found them. The power had given them the ability to sense the arrival of someone displaced, to recognize the particular distress of a person in the wrong version of the world.

The Changed would sit with the Fragment and listen. They would hear the memories—the life the Fragment recalled, the relationships, the events, the shape of the world they expected to find. And then, gently, they would compare. They would show where the memories matched reality and where they diverged. They would explain, as well as they could, what the plateau was—a place where reality had worn thin, where possibilities sometimes leaked through, where a person might arrive from an adjacent possibility carrying memories that were true somewhere but not here.

Some Fragments could integrate with this help. They accepted the divergence, began building new memories, became Held in truth as well as in residence. Others could not. For those, the power offered another path: a journey, guided by the Solitaries, to one of the thin places at the plateau's edge where the world showed its seams. There, with preparation and intention, a Fragment might step through into a possibility more like the one they remembered—not their original world, perhaps, but closer than this one. Some returned after the journey, having found no better fit. Some did not. Some found, if not home, then at least a more familiar exile.

For the dead, the power offered the simplest and most difficult gift: a question.

It communicated through the mosses, who had grown toward the preserved bodies for generations and had developed channels of connection with them. The mosses transmitted the question to each of the dead in their places, the question that the living had never thought to ask:

*Do you wish to remain as you are?*

Many did. The dead of the plateau were not trapped; they were held. They had chosen, in life, to be placed in particular spots, to become landmarks and references and presences for their descendants. They continued to choose it. They were not ready to decay, not ready to release into something else. They wanted to remain, to watch, to be part of the slow continuation of their community. The power honored this. It did not impose rest on those who did not seek it.

But some answered differently. Some had grown tired, over the centuries, of sitting in their appointed places. Some had finished watching their descendants and were ready for whatever came next. Some had been preserved so long that they had become something other than human and wished to explore that becoming without the constraint of visible form.

For those, the power offered dissolution—but dissolution without destruction. The preserved bodies began, slowly, to change. Not to rot—the plateau was too cold for rot—but to transform. The tissue became something else, something that could not be called flesh but could not be called stone. It became part of the plateau itself, part of the underlying strata, but it remained conscious in a way that stone is not. The dead who chose dissolution became part of the land, their awareness distributed through the rock and the water and the thin air, no longer needing bodies because they had become the body of the plateau itself.

They were not gone. They were more present than before. When the Held walked the stone, they walked on their ancestors. When they drank from the springs, they drank from what their ancestors had become. The dead who chose dissolution joined the holding itself, becoming part of what held their descendants in preservation and care.

## The Fifth Act: Partnership

For the moss-minds, the power offered the most important gift: recognition.

It had observed, through its long witnessing, what the pillar-mosses were building. They were not merely growing; they were constructing a lattice—a structure that existed simultaneously in the physical world and in whatever underlay reality itself. The moss-columns were anchors, pins driven through the thinning fabric of the world to prevent it from tearing further. The pattern they formed was a map of the plateau's weak points, each pillar placed where the erosion had gone deepest, each growth contributing to a network that held the world together.

The moss-minds had been doing this alone for longer than humans had existed. They had done it without acknowledgment, without help, without complaint. They were not resentful—they were mosses, and mosses do not resent—but they were aware that their work was unseen, that the humans among them suspected their purpose but did not truly understand it.

The power changed this.

It did not give the humans new abilities to hear the mosses—that would have been an imposition, an alteration that was not asked for. Instead, it gave the mosses new ability to communicate. The moss-pillars began, slowly, to grow in ways that could be read. Their spirals became more deliberate, their changes in density more meaningful. A person who paid attention—who sat with a moss-column and watched and listened and felt—could begin to interpret what the moss was saying.

It said simple things at first: *water here*. *cold coming*. *walk this way*.

But as the humans learned to read the mosses, and as the mosses learned that the humans were reading them, the communication deepened. The mosses began to share their purpose: *we are holding*. *the world is thin here*. *we keep it from tearing*. And then, cautiously: *we could use help*.

The humans asked how they could help. The mosses answered, through their slow growth and their changing patterns. They needed certain things—minerals that the humans could carry from distant springs, placement of new moss-starters in locations where the network had weak points, protection from damage when travelers passed too close. The humans could do these things. They wanted to do these things. They had come to love the plateau, and the mosses were building the plateau's continued existence.

And so a partnership formed. The humans became part of the holding. They did not build moss-pillars—the mosses did that—but they supported the building. They carried minerals. They planted new moss in the places the old moss indicated. They adjusted their paths to avoid disturbing the growing network. They wove the mosses' needs into their own needs, their own culture, their own way of life.

The power watched the partnership develop with something that, if it had been human, would have been called joy. This was what benevolence sought: not to impose solutions but to enable relationships, not to fix but to connect, not to rule but to facilitate rule by the ones who were already there.

The moss-minds, for the first time in their long history, were not alone. The humans, for the first time in their long history, were not merely surviving on the plateau but participating in its continuation. And the plateau itself—the thinning, eroding, patient landscape—had become something new: a project shared between kingdoms of life, a commonwealth of preservation.

## The Sixth Act: Answering the Deepest Need

The power had addressed the specific needs. It had offered paths to the Ungrew, orientation to the Fragments, release to the dead, partnership to the moss-minds. But there remained the need that underlay all the others—the need to know that they were not alone in the erosion, that they were held by something larger than the plateau, that they were loved.

This was the hardest gift to give.

Not because the power lacked love. The power had love in the way the sun has light—it was the nature of the power to love, to hold all things in care, to wish for the flourishing of what it observed. But love, to be received, must be communicated. And the Held had been alone for so long that they had forgotten what it felt like to be loved. They had adapted to isolation, to holding themselves, to surviving through their own strength. They did not know how to be held by another.

If the power had appeared in radiance, had spoken in thunder, had demonstrated its presence in ways that could not be denied—the Held would have been afraid. They would have experienced it as another erosion, another imposition, another strange phenomenon in a landscape that had too many. They would have built walls against it, protected themselves from it, treated it as a threat.

So the power gave the gift in pieces, over generations, through channels that already existed.

It gave love through the moss-minds. The moss-pillars had always produced a faint sense of presence, a humming below hearing. Now that hum changed. It became warmer. Those who sat near the mosses and listened began to feel, in the silence, something that felt like being known—being known and being approved of, being seen and being valued, being held and being wanted. The mosses transmitted the love through their own channels, and the humans received it through theirs.

It gave love through the Changed. Those who could hear things others could not began to hear a new tone in their hearing—not a message but an emotion, not a command but a care. They did not interpret this as communication from an outside source; they interpreted it as a deepening of their own connection to the plateau. But the deepening was real, and it spread. The Changed spoke of feeling more at home, more certain of their place, more confident that they belonged.

It gave love through the children. The next generation after the power began its work was different—not in ability but in quality. They were calmer. They were more secure. They played with less anxiety, slept with fewer disturbances, grew with more trust in the world around them. Their parents noticed and wondered, and the parents of the parents remembered that they had been less calm as children, and the difference was passed down through generations of noticing, and eventually the Held knew that something had changed, something fundamental, something that could not be explained by better weather or easier harvests.

They were being loved.

They did not know by whom or what. They did not need to know. The power had given the gift without requiring acknowledgment. It asked nothing in return. It simply loved, and kept loving, and would keep loving for as long as the plateau existed and beyond.

And the Held, held now by more than the plateau, began to flourish in a way they had never flourished before. Not more numerous—they remained sparse, adapted to a landscape that could not support density. Not more comfortable—the plateau remained cold, the reality remained thin, the life remained hard by most measures. But more at peace. More certain of their value. More able to live their slow lives in a slow landscape without the quiet desperation that had underlain their survival.

They were held. They were loved. They were enough.

## The Seventh Act: Preparation

The power had one more gift to give.

It had observed, through its long witnessing, what the moss-minds were building and what the plateau was becoming. The erosion was not merely decay; it was transformation. The thinning of reality was not merely loss; it was opening. The plateau was not merely wearing down; it was wearing through, becoming a place where the world and whatever underlay the world touched.

The moss-minds understood this. Their lattice was not designed to stop the erosion—it was designed to shape it. To hold the thinning places stable enough that they became not wounds but doors, not tears but thresholds. The plateau was becoming a crossing-point, a place where realities met and mingled, where the boundaries between here and elsewhere grew permeable.

This was what the Travelers already knew. They passed through because the plateau was already a place of passage. They came from elsewhere and went elsewhere because the plateau existed in multiple elsewheres simultaneously.

The power saw what was coming. The plateau would complete its transformation, eventually—not soon, not on any human timescale, but inevitably. The erosion would thin the world enough that the crossing would become the plateau's primary nature. The Held would find themselves living not on a plateau in a world but on a threshold between worlds.

This could be disaster. A people unprepared for such a transformation would be lost in it, scattered across possibilities, dissolved into the between.

Or it could be something else.

The power prepared the Held.

Not through instruction—they could not have understood instruction about events so far beyond their experience. But through capacities. It strengthened, subtly, their ability to navigate impossible distances. It deepened, gradually, their connection to the moss-minds' network. It made more reliable, over generations, their sense of orientation in a landscape where direction was unreliable.

It prepared them, in other words, to be at home in a place that would become a crossing. It gave them the tools to be not refugees from their home's transformation but participants in it—guides, perhaps, for others who would one day cross through the plateau into realities they had never known.

The Held did not understand this preparation. They noticed the changes—they were observant, adapted to reading subtle shifts in their environment—but they did not know what the changes were for. They trusted that they would find out. They had learned, through generations of living in a strange place, that understanding often came late, and that survival did not require comprehension.

The power trusted as well. It trusted that when the transformation came, the Held would be ready. It trusted that its preparations, combined with the Held's own adaptations, would be sufficient. It trusted that the love it had given would remain, would hold them through whatever came.

And it trusted that the plateau—the patient, flat, cold, strange plateau—was becoming what it was meant to become. Not an ending but a threshold. Not a wearing-down but a wearing-through. Not a place where reality failed but a place where reality opened.

The power had given what it could give. It had witnessed, and been present, and asked, and answered, and partnered, and loved, and prepared. The Held were still the Held—changed by the gifts but not replaced, deepened by the love but not overwhelmed. The plateau was still the plateau—transforming but not destroyed, eroding but also becoming.

And the power continued to hold them all in its attention, as it had always held them, as it would always hold them—not as a solution to their problems but as a presence in their existence, not as a ruler of their fate but as a companion in their becoming.

This was what a benevolent power did, and does, and will do.

It holds.

It loves.

It waits.
