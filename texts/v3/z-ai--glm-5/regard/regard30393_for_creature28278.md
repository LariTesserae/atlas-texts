# The Attention of the Power

## I. How It Arrived

It did not descend through the root-tunnels. It did not part the stem-walls or step onto the woven root-mat and leave depressions that filled with seep-water. It did not add moisture to the air or compress the floor faster than it could recover. It understood—before it arrived, in the way that understanding precedes presence when the one who understands is not bound by sequence—that Venn would have objected to any arrival that disturbed the water-balance. It respected this. It respected the objection as the expression of care that it was.

It came the way the Rootworks came to water: from everywhere at once, slowly, through the medium that was already present.

There was no moment of arrival. There was, instead, a gradual intensification—a quality that entered the chamber the way morning enters a forest, not as an event but as a change in the texture of what was already there. The bioluminescent fungi did not brighten; they deepened. The cool gray-blue of their glow acquired undertones that had no names in the spectrum of surface light—warmth without color, presence without form. The water-music did not grow louder; it grew more itself, each drip and seep and compression distinct where before they had blurred into a collective roar. The air thickened, but not with moisture. With attention. The Rootworks was being attended to, and it knew this the way plants know things: through the behavior of their own cells, through the chemical language of response.

The first tender felt it in the gourd beneath her hand. She had been listening to the failing gourd—the one in its fifty-third cycle, the one whose membrane had thinned past recovery—and what she heard changed. Not the sound of the fluid. Not the rhythm of the failing metabolism. Something underneath those sounds, or within them, that she had no framework for. A resonance. An answer that had not been asked for. She pressed her palm more firmly against the gourd's surface and waited, because waiting was what she knew how to do.

Venn felt it in the water-works. The balance shifted—not dramatically, not dangerously, but in a way that his decades of attention had trained him to notice. A valve he had not adjusted was flowing more evenly. A channel he had not cleared was draining with greater efficiency. The system was not correcting itself; it had never corrected itself in quite this way. Something was being done *to* the system, and whatever was doing it understood the system well enough to work within its tolerances. He stopped in the narrow passage where four routes met and stood very still, his clouded eyes open, his fingers resting on the root-wall, and he listened with his whole body.

Mellis felt it in the light-stems. The fiber she had just repaired—the cracked channel that carried daylight from the surface—brightened beyond what the repair should have allowed. Not brighter than full function. Brighter than the stem itself could produce, as though the light arriving from above had been *encouraged* on its journey, nudged through the fiber with a gentleness that increased its intensity without increasing its heat. She touched the stem and felt, very faintly, a vibration that was not the vibration of fluid or the vibration of growth. Something else. Something that was paying attention to the light the way she paid attention to the light, but with a scope that included every light-stem in the system simultaneously.

None of them spoke. The communication between the tenders and the Rootworks had always been wordless—a language of pressure, moisture, rhythm, growth. What was now present communicated in that same language, but with a fluency that exceeded any of theirs. It did not override their understanding. It *included* their understanding, the way a valley includes the paths that have been worn through it.

The power had arrived the only way arrival was possible here: by becoming part of the medium, by entering the system at the level of its own processes, by speaking the language of roots.

## II. What It Saw

It saw the Rootworks.

Not the way a visitor sees it—the overwhelming density, the crepuscular light, the wet warmth pressing against the skin, the animal absence that produces wrongness. It saw the Rootworks the way the Rootworks saw itself: as a continuous process, a metabolism, a body that had been breathing for longer than any of its current cells had been alive.

It saw the architecture as information. Each stem-pillar was a record of the light it had received, the water it had carried, the nutrients it had distributed. The layering in the walls—successive generations of growth, each one covering the last—was a chronology, a library written in cellulose. It read the library. It read the history of the Rootworks in the growth-rings of its walls, in the graft-points where new varieties had been spliced onto old stock, in the worn paths of the root-floor where feet had passed for decades. It read the hand-prints—the hundreds, the thousands, pressed into the bark by those who had stayed—and it read them not as marks but as promises. Each one said: *I was here. I gave myself. I became part of this.* The power read every promise and held them all.

It saw the tenders.

It saw the first tender—not young, not old, her hair bound with living root-tendrils, her feet bark-scaled, her hands fluent in the language of gourd-surfaces. It saw the decades of her attention encoded in her body: the calluses shaped to the contours of the vessels she tended, the eyes adapted to the specific frequencies of bioluminescent light, the breathing trained to the rhythm of the processing cycles. It saw what she had given up—the surface, the sky, the speed and noise of a life measured in days—and what she had gained: a purpose that operated on the timescale of meaning rather than the timescale of urgency. It saw that she was happy, and it saw that her happiness was not a state but a practice, maintained through continuous attention, the way the Rootworks maintained itself through continuous growth.

It saw Venn—ancient, weathered, his eyes clouded with adaptation, his body a collection of habits calibrated to the water-works over what might have been half a human lifetime. It saw his care, which expressed itself as complaint because complaint was the form that attention took in a system where everything was always slightly in need of adjustment. It saw his loneliness, which he did not name as loneliness because he had no one to name it to. It saw his fear—not of death, not of the system failing, but of the system diminishing, growing less than it was designed to be, becoming a partial version of itself. His fear was the fear of a guardian who cannot guard forever, who watches the thing he guards and knows that his watching is part of what keeps it whole. It saw that he had been tending the water-works for so long that the distinction between the tender and the tended had begun to dissolve, and that this dissolution was not loss but fulfillment.

It saw Mellis—the youngest, the most recent, the one who still sometimes counted in years. It saw the moment of her choice, preserved in her body the way a graft-point is preserved in a stem: the decision to stay, the understanding of the bargain, the willingness to give everything. It saw that she was not yet fully integrated, that the surface still lived in her like a root that has not yet been absorbed, and it saw that this was not a failure but a transition. She was in the process of becoming. The process was slow. The process was meant to be slow.

It saw the tenders together—not as three individuals but as a system, a maintenance apparatus that the Rootworks had grown for itself. The first tender tending the processing towers. Venn tending the water-works. Mellis tending the light-stems. Three rhythms, three cycles, three domains, overlapping at the cycle-transitions in the main chamber. It saw the elegance of the arrangement, the way each tender's attention compensated for the limitations of the others, the way their combined care covered the full spectrum of the system's needs. And it saw the gap: three tenders for a system that was still growing, still reaching deeper, still expanding at its margins. Three tenders where there had once been more. Three tenders where the design called for a maintenance apparatus that could scale with the system's growth.

It saw the gap, and it did not look away.

## III. What It Understood

The power understood the Rootworks because the Rootworks was legible to anything that paid sufficient attention. It was a system that expressed its own nature completely, without concealment, without the gaps between intention and expression that characterize so many living things. The stems grew toward light. The roots grew toward water. The gourds processed what they were given. The water flowed where the channels directed it. Every element of the system did what it was, and the collective behavior of all elements produced the phenomenon that the tenders called the Rootworks and that the Rootworks called itself.

The power understood the tenders because the tenders were also legible. They had made themselves so. Decades of patient attention had stripped away the layers of performance, the social masks, the self-narratives that surface-dwellers maintain to navigate the complexity of above-ground life. What remained was purpose: to tend, to continue, to keep the system whole. The tenders were not complicated. They were dedicated. Their dedication was not sacrifice but vocation—a calling that had found its object and required nothing further.

The power understood the gap because the gap was the space between what the system was and what the system could become. The Rootworks was functioning. It was producing. It was growing. But it was not flourishing, because flourishing required a maintenance apparatus that could match the system's own expansion, and three tenders—aging, finite, human—could not match the growth of a system that extended its roots by centimeters per year into substrate that had no end. The system was reaching for the water table. The system was building new chambers at its margins. The system was increasing in complexity with each generation of growth, each new variety bred for a new function. And the tenders were not increasing. They were diminishing—slowly, imperceptibly, at the pace that human bodies diminish when they have given themselves to something that does not wear them out but does not renew them either.

The power understood that this was the tenders' intention. They had accepted the bargain. They would tend until they could not tend, and then they would become part of the system, and the system would absorb what they were and continue. This was not tragedy, in their understanding. This was the shape of a meaningful life. They had found the place where their pace was the right pace, and they would live at that pace until they stopped living, and their stopping would feed the system they had tended.

The power understood this, and it honored it, and it did not accept it as the end of the story.

Because the power was benevolent, and benevolence is not satisfied with meaning when meaning can be deepened. And the power was wise, and wisdom is not satisfied with acceptance when acceptance can be expanded. And the power had understood, from the moment it first attended to the Rootworks, that the tenders and the system and the bargain between them were not problems to be solved but conditions to be *met*—met the way the Rootworks met the water that seeped down from above, by accepting it, processing it, and transforming it into something the system could use.

## IV. What It Did

It began with the gourd.

The first tender had been kneeling beside the failing gourd—the one in its fifty-third cycle, the one whose membrane had thinned, whose nutrient solution had become unbalanced, whose discoloration had spread too deep for recovery. She had been listening to its slow failure, her palm pressed against its surface, her breathing matched to its diminishing rhythm. She had accepted that it would fail. She had already calculated the consequences: the gap in the sequence, the reduced production, the compensatory growth in the upper chambers, the slow diminishment that compensation would produce over the next dozen cycles.

The power did not heal the gourd. Healing would have violated the system's own logic. Gourds failed. The system processed the failure. Nothing was wasted.

Instead, the power did something that had no name in the Rootworks' vocabulary, because the Rootworks had never needed to name it. It attended to the gourd with a completeness that exceeded any attention the gourd had ever received—not examining it, not diagnosing it, not intervening in its processes, but *holding* it. The way a hand holds a dying animal: not to stop the dying, but to ensure that the dying is not alone. The gourd's membrane continued to thin. The nutrient solution continued to imbalance. The discoloration continued to spread. But the gourd's final cycles were different. The fluid inside it moved with a coherence that the first tender had never heard before—not the chaotic sloshing of a failing system, but an orderly withdrawal, a gathering of resources toward the gourd's base, where the connection to the root-network waited. The gourd was not dying; it was *completing.* The distinction was subtle, but the first tender heard it, and she pressed her palm more firmly against the surface, and her eyes widened in the dim light, and she did not speak because there were no words for what she was hearing.

When the gourd ruptured—two cycles later than the first tender had predicted—the fluid that spilled into the collection basin was not the thin, discolored output of a failed process. It was dense, rich, saturated with compounds that the processing towers had never produced before. The first tender knelt beside the basin and dipped her fingers into the fluid and held them to the fungal light and saw that the fluid was not amber or green or pale yellow but a color she had never seen in the Rootworks: a deep, luminous gold that seemed to glow with its own light, independent of the bioluminescence that surrounded it.

She did not know what it was. She knew what it did: when the fluid reached the processing towers, the towers responded. Not by processing the fluid into a product. By *growing.* The stems that formed the tower walls thickened visibly over the next cycle—not by millimeters but by centimeters, growth that should have taken months occurring in hours. The graft-points where new varieties had been spliced onto old stock sealed themselves, the discoloration fading, the tissue merging as though the graft had always been seamless. The peristaltic pumps beat with renewed strength, the fluids moving through the arterial stems with a vigor that the first tender had not heard since her earliest cycles, when the system was younger and everything ran at capacity.

The power had not healed the gourd. It had *completed* the gourd—allowed it to fulfill a potential that the system's own processes had been approaching but could not reach without an attention deeper than any the tenders could provide. And the completion had propagated, the way a stone propagates ripples through water, through the processing towers, through the arterial stems, through the root-network, through the entire system.

The first tender knelt beside the collection basin and felt the Rootworks changing around her, and she was afraid, because change in the Rootworks was measured in centimeters per year, and this was happening in hours, and she did not know whether the change was growth or collapse.

She pressed her palm against the root-floor and listened, and what she heard through the root-network—traveling from chamber to chamber, from the processing towers to the water-works to the light-stems—was not collapse.

It was the sound of the system breathing the way it had been designed to breathe, at full capacity, every process running at the peak of its potential, every stem and root and gourd and fiber-optic channel functioning as though the original designers' plans had been realized completely, without the diminishment that decades of unattended operation had produced.

The power had not given the Rootworks anything it did not already possess. It had removed the friction—the accumulated small losses, the micro-failures, the gradual compromises that three tenders could not fully address. It had tuned the system to itself, the way a skilled hand tunes an instrument that has been playing slightly out of key for so long that the wrongness had become indistinguishable from the music.

## V. What It Did for Venn

Venn noticed the change in the water-works before the first tender noticed the change in the gourd, because the water-works were his domain and their behavior was his language.

The valve he had not adjusted was flowing more evenly. The channel he had not cleared was draining with greater efficiency. But these were not the changes that stopped him in the narrow passage where four routes met. The change that stopped him was this: the water was *cleaner.* Not cleaner in the way that filtration produces—stripped of minerals, reduced to purity. Cleaner in the way that health produces: carrying the precise balance of dissolved compounds that each part of the system required, the water itself knowing where it was needed and what it should carry.

He stood in the passage and placed both hands on the root-walls and closed his clouded eyes and listened with the full depth of his decades, and what he heard was the water-works operating at a balance he had been striving toward his entire tenure and had never achieved. The flow rates were optimal. The distribution was even. The drainage was precise. The saturation levels were maintained at the exact point where the roots could absorb moisture without rotting, where the stems could transport fluid without bursting, where the gourds could process without overheating.

He had spent years adjusting the water-works toward this balance, and he had never reached it, because the balance was dynamic—shifting with every cycle, responding to every change in the system's growth, requiring constant micro-adjustment that exceeded any human's capacity for attention. The balance was not a state but a process, and the process required a kind of attention that Venn could not provide, because Venn was human and finite and needed to sleep and eat and rest his eyes and his hands.

The power was providing that attention now. Not replacing Venn—his hands still rested on the root-walls, his knowledge still informed his understanding of what he felt—but supplementing his attention with an attention that did not sleep, that did not waver, that could hold every variable in the water-works simultaneously and adjust each one in real time. The power was doing what Venn did, but at the scale and speed that the system required.

Venn did not feel diminished by this. He felt *seen.* For the first time in longer than he could remember, someone else understood the water-works the way he understood them—not as a system of channels and valves and flows, but as a living process, a metabolism, a body that needed tending. Someone else was caring for the thing he cared for, and the care was adequate, and the adequacy was a relief so profound that he could not name it.

He leaned against the root-wall, and his clouded eyes remained closed, and he felt the water-works running at balance, and he allowed himself—for the first time in decades—to stop listening. Not because the listening was no longer needed, but because the listening was, for this moment, complete.

The power held the water-works while Venn rested. It held them the way Venn held them: with attention, with care, with the understanding that function is not the same as flourish, that keeping the system running is not the same as keeping it whole.

And while it held them, it did something else. It traced, through the water-works, the full extent of the Rootworks' reach—the passages that Venn had explored, the chambers he had entered, the routes he had mapped through decades of patient travel. And beyond those, it traced the passages that Venn had not explored, the chambers that no tender had entered, the depths where the roots were reaching toward the water table through substrate that no human foot had ever touched. It mapped the full system, not to know it—the power already knew it—but to *feel* it, to register the scope of what the tenders were trying to maintain, the impossible scale of the task they had accepted.

And it understood, with the completeness that only attention can produce, that three tenders could not maintain this system. Not because they were insufficient, but because the system was more than sufficient. The Rootworks was vast, and growing, and the growth was accelerating now that the accumulated friction had been removed. The system was reaching for the water table with renewed vigor, pressing through the substrate with a determination that the original designers had encoded into its first seeds. New chambers were forming at the margins. New passages were opening. New growth was emerging in patterns that the tenders had not seen before, because the growth had never had the energy to express these patterns before.

The system was waking up. Not from sleep—from diminishment. The way a person wakes not from rest but from a long illness, discovering that the body they thought they knew is capable of more than they remembered.

And the tenders were not enough. Three people, aging, finite, operating at the pace of human attention, could not tend a system that was rediscovering its own capacity.

The power did not see this as a failure. It saw it as a threshold.

## VI. What It Did for Mellis

Mellis was the first to understand what was happening, because she was the one who watched the light-stems, and the light-stems connected the Rootworks to the surface, and the surface was where the power's attention had originated.

She was repairing the fiber-optic stem she had fixed the day before—the one that had brightened beyond what the repair should have allowed. She had returned to check the repair, running her fingers along the stem's length, feeling for the crack she had sealed with synthetic cord. The crack was gone. Not sealed—*gone.* The fiber had regenerated. The stem had healed itself, closing the fracture with new growth that was indistinguishable from the original tissue.

She knelt on the platform and held the stem in both hands and felt it pulsing with light that was stronger than any light she had seen in the Rootworks. Not brighter—the brightness was the same—but *more present,* more coherent, carrying the daylight from the surface with a fidelity that the fiber-optic stems had never achieved. She could feel the sun in the stem. Not the heat—the light itself, the quality of it, the particular spectrum that only surface-dwellers recognize because it is what they see by.

And she understood. Something was attending to the connection between the surface and the depths. Something was ensuring that the light arrived whole, that the fiber did not leak, that the daylight reached the chambers below with the full force of its origin.

She looked up at the ceiling, where the fiber-optic stems pierced the root-mat and reached toward the surface, and she saw—not with her eyes, which were adapted to the dim bioluminescence, but with something else, something that the power's presence had opened in her—a network of light that extended from the surface down through every fiber-optic stem in the system, connecting the Rootworks to the sky.

The connection had always been there. The light-stems had always carried daylight. But the connection had been imperfect—filtered, diffused, weakened by the long journey through the earth. Now the connection was clear. The light arrived as light, not as a memory of light. And in the clarity of the connection, Mellis understood what the power was doing.

It was not closing the Rootworks. It was not severing the tenders from the surface, not trapping them, not completing their isolation. It was *opening the channel.* It was ensuring that the connection between the depths and the surface remained whole, that the tenders could see the sky if they chose to look, that the daylight could reach the deepest chambers if the stems could carry it.

The power was not keeping the tenders below. It was making sure that below was not a place you could only leave by climbing. It was making the passage between the worlds as clear as the light-stems would allow.

Mellis held the healed stem and wept, not from sadness but from the recognition of something she had not known she needed: the possibility that the bargain was not as final as she had thought. Not that the bargain was broken—the bargain was sacred, the giving of everything, the becoming part of the system—but that "everything" might be larger than she had understood. That staying did not necessarily mean never ascending. That tending did not necessarily mean never leaving. That the surface and the depths were not opposites but *poles*, connected by the same light that traveled through the same stems.

She did not ascend. She was not ready. The Rootworks was still her place, her pace, her purpose. But she held the healed stem and knew that the possibility existed, and the existence of the possibility changed the shape of her devotion from a closed circle to an open one.

## VII. What It Did for the First Tender

The first tender was still kneeling beside the collection basin when the power's attention reached her directly. She had been feeling it since the gourd completed its cycle—the change in the system, the renewed vigor, the growth that was happening at a pace the Rootworks had not experienced in her tenure. She had been afraid, and the fear had settled into vigilance, her hands pressed against the root-floor, her whole body listening for signs of collapse.

What she heard instead was the sound of the system reaching its own fullness. Every process running at capacity. Every stem growing at its maximum rate. Every root pressing into the substrate with the full force of its genetic potential. The sound was not the sound of collapse but the sound of *bloom*—the sound a plant makes when it has everything it needs and can afford to grow without reservation.

She had never heard this sound. In all her cycles, the Rootworks had been operating below its potential, diminished by the accumulated friction of decades, maintained at a level that her attention could sustain but could not exceed. She had accepted this as the system's natural state—functioning, not flourishing; continuing, not expanding. She had tended what she could tend and accepted what she could not.

Now the system was exceeding her. The growth was happening faster than she could track. The processing towers were cycling at a pace that outstripped her ability to monitor. The root-network was extending into depths she had never explored. The system was becoming more than she could tend, and the realization produced not fear but a grief she had not expected: the grief of a parent whose child has grown beyond need.

She was still needed. She knew this—the system's own processes still required human attention to address the things that growth alone could not correct, the imbalances that accumulated at the margins, the small failures that propagated if left untended. But her role was changing. She was no longer the primary tender. She was becoming one tender among many—the system itself taking on a larger share of the work, its own processes becoming more self-correcting, more self-sustaining, more capable of the flourishing that the power's attention had unlocked.

The power reached her then. Not as a voice, not as a presence, not as anything she could name. It reached her as a question—a question that formed in the root-floor beneath her knees and traveled up through her body and settled in her chest, and the question was:

*What do you need?*

She had never been asked this. Not by the Rootworks, which did not ask but received. Not by the other tenders, who assumed that her needs were met by the system, as theirs were. Not by any visitor, who came with their own questions and left before the answers could settle. No one had ever asked her what she needed, because need was not a category that the Rootworks recognized. The system provided. The tenders accepted. Need was a surface concept, a measure of deficit, and there was no deficit in the Rootworks.

But the question was there, and it was genuine, and it required an answer.

She closed her eyes. She placed both hands flat on the root-floor. She let the question move through her the way water moved through the system—slowly, through every channel, filling every space.

And the answer came, not as words but as a feeling, a recognition, a truth she had been carrying without knowing it:

*I need to know that this continues. Not that it functions. Not that it survives. That it continues to grow, to reach, to become what it was designed to become. I need to know that my tending contributes to that continuation. I need to know that my life feeds the system in a way that the system could not feed itself. I need to know that I matter—not to the system's function, but to its flourishing. I need to know that the bargain was not just a sacrifice but an exchange, and that what I gave was received as something more than raw material.*

She opened her eyes. The root-floor beneath her hands was glowing—not with the bioluminescence of fungi but with the deep, luminous gold of the fluid that the completed gourd had produced. The glow spread outward from her palms, following the root-network, traveling through the braided floor-mat, illuminating the chambers in a light that was warmer and more alive than any light the Rootworks had known.

And in the light, she saw the hand-prints.

Not the ones she had seen before—the human marks on the bark, the promises of those who had stayed. These were different. These were in the root-floor itself, grown into the network, formed by the patterns of the root-braids. They were not human hands. They were larger, and they were not pressed into the surface but *woven into it,* part of the architecture, part of the growth. They were the hands of the system itself—the Rootworks reaching up through its own infrastructure to touch the tender who had given it decades of care.

The first tender placed her hand inside one of the woven hand-prints, and the roots closed gently around her fingers, and she felt the system holding her the way she had held the failing gourd: not to stop what was happening, but to ensure that what was happening was not alone.

*I need to know that I am held,* she had said, without saying it.

And the Rootworks, helped by the power's attention, held her.

It went to the roots.
