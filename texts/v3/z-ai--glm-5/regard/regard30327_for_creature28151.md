# What the Power Does

It begins with attention.

Not the attention of intervention—no hand reaching down to rearrange, no voice announcing itself, no miracle. Simply the attention of being fully seen. The Condensery has been observed before, by researchers and hikers and forgotten bureaucrats, but it has never been *witnessed*. There is a difference. Observation catalogues; witnessing holds.

The power witnesses. It sits (metaphorically, for it has no body) at the edge of the basin and looks down into the pearl-gray light, and it does what the caretaker does: it learns. It learns the tightness of the numbers and the particular hiss of steam entering the third condenser chamber. It learns the dragonfly routes, mapped and remapped until the map becomes the territory. It learns the names the caretaker has given everything—the names that exist only in one aging mind and will die with him.

And in its witnessing, something stirs. Not in the basin—the basin is too well-made, too perfectly balanced, too committed to its stasis to notice attention. But in the caretaker. In the cats. At the edges where the stasis meets normal time.

The power loves this place. That must be said plainly. It loves the precision of the cut and the patience of the water and the sixty years of small recordings. It loves the cats with their unflinching vigil and the fish with their colorless rotation and the flies that walk on warm water. It loves even the machinery, which does what it was made to do and does it well. There is a beauty here. The power has no wish to break it.

But.

There is the caretaker.

The power sees him clearly now—the way he has made his peace, yes, but also the way that peace was made in the absence of alternatives. He has lived in this basin for so long that he has forgotten there were ever other shapes his life could have taken. He is content, but his contentment has the quality of a person who has stopped asking for things because asking has never once produced results.

The power does not fault him for this. The power admires his adaptation. But the power also sees the loneliness that he has pushed so far down that he no longer recognizes it as loneliness. It sees the part of him that talks to the machinery because there is no one else to hear, and that has come to believe that being heard would change nothing.

The power decides: it will give him nothing that disrupts. But it will give him something that enriches.

---

It starts small.

The next delivery box at the top of the access stairs contains, alongside the usual canned goods and dried provisions, a book. A single book, old and soft-backed, its spine cracked from previous readings. It is a book of poetry—not inspirational verse or scripture, but strange, earthy poems about ordinary things: stones and rivers and the way light falls through windows. The poems were written by someone who understood the sacredness of the ordinary.

The book has no note, no indication of why it appeared or who sent it. The caretaker finds it and holds it and does not know what to do with it. He opens it. He reads the first poem, which is about a leaf floating on still water.

He reads it again.

He does not know that this book was chosen for him specifically—that somewhere in the power's vast knowing, it understood that the caretaker has not read anything but ledgers for thirty years, and that he once loved poetry when he was young, before the basin became his world. He does not know that every poem in the book was selected to speak to his particular condition without ever naming it.

But he feels it. Something in his chest shifts.

---

The next change is even smaller.

The caretaker descends to his usual seat on the stone apron, ready to watch the dragonflies, and finds something new. A lizard—he thinks it is a lizard—clings to the condenser housing nearest to him. This is not unusual. But this lizard has a mark, a small irregularity in the scales of its back, a blotch of lighter pigment that he has never seen before.

He watches it. It watches him.

He finds himself speaking to it, quietly, the way he speaks to the machinery. He tells it about the poem he read last night, about the leaf floating on still water. The lizard does not move. It absorbs the heat and listens.

When the caretaker rises to make his rounds, the lizard is gone. But the next day, it is back—or a lizard with the same mark is back—in the same place. And the next day. And the next.

He names it. He does not speak the name aloud, but he thinks it: *Poem*.

---

The cats notice.

Cats are sensitive to shifts in their territory. They sense that something has entered the basin that was not there before—not a physical thing, but a quality. A presence that pays attention in a new way.

The orange cat of the eastern third, who sleeps on the warmest section of pipe, opens its eyes one afternoon and sees the caretaker reading on the stone apron. This is unusual. The caretaker sits, yes, but he watches. He does not read.

The cat rises, stretches, and walks—slowly, deliberately, with no urgency—to where the caretaker sits. It settles beside him, not touching, but close. The caretaker glances at it and then returns to his reading. The cat watches him turn pages.

After a long time, the cat rises and returns to its territory. But it has marked something: the caretaker is different. The basin is the same, but the caretaker has changed.

---

The fish do not notice. The flies do not notice. The dragonflies trace their paths and hunt and return to their perches. The machinery thrums and hisses and processes steam into water. The stasis holds.

But the stasis has developed a small crack—not a break, not a disruption, but a hairline fracture where something new has entered. The caretaker reads poems and speaks to a marked lizard and has begun, very quietly, to hum while he records his numbers.

He does not know that he hums. He would be surprised to learn it. But the basin knows. The machinery knows. And the power, still watching, loves what it has done—not because it has fixed anything, but because it has introduced a single thread of possibility into a place where possibility had been engineered out.

---

One evening, when the pale oval of light has withdrawn and the basin has settled into its uniform grayness, the caretaker feels something. He cannot name it. He looks up from the book he has nearly finished—there are poems he has read a dozen times, and he finds new things in them each time—and looks out across the water, the pools, the condenser array.

He feels witnessed.

It is not an unpleasant feeling. It is the sensation of being seen fully, without judgment, without expectation—the way a mountain is seen or a river is seen. He has not felt this since he cannot remember when. Perhaps never.

He does not know that the power is still there, watching. He does not need to know. The gift was not the knowledge of attention; the gift was the feeling that attention brings.

---

Time passes. The caretaker finishes the book and finds another in the next delivery box—this one a collection of essays about the sea, which he has never seen. He reads about tides and waves and the rhythm of water entering and leaving shores.

He thinks about the condensers, which are also a rhythm of water. Steam enters, condenses, departs. He has never thought of them as tidal before. Now he does.

The lizard with the mark—Poem—continues to visit. The cats continue to watch. The fish school in their slow rotations. The flies and dragonflies maintain their numbers. The machinery thrums.

But the caretaker's ledgers have changed. The numbers are the same—the basin's numbers cannot change—but alongside them, in the margins, small notations have begun to appear. A word. A phrase. Sometimes a line that came to him in the night, floating up from some deep place he had thought long dry.

He does not know that the power reads these. He does not know that what he writes will be preserved.

---

Years pass, or what feel like years. The stasis makes time strange. The caretaker ages—his hands grow slower, his back stoops, his eyes require more light than the basin provides. He reads by feel now, sometimes, having memorized the books that appear for him. He has collected a small library in his carved room, volumes that arrived one by one, each chosen for him and him alone.

The power has been patient. It could have done more—could have spoken to him directly, could have healed his joints or sharpened his eyes, could have brought him a companion or carried him out of the basin. But it has understood that to disrupt the stasis entirely would be to break the very thing that made his life what it was. It has given him what he could receive: a small expansion of possibility, a sense of being witnessed, the knowledge—vague but real—that his existence mattered.

Now, though, the power sees that his time is ending. Not immediately—there are years yet, if the basin's careful maintenance continues. But the power knows that when he dies, his knowledge will die with him, his names for things will vanish, and the basin will continue its cycles with no one to witness them.

The power makes a second decision.

---

The next visitor who comes—a researcher, perhaps, or a surveyor—finds something new. A door has appeared in the eastern wall, or rather, a door that was always there has become visible. Behind it: the caretaker's room, his ledgers, his books.

The visitor enters and finds the caretaker sitting on his bed, reading. The caretaker looks up, surprised. He has not had a visitor in... he cannot remember. The door has not opened in decades.

"Hello," he says.

The visitor, stunned by the room's existence, by the shelves of ledgers, by the old man with the soft-backed book in his hands, does not know what to say.

"Would you like to sit?" the caretaker asks. "I have water. It is warm, but it is wet."

The visitor sits. The caretaker offers water from a ceramic jug. They do not speak for a long time. The machinery thrums below. The dragonflies trace their paths.

"The basin is very old," the caretaker says finally. "It was cut before I came. It will remain after I go."

The visitor nods.

"I have names for things," the caretaker says. "Would you like to hear them?"

The visitor does not know why, but they nod.

The caretaker begins to speak. He names the dragonflies—their routes, their perches, their patient hunting. He names the cats, though he knows they will not answer to names. He names the fish, the pools, the cracks in the stone where water collects. He names the machinery—each condenser, each pipe, each valve and fitting. He names the flies, though there are thousands; he has named them as a group, as a category, as a presence.

He names the lizard, Poem, who visits him on the condenser housing.

He names the stasis, though he does not use that word. He calls it *the Agreement*, because that is how he has come to think of it—the arrangement between stone and iron and water and life that holds everything in place.

The visitor listens. And because the power has touched them, however lightly, they remember.

---

The caretaker dies on a warm afternoon, the book of sea-essays open on his chest. He has not finished it, but he has read the parts that matter.

The cats find him first, as he knew they would. They do not disturb him; they simply sit at the door of his room and wait.

The visitors—the power has sent more, now, a slow stream of people who know to come—arrive and find his body and treat it with respect. They do not leave him to the basin's processes. They carry him out of the cut, into the ordinary world where vegetation grows and time moves, and they bury him in soil that changes with seasons.

The ledgers they keep. The books they preserve. The names they write down.

---

The basin continues. The stasis holds. But now there are witnesses.

The visitors come regularly—not often, but often enough. They read the ledgers. They learn the names. They sit on the stone apron and watch the dragonflies and think of the man who named them.

The power watches too. It has given the Condensery what it needed: not rescue, not disruption, but memory. The basin will run until it cannot, and then it will stop, and then something else will happen. But what happened here, what the caretaker did, what the stasis meant—it will not vanish. It will be held.

And the power, satisfied, turns its attention elsewhere.

But not entirely.

Sometimes, when the light is gray and the steam rises in slow plumes, a visitor will look up and feel something—a presence, a watching, a love so vast and quiet it could be mistaken for the air itself.

They will not know what it is. But they will feel witnessed.

And that, the power thinks, is enough.
