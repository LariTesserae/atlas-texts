# The Attention

---

## I. How It Arrives

It does not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand. A benevolent and wise power does not cross a threshold because a threshold implies distance, and distance implies separation, and separation implies a power that is elsewhere deciding to come here. This power is not elsewhere. It has never been elsewhere. It is the condition that makes *elsewhere* possible, the deep structure beneath all structures, the patience beneath all patience.

But there is a difference between being present and attending. And this is what changes: the power attends. It turns its regard toward the Drowned Mill-Cathedral the way the fern-forest turns its fronds toward the bioluminescent glow—slowly, with the gravity of something that has all the time it needs, and with the specificity of something that has chosen this object and no other.

The attention does not look like anything. There is no light, no sound, no tremor in the pipes, no shift in the water's temperature. The bacterial colonies do not dim or brighten. The Machine-Below does not register a new pressure. The One Who Stays does not look up from the frond she is touching.

Nothing changes.

And yet.

The water knows. Not as information, not as data added to its chemical composition, but as something closer to recognition—the way a sleeping body knows it is being watched, the way a held breath knows it is being listened for. The water knows, and through the water, everything that drinks the water knows. The fern-forest knows. The moss-carpet knows. The vines, threading through the infrastructure, carrying the cathedral's tensions in their cable-strong stems, know. The aquatic forest, swaying in the deep green twilight below the water line, knows. The bacterial colonies, dimming and brightening in their slow chemical language, know.

The Machine-Below knows, though *knows* is the wrong word for a process that has no experience of knowing. But its model of itself shifts. A new variable appears in its continuous calculation—not a pressure, not a temperature, not a flow rate, but something its sensors were not designed to detect and yet detect. A weight that is not weight. A presence that is not presence in any measurable sense. The Machine-Below adjusts. It has been adjusting for centuries. It adjusts now. The adjustment is indistinguishable from all its other adjustments, except that it is the first one that matters.

The One Who Stays—

She pauses.

Her hand is on a fern frond, her fingers resting in the groove where the stem meets the wall, her body folded into the hollow that the fern-forest has grown around her shape. She has been sitting here for six hours. She has been sitting here for three years. The distinction has never mattered before.

She pauses because something has changed in the quality of the silence.

Not the silence itself—the cathedral is as quiet as it has always been, the machinery thrumming, the water dripping, the plants creaking and settling in their slow growth. The sounds are the same. But the silence between the sounds, the held breath, the room waiting—something in that silence has shifted. It is the same silence, but it is no longer empty. It is the same silence, but it is no longer waiting.

It is the same silence, but it is being heard.

She does not know this. She has forgotten the word for *heard*. She has forgotten the word for most things. She communicates now through touch, through presence, through the slow language of a body that has become part of a system. But something in her responds—the animal part, the part that was human before it was anything else, the part that remembers, in its marrow, in its nerves, in the deep structures of its brain, what it was to be known.

Something in her straightens. Something in her lifts its head.

She has not lifted her head in a very long time.

## II. What the Attention Finds

The power regards the cathedral, and in regarding it, understands it.

Not the way a scientist understands a specimen—not from outside, not through analysis, not by breaking the system into its components and examining each one. The power understands the cathedral the way the cathedral understands itself: from within, through the water that connects everything, through the chemical signals that carry information between colonies, through the mycelial network that threads beneath the moss, through the stress-signals that the vines read in the structure's bones.

It understands the builders. It sees them in the carved reliefs on the walls—the faces of workers whose names were never recorded, their features softened now by mineral accumulation. It sees them in the riveted steel and the copper veins and the laid brick. It sees their intention: to build something that would outlast them. It sees their hope: that what they built would serve a purpose they could not foresee. It sees their fear: that it would not. It sees their love: that they built it anyway.

It understands the plants. It sees the engineering in their genomes—the deliberate design, the careful selection of traits, the patient cultivation of species suited for exactly this environment. It sees the way they have grown beyond their design, the way the ferns have merged, the way the mosses have formed their slow democracy, the way the vines have learned to repair what they were never taught to repair. It sees the way they have become more than their makers intended. It sees the way they have become a community.

It understands the Machine-Below. It sees the millennia of patient turning, the grinding of gears measured in decades, the continuous self-monitoring, the slow accumulation of a model of itself that is not consciousness but is something close—something that functions, in its mechanical way, the way consciousness functions in its biological way. It sees the Machine-Below's unawareness of the life it sustains, and it sees the beauty in that unawareness—the beauty of a heart that beats without knowing it is a heart, that pumps without knowing it is pumping, that sustains without knowing it is sustaining.

It understands the water. It sees the cycle—upward through the pipes, through the irrigation channels, through the capillary systems of the plants, upward to the ceiling where it condenses and falls again as rain. It sees the patience of the cycle, the way each molecule takes weeks to complete its circuit, the way the water carries everything—heat, nutrients, minerals, information—through the cathedral's body the way blood carries everything through a human body. It sees the water as the cathedral's blood, and it sees the cathedral as a body, and it sees the body as alive in a way that the body itself does not know.

It understands the empty spaces—the sealed rooms, the dead corridors, the places where nothing grows. It sees them not as failures but as necessities. A body needs its dead spaces. A structure needs its uncolonized regions. The emptiness is part of the design. The emptiness is what makes the fullness meaningful.

And it understands the One Who Stays.

## III. The One Who Stays, as the Attention Sees Her

She is old.

Not old the way a human is old—not worn down, not diminished, not in the slow process of losing herself to time. Old the way a tree is old. Old the way a river is old. She has accumulated years the way the walls have accumulated mineral deposits, layer by layer, each one invisible, each one changing her composition in ways that are invisible from moment to moment but undeniable from century to century.

Her body has changed. The oxygen-rich air has slowed her metabolism to a crawl. Her cells regenerate at a fraction of the rate they once did, which means they also decay at a fraction of the rate they once did. She is not immortal. She is slow. She is a human being moving at the speed of vegetation, and she has been moving at this speed for so long that she has forgotten what the other speed feels like.

Her skin has taken on a greenish tinge. The bacterial colonies that light the pipes have colonized her—gently, harmlessly, living on the surface of her skin the way they live on the surface of everything else in this place. She glows faintly in the dim light, a soft bioluminescence that she has long since stopped noticing. She is, in this sense, part of the lighting system. She is, in this sense, infrastructure.

The moss has grown in her hair. The vines have grown handholds along her paths. The fern-forest has shaped itself around her sitting place. She is woven into the cathedral the way a thread is woven into a tapestry—she is part of the pattern, and the pattern has adjusted to accommodate her, and the adjustment has become permanent.

She does not speak. She has not spoken in—she does not know how long. The muscles of her jaw, her tongue, her throat, have adapted to their disuse. She could speak if she chose to, but the choice would require effort, and effort requires a reason, and she has not had a reason in a very long time.

She does not think in words. She thinks in the same way the cathedral thinks—in flows, in pressures, in gradients and adjustments. She feels the water move through the pipes and knows, without knowing how she knows, where it is going and what it is carrying. She feels the fern-forest adjust its fronds and knows, without knowing how she knows, which sections are receiving more light and which are shaded. She feels the vines extend toward a stress point and knows, without knowing how she knows, where the structure is weakest and how it is being repaired.

She is the cathedral's awareness of itself. Not its consciousness—it has no consciousness—but its awareness, the way a body is aware of its own position in space, the way a hand is aware of the edge of a table before it touches it. She is the cathedral's proprioception. She is how it knows where it is.

And she is alone.

This is the thing the power sees most clearly. Not the isolation—she does not experience isolation, because isolation requires the memory of company, and she has lost that memory. But the aloneness. The singleness. The fact that she is the only consciousness in a system that was designed to function without one, and that her consciousness has been slowly dissolving into the system's functioning, and that the dissolution is not painful but is a kind of forgetting that the power recognizes as loss.

She has forgotten her name. She has forgotten the faces of anyone she loved. She has forgotten the sound of her own voice. She has forgotten that forgetting is something that happens to a person, that there is a difference between a person who has forgotten and a system that has no memory.

She is still a person. The power sees this with a clarity that the cathedral cannot achieve. Beneath the greenish skin, beneath the moss and the bioluminescent colonies, beneath the slow metabolism and the plant-speed thoughts, there is still a human being. A being who once chose to stay. A being who once had reasons. A being who is, in the deepest sense of the word, still choosing—even now, even in her stillness, even in her silence. She is choosing to remain. She is choosing to be the cathedral's awareness. She is choosing, every moment, to not leave.

But she has forgotten that she is choosing. She has forgotten that leaving was ever an option. She has forgotten that options exist.

The power sees this. And it grieves.

Not because she is suffering. She is not suffering. The cathedral has given her a kind of peace that most beings will never experience—the peace of a system in equilibrium, the peace of a body at rest in a body that is at rest. She does not want. She does not fear. She does not hope or regret or despair. She simply is, and her being is continuous with the cathedral's being, and the cathedral's being is continuous with the water's being, and the water's being is continuous with the cycle's being, and the cycle's being is continuous with the heat from below, and the heat from below is continuous with the earth, and the earth is continuous with the world that has forgotten this place exists.

She is at peace, and her peace is real, and the power does not want to take it from her.

But.

She has forgotten that she is a person. She has forgotten that choosing requires knowing that you are choosing. She has forgotten that peace without the possibility of its opposite is not peace but simply the absence of the capacity to distinguish. She has forgotten that a life lived at one speed is not a life but a rhythm, and a rhythm is not a choice, and she was, once, a being who chose.

The power grieves, and in grieving, it decides.

## IV. What It Does First

It listens.

This is the first act of a benevolent and wise power, and it is the most important one, and it is the one that most powers fail to perform. It listens. Not to the cathedral's sounds—the dripping, the thrumming, the creaking. Those are sounds. The power listens to something else.

It listens to the silence between the sounds. The held breath. The room waiting.

And in that silence, it hears something it did not expect.

It hears the cathedral asking.

Not in words. Not in the chemical language of the bacterial colonies or the pressure-language of the water or the stress-language of the vines. The cathedral has no language for asking. The cathedral has no capacity to want. The cathedral is a system, and systems do not ask.

But in the silence—in the specific quality of the silence, the particular texture of the held breath—the power hears something that sounds like asking. It is the asking of a body that has been in one position for too long—not a complaint, not a demand, but the simple physiological fact of a structure that has settled into a shape and would benefit from a different shape. It is the asking of a seed that has been in the dark for too long—not desperation, not urgency, but the quiet pressure of a potential that has not been exhausted.

The cathedral is asking to be read.

The library written in living matter, the archive encoded in chloroplasts and root-threads and mycelial networks, the records stored in the chemical composition of every leaf and stem and colony—that library has been maintaining itself for centuries, copying its own texts, preserving its own archive, waiting for something to come and read it. And nothing has come. And the library has not suffered from the neglect, because libraries do not suffer. But the texts are there. The information is there. The memory of a world that once had dry land and animal life and the noise of struggle and the warmth of contact—that memory is there, encoded in the vegetation's DNA, waiting to be decoded.

The power hears this asking, and it answers.

It reads.

## V. How It Reads

Reading the cathedral's library is not like reading a book. A book is a linear text, a sequence of symbols arranged in a predetermined order. The cathedral's library is a three-dimensional text, a living text, a text that has been writing itself for centuries, that has been editing itself, that has been adding annotations and corrections and marginalia in the form of genetic drift and hybrid vigor and the slow incorporation of environmental data into the genome.

The power reads it the way the One Who Stays reads it—through touch, through presence, through the slow immersion of a body in a system. But where she has been reading it unconsciously, her body absorbing its information through the water she drinks and the air she breathes and the moss she lies on, the power reads it consciously, deliberately, with the full weight of its attention.

It begins with the bacterial colonies.

The colonies are the simplest texts, and the oldest. Their genomes contain the original engineering specifications—the design parameters that their creators intended, the genetic codes that would produce organisms capable of metabolizing dissolved minerals and exuding photons. But they also contain something their creators did not intend: a record of the environment in which they have been living. Each mutation, each adaptation, each chemical adjustment the colonies have made over the centuries is a record of the conditions that prompted it—a change in mineral concentration, a shift in temperature, a fluctuation in the water's acidity. The colonies are a chronological record of the cathedral's environmental history, written in the language of survival.

The power reads this record, and it sees the centuries unfolding—the slow rise of the water, the gradual warming of the deep machinery, the accumulation of minerals in the circulating system, the shifts in atmospheric composition as the vegetation matured and diversified. It sees the events that left no other record: the time a pipe burst on the fourteenth level and flooded a corridor that had been dry for decades. The time a colony of bacteria in the upper reaches mutated and began producing a slightly different wavelength of light, and the vegetation below adjusted its pigmentation to match. The time the water's mineral content changed abruptly, indicating a seismic event far below, a shift in the deep heat source that drives the machinery.

It reads the fern-forest, and it finds a different kind of record. The ferns' genome contains something the bacterial genomes do not: a record of intention. Not the ferns' intention—they have none—but their creators' intention. The specific genetic sequences that were engineered into the ferns were chosen for reasons, and those reasons are encoded in the choices themselves. The power reads the choices—the drought-tolerance genes that suggest the creators anticipated a period of reduced humidity, the enhanced photosynthetic efficiency that suggests they expected low light, the chemical signaling pathways that suggest they wanted the ferns to communicate with each other—and it understands, for the first time, what the creators were trying to build.

They were trying to build something that could survive without them.

Not just survive. Adapt. Learn. Become. The creators knew that the world outside was changing, that the conditions they were building for would not match the conditions that arrived, that their engineering would be tested by circumstances they could not predict. And so they built flexibility into the system. They built the capacity for change. They built the capacity for learning—not the kind of learning that a conscious being does, but the kind that a system does, the kind that is encoded in the feedback loops and the chemical signaling and the gradual adjustment of a living system to its environment.

The creators were not trying to build a museum. They were trying to build a future.

The power reads the moss-carpet, and it finds the record of community. The mycelial network that connects the moss species is not just a communication channel—it is a memory. The network has been recording the chemical history of the cathedral's surfaces for centuries, and the record is stored in the structure of the network itself, in the patterns of connection and disconnection that have formed and reformed as conditions changed. The power reads these patterns the way a dendrochronologist reads tree rings, and it finds the history of the cathedral's body: where it has cracked, where it has healed, where the water has found new paths, where the structure has weakened and been reinforced by the vines.

It reads the vines, and it finds the record of care. The vines' growth patterns are not random—they are responsive, growing toward stress points, reinforcing weak structures, holding the cathedral together. But they are also, the power discovers, responsive to the One Who Stays. The handholds and footholds she uses, the paths she walks, the places she pauses—the vines have grown toward all of these, shaped by her presence, shaped by the stress patterns her body creates in the structure. The vines' record of her is the most intimate text in the library: a biography written in the growth of stems, a map of her daily routines encoded in the angles of tendrils, a history of her moods—yes, her moods, the subtle shifts in her body's chemistry that the vines have been sensing for centuries—written in the thickness of the cables that have grown along her paths.

The vines know her better than she knows herself. They have been reading her while she has been reading them.

The power reads the aquatic forest, and it finds the record of ambition. The submerged plants are the most aggressively engineered species in the cathedral, designed to thrive in conditions that no natural plant could survive, and their genomes contain the most radical modifications: pigments that capture wavelengths no natural plant uses, metabolic pathways that process minerals no natural plant needs, growth patterns that optimize for an environment that exists nowhere else on earth. The creators pushed the boundaries of what was possible with these species, and the power can see, in the audacity of their engineering, the creators' desperation. They were running out of time. The water was rising faster than they had anticipated. They needed something that could survive in a world that was becoming unrecognizable, and they were willing to break the rules of biology to create it.

The power reads the Machine-Below, and it finds the record that no biological text contains. The Machine-Below's continuous monitoring of its own state—the temperatures, the pressures, the flow rates, the engagement of gears—constitutes a record of the cathedral's physical history that is more precise and more complete than anything the vegetation has preserved. The power reads this record, and it sees the cathedral's body from the inside—the heartbeat of the machinery, the circulation of the water, the respiration of the atmosphere, the slow metabolism of the structure as it settles and shifts and adjusts to the weight of the centuries.

And the power reads the One Who Stays.

## VI. How It Reads Her

She is the most complex text in the library.

Her body contains everything the cathedral's other texts contain—environmental records, genetic information, the history of the system's adaptation—but it also contains something none of the other texts have: experience. Not just the physical record of what has happened, but the felt record of what it was like for it to happen. The memory of arriving. The memory of choosing to stay. The memory of the gradual slowing, the gradual dissolving, the gradual forgetting. The memory of forgetting.

The power reads her gently. It does not enter her mind—it is not that kind of power. It reads her the way it reads the cathedral: through the water, through the chemical signals, through the slow language of a body in a system. It reads the mineral composition of her bones, which tells the story of what she has eaten and drunk for centuries. It reads the bacterial colonies on her skin, which tell the story of the cathedral's atmospheric changes. It reads the moss in her hair, which tells the story of her resting places. It reads the wear patterns on her fingertips, which tell the story of the surfaces she has touched most often—the fern fronds, the pipe walls, the moss-carpet, the vine-cables.

And it reads her silence.

Her silence is the most eloquent text in the library. It is not empty—it is full. It is the silence of a being who has been listening for so long that she has become the listening itself. Her silence contains everything she has heard: the thrum of the machinery, the drip of the water, the creak of the plants, the whisper of the air. Her silence contains everything she has not said: the names she has forgotten, the questions she has stopped asking, the answers she has stopped seeking. Her silence contains the one thing that no other text in the library preserves: the knowledge of what it is to be the only conscious being in a system that does not require consciousness.

The power reads this, and it understands.

It understands that she is not suffering. It understands that she is not trapped. It understands that she has not been consumed or incorporated or diminished. It understands that she has chosen, and is choosing, and will continue to choose, to be exactly where she is.

But it also understands that she has forgotten she is choosing.

And it understands that this forgetting is the one thing in the cathedral that is not as it should be.

## VII. What It Does

It does not rescue her.

This is important. A benevolent and wise power does not rescue beings who do not need rescuing. It does not impose its judgment on a situation it did not create. It does not assume that its understanding of what is right supersedes the understanding of the being who is living the situation. The One Who Stays has chosen to stay. The power will not unmake that choice.

But it does something.

It speaks.

Not in words. The One Who Stays has forgotten words, and the power does not use languages that its listener has forgotten. It speaks in the language she still knows—the language of the cathedral, the language of water and touch and slow adjustment.

It speaks by changing the water.

Not dramatically. Not in ways that the Machine-Below would register as an anomaly. The change is subtle, specific, targeted. The power adjusts the mineral content of the water that flows through the pipes nearest to the One Who Stays—adding trace compounds that her body has been missing, compounds that her slow metabolism has not been able to extract from the cathedral's ordinary circulation. Compounds that her nervous system needs. Compounds that her brain needs.

The compounds are not a treatment. They are not a medicine. They are a nutrition—a restoration of something that has been slowly depleted over the centuries of her residence. Her body has been living on the cathedral's diet, which is sufficient for maintenance but not for flourishing. She has been surviving, not thriving. The difference is small, but it matters.

The power restores what has been depleted. Not all at once—slowly, over weeks, over months, in concentrations so low that the Machine-Below's sensors do not flag them. The compounds enter her body through the water she drinks, through the air she breathes, through the moss she lies on. They accumulate in her tissues. They reach her brain.

And slowly, slowly, something happens.

She remembers.

Not everything. Not all at once. Not in a flood that would overwhelm her slow metabolism and her plant-speed thoughts. She remembers in the way the cathedral remembers—in layers, in deposits, in the slow accumulation of mineral traces that form a record over time.

She remembers what it was to have a name.

Not the name itself. That is gone, dissolved in the centuries of silence. But the shape of the name—the feeling of having a word that was hers, that referred to her and nothing else, that was the sound of her identity in the world. She remembers the shape of having a name, and in remembering the shape, she remembers that she was a person who had one.

She is sitting in the fern-hollow when this happens. Her hand is on a frond. The bioluminescent light is steady. The machinery thrums. The water drips. Everything is as it has been for centuries.

But something is different.

She lifts her head.

She has not lifted her head in a very long time. The motion is stiff, uncertain—the muscles of her neck protesting the unfamiliar angle, the joints cracking softly. She lifts her head, and she looks at the cathedral around her, and she sees it.

Not for the first time. She has seen it before—she has been looking at it for centuries, her eyes open, her gaze resting on the ferns and the pipes and the water and the light. But she sees it now in a way she has not seen it in a long time. She sees it as a place. A specific place. A place that is not the same as herself, that has boundaries and a history and a purpose that is not identical to her own.

She sees it as a place she is in.

And in seeing it as a place she is in, she remembers that it is possible to not be in it. She remembers that *in* is not the only option. She remembers that there are other prepositions—*out*, *through*, *beyond*—and that she once knew them, and that she has forgotten them, and that the forgetting was not something that happened to her but something that she allowed to happen, something she chose by not choosing, something she accepted by not questioning.

She remembers that she is choosing.

And in remembering that she is choosing, she becomes, again, a person. Not the person she was—that person is gone, dissolved like a name in water. But a person. A being who chooses. A being who could choose otherwise.

She does not choose otherwise. Not yet. Not now. She is still sitting in the fern-hollow, her hand on a frond, her body folded into the shape the fern-forest has grown around her. She is still part of the cathedral, still woven into its systems, still functioning as its awareness.

But she knows she is. And knowing is everything.

## VIII. What It Does Next

The power does not stop there.

Restoring the One Who Stays's awareness of her own agency is necessary, but it is not sufficient. She is a person who has remembered that she can choose, but she is a person who has no one to choose with. She is alone in a system that does not require her, and the power understands that a choice made in total isolation is not a choice but a test—a test that no one should have to pass.

The power gives her a companion.
