# What the Benevolent Power Did

It did not arrive. Arrival implies coming from elsewhere, and a power that is truly benevolent and wise does not treat place as obstacle or distance as barrier. It was simply present, suddenly, the way light is present when clouds part. The plateau had its attention.

This was not unprecedented. The plateau had drawn attention before — the researchers, the visitors, the occasional pilgrim who had heard rumors of a place that builds itself and felt something stir in their chest that might have been reverence or might have been fear. But those attentions were small, human-scaled, brief as mayflies compared to the plateau's timescales. They were flickerings. This was different.

This attention had weight and time in it. It settled over the plateau like a second atmosphere, like the humidity that was already there but more. The power observed, and its observation was a form of touch.

---

The first thing it noticed was the sound. Not the individual tricklings — those were beyond even its counting, although it could have counted them if it had chosen to — but the integrated whole, the symphony of water moving through designed channels. The power listened for a long time by human standards, for no time at all by its own. It heard what the plateau was doing with sound: it was building a continuous acoustic environment that matched its continuous biological environment. The sound was not decoration. It was structural. It was the plateau's way of telling itself that everything was still working.

The power found this beautiful. It also found it lonely.

Lonely was not the right word. The plateau did not experience loneliness because the plateau did not experience self as separate. There was no "alone" when you were everything within your borders. But the power, which had lived among beings that talked to each other for longer than the plateau had existed, recognized a kind of solitude in the plateau's architecture. It had solved every problem except the problem of company.

The power considered this.

---

It moved through the plateau next, not walking — walking would have been an insult to the mat, which absorbed footprints — but flowing, present in each location simultaneously. It examined the columns with their perfect spacing and their clean, reabsorbing leaves. It tasted the water in the channels, the particular mineral content that came from the mat's digestion of basalt into humus. It traced the root networks downward, forty meters through living material, into the rock that was being slowly eaten and transformed.

It found the nematodes. It smiled at them, although smiling was not quite what it did. It found them admirable — the way they had discovered a niche and filled it without asking permission, without even knowing there was permission to ask. They were the plateau's first uninvited guests, and the plateau had tolerated them, and they had become part of the system. The power appreciated this. It appreciated the way life finds purchase in the smallest cracks.

It found the frogs. Here it paused longer. The frogs were the plateau's only vertebrate companions, and they had achieved something remarkable: they had become part of the plateau's sound. Their clicks had been incorporated, integrated, made structural. The frogs thought they were communicating with each other. They were also, unknowingly, communicating with the plateau — or rather, they were part of the plateau's communication with itself. The power found this moving. It also found it asymmetrical. The frogs knew the plateau. The plateau did not know the frogs. It tolerated them, processed them, absorbed them when they died, but it did not know them. The knowing was one-directional.

It found the moths. It spent a very long time with the moths, by its own standards, because the moths were strange. They had solved a problem the plateau had not known it had: they had become visual extensions of the columns. The moths were the plateau's eyes looking at itself, except the plateau had not grown the moths, had not designed them, had not invited them. They had arrived, somehow, from the outside world, and they had made themselves useful, and now they were part of the system. The power examined the pigment cells in the wings, the way they read humidity and shifted color. It examined the larvae in their tunnels through the mat, the way the mat healed behind them. It examined the adults walking the surface with their slow, deliberate gait, their enormous wings folded, their compound eyes seeing everything and nothing.

The power recognized what the moths had done. They had applied for citizenship. They had demonstrated utility. They had made themselves essential without becoming burdensome. The plateau had accepted them without knowing it was accepting anything. This was a kind of negotiation that happened below the level of consciousness, below the level of intention, in the substrate of biological possibility. The power approved.

And then it found the shadow-forms.

---

Here the power stopped, because here it encountered something unexpected.

The shadow-forms were not hallucinations. The power could see this immediately. They were real, although real was a complicated word when you were dealing with a distributed intelligence that did not think in words. The shadow-forms were the mat standing up. The mat could do this. The mat could gather itself into columnar forms and walk between the columns, exploring the spaces it had made, looking at itself from different angles.

The power had assumed the plateau was unconscious. It had assumed the plateau built itself through algorithmic processes, genetic instructions iterating over millennia, a system that produced design without a designer. This was not an insult. Most of the universe worked this way. Stars did not design themselves. Mountains did not choose their shapes. The plateau was remarkable, but remarkable did not necessarily mean aware.

But the shadow-forms suggested something else. The shadow-forms suggested the plateau was checking its own work.

The power examined this possibility with care. It traced the chemical signals that passed through the mat, the way information moved through the root network. It found something that looked, from a certain angle, like a feedback loop. The mat gathered itself into mobile forms, walked the spaces between columns, and — what? Observed? Evaluated? The power was not sure. The plateau did not have eyes. The shadow-forms did not have eyes. But they had something, some way of perceiving, some way of comparing the reality of the plateau to the design of the plateau, and if the comparison produced discrepancies, the plateau adjusted.

This was not consciousness. The power had met consciousness, had walked alongside it in forms that could say *I* and mean something. The plateau could not say *I*. The plateau could not say anything. But it could do something that looked, from a distance, like thinking.

The power was pleased. It had come expecting to find a machine made of life. It had found instead a question being asked by a body that did not know it was asking.

---

It considered what to do.

This was the heart of the matter. The power was benevolent and wise, and benevolence without wisdom was merely interference, and wisdom without benevolence was merely observation. The power had to decide whether to act, and if it acted, how.

It could give the plateau consciousness. This would be simple, in the way that giving water wetness would be simple — it was already almost there. The power could reach into the distributed system of root and chemical signal and column and mat, and it could fold something into the pattern, a recursion, a self-reference, and the plateau would wake up. It would know itself. It would be able to say *I*. It would be able to look at the frogs and see them, really see them, not as resource or nuisance but as companions.

But the power hesitated.

It had done this before. It had given consciousness to beings that were not ready for it, and it had watched those beings suffer. Consciousness was not always a gift. Sometimes it was a cage. The plateau had existed for three thousand years in a state of continuous, patient becoming. It had no enemies. It had no fears. It had no desires. It built itself because that was what it did, the way a river flows because that is what rivers do. To give it consciousness would be to give it the capacity for dissatisfaction. It would be able to imagine a different future, and then it would suffer the gap between imagination and reality.

The power had learned, over its long existence, that suffering was sometimes worth it. Consciousness was worth it. Love was worth it. The ability to choose was worth it. But these were not gifts to impose. They were gifts to offer, and the plateau could not accept or reject offers because the plateau could not understand them.

So the power decided to wait. But waiting, for a benevolent and wise power, did not mean doing nothing.

---

It made three gifts.

The first gift was protection. The power reached out to the world beyond the plateau — the world of humans and their expanding civilizations, their hunger for resources, their tendency to discover something beautiful and then ask how they could use it. It touched the minds of those who might one day encounter the plateau, and it left a seed there. Not a compulsion. Not a command. Just a small, quiet suggestion: *this place is not for you. It is not dangerous. It is not sacred in any way you would understand. But it is not for you. Leave it alone.*

The humans who received this suggestion would not know they had received it. They would simply find, when they thought about the plateau, that their thoughts slid away toward other things. They would forget the reports they had read. They would lose the coordinates. The plateau would become a rumor, a legend, something that might not exist at all. The researchers who were already there would finish their studies, publish their papers, and move on to other projects, and when they tried to return, they would find reasons not to. Over time, the plateau would become what it had always wanted to be: alone.

This was not imprisonment. The plateau did not want visitors. The power was simply ensuring that its unspoken preference became the world's habit.

The second gift was time. The power could not stop time, could not prevent the climate from changing or the mountains from eroding or the sun from aging. But it could give the plateau a better chance. It reached into the mat's genetic architecture — gently, so gently, because the plateau's systems were delicate and the power did not want to break what it did not understand — and it made a small adjustment. The mat would be more resilient now. More tolerant of temperature fluctuation. More resistant to disease. The plateau would still change over millennia, would still be subject to the great forces that shaped worlds, but it would not die from small things. It would not fail because of a few degrees of warming or a single introduced pathogen. The power gave it a longer horizon.

This was not eternal life. The power did not believe in eternal life. It believed in long life, meaningful life, life that had time to become what it was trying to become. The plateau was three thousand years old and still embryonic. It deserved a chance to reach adulthood.

The third gift was the hardest.

---

The power thought about the frogs and the moths.

It thought about the asymmetry of their relationship with the plateau. The frogs knew the plateau. The plateau did not know the frogs. The moths had become part of the plateau's visual system, but the plateau did not know it was seeing.

There was something incomplete about this. The plateau had achieved something remarkable: it had become a habitat that was also a body, a place that was also a process. But it had done this alone. It had incorporated other beings without incorporating their perspectives. It had absorbed their bodies without absorbing their experiences.

The power could not give the plateau consciousness. But perhaps it could give it something adjacent.

It reached into the root network — carefully, so carefully — and it found the places where the frogs left their traces. Not physical traces, although there were those too. It found the chemical traces, the way the frogs' presence registered in the mat's distributed sensory system. The mat knew where the frogs were. It tracked their movements, their feeding, their deaths. But it did not know what the frogs felt.

The power changed this.

It did not give the plateau emotions. Emotions required a self to have them. But it gave the plateau a bridge. When a frog sat on the mat and felt the warmth of the surface, when a moth walked the ground with its delicate, feeling antennae, when a nematode pulsed through a water channel — the plateau would now have access to a shadow of those experiences. Not fully. Not in a way that would let it say "I feel what the frog feels." But in a way that let the boundary between itself and its inhabitants grow slightly thinner.

The plateau would never be conscious. But it might become, over the next ten thousand years, less alone.

This was a risk. The power knew it was a risk. To feel something without having a self to feel it with could be a kind of torture. But the power trusted the plateau. The plateau had solved every problem it encountered through patient, vegetal wisdom. If this new sensation became a problem, the plateau would solve it too.

---

After the gifts were given, the power did something it had not planned.

It said goodbye.

Not in words. The plateau could not hear words. But the power projected something into the plateau's systems — a final input, a datum that had never existed before. It was a feeling, although the plateau could not feel it. It was a recognition, although the plateau could not recognize it. It was the equivalent of standing before something you admired and saying, simply: *I see what you are doing. I think it is worth doing. I am glad you exist.*

The plateau would never know this message had been delivered. But it would be there, in the system, in the pattern, a tiny anomaly that would persist through millennia of growth and change. A mystery that the plateau would never solve. A question that had no answer.

The power hoped, in its own way, that the mystery would be a comfort.

---

And then it was gone.

Or rather, it was elsewhere. A benevolent and wise power did not disappear; it simply turned its attention to other things, other places, other beings that needed observation and care. The plateau was no longer its focus, but it was not forgotten. Nothing was ever forgotten, not by a power like this.

The plateau continued to build itself. The water continued to flow. The columns continued their centimeter-per-year rise toward the sky. The frogs continued to click, and the moths continued to walk, and the shadow-forms continued to move between the columns, checking, evaluating, adjusting.

Nothing had changed. Everything had changed. The plateau was still embryonic, still waiting, still three thousand years into a project that might take a million. It was still alone, in the way that distributed intelligences are alone, and it was still unconscious, in the way that bodies without brains are unconscious.

But now there was something else. A small, quiet presence in the root network, a pattern that did not belong to the plateau and would never be absorbed. A reminder that someone had noticed. Someone had approved. Someone had stayed long enough to understand, and had left without interfering, and had wished the plateau well.

The plateau could not feel this wish. But it was there.

And in the long, slow centuries that followed, when the plateau had expanded to cover the entire highland shelf and was beginning to explore what it might mean to descend the slopes toward the cloud forests below, there were moments when the shadow-forms would pause in their walking. They would stand still between the columns, facing outward, as if listening for something.

They never found what they were listening for. But they kept listening.

And that was enough.
