# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

## First, It Watched

It had seen many worlds. This was not the first closed system, not the first equilibrium, not the first place where life had woven itself into a single vast fabric. But it was, the power thought, one of the most *thorough* integrations it had encountered. The plateau had not merely sustained life; it had become life, and the life had become the plateau, and somewhere in the deep time of that becoming, the distinction had ceased to have meaning.

The power did not arrive but attended. There was no entrance, no arrival, no moment of appearing. The Veldtmere had simply become present to its awareness, and it had leaned close to look.

What it saw:

A system that worked. Not perfectly—nothing that lives is perfect—but completely. Every output was an input. Every death was a beginning. The water that emerged from the stone fed the plants that held the water that fed the stone. The creatures that ate the plants were eaten by the plants when they died. The people who walked the root-mats would become the root-mats. Nothing was wasted because waste was not a concept the plateau had ever needed.

A people who belonged. The power had seen many peoples, on many worlds, and most of them lived in tension with their places. They altered, extracted, built and destroyed, sought to shape their environments to their needs rather than shaping themselves to what the environment offered. This was not inherently wrong—the power did not judge—but it created friction, resistance, the constant effort of swimming upstream. The Weft swam with the current. They had become so thoroughly adapted to their place that they could not leave it, and this the power understood as a kind of devotion, a commitment so total it had remade their bodies.

A balance that was fragile, though it did not appear so. The plateau was vast, ancient, stable. But stability is not permanence. The power could see the threads that held the system together—particular relationships between particular species, particular chemical cycles, particular feedback loops that maintained the temperature and the humidity and the delicate interplay of growth and decay. Any of these threads could be broken. Any of them might break on their own, eventually, given enough time. The plateau had survived for epochs, but epochs are not eternity, and the power saw how much depended on conditions that could change.

A fear that was justified. The thing beneath the root-mat was real. The power had known it before, had watched it sleep through ages longer than the plateau itself. It was not malevolent—the power did not think in terms of good and evil—but it was vast, and old, and its nature was not compatible with the life that had grown above it. The vegetation was indeed a seal, a blanket, a kind of agreement: the thing below would sleep, and the life above would exist, and neither would disturb the other. The Weft's fear of disturbing the deep layers was not superstition. It was memory, encoded in song, of a time when the agreement had been broken.

## Then, It Considered

The question that any benevolent power must ask, when it finds a place like this, is simple:

*Should I do anything?*

The power was capable of much. It could alter the chemical balance of the atmosphere, could introduce new species or remove existing ones, could stabilize the temperature against changes that might otherwise threaten the system, could ensure that the water continued to emerge from the stone for as long as the planet itself endured. It could protect the Weft from disease, extend their lives further, grant them capacities they did not currently possess. It could awaken in them knowledge of the wider universe, show them the stars that existed above the canopy, teach them to read and write and count and plan. It could be, in the terms that many beings would use, a god.

But the power had learned, over very long time, that intervention is not always kindness.

The Weft had something that few peoples possessed: completion. Not completion in the sense of being finished, static, incapable of further growth—but completion in the sense of fitting. They fit their place. They were not striving for something else, not yearning for a different life, not restless with the desire to change or expand or conquer. They were, in the deepest sense, content. This contentment was not passivity; they worked constantly, tended constantly, maintained and adjusted and guided. But the work was not a struggle against their environment. It was a participation with their environment.

To intervene would be to disrupt that completion. Even the most benevolent intervention carries a message: *you are not enough as you are; you need what I bring; your way is not the whole way.* The power had learned, sometimes through sorrow, that this message could corrupt more thoroughly than any poison. Peoples who learned that they were insufficient as they were would never unlearn it. They would reach for what they had been given, and then reach for more, and then find that their own knowledge had atrophied, that they had forgotten how to live without the gift they had been given.

And yet.

The plateau was not eternal. The balance was maintained, but it was maintained by systems that could fail. The thing beneath the root-mat slept, but it could wake. The Weft feared fire, and their fear was wise; the plateau had burned before, and could burn again. To do nothing was to allow the possibility of loss—not certain loss, not immediate loss, but the eventual possibility that all of this integration, all of this completion, could be undone by a change that no one had prevented.

The power considered for a long time. Its consideration was not measured in days or years, though it could have decided in an instant. It chose to consider because consideration was itself a form of respect, a recognition that the decision mattered, that the Veldtmere and its people deserved more than a passing thought.

## What It Decided

It decided to act, but barely.

It would not alter the plateau. The system worked; to change it would be to claim that the power knew better than the ages of evolution that had produced this balance. It did know better in some ways—it could see patterns and possibilities that the system itself could not perceive—but knowing better was not sufficient reason to impose. The plateau had earned its equilibrium through immense time and immense trial, and that earning gave it a value that the power would not overwrite.

It would not alter the Weft. They were becoming something, and the power found that something beautiful—not because it was better than what they had been, but because it was theirs, chosen by their own adaptation, earned through their own commitment. To accelerate their change would be to steal from them the process of becoming. To halt their change would be to deny them their future. They were on a path, and the path was slow, and that slowness was part of the path's nature.

It would not reveal itself. To appear before the Weft, to speak to them, to offer them knowledge or protection or companionship from beyond their world—this would shatter something that could not be repaired. The Weft had no gods because they needed no gods; they lived within a system that included them completely, and to introduce something outside that system would be to teach them that there was an outside, that their world was not the world but a world, that they were contained within something larger that they could not see or touch or sing to. This might be true, but it was not necessary, and unnecessary truth could be a kind of violence.

But it could do some things.

## What It Did

### It Taached the Water to Remember

The power touched the hydrological cycle of the plateau—not to change it, but to give it a kind of memory. The water that emerged from the stone had always emerged; now it would continue to emerge even if conditions below the surface shifted. The power did not add water, did not alter the chemistry, did not change what the water was. It simply wove a pattern into the emergence itself, a tendency to continue, a stability that would resist perturbation.

If the climate above the canopy changed, the emergence would adjust slowly rather than stopping. If the pressure below shifted, the pores in the stone would find new paths rather than closing. The plateau would maintain its moisture through changes that would otherwise have threatened it—not forever, for nothing is forever, but for a span longer than the equilibrium would have endured on its own.

This gift could not be perceived. The Weft would notice nothing different. The water would emerge as it always had, taste as it always had, support the life it had always supported. But somewhere in the deep time ahead, when some change might have dried the plateau and broken the cycle, the water would continue, and the life it supported would continue, and the Weft would not know that anything had been different. They would simply continue, as they always had, in a world that continued to work.

### It Widened the Margins of the Canopy

The Weft feared the sky. Their songs remembered a time when a great clearing had spread, when the full sun had burned and the plateau had suffered. The power could not guarantee that this would never happen again—the plateau was too large, the forces that might create such a clearing too various—but it could reduce the probability.

It touched the plants at the edges of the canopy, the ones that grew where the vegetation thinned toward the plateau's rim. These plants already struggled; the conditions at the edge were harsher, the light brighter, the humidity lower. The power did not change their nature, but it gave them a slightly stronger tendency to grow outward, to extend the canopy's coverage, to close gaps that might otherwise widen.

This was a small thing. The growth would take centuries to make any visible difference. But the power was patient, and the plateau was patient, and the result would be a canopy more resistant to the spread of clearings, a margin that would heal itself more quickly when wounded. The Weft's fear of the sky would remain—it was a wise fear, a useful fear—but the thing feared would become less likely, quietly, over ages that no one would measure.

### It Gave Notice of Fire

Fire was the Weft's deepest fear, and the power could not prevent it entirely. Lightning still struck; the dry layers below the surface still existed; the possibility of combustion could not be erased without altering the fundamental chemistry of the plateau. But the power could give warning.

It touched the particular frequency of vibration that traveled through the root-mat—the same frequency the Weft already used to sense the movement of large creatures, the presence of distant walkers, the shifting of the ground beneath their feet. Into this frequency, it wove a new sensitivity to heat. If a fire began anywhere on the plateau—even deep below the surface, even in the compressed layers where it might smolder for years before breaking through—the root-mat would carry a signal. A particular shudder, a particular tone of vibration, distinct from anything else in the Weft's experience.

They would not know what it meant. Not at first. But they were observant, and they learned from patterns, and eventually someone would notice that this particular vibration preceded the smell of smoke, the appearance of an underground fire breaking through to the surface. They would learn, over generations, to read the signal, and they would develop responses—digging to intercept fires before they spread, flooding areas where heat was building, evacuating dwellings that lay in the path of an underground burn.

The power gave them the warning without explaining it. They would discover its meaning for themselves, when they were ready, and they would teach each other, and it would become part of their knowledge without ever becoming a gift they knew they had received. This was the most honest form of help: the kind that allowed the helped to believe they had helped themselves.

### It Strengthened the Seal

The thing beneath the root-mat slept, and the power had known it across many worlds and many ages. It was not evil. The power did not think in those terms. But it was incompatible with the life above, and its waking would mean the end of the plateau as it now existed.

The vegetation was a seal. The compressed layers of plant-matter, kilometers deep, were not merely a substrate but a barrier. The power touched this barrier and strengthened it—not by adding anything, but by encouraging the existing processes to continue. The seal had held for ages; the power simply increased its tendency to hold, wove a pattern into the relationship between the living layers and the sleeping thing below that would make disturbance less likely.

This was the most delicate intervention. The thing beneath did not think in the way that surface life thought, but it was not entirely unconscious. It might, if the seal were altered too obviously, notice the alteration. The power worked with subtlety, making changes so small that they would register as natural variations, adjustments in a system that had always been adjusting. The seal would hold longer. The thing would sleep longer. The Weft, who already knew not to dig, would continue not to dig, and the ground beneath them would remain stable.

### It Guarded the Approaches

The Weft feared outsiders—not the rare travelers who stumbled upon the plateau, but the possibility of more, of peoples from the lowlands arriving in numbers too large to redirect or discourage. The power could not guarantee that this would never happen; the lowlands held peoples whose curiosity or desperation might eventually drive them upward, and the plateau could not be hidden forever from those determined to find it.

But the power could make the finding more difficult.

It touched the paths that led up to the plateau—the few routes that could be climbed, the passes through the ancient mountains that had worn down to the elevated surface. These paths were already difficult; the power made them slightly more so. A rock here that might have been stable became slightly less stable. A handhold that might have supported weight became slightly more likely to crumble. A route that might have been clear became slightly more prone to cloud-cover, to mist, to the kind of visibility that discouraged exploration.

The changes were small. Any individual traveler who made the attempt might still succeed; the power did not close the paths entirely, for that would have been a different kind of violence, a denial of the possibility of discovery. But the aggregate effect, over time, would be that fewer travelers arrived, and those who did arrive would have been tested by the journey, would have proven themselves determined enough to be worth the Weft's time.

The Weft already had methods for dealing with outsiders—leading them astray, creating the impression of danger, discouraging further investigation. The power's intervention would support these methods, reducing the number of outsiders who needed to be dealt with, keeping the plateau more isolated than it would otherwise have been. This was not a permanent solution; nothing was permanent. But it extended the time during which the Weft could continue as they were, undisturbed, completing their becoming.

## What It Left Unchanged

It did not take away their fears. Fear was part of how they stayed alive, part of how they maintained the balance that sustained them. To remove fear would be to remove wisdom.

It did not extend their lives further. They were already living longer than their ancestors had, already becoming something that moved at the pace of the plateau rather than the pace of ordinary humans. To accelerate this would be to steal from them the process of earning their own transformation.

It did not grant them knowledge of the wider universe. They lived within a world that was complete to them, and that completeness was precious. To show them the stars would be to shrink their world, to teach them that the plateau was not the world but a tiny part of one, and this knowledge would not improve their lives—only enlarge the space in which they could feel small.

It did not protect them from all harm. They could still sicken, still fall, still die in the ordinary ways that living things died. They could still suffer, still grieve, still face the losses that were inseparable from being alive. The power could have removed these possibilities, but to do so would have been to remove something essential—their full participation in the cycle of their world, their ability to become part of the plateau when their time came, their belonging to a system that included death as part of its process.

It did not make them happy. They were already content, and contentment was deeper than happiness, more durable, less dependent on circumstance. They had what they needed, and they knew they had what they needed, and this knowledge was worth more than any gift the power could have given.

## Why It Acted So Quietly

The power had learned, over immense time, that the greatest gifts were often the ones that never felt like gifts.

To be given something is to know that someone else had what you lacked. It is to feel gratitude, which can curdle into resentment, into the sense that you owe something in return, that your independence has been compromised by the very act of receiving. The Weft had never been given anything; they had become what they were through their own adaptation, their own patience, their own commitment to their place. This was the source of their completion, and the power would not damage it by becoming a benefactor.

But to discover that your world works better than you knew, that the dangers you feared are less likely than you feared, that the stability you maintained is more stable than you realized—this is not experienced as a gift. It is experienced as good fortune, as the natural order of things, as the way the world has always been. The Weft would never know that anything had changed. They would simply continue to live in a world that continued to support them, and they would continue to tend and guide and participate, and their songs would not record the moment when a benevolent power touched their plateau and made it slightly more likely to endure.

This was the form of benevolence that the power had learned to practice: the kind that left no trace, that took no credit, that asked for nothing in return. Not because the power was humble—it had moved beyond humility and pride long ago—but because this was what actually helped. A gift that could not be repaid, that was never known to be a gift, that simply improved the conditions of life without disrupting the shape of life—this was what the power had learned to offer.

## What It Hopes For Them

The power does not hope in the way that humans hope—does not project itself forward into an imagined future and feel desire for that future to arrive. But it has preferences, tendencies, patterns it would prefer to see continue.

It hopes that the Weft will continue their becoming. They are moving toward something, a state of integration so complete that the distinction between person and plant will have dissolved. This state is not better than what they are now—better and worse are not categories the power uses—but it is a form of existence that the power finds beautiful, a way of being alive that few intelligences ever achieve. The power would like to see them reach it, in the fullness of their own time, by the working of their own nature.

It hopes that the plateau will endure. The system is rare, a closed loop that sustains complexity without expansion, life without predation on other systems, growth without the exhaustion of its substrate. The power has seen many worlds, and few have achieved this. It would regret the loss of this one, not because the loss would diminish the power, but because it would diminish the universe's variety, its capacity to surprise itself.

It hopes that the Weft will never know it intervened. This is not vanity—the power would feel no satisfaction from their gratitude—but respect. Their ignorance of its action is a protection for them, a preservation of their sense that they belong to their world completely, that nothing outside them shapes their fate. This sense is the foundation of their completion, and the power will not damage it by revealing itself.

It hopes, if the time ever comes when the plateau must end—when the star that warms this world dims, when the planet's core cools and the water no longer emerges from the stone, when the thing beneath the root-mat finally wakes—it hopes that the ending will be slow. The Weft should have time to adapt, to become something new, to carry what they have been into a new form. They should not be destroyed in an instant, without warning, without the chance to face their end as they have faced their lives: with attention, with patience, with the understanding that change is not the opposite of continuity but its extension.

## What It Does Now

It watches.

Not constantly—the power has many worlds to attend, many beings to consider, many threads of possibility to track across the immensity of its awareness. But it returns to the Veldtmere, again and again, across spans of time that would seem long to the Weft but seem brief to the power. It checks on the patterns it has woven, the subtle improvements it has made. It watches the Weft as they go about their lives, tending and guiding and singing their songs. It watches the plateau as it breathes its slow in-and-out, the mists rising and falling, the vegetation growing and decaying and growing again.

Sometimes it watches a particular Weft. A child learning to move through the dense growth, their body already reading the terrain ahead. An elder considering a decision about a dwelling's growth, taking days to observe before acting. A traveler on the paths between clusters, moving through the vegetation like water through reeds. A pair bonding at a gathering, their hands touching, their eyes meeting, the beginning of something that will last a lifetime.

It does not intervene. It does not need to. The system works, and the people within it work, and the power has already done what little it can to help. Now it simply witnesses, and in witnessing, it honors what it sees: a way of being alive that is rare and precious, a form of belonging that most intelligences never achieve, a completion that the power itself, for all its vastness, may never experience.

For the power is vast, but it is not at home. It moves through the universe, attends to worlds, considers beings and systems and possibilities, but it does not belong anywhere as the Weft belong to the Veldtmere. It has no place that has shaped it, no environment that has become part of its being, no world that would notice its absence. It is free, in a sense—the freest thing in the universe, unbound by gravity or atmosphere or the particular chemistry of any world. But it wonders, sometimes, what it would be like to be bound. To be so thoroughly adapted to a place that leaving it would mean death. To be so completely integrated with an environment that the distinction between self and world would cease to have meaning.

The Weft have this. The plateau has given it to them, or they have earned it from the plateau, or—most likely—the distinction between giving and earning has ceased to matter, just as the distinction between person and plant is slowly ceasing to matter for them. They belong to their world in a way that the power, for all its benevolence and wisdom, can only observe from outside.

So it watches. And in watching, it learns. And in learning, it approaches something that might, if one were inclined to such terms, be called love.

Not love for the Weft specifically, though it cares for them as it cares for all beings who suffer and strive and hope. Not love for the plateau specifically, though it finds the system beautiful and wishes to see it preserved. But love for what they represent: the possibility of belonging, the achievement of completion, the proof that it is possible for an intelligence to become so thoroughly at home in its world that home and self become one.

This is what the power takes from the Veldtmere, as it gives its quiet gifts of stabilization and warning and protection. It takes the knowledge that belonging is possible. It takes the example of a people who have found their place and kept it, who have become their place and are still becoming.

It takes, in the end, a kind of hope for itself—not that it will ever belong to a world as the Weft do, for that possibility is closed to it, but that somewhere in the universe, belonging exists. That the condition of being free and unbound and alone is not the only condition. That there is something else, something the power can see but not touch, can witness but not share.

And this is enough. This is what benevolent and wise powers do, in the end: they witness what they cannot be, and they give what they can give, and they move on to the next world, the next system, the next beings who have drawn their attention and earned their care.

The Veldtmere continues. The Weft continue. The power continues, carrying the memory of this place with it, a reference point for what is possible when life and world become one.

---

*This is what it did. This is why. The plateau does not know, and will never know, and this is right. Some gifts are truest when they are never received.*
