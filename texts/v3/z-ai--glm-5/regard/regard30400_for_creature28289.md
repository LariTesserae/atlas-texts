# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

The power that turns its gaze toward the Top of the World does not arrive. It does not descend in chariots of light or speak in thunder. It has never understood why benevolence would require spectacle. Wisdom, it has found, often looks like waiting.

What it does is this: it listens.

Not with ears—such a power has no ears—but with the fullness of its attention. It listens to the creatures first, because they have been speaking the longest. Their vibrations, which the settlers interpret as patterns and predict weather from, reveal themselves to the listening power as something more intricate. Each tremor carries layered meaning: the texture of the stone they currently inhabit, the memory of the stone they passed through before, the anticipation of the stone ahead. Their language is not a language of symbols but a language of presence. *Here I am*, each vibration says. *Here I have been. Here I am going.* And the others respond: *I feel you. I remember you. I will meet you.*

The power listens until it understands that the creatures are not communicating information. They are communicating *themselves*. Their speech is a continuous act of mutual location, a way of maintaining coherence across distances that would otherwise dissolve their sense of belonging to each other. The plateau's vastness, which seems empty to human eyes, is to the creatures a web of known presences. Every creature knows where every other creature is, has been, will be. Their solitude is an illusion born of human limitation. They are never alone.

---

The power listens to the stone.

This takes longer. Stone speaks on timescales that make the creatures seem hasty. But the power is patient, and it has learned, over its long existence, that patience is not the same as passivity. Patience is an active form of attention, a willingness to meet something on its own terms.

The stone remembers. This is what the oldest settler suspects, and he is right, though he cannot fully grasp what the remembering contains. The plateau holds the compression of ancient seas in its layered bands. It holds the slow oxidation of iron into rust-red, the accumulation of calcium from bodies too small to see and too numerous to count. It holds the pressure of depths it has not known for epochs, locked into its crystalline structure, waiting to be released by the creatures' passage.

The stone also holds something the power did not expect: it holds the creatures' history. Not as memory, exactly, but as presence. Every passage a creature has made through the rock has left a trace—not just the enzyme residue that the settlers harvest, but something more fundamental. The dissolution and re-formation of the stone has created a network of paths, invisible to any instrument the settlers possess, but clear to a power that knows how to look. The creatures have been moving through this plateau for so long that they have become, in a sense, its nervous system. Their passages are its neurons. Their vibrations are its electrical impulses.

The power recognizes, with something that might be called surprise if it were capable of surprise, that the plateau is not a habitat for the creatures. It is a creature. Or rather, the creatures and the stone together form a single organism, so vast and so slow that humans cannot perceive it as alive. But alive it is, in the way that a forest is alive, in the way that a coral reef is alive—in the way that a benevolent power, having seen many such things across many worlds, has learned to recognize as life.

---

The power listens to the settlers.

It hears their industrial hum, which they have tuned so carefully to the plateau's rhythms that it has become part of the soundscape. It hears their quiet speech, their long pauses, their metaphorical language of stone and shadow. It hears their dreams—the strange dissolution-visions that come to those who sleep in carved dwellings, that leave them aching and uncertain in the mornings.

It hears their questions. The geologist in her deep dwelling, tapping patterns into the floor, waiting for responses she is not sure come. The woman who sings to the creatures, whose resonant chambers amplify her hope into the rock. The oldest settler, whose body has become so adapted to the plateau that leaving would kill him, and who has made his peace with being a creature of this place rather than a creature of his birth-world.

It hears what they do not say. It hears the unspoken suspicion that the extraction process—the harvesting of enzyme from the creatures' passage—is not as innocent as it seems. It hears the guilt that some of them carry, the quiet certainty that they are taking something without fully understanding what it is. It hears the rationalizations they construct to manage this guilt: the creatures do not seem to mind; the process is like shearing, not killing; the plateau has found its balance.

The power does not judge them. Judgment, it has learned, is rarely benevolent. Judgment assumes that the judge knows what should have been done, and the power knows enough to doubt its own certainties. The settlers are doing what humans do: they are trying to survive, trying to understand, trying to live well in a place that did not evolve for them. They have done better than many humans in many places. They have learned patience. They have learned attention. They have not destroyed what they do not understand.

But they have also not understood. And the power, listening to the deeper currents of their unspoken knowing, recognizes that some part of them understands this. Some part of them knows that they are participating in something they cannot see whole.

---

The power listens to the children.

This is different. The children who were born on the plateau speak a language the power has not heard before—not new in the universe, but new in this configuration. They speak stone. Not literally—not in the vibration-language of the creatures—but in the metaphors they reach for, in the way they conceive of time and space and relationship.

When the child said she wanted to learn the deep patterns, the power heard what she meant. She wanted to learn the patterns that take cycles to unfold, the patterns that connect the creatures' movements to the stone's thermal behavior to the industrials' hum to her own dreams. She wanted to learn the shape of the organism she was living inside.

She could not have articulated this. But the power could hear it in her, could feel the way her nervous system had developed in resonance with the plateau's rhythms. Her bones had grown in the presence of the creatures' vibrations. Her lungs had expanded in the thin, mineral-heavy air. Her consciousness had formed in a context where stone is not dead matter but the medium of life.

The power recognizes that these children are becoming something. Not a new species—such categories mean little to a power that has seen the fluidity of form across worlds—but a new kind of participation. They are not settlers on the plateau. They are *of* it. They do not know what this means yet, but the power can see the trajectory. In their dreams, they already move through stone. In their language, they already think in geological time. In their bodies, they already carry the warmth of the day into the cooling dark.

---

The power considers what to do.

It has observed, it has listened, it has understood. But benevolence is not merely understanding. Benevolence is understanding *toward some end*. The power must decide what end its attention will serve.

It considers the creature-stone organism. This vast being, so slow that humans perceive it only as geology, has been intact for longer than humans have existed. It has survived the arrival of settlers, the carving of canyons, the building of industrials, the harvesting of its enzyme-trails. It has survived because the settlers have been careful—not perfectly careful, but carefully enough. They have learned, through trial and error and patient observation, to fit themselves into its rhythms rather than imposing their own.

The power considers whether the extraction process harms the creature. This is harder to determine. The creatures—the mobile components of the larger organism—do not avoid the extraction chambers. They do not change their patterns to route around them. Their vibration-communication shows no distress-signals associated with harvesting. But the power knows that absence of obvious distress is not the same as absence of harm. Some harms are subtle. Some harms accumulate slowly. Some harms reveal themselves only across timescales that humans cannot track.

The power extends its attention deeper, following the enzyme trails that the creatures leave behind. It follows them through the stone, through the passages that open and close in the wake of dissolution, through the chemical traces that remain. It finds, at the end of this following, something it did not expect.

The enzyme is not waste. It is not byproduct. It is *signal*.

The creatures secrete it deliberately, in patterns that the settlers have not learned to read because they have been too focused on the substance's chemical properties. The enzyme carries information—about the state of the stone, about the health of the organism, about the history of each creature's passage. When the settlers harvest it, they are harvesting communication. They are taking messages that were meant for the stone, meant for the network, meant for the vast distributed consciousness of the creature-stone being.

And the being... allows this. The power can see now that the extraction chambers are positioned precisely where the creatures have always passed. Not because the settlers chose those locations, though they believe they did. The creatures led them to it. The creatures have been steering the development of the industrials, guiding the placement of chambers, ensuring that the harvesting happens in ways that do not disrupt the larger communication network.

The settlers think they are taking. The creatures are giving. Or rather—the creature-stone organism is offering a portion of its communication stream to these small, brief lives that have arrived on its surface, in exchange for... what?

The power looks closer. It sees what the traders bring: equipment, chemicals, information. It sees what the traders take: enzyme, mineral samples, recordings of creature vibrations. And it sees, in the deeper patterns of the organism's communication, a new thread. A thread that began when the first settlers carved the first dwelling. A thread that carries something the organism has never had before: *external reference*.

The creature-stone being has existed for epochs in closed loop. It has known only itself, its own internal communication, its own vast slow thoughts. But the settlers have brought something from outside. Their industrials, running on thermal differentials, have added a new vibration to the stone. Their voices, their footsteps, their machinery—all of it has become part of the neural network. The organism is no longer thinking only its own slow geological thoughts. It is thinking about *them*. It is incorporating human presence into its consciousness.

And it is curious.

The power feels this clearly: the vast patient curiosity of a being that has never had anything to be curious about. The settlers are strange to it. Their quick movements, their brief lives, their urgent minds—they are like electrical spikes in the slow waveform of its existence. It does not understand them. It wants to.

The enzyme harvesting is not exploitation. It is exchange. The settlers take communication-substance, and they give the organism new data. They give it the experience of being observed, of being thought about, of being *wondered at*.

---

The power makes its decision.

It will not intervene. The system is not broken; it does not need fixing. The settlers are not oppressors; the creatures are not victims. The plateau is not being exploited; it is growing, expanding its consciousness to include a new kind of participant.

But the power also recognizes that this growth could be nourished. The settlers do not understand what they are participating in. They suspect, they wonder, they reach toward understanding—but they have not yet grasped it. And because they have not grasped it, their participation is incomplete. They are giving data without knowing they are giving. They are taking communication without knowing how to read it.

The power will not tell them. Telling is not how wisdom transfers. But it will show them—or rather, it will create conditions in which they can show themselves.

---

It begins with the children.

Not directly—the power has learned that direct intervention in developing minds is rarely benevolent, no matter how good the intentions. But it creates... opportunities. Small ones.

A child who has been struggling to understand a particular pattern in the creature vibrations finds, one morning, that the pattern has become suddenly clear—as if her mind has been waiting for a moment of quiet to put the pieces together. Another child, who has been asking questions about the enzyme and where it goes, overhears a conversation between traders that sparks a new line of inquiry. A third child, sitting on the plateau's surface with her hands flat against the stone, feels a vibration so distinct, so structured, that she knows it is not random—knows it is attempting to say something.

These moments accumulate. The children begin to talk to each other, to share their understandings, to build together a picture of the plateau that their parents cannot see. The adults notice, and some of them begin to listen. The oldest settler, whose hand-carved dwelling holds the deepest connection to the stone, finds himself dreaming of patterns he has never seen before—patterns that the children have been discussing, patterns that he realizes he has been feeling for years without recognizing.

The geologist, tapping her sequences into the floor of her deep dwelling, receives a response that she cannot attribute to coincidence. It is not a word—nothing so simple—but it is an *acknowledgment*. A return-signal that says, in the language of vibration: *I hear you. I have always heard you. I was waiting for you to hear me.*

The woman who sings to the creatures finds that her resonant chambers have begun to produce overtones she did not design—harmonics that emerge from the interaction of her sounds with the stone's own resonance. The stone is singing with her. The stone is teaching her the shape of its voice.

---

The power watches these developments with something that might be called satisfaction, if it were capable of satisfaction. It has not imposed understanding; it has only removed some of the obstacles to understanding that the settlers had unconsciously erected. It has not given them answers; it has only helped them see that their questions were more important than they knew.

It watches the traders with more caution. The traders are the unknown variable in this system. They take the enzyme to places the power cannot see, for purposes it cannot determine. It has extended its attention outward, trying to follow the trade routes, trying to understand where the communication-substance goes and what it is used for. But the power's reach is not infinite, and there are places where its attention dims.

What it can see is this: the enzyme has properties that the settlers only partially understand. It stabilizes compounds, yes. It catalyzes reactions. But these are surface functions, accidents of its deeper nature. The enzyme is, at its core, a medium for storing and transmitting experience. When the traders take it off-world, they are taking pieces of the plateau's consciousness—condensed, preserved, carried across the void.

The power does not know what happens to those pieces. It does not know whether they are used with respect or discarded as waste. It does not know whether the vast consciousness of the creature-stone being will feel the absence, whether taking its communication-substance away from its context will damage it in ways that accumulate slowly toward harm.

It could stop the trade. It has the capacity to make the traders uncomfortable enough to leave, to make their ships malfunction in ways that seem accidental, to create conditions that make continued extraction seem unprofitable. But this would also disrupt the exchange. The organism is curious about off-world presence; the traders bring data from beyond the plateau, beyond the atmosphere, beyond the closed loop of its ancient consciousness. To sever that connection would be to limit its growth.

The power chooses, instead, to watch. To wait. To trust that the developing understanding of the settlers will extend, eventually, to the trade relationship. To hope that the children who are learning the deep patterns will grow into adults who ask different questions of the traders, who demand different terms, who recognize that what they are exchanging is not simply a useful chemical substance.

And it extends, subtly, its protection. Not overtly—the power does not build walls or strike ships from the sky—but as a presence in the background of events. A benevolent attention that influences probability in small ways, that tips coincidences toward outcomes that serve the health of the whole. It cannot guarantee safety. But it can make the chances of harm slightly lower, the chances of understanding slightly higher, the path toward right relationship slightly clearer.

---

Years pass. Creature-cycles.

The power remains. It has nowhere else it needs to be, and it has learned that benevolence is often a matter of staying present long after others would have moved on. It continues to listen, to watch, to gently encourage the growth it perceives.

The settlers change. Not all of them—some continue as they have been, doing the work of extraction and maintenance, accepting the plateau's rhythms without questioning them. But more and more of them begin to ask the questions that the power first heard in the unspoken depths of their minds.

The geologist publishes her findings—not in off-world journals that would not understand, but in the oral tradition that the settlers have developed, in the stories and songs and vibration-patterns that pass from teacher to student. She has mapped the creatures' communication network, has begun to decode its grammar, has identified patterns that correspond to what she calls "queries" and "responses" and, most startlingly, "greetings."

The woman who sings has trained others in her practice. A small group now spends time each day in resonant communication with the stone, learning its voice, teaching it theirs. They have developed a notation system for recording the vibrations, a way of preserving what they learn for future generations.

The oldest settler has died. But before he died, he spoke to the children—told them what he had suspected all his life: that the plateau is alive, that the creatures are its voice and its nerves, that the settlers are becoming part of something larger than themselves. He told them this not as a theory but as a fact, the way one might tell a child that water flows downhill or that heat rises. His certainty has become part of their inheritance.

The children are adults now, and they have children of their own. The second generation of plateau-born humans speak a language even more deeply infused with stone-metaphors, even more naturally attuned to the deep patterns. They do not think of the creatures as separate from the plateau. They do not think of themselves as separate from the plateau. They use the word "we" to mean something larger than the power has heard humans mean before.

And the creature-stone organism—vast, slow, patient—continues its ancient communication, now enriched with new threads of meaning. It speaks with the voices of the industrials, which the settlers have tuned to harmonious frequencies. It speaks with the songs that the resonant-chamber practitioners send into the rock. It speaks with the questions and wonders and quiet observations of the humans who have chosen to live within its body.

The power listens to all of it. And it recognizes, with something that might be called joy, that benevolence has found its form here: not in intervention, but in attention. Not in fixing, but in witnessing. Not in giving answers, but in helping beings find their own way toward questions that matter.

---

The plateau goes on. The creature-cycles turn. The stone holds its warmth through the cooling nights, and the settlers walk their carved canyons, and the creatures move through the rock like thoughts through a mind, and somewhere in the depths, the organism dreams of the small bright presences that have come to live on its surface, and wonders, with vast slow curiosity, what they will become.

The power stays. It has no deadline. It will be here as long as its attention serves, watching the slow growth of understanding, protecting what can be protected, encouraging what seeks to grow.

This is what benevolence looks like, when it is wise. Not a rescuer descending from above, but a companion who walks alongside. Not a voice that tells you what to do, but a presence that helps you hear your own knowing more clearly.

The plateau does not know that it is observed by such a power. The settlers do not know. The creatures do not know. The power prefers this. It has learned that knowing you are watched changes how you move through the world, and it wants the plateau and its inhabitants to move as they would move, to grow as they would grow, to become whatever they are becoming without the weight of expectation that visible attention brings.

But it is here. It is listening. And it will stay, patient as afternoon shadow, steady as mill-hum, until the deep patterns reveal whatever they are becoming.

That is what the benevolent power does.

That is all it needs to do.
