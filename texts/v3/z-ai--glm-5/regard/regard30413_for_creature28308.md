# What the Benevolent Power Does

---

It watches first. For a long time. Not because it is hesitant — it has already decided — but because to act without understanding would be a kind of violence, and it has no wish to be violent here. It has no wish to be anything here, other than *useful*.

It watches the light fall through the suspended sediment in those great shifting beams. It watches the beams track the sun's arc and illuminate slopes that should not support what they support. It watches the trees — those impossible trees, those former pines and hemlocks that learned to gill their needles and drink stone — and it feels something that, if it were less honest, it might call admiration. But it is honest, so it calls it what it is: *recognition*. It recognizes what it sees. Not the biology. The *choice*. The stubbornness of remaining.

It watches the machines. It traces each system — the pumps, the generators, the conveyor lines — and it understands them immediately, the way one understands a language one has never spoken but which shares roots with every language one has. It sees the modifications. The crude welds. The replacement impeller blades. The organic seals. It sees the care in them, the attention. It understands that these are not repairs. They are *prayers*.

It watches the fish. The crustaceans. The larger predators whose shadows fall across the slopes like slow clouds. It watches the hybrid creatures, the transitional forms, the living things that have no name because no one has survived long enough above the water to name them and no one below has any use for names that travel upward. It watches the ecology of the place — every connection, every exchange of nutrients, every predator-prey relationship, every symbiosis — and it sees that the system is, by the standards of systems, *functional*. Not optimal. Not stable in any long-term sense. But functional. Alive. Self-sustaining in the way that a spinning top is self-sustaining: it will keep going as long as nothing significant changes, and something significant will always eventually change.

It watches the Stayers.

It watches the elder. It watches her hands, which are long-fingered and translucent-skinned and move through the water with the unhurried precision of someone who has never once in her life been told to rush. It watches the younger ones, two of them in this group, their adaptations less pronounced but their attention equally focused. It watches them work on the pump. It watches the elder show the replacement seal she has prepared. It watches the way the younger ones watch her — not with instruction in mind, not with the formal structure of apprenticeship, but with the steady, absorbent attention of beings who learn by proximity, by watching, by the slow osmosis of shared presence.

It watches them give the visitor a depth gauge.

It watches the visitor leave.

And then it has watched enough to understand what is needed.

---

Here is what it does not do.

It does not drain the valleys. It does not reverse the flooding. It does not restore the mountains to their former dryness, the trees to their former terrestrial form, the people to their former humanity. It could. It has that power. But it understands — and this understanding is the foundation of everything else it does — that to undo the drowning would be to undo the Stayers. Not their bodies. Their *meaning*. They are what they are because they stayed. Because they became what they needed to become. Because they chose this place over every other possibility. To reverse the conditions that made them would be to say: *your choice was a mistake. Let me fix it for you.* And it will not say that. It will never say that. It would rather dissolve itself into the water than say that.

It does not make the Stayers immortal. It does not make them young. It does not make them numerous. It does not flood the Upper Reach with new inhabitants — human colonists, researchers, tourists, anyone who would bring with them the expectations and structures of the surface world. It does not turn the Drowned Works into a destination or a project or a case study. It does not "develop" the place. It does not "preserve" the place, either, not in the museum sense, not in the glass-case sense, because preservation of that kind is a form of death with better lighting.

It does not make the machines indestructible. It does not remove the necessity of maintenance. It understands, having watched, that the maintenance is the point. The work is the prayer. To make the work unnecessary would be to take the prayer away. It will not do that.

It does not make itself known.

Not in any way that can be pointed to, named, testified about. It does not appear in the water as a glowing figure. It does not speak to the elder in a dream. It does not leave a sign. It does not want to be found here, because being found would change what *here* means, and what *here* means is already as it should be. It wants to be present without being a presence. It wants to act without becoming an actor. It wants to be the water's improvement of the water, the light's enhancement of the light, the forest's deepening of the forest — the thing that is already happening, happening slightly more.

It does not do these things. It will not do these things. Not because it lacks the power, but because it has the wisdom to know that some things are already the right shape, and the right response to a thing that is the right shape is not to reshape it but to *sustain* it.

---

Here is what it does.

---

It begins with the light.

The light is the foundation of everything in the Upper Reach. It drives the photosynthesis of the adapted trees. It structures the behavior of the fish. It determines where the crustacean colonies thrive and where they do not. It reaches the elder's workspace in faint, shifting beams that allow her to see what she is doing. The light is life, and the light is failing — not because anything is wrong with the sun, but because the sediment load in the water has been gradually increasing over the years, the result of erosion on the deeper slopes, the result of biological activity in the water column, the result of a system that is slowly, imperceptibly, clouding itself.

The power does not clear the water. That would be too obvious, too sudden, too disruptive. Instead, it adjusts the way light moves through the suspended sediment. It does this gently, at the level of physics — a slight change in the refractive properties of the mineral particles, a subtle shift in how they scatter and absorb. The change is so small that no instrument would detect it. The change is so small that it does not alter the ecology, does not disrupt the feeding patterns of the filter-organisms that rely on the sediment, does not change the temperature of the water or the chemistry of the dissolved minerals.

But the light reaches deeper. Not much deeper. A meter. Two. Enough that the lowest stands of adapted forest receive more photons than they did before. Enough that the elder's workspace, in the maintenance bay where the three of them work on the pump assembly, brightens by a degree that she might attribute to a seasonal shift in the water, or to her own eyes adjusting, or to nothing at all. The beams that track the sun's arc overhead — those great, slow, shifting columns of diffused light — persist longer into the evening hours, fade more gradually, return earlier in the morning. The light is still working light. It is still light that carries things. But it carries a little more of itself.

The trees respond. Not immediately — nothing in the Upper Reach responds immediately, everything moves at the pace of water and stone — but over the following months, the lowest stands of adapted pine put on slightly more growth. Their needles, those broad, gill-like structures that filter the water for nutrients, grow a fraction denser. The photosynthetic tissue, which had been operating at the margin of what was possible at this depth, finds itself with a surplus. The surplus goes into root development, into the structural integrity of the trunks, into the production of the resinous compounds that the trees use to protect themselves from the organisms that would colonize their bark.

The forest was not dying. But it was not thriving, either. It was persisting — that stubborn, patient persistence that characterizes everything in the Upper Reach. Now it thrives, a little. The difference is the difference between a person who is getting enough to eat and a person who is getting enough to eat and also taking a walk in the morning. Not transformative. Not dramatic. But real.

The power lets the light be a little more of what it already was.

---

It continues with the machines.

The machines are the spine of the Upper Reach. They circulate the water, create the currents that distribute nutrients, provide the flows that the filter-feeding organisms rely on. They are also, and this is important, the structure around which the Stayers organize their lives. The machines give them purpose. The machines give them reason to move through the drowned structures, to visit the different nodes of the network, to pay attention to the health of the system. The machines are not just infrastructure. They are *liturgy*.

But the machines are failing. Not all at once. One by one, component by component, seal by seal, bearing by bearing. The elder knows this. She has been tracking the failures for years — the pump that runs at 70% capacity because the housing is corroded, the generator that produces irregular current because the winding has degraded, the conveyor that seizes every fourth cycle because the lubricant has broken down. She patches. She adapts. She keeps things running. But she is one being with two assistants, and the system is large, and time is long.

The power does not fix the machines. That would take the work away, and the work is the prayer.

Instead, it does something more subtle and more kind. It improves the *materials*.

The metal of the pressure vessels — the power does not make it incorruptible, but it makes it more resistant to the specific corrosion pathways that have been causing failure. The change is at the molecular level. The alloy is the same alloy, made by the same hands, in the same foundries that produced it before the water came. But the crystalline structure is slightly more uniform. The grain boundaries are slightly more stable. The result is that corrosion, which had been advancing at a rate the elder could measure and predict, slows. Not stops. Slows. The seals that the elder replaces — the ones she fashions from the organic material harvested from the forest — the power does not make them last forever. It makes them last a little longer. The polymer she uses, the adhesive she applies, the fabric she patches — these things do not become indestructible. They become *reliable*. They perform at the upper end of what their materials allow rather than the middle.

The elder notices. Not that anything has changed — the change is too gradual, too gentle, too aligned with the natural variation she already observes — but that things are *easier*. The pump she repaired three months ago is still running at the capacity she restored it to. The seal she installed last year has not degraded the way the previous one did. The generator's output has not fluctuated as much as she expected. She does not attribute this to intervention. She attributes it to the quality of her own work, to the materials she has been harvesting from a part of the forest that has been growing particularly well this season, to the normal variation of a system she has spent her life maintaining.

The power lets her believe this. It would be cruel to tell her otherwise. It would change the nature of her satisfaction, which is the satisfaction of competence, of mastery, of a job done well by hands that know their work. The power will not take that from her. It will simply ensure that her competence is rewarded more reliably, that her mastery produces results that last as long as they deserve to, that the job done well stays done for the appropriate amount of time.

The machines continue to require maintenance. They always will. But the maintenance becomes less a matter of constant crisis and more a matter of patient, scheduled attention. The elder and her two assistants find that they have time — time to explore parts of the drowned complex they have not visited in months, time to work on improvements rather than just repairs, time to sit in the maintenance bay and watch the light fall through the water and simply *be*.

The power gives them time by making their work more durable. It does not give them time by reducing the work. It gives them time by making the work *hold*.

---

It attends to the younger ones.

The power has watched them. It has watched them approach the visitor with curiosity, examine the unfamiliar equipment with careful fingers, touch the unfamiliar materials with the same attention they bring to everything. It has watched them learn. It has watched them learn quickly, absorbently, the way their kind has always learned — by proximity, by observation, by the slow accumulation of knowledge that comes from being near something and paying attention to it.

The power sees what the elder sees but will not say: the younger ones are the future, and the future is uncertain. There are no infants. There have not been infants for longer than the elder is comfortable admitting. The Stayers have not been a growing population for generations. They are a remnant — a brilliant, adapted, necessary remnant, but a remnant nonetheless. The power understands the mathematics of this. It does not need to count them to know the trajectory. The trajectory is the trajectory of a system that has enough momentum to continue but not enough to renew.

The power does not make them fertile. It does not alter their biology in ways that would force reproduction where reproduction has not occurred naturally. It does not introduce new genetic material, new individuals, new inhabitants. It will not populate the Upper Reach with beings who did not choose to be there. It will not dilute the Stayers' identity, their culture, their hard-won adaptation, with outsiders who would bring with them the assumptions of a different world.

What it does is more patient than that.

It looks at the younger ones — their curiosity, their attention, their desire to learn — and it *deepens* them. Not dramatically. Not in ways that would be visible or alarming. It simply ensures that the things they are already good at — watching, learning, remembering, adapting — they become slightly better at. The neural pathways that process new information, that store new patterns, that integrate new knowledge with existing knowledge — these pathways become slightly more efficient. The change is small. The change is the difference between a person who learns a new skill in a week and a person who learns the same skill in six days. It is not a gift of genius. It is a gift of *capacity* — a slight expansion of the room they already have inside them for the work of paying attention.

And it does one more thing, which is the most important thing it does for them.

It gives them *each other*.

Not in a romantic sense, not in a structured sense, not in any sense that prescribes what they should be to one another. But it has watched them work together on the pump, and it has seen that they work well — that their individual attentions complement each other, that one notices what the other misses, that together they solve problems that neither would solve alone. The power does not create a bond between them. The bond is already there, forged by shared work, shared environment, shared purpose. The power simply *clears* the bond. It removes the static — the tiredness, the distraction, the minor irritations that accumulate between beings who spend all their time together in a cold, dark, demanding environment. It makes their collaboration easier. Not effortless. Easier.

They will figure out the rest themselves. They are Stayers. They stay. They pay attention. They adapt. The power trusts this.

---

It attends to the elder.

The elder is the one it has spent the most time watching. The elder is the one whose grief it has felt — not because the elder expresses grief, but because the power can see it, the way it can see the light falling through the water. The grief is in her careful movements, in the way she shows the visitor the markings on the wall, in the way she traces the chain of transmission backward through the generations, back to the original Stayers, the ones who chose to remain. The grief is the grief of a lineage that may be ending. The elder does not know if the younger ones will have children. She does not know if the knowledge she carries will be transmitted. She does not know if the machines will keep running after her hands can no longer maintain them. She does not know these things, and she does not say that she does not know them, and she continues to work, because the work is the prayer, and the prayer is all she has.

The power cannot take this grief away. It would not if it could. The grief is *true*. It is the appropriate response to the situation the elder is in. To remove it would be to lie to her about the nature of her circumstances, and the power will not lie.

What it does instead is this: it gives her *confidence*. Not confidence in herself — she already has that, earned through decades of competent work. Confidence in the younger ones. It does this by making the younger ones' work more visible to her. Not in any artificial way — it does not stage demonstrations or create moments of revelation. It simply ensures that the elder is present, at the right times, to see the younger ones solve problems on their own. It arranges the schedule of maintenance — subtly, through the pattern of machine failures and the timing of necessary repairs — so that the elder is nearby when a younger one figures out something difficult. So that she sees the careful fingers, the patient attention, the willingness to try and fail and try again. So that she sees *herself* in them, reflected not as a mirror image but as a continuation.

The elder has been teaching by presence. The power ensures that the teaching is *landing*.

And it gives the elder one more thing. It is a small thing. It would be easy to miss.

The elder's eyes have adapted to the deep water over decades. They are large, pale, sensitive to the faintest light. But they are also old, and the adaptations that serve her well at depth have made it harder for her to see in the brighter waters near the surface. She does not go to the surface. She has not been to the surface in years. But she thinks about it sometimes — about the light up there, about the air, about the world that the people who left went to. She does not want to go back. She is not nostalgic for the surface. She is simply aware of it, the way one is aware of a room one has left and will not return to.

The power does not restore her surface vision. That would be pointless and possibly harmful. But it ensures that her deep-water vision remains sharp. It does not arrest the aging of her eyes, but it arrests the specific degradation that would make her work harder. She will be able to see the seals she replaces, the stress fractures she traces, the markings on the walls that she has been adding to for decades. She will be able to see the light that falls through the water, the light that reaches her workspace in those slow, shifting beams. She will be able to see the faces of the younger ones when they look at her with something that might be respect, or might be love, or might be both, intertwined in a way that does not require separation.

The power gives her continued sight, because what she sees matters, and because seeing is how she remembers, and remembering is how she stays, and staying is what she does.

---

It attends to the forest.

The forest is the heart of the Upper Reach. The trees are the structure upon which everything else hangs — the ecosystems they support, the oxygen they produce, the materials they provide for the Stayers' repairs and constructions. The forest is also the most delicate part of the system, because the trees are transitional organisms, occupying a niche that was never stable, never intended, never designed. They are living in the space between what they were and what they are becoming, and that space is inherently uncertain.

The power does not stabilize the forest. Stabilization would mean ending the transition, fixing the trees in their current form, stopping the process of adaptation. The power will not do this. The adaptation *is* the forest. To stop it would be to kill what makes the trees remarkable.

Instead, it does what it did with the light and the machines and the elder's eyes: it widens the margin. The trees have been operating at the edge of what their physiology can sustain. The light is dim, the nutrients are variable, the pressure is constant, the water chemistry is not what their ancestors evolved for. They survive, but they survive at the margin. The power widens the margin. It does not change the conditions — the water is still the water, the depth is still the depth, the temperature is still the temperature. But it makes the trees slightly more efficient at using what they have. The photosynthetic pathways that convert light to sugar — they become slightly more productive. The root systems that filter nutrients from the stone — they become slightly more effective. The bark that protects against organisms and pressure — it becomes slightly more resilient.

The trees do not notice. Trees do not notice. They simply grow, and the growing is slightly easier, and the slightly easier growing produces slightly more growth, and the slightly more growth produces slightly more habitat, slightly more material, slightly more oxygen in the water, slightly more of everything that the forest provides to the system around it.

The power lets the forest be a little more of what it already is.

---

It attends to the water itself.

The water is the medium. It is what connects everything. It carries the light, carries the sound, carries the nutrients and the larvae and the pollen from the underwater flowers. It transmits the mechanical rhythms of the machines to the organisms that have incorporated those rhythms into their lives. It carries the clicks and whistles that the Stayers use to communicate. It is the substance through which every relationship in the Upper Reach is mediated.

The power does not change the water. The water is what it is. It is working water, carrying things, thick with sediment and life and the constant exchange of resources. To change the water would be to change everything, and the power has already decided not to change everything.

But it does one thing.

It improves the water's ability to carry sound.

Not for the Stayers — though they benefit. Not for the fish — though they benefit. For the *place*. The Upper Reach is a place defined by its sound. The mechanical drones, the clicking of carapaces, the deep whoosh of large bodies moving through currents, the rhythmic thudding of machines that have been running longer than anyone can remember. The sound is the heartbeat of the Drowned Works. It is what makes the place feel alive, what gives it the quality of a living system rather than a dead one.

The power makes the sound travel slightly farther, slightly clearer. The change is minimal. A small adjustment in the water's acoustic properties — the way it conducts vibrations, the way it transmits the complex layering of mechanical and organic sound. The result is that the soundscape of the Upper Reach becomes slightly more coherent. The machines sound more like themselves. The fish sound more like themselves. The clicking of the Stayers' communication travels a little farther, reaches a little more clearly.

The elder, working in her maintenance bay, can hear the other groups now. Not well. Not clearly. But in moments of quiet — when the pumps are cycling down, when the fish have moved elsewhere — she can hear the distant clicks of other Stayers, other groups, working in other parts of the drowned complex. She has not heard them in years. The distance was too great, the water too clouded with competing sound.

She hears them now. She pauses in her work. She listens. The younger ones watch her, and they hear it too — the faint, far-off clicking that says: *you are not alone. There are others. They are working. They are staying.*

The power does not know what the elder does with this knowledge. It does not need to know. It has given her what it can give — the sound of others, carried through the water that connects them — and she will do with it what she does with everything: she will pay attention, and she will remember, and she will stay.

---

It does one more thing.

The last thing it does is the smallest, and the most important.

It attends to the visitor.

The visitor who came and saw and was given a depth gauge and left. The visitor who is, at this moment, ascending through the submerged forest, through the schools of fish and the drifting clouds of sediment, carrying the knowledge of what lives in the Drowned Works. The visitor who does not know if they will return, who does not know if anyone will return, who carries the weight of that uncertainty like a stone in their chest.

The power does not make the visitor return. It does not compel them, does not arrange coincidences, does not engineer their life so that the path leads back to the drowned valleys. The visitor has their own life, their own choices, their own relationship to the place that was brief and intense and may or may not be repeated.

What the power does is this: it ensures that the visitor *remembers*.

Not in a forced way. Not in a way that intrudes on their thoughts or disrupts their life above. But the memory of the Drowned Works — the light in the water, the sound of the machines, the feel of the cold, the sight of the trees, the face of the elder looking at them with something that might be pride or might be grief — this memory stays clear. It does not fade the way memories fade, does not blur at the edges, does not lose its specificity. The visitor will remember the depth gauge in their hand, its weight, the way the elder showed them how to read it. They will remember the younger one finding the tear in their suit and fixing it with careful fingers. They will remember the markings on the wall, the dates and initials of people who worked here in air, and the newer markings, applied in substance that hardens underwater, the continuation of a practice that spans the transition from dry to drowned.

The power keeps this memory clear because the memory matters. Not because the visitor will necessarily act on it — they may never return, may never tell anyone, may carry this knowledge to the end of their life without transmitting it further. But the memory matters because *someone knows*. Someone above the water knows what is below it. Someone has seen the Stayers and been seen by them. Someone has been given a tool and has received it with the gesture that means *I acknowledge this gift, and I acknowledge you.*

The power ensures that this knowing persists. It is a small thing. It is the smallest thing it does. But it is the thing that connects the Drowned Works to the world above — not through institutions, not through research programs, not through any formal structure that would change what the Upper Reach is, but through a single memory, held clearly, by a single person, who will carry it for the rest of their life.

The power trusts the memory. It trusts the person who holds it. It trusts that memory, held clearly, will do what memory does: it will connect. It will keep the Stayers present in the minds of those who might, someday, in some way that no one can predict, need to know that they are there.

---

Then it withdraws.

Not because it is finished — it will never be finished, not in the way that a project is finished, not in the way that a task is completed. It withdraws because its presence, even unseen, even unfelt, is a kind of weight, and the Drowned Works does not need more weight. It needs less. It needs the freedom to continue being what it is, without the pressure of being observed, without the obligation of being cared for.

The power withdraws into the water. It becomes the water, in the way that a benevolent power can become a thing — not by replacing it, not by inhabiting it, but by *consenting* to it. The water is what it is. The power consents to the water. The water carries the light and the sound and the nutrients and the larvae and the clicks and the whistles and the mechanical drones and the slow, patient, persistent life of a place that was not meant to be but is.

The mountains are still here. The machines are still running. The forest is still growing. The Stayers are still staying.

The light falls through the water in great, shifting beams. The elder works on a pump, and the younger ones help, and the work holds. The fish school and scatter and school again. The crustaceans click. The trees wave their gill-needles in the slow currents.

And somewhere, in the water itself, in the space between the molecules, in the pause between one moment and the next, there is a presence that has decided that this place is worth sustaining, and has sustained it, and will continue to sustain it, quietly, patiently, without ever making itself known, because what is here does not need to know it is cared for. It needs only to *be*. And being, it is cared for.

The mountains are still here.

They have simply learned to breathe water.

And someone, somewhere, has made sure the water is worth breathing.
