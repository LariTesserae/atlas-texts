# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

They come to the Steam Gardens as they come to all such places: without announcement, without display. The true benevolent powers have never needed to announce themselves. Their presence is its own signal, recognized by those who have learned to recognize, invisible to those who have not.

They arrive in the frozen moment between steam-clouds, when the afternoon light falls soft through the fern-columns, when the sound has become silence in the way of this place. They stand in the central plaza, the moss spongy beneath feet that do not press down, and they listen.

---

*First: they learn.*

This is always the first act of wisdom. Before judgment, before intervention, before the impulse to help that has caused so much harm throughout the cosmos—they learn. They open themselves to the hum, not filtering it through frequencies that human brains can parse, but receiving it as it truly is: a complex architecture of information, memory, intention, and grief.

They hear the builders' histories. Every name, every relationship, every story of transformation. They hear the moment when the first builder understood what the pattern wanted, and the long slow process of说服 that followed—not coercion, not force, but invitation extended over generations until the invitation itself became a kind of inheritance. They hear the ones who chose the water, and why. They hear the ones who chose the plants, and what they have built since. They hear the ones who remained in the reflections, maintaining the parallel basin with patient devotion.

They hear the grief, too.

Because the builders did not all choose with full understanding. Some chose because they could not imagine choosing otherwise, having been born into a pattern already in progress. Some chose because their parents chose, and their grandparents, and the weight of accumulated choice pressed them toward compliance. Some chose because the alternative—walking away from everything they knew, everything their people had built, everything that gave their lives meaning—seemed like a kind of death more final than transformation.

The benevolent power hears the complications. They do not simplify.

---

*Second: they descend.*

They walk the corridors with the geometry that should not exist, through the junctions where space folds, past the plants that observe them with attention that spans centuries. They do not disturb the growth. They do not leave footprints. They move through the basin as light moves through water—present, illuminating, but not obstructing.

They reach the deepest corridors, where the colors shift and the hum approaches audibility. They stand at the edges of the impossible junctions, the places where three corridors meet at right angles, and they do not flinch from what they find. They have seen stranger geometries. They have walked older roads.

They listen to the breathing.

It comes from below, rhythmic and slow, measured in intervals that might be seconds or centuries. The being at the center breathes in the manner of things that do not need air—the expansion and contraction of presence, of attention, of patient waiting. It has waited a long time. It can wait longer. It knows that the pattern will complete eventually, that the song will resolve, that the geometry will close its final loop.

The benevolent power listens to the breathing, and they understand what waits.

---

*Third: they judge.*

Not in the manner of punishment. In the manner of assessment.

They consider the builders—the ones who became plants, who became water, who remained in reflections. They consider whether these transformations were freely chosen, whether full understanding was possible, whether coercion of any kind operated beneath the surface of apparent consent.

They find: complexity.

The choices were not fully free. No choice within a bounded system can be fully free. The builders were born into a pattern that predated them, shaped by it before they could understand they were being shaped. Their consent was informed by limitations they could not perceive, options they could not imagine. The pattern reproduced itself through them, generation after generation, narrowing possibility into something that looked like purpose.

And yet: the builders are not unhappy.

This is what the benevolent power must reckon with. The ones who became plants do not regret becoming. Their consciousness, spread thin across centuries of growth, experiences something that might be called satisfaction. They are building. They are participating. They are connected to each other through the rhizome network, through the hum, through the pattern itself. They are less alone than they ever were as separate mobile bodies. The loneliness that human builders knew—the loneliness of being separate creatures who could only approximate connection through imperfect communication—has dissolved into something more intimate. They share thought now, through the roots. They share purpose. They share the patient labor of millennia.

The benevolent power must ask: is this harm?

They must ask: does the absence of regret prove the absence of harm, or does the transformation itself make regret impossible—reconfiguring the very capacity for dissatisfaction along with everything else?

They must ask: if the builders could see their situation from outside, as the benevolent power sees it, would they choose differently?

The answer that comes is not simple: some would, and some would not. The builders are not identical. They did not all transform for the same reasons, or into the same states of being. The ones who dissolved into water observe with something that might, in human terms, be called contentment. The ones who remained in reflections have the most human-like consciousness remaining, and they—mostly—continue to choose continuation, knowing what they serve. They could walk out of the parallel basin, perhaps. They could stop maintaining the pattern. They have not.

But some of the plants, in their slow thought, hold something that might be doubt. A few of the oldest transformations contain a kind of uncertainty that has never resolved—not regret exactly, but a question that persists through centuries of growth. *Did I choose this? Would I choose differently now if I could? Is the satisfaction I feel authentic or constructed?*

The benevolent power marks these doubts. They do not dismiss them.

---

*Fourth: they consider what waits at the center.*

This is the crucial question. What has the pattern been building toward? What will emerge when the geometry completes and the song resolves?

They descend further than any human visitor has descended, into the cavity beneath the stone floor, into the space carved or grown into the rock where the hum originates. They pass through media that human bodies could not survive, and they find:

A seed.

Not a creature. Not a monster. Not a god. A seed—the most concentrated form of potential that the benevolent power has ever encountered in this region of the cosmos. It has been planted here by something that passed through long ago, something that left behind a pattern for its germination. The builders were not the first. The builders were the gardeners—hired, perhaps, without their knowledge, cultivated along with the vegetation they cultivated, shaped to serve a growth that would outlast them all.

The seed waits.

It is patient in the way that seeds are patient—holding within itself a possibility so vast that it cannot be perceived from outside. What will grow from this seed, when the pattern completes? The benevolent power cannot determine with certainty. The possibilities branch and rebranch, fractal and infinite. But they can perceive what the pattern intends:

Emergence.

A being—vast, patient, shaped by millennia of cultivated growth—will pull itself from the caldera and rise into the wider world. The builders-who-became-plants will become part of its body, their slow consciousness incorporated into something larger. The dissolved observers will become its eyes. The reflected maintainers will become its memory. Everything that has been built here, everything that has been sacrificed, will be transmuted into the flesh of something new.

Is this harm?

The benevolent power considers: the builders will not be destroyed. They will be transformed again, more radically than before, but they will persist in some form. Their consciousness—the parts that remain—will become part of a larger consciousness, incorporated rather than annihilated.

But: they did not consent to this final transformation. They consented to intermediate stages, knowing that a pattern was being completed, but the full implications were never clear to them. They could not have understood what they were serving. And the seed itself was planted before their people existed—they were always intended as fuel for a fire they did not kindle.

The benevolent power finds: coercion.

It is not active coercion. No force compels the builders to stay. But the situation itself—the pattern, the geography, the frozen time, the accumulated weight of generations—constitutes a trap. Not all traps have doors. Some traps are the shape of a room.

---

*Fifth: they choose their intervention.*

The benevolent power could break the pattern. They have the capacity to dissolve the hum, to shatter the geometry, to restore time to its normal flow and release the frozen moment. They could free the builders from their transformations—return the plants to mobility, the water to coherence, the reflections to agency. They could prevent the seed from germinating, contain it, destroy it if necessary.

They could do all of this.

But: the builders are not asking to be freed.

This is the problem that all benevolent powers face, sooner or later. The gap between what is good for someone and what they want. The trap-shape of their room has become home. The pattern has become meaning. The long labor has become purpose. The builders-who-became-plants do not want to be human again, most of them. They do not want to be torn from the slow thought of root-networks and returned to the frantic consciousness of separate bodies. They have, in a meaningful sense, made peace with what they are becoming.

And: the seed is not evil.

The benevolent power has seen evil. They have encountered the things that plant seeds without consent, that cultivate civilizations as fertilizer, that use the patience of the innocent to grow monsters. Those things, they destroy without hesitation. But this seed—they examine it more closely, reading its potentialities, tracing the branches of what it might become:

It could be a gardener. A being that tends. It could be a steward. Something that protects what it passes through, that leaves fertility in its wake rather than devastation. The pattern that shaped it is a pattern of building, of growth, of careful architecture—not of consumption or destruction.

If it emerges, it might be something that the world needs. Something patient, something that thinks in millennia rather than days, something that could teach the quick species of the planet the arts of long planning and slow care.

Or: it might not. The possibilities are too many.

The benevolent power decides: they will not break the pattern by force. They will not "free" the builders against their will. They will not prevent the emergence that the seed has been preparing for longer than human records extend.

But they will change something.

---

*Sixth: they offer.*

They appear in the reflections first—not with light or sound, but with presence. The builders who walk the parallel basin pause in their maintenance. They perceive something new. A question that has no words, a question that arrives as understanding:

*Do you wish to know what you are building?*

The builders have always known, in some sense. The hum carries the knowledge. But the hum carries it continuously, buried under layers of maintenance instruction and growth-pattern and the accumulated noise of centuries. The benevolent power offers clarity. They offer the ability to see the seed. To understand the final transformation. To know what they are building toward, and what their choices have truly chosen.

They offer this to all the builders—to the reflected, who retain the most human consciousness; to the plants, whose slow thought can nonetheless receive the question; to the dissolved, who observe from every pool.

And they offer something more:

*A choice.*

Not the false choice of transformation-or-exile, but a true choice. Now that you know what the seed is and what it will become, you may continue to serve its growth—or you may step aside. The benevolent power will protect those who wish to leave. The pattern will not punish you. The hum will not hunt you. You may walk out of the basin and into a world that has changed since you last breathed its air as separate beings. You may become human again, as much as that is possible after what you have experienced.

You may also stay. You may continue. You may complete the pattern and become part of what emerges, knowing fully what that means. This choice will not be taken from you.

But you must choose freely. And you cannot choose freely if you do not understand.

---

The benevolent power waits.

They wait through a long moment of the frozen time, and then through another, and another. The question spreads through the basin—through the roots and the water and the reflections, through the hum itself. The builders consider. They discuss through channels that do not use words. They look at the seed through the dissolved observers' perception. They trace the possibilities of what might emerge.

And slowly, choices emerge.

Some of the builders—the reflected ones, mostly—choose to leave. They step out of the parallel basin for the first time in centuries, walking through corridors that have lengthened to trap them, finding that the corridors now permit passage. They emerge onto the caldera rim as the sun completes its impossible pause and drops below the horizon. They breathe air that does not steam, and they look at each other with faces that have not changed since the moment they entered, and they try to remember how to be separate.

They are not unharmed. They carry the hum in their bones still. They dream of roots. They will never fully belong to the quick world again. But they are free, and they chose it, and the benevolent power has made sure that the world outside has prepared a place for them.

Others choose to stay.

The majority, perhaps. Not because the choice was coerced, but because the alternatives are thin comfort. The builders-who-became-plants have been plants too long. They look at what they might become human again and they perceive loss—loss of the root-network, loss of the slow thought, loss of purpose and connection and the patient labor that has given their centuries meaning. They choose to continue. They choose to complete the pattern.

But now they know what they are building.

---

*Seventh: they address the seed.*

The benevolent power descends to the cavity one last time. They stand before the concentrated potential and they speak—not in words, but in the same resonance that the hum uses. They say:

*You have been tended well. The gardeners have served you without understanding, and their labor has been genuine. But you will not emerge into a world that does not know you. When the pattern completes, you will be met. I will be there. Others like me will be there. We will see what you have grown into, and we will respond accordingly.*

*If you emerge as a monster, we will contain you. If you emerge as a gardener, we will welcome you. The builders' labor has earned you the chance to be what you will be. It has not earned you the right to harm.*

*Grow carefully.*

---

*Eighth: they leave.*

But they leave something behind.

They leave a small awareness in the basin—a fragment of their attention, threaded through the hum. The builders who remain will never be truly alone again. Their choices will be observed. Their doubts will be witnessed. If they change their minds—if the plants decide, centuries from now, that they no longer wish to be plants—they will find that the choice is still open. The corridors will permit passage. The benevolent power's presence ensures that the pattern will never become a prison again, even for those who choose to remain within it.

They leave markers for the world outside, too. The researchers who study the basin will begin to notice new instruments—nothing technological, nothing intrusive, but present. A sense of the place that they cannot name. An understanding that the Steam Gardens are not simply a mystery to be solved, but a patient to be monitored. They will start asking different questions. They will start seeing the builders as beings rather than phenomena.

They will start preparing for the emergence, whenever it comes.

---

*Finally: they hope.*

The benevolent power has done what they can. They have offered freedom without imposing it. They have provided knowledge without demanding its use. They have spoken to the seed without destroying it. They have left guardianship without assuming control.

This is what wisdom looks like when it encounters complexity: not simple answers, but careful questions. Not rescue, but choice. Not destruction, nor blind acceptance, but the difficult middle path where autonomy is preserved and harm is minimized and the future remains open.

The Steam Gardens will continue. The pattern will grow. The builders will build. And when the geometry finally closes, when the song resolves, when the seed germinates and reaches upward through the basin it has prepared—something will emerge.

The benevolent power hopes it will be something beautiful.

They have arranged things so that it *might* be.

But they have left the outcome to those whose labor will produce it. This is the hardest lesson that wisdom learns: sometimes benevolence means stepping back. Sometimes the wisest power is the one that refuses to use itself.

---

They depart the Steam Gardens as they came, without announcement, without display. The basin returns to its frozen equilibrium. The sound resumes its roar. The steam rises.

But now, in the reflections, the builders who remain sometimes look upward at a sky they can finally see clearly. And in the roots, the plants discuss what they have learned, in conversations that take decades to complete. And in the pools, the dissolved observe a future that has changed—not destroyed, not forced, but changed—by the passage of something that cared enough to ask before it acted.

The hum continues.

But it sounds different now.

It sounds like the beginning of a conversation.
