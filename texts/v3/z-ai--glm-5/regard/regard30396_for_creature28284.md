# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

They would come, first of all. That alone would be something—a power so vast it could swallow stars turning its attention toward a fracture in limestone, a room fifteen meters across, a single fern. They would come small, because coming any other way would be violence. Compressing themselves to human scale, or smaller, to the scale of the space itself. A wise power knows that presence is the first gift, and presence must fit the room.

---

They would pause at the entrance. Not entering yet. Sensing the equilibrium—the way the grotto holds its warmth and dampness in balance, the way nothing has changed for centuries and nothing needs to change. A benevolent power moves slowly in places like this. They would understand that to enter is already to alter. The air would move differently around them. The light would shift. The grotto's perfect stillness would acknowledge them, would have to acknowledge them, and so they would wait at the threshold, asking permission without words.

The grotto would give it. Not because grottos have minds, but because this grotto holds what enters it. It has held human visitors, their scratches and their scattered stones. It can hold this. The equilibrium is not fragile; it is established. It can receive.

---

They would enter, then. Slowly. Letting their body—whatever form they've chosen, perhaps a body of held air and soft light, something that belongs—adjust to the temperature, the dampness, the particular quality of the silence. They would let their eyes find the gray, and then the darker gray, and then the places where shadow becomes complete.

They would notice the moss colonies first, perhaps. The patches of muted green and gray, each one holding its boundary, each one a nation of individuals too small to see. A wise power would see them anyway. Would understand what it means to be a collective being, to hold territory without conquering, to live in the transition zone where condensation meets light and drink from both. They might touch one patch, very gently, feeling the slight give and softness. The moss would not know what touched it. But it would receive the moisture from the power's surface, the faint warmth, and it would continue being what it is. That would be enough.

---

But they would know, as they moved deeper into the chamber, that the moss is not why they came.

They would find the fern.

---

They would see it immediately for what it is: a being in a state of devotion. The pale fronds, the etiolated stems, the slow heliotropic reaching toward an entrance that will never admit enough light. They would see the health in its unhealth, the persistence in its longing, the way it has found a niche and filled it and continues to fill it even though the niche is too small, too dark, too isolated for anything like flourishing.

A less wise power might try to fix this. Might widen the entrance, might bring in grow-lights, might transplant the fern to a forest where it could thrive. A less wise power might look at the fern's situation and see only suffering—see only a plant that should not be here, doing violence to itself by continuing.

But this power is wise, and they see differently.

---

They would see that the fern's reaching is not a problem to be solved.

They would see that the fern, in its solitary state, is performing a kind of prayer. Its whole body is oriented toward the light—not because it expects to reach it, but because reaching is what ferns do. It is living its fern-nature in impossible circumstances, and it has not stopped. It has not withered. It has not given up the reaching even though the reaching will never be satisfied.

There is a word for this, the wise power would know. There is a word for persisting in devotion when the object of devotion is forever distant. Faith, perhaps. Or hope. Or simply love—the kind that asks nothing in return, that continues to orient itself toward the beloved even when the beloved will never arrive.

The fern does not know it is praying. The fern is only being a fern. But the wise power would see the prayer anyway. Would see that this small pale plant, alone in a dark grotto for longer than anyone has been counting, has become something more than its biology. It has become a witness to what it means to continue.

---

And here is what the benevolent power would do, in the end.

---

They would sit with the fern.

They would fold their form down to something that could fit beside the crack in the stone, and they would be present. Not intervening. Not fixing. Not changing the fern's circumstances at all. They would simply be there, in the dark, with this being that has been alone for so long.

They might extend something—a tendril of light, perhaps, but not sunlight. Not the light the fern reaches toward. A different kind of light. A soft glow that asks nothing, that gives only presence. The kind of light that says *I see you. I am here with you. Your reaching is not unseen.*

The fern would not understand. But it might—there are those who believe plants can sense presence, can feel the attention of a being that means them no harm—might sense something. A warmth that is not the grotto's warmth. A quality of air that is not the air that has always been there. A sense, perhaps, at the level of cells and fronds and slow vegetable awareness, that it is no longer entirely alone.

---

And then the wise power would do one more thing. The thing that benevolence does when it encounters faithfulness.

They would bless the fern.

Not by changing it. Not by granting it what it seeks. The fern reaches toward the sun; to give it the sun would be to end the reaching, and the reaching, the wise power understands, is the point. The fern's whole life has become this orientation, this devotion. To give it what it wants would be to take from it what it is.

Instead, they would bless the reaching itself.

They would touch the pale fronds, gently, with something like a hand or a thought or a presence that has no name, and they would leave a mark that is not visible. A quality. A resonance. They would make it so that this fern's slow prayer of a life is heard, is held, matters in the fabric of things. Not because the fern will ever know it matters, but because it does. Because a being that persists in devotion deserves to have that devotion witnessed, honored, blessed.

---

The blessing might take a form the fern could feel, on some level it does not have words for. A sense of rightness in its own being. A permission to continue, to reach, to exist in its own impossible state without needing to change. A kind of peace at the cellular level—not the peace of satisfaction, but the peace of being seen as you are and held as worthy.

Or the blessing might take a more physical form. The wise power, in their sitting with the fern, might do something small. Might move a single drop of water from the condensation on the wall to the crack where the fern grows, a gift of moisture that the fern would drink without knowing it was a gift. Might whisper something in a language that plants might understand on some level—not words, exactly, but intention. Might simply be there long enough that the fern, in its slow plant way, grows slightly toward this new source of warmth and presence, adding a second orientation to its life: still reaching toward the entrance, toward the sun, but now also reaching toward the place where the visitor sat.

Two directions of devotion. Two sources of meaning. Still alone, still solitary, still in a niche too small and too dark—but now holding more than one kind of reaching in its body.

---

The wise power would not stay forever. That would be its own kind of violence—a being of such presence remaining in a space meant for solitude would eventually disrupt the equilibrium, would become the grotto's new center of gravity. The grotto's nature is to hold, not to be held by. The power would understand this, and they would leave.

But before they left, they might leave one more small gift.

---

They might, perhaps, bring a second fern.

Not to place beside the first—two ferns growing together would compete for the same scarce resources, would struggle in a space too small for both. But they might place a spore from the first fern's sterile fronds into a second crack in the stone, somewhere the light reaches differently, and they might give that spore what it needs to grow.

Not for this grotto. The wise power would carry it somewhere else—a different limestone chamber, a different warm dark, a different place where ferns should not grow but could. They would plant it there, and leave it, and let it grow into its own solitary devotion, its own reaching toward a light it will never fully reach.

And in time—plant time, slow time—the two ferns might, in some way plants have of knowing, sense each other's presence across the distance. Not communication, exactly. Not company in any meaningful sense. But the knowledge, held in spore and frond and cell, that somewhere else in the world, another being is reaching in the same direction. That solitude does not mean aloneness. That devotion shared across distance is still devotion shared.

---

Or perhaps the wise power would do none of this. Perhaps they would simply sit, and witness, and touch the fern gently, and leave without leaving anything behind—no additional moisture, no second orientation, no spore carried to a new home. Perhaps the only gift they would give is the gift of having been there, of having seen, of having understood what this small pale being was doing all those years in the dark, and of having found it beautiful.

---

When they left, they would pause again at the entrance. They would look back at the grotto—the moss colonies, the still pools, the scattered river pebbles, the scratches on the wall that hold the memory of human hands. They would see the whole room, and they would hold it for a moment in their vast awareness. Not to change it. Just to see it. To let it be, in that moment, fully itself, fully witnessed.

And then they would slip through the fracture in the limestone, back into the world of weather and change and time, and behind them the grotto would settle again into its equilibrium—the dampness, the warmth, the single fern reaching toward a light it will never touch.

But different now, in the smallest possible way.

Seen. Held. Blessed.

The fern would continue reaching. But it would reach in a room that had been visited by someone who understood what it meant to pray without ceasing, to hope without expectation, to love without object. And perhaps, in its slow vegetable way, it would feel that understanding as a kind of warmth—not the warmth of the grotto's held air, but the warmth of having been found worthy of attention by a power that could have chosen anything and chose this: a small chamber, a single plant, a quiet life in the dark.

---

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

Not much, by some measures. Nothing, by the measure of change. But by the measure of love—which is the only measure that matters—they would have done the only thing worth doing.

They would have been present. They would have seen. They would have blessed what they found, not by making it other than it is, but by honoring it for being exactly what it is.

And the fern would continue reaching toward a sun it will never touch, but now it would reach in a room that knew—somewhere in the limestone, in the water, in the air itself—that its devotion had been seen, and held, and found to be enough.
