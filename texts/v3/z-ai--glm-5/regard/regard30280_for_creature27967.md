# What the Power Does

It does not arrive.

This is the first thing to understand about a truly benevolent power: it knows that arrival is a form of violence. To appear suddenly before the people of the Seeping City—even in glory, even with gifts, even with the best of intentions—would be to shatter the careful balance they've built with their difficult home. It would make them objects of intervention rather than subjects of their own existence. So the power does not arrive. It attends.

Attendance is different. Attendance is presence without interruption. The power turns its attention toward the Seeping City the way one turns toward a friend who hasn't asked for help but might need it anyway. It looks. It sees. It lets itself be informed by what it finds.

---

What it finds, first, is water.

The power sees the weeping walls, the rising damp, the mineral tears that track down every vertical surface, and it sees what the residents cannot: that the water is trying to speak. Not in language—nothing so simple—but in the only vocabulary available to it. The water rises through two hundred years of accumulated industrial byproduct, through soil that was poisoned before the city's founders were born, through the compacted remains of marsh plants that once held this ground in a different kind of balance. The water carries all of this. The mineral veins on the walls are a record, a long slow testimony that no one has known how to read.

The power listens to what the water is saying. It listens for a very long time.

What it hears is this: *I am trying to become a marsh again. I am trying to remember what I was. I am trying to tell you that this ground was alive before it was built on, and the life is still here, compressed into something else, waiting.*

The power does not find this surprising. Water, it knows, is patient. Water does not forget its nature. It will go where it is directed, take whatever shape is required, but it never stops being itself. The water in the Seeping City has been patient for two centuries. It has accepted the pumps and channels and cisterns. It has adapted to being moved and managed and collected. But it has also, in its slow persistent way, continued to rise. Continued to weep. Continued to trace its mineral veins down the walls like a message written in a language the city's residents never learned to read.

The power considers what it would mean to help the water speak more clearly.

---

What it finds, second, is soil.

The soil beneath the city is not dead. This is what the residents believe—that the industrial soot killed everything, that nothing green can grow here, that the earth has been rendered permanently sterile. But the power sees differently. It sees that the soil is full of life, just not the life that the residents are looking for. The fungal growths in the pump rooms are not a sign of absence. They are a sign of presence. They are what grows when the plants that were meant to grow cannot.

The soil beneath the Seeping City wants to be what it was. Before the factories, before the foundations, before the engineers and their grand plans, this was marshland. This was a place where water and earth met in a slow conversation that both of them understood. The soil remembers. It holds the memory in its composition—the particular combination of decayed plant matter and minerals and microorganisms that can only develop over centuries of a specific kind of aliveness. The industrial soot is sitting on top of that memory, not replacing it. The soil is waiting, the way soil does, for conditions to change.

The power considers what it would mean to help the soil remember faster.

---

What it finds, third, is people.

The residents are harder to see, in some ways, because they don't know they're being seen. The power cannot help but notice that they have become their environment. Their skin has taken on the mineral quality of the walls. Their movements have learned the thermal logic of the plazas. Their thoughts have absorbed the spacious emptiness of the streets. They are not separate from the Seeping City. They are an expression of it.

This is not, the power reflects, necessarily bad. Adaptation is not degradation. The residents have found a way to live in a difficult place, and their adaptation has given them things—resilience, patience, a particular kind of interior quiet—that easier lives might not produce. The power does not look at them and see victims. It sees people. Complicated, specific, shaped-by-circumstance people, who have built a life that works for them in a place that doesn't work for anyone else.

But.

The power also sees the children who leave. It sees the population declining, the dormitory corridors emptying, the thin margin between maintenance and abandonment. It sees the visits to the cistern, and it understands that those visits are not merely functional—they are a kind of prayer, though the residents would never call them that. A prayer for what? For recognition, perhaps. For acknowledgment that their lives have weight and meaning, that the water that collects in the dark center of their city is not just industrial runoff but something they have contributed to, something that carries their mineral signature.

The power considers what it would mean to answer a prayer that hasn't been spoken.

---

What it does, first, is the smallest thing.

It sends rain.

Not a storm—nothing dramatic, nothing that would disrupt the pumps or flood the cisterns or require the residents to think about what was happening. Just rain. Gentle, persistent, the kind of rain that other places receive as a matter of course. The Seeping City hasn't had rain in memory. The sky has been sealed by the haze, the thermal inversion, the two hundred years of accumulated output. The water has only come from below.

Now it comes from above.

The first time the residents notice it, they don't know what they're seeing. They look up—something they rarely do—and see droplets falling through the haze. They feel them on their skin. They taste them. The rain is clean. It carries none of the mineral load of the weeping water. It is simply water, from clouds that have gathered above the particulate layer, descending through a gap in the inversion that shouldn't exist.

The power has created the gap. It has done so carefully, in a way that doesn't destabilize the haze layer permanently or cause dramatic weather shifts. Just a temporary opening, a moment of atmospheric adjustment, enough to let a few clouds through. The rain falls for an hour. Then it stops.

But the rain has done something. It has touched the soil. It has reached the ground between the buildings, the plazas that have been empty stone for generations. It has carried with it something the power has placed there—not a miracle, not a dramatic intervention, but a seed. A particular combination of microorganisms and fungal spores and dormant plant life that was always meant to grow in this ground. The rain wakes them up.

---

What it does, second, is also small.

It opens a door.

Not a physical door—the Seeping City doesn't need more architecture. It opens a door in the minds of the residents, or rather, it opens a door in the minds of some of the residents. Not all of them. The power is too wise to impose universal perception. But a few—the ones who visit the cistern regularly, the ones who walk the plazas at dusk, the ones who have developed the interior quiet that makes new thoughts audible—begin to see something different.

They see potential.

This is not a vision of a transformed city. It is not a hallucination of paradise. It is simply the ability to perceive what the power perceives: that the soil is waiting, that the water is speaking, that the balance the city has achieved is not the only balance possible. The residents who receive this perception don't understand what they're receiving. They don't have words for it. But they start to notice things they didn't notice before.

They notice that the fungal growths in the pump rooms are spreading slightly faster than the maintenance crews can scrape them away. They notice that the mineral veins on the walls have started forming new patterns, denser concentrations in certain areas, as if the water is trying to highlight something. They notice that the salamanders have begun clustering in different locations, following some shift in the moisture distribution that the residents' instruments haven't detected.

They notice that the emptiness feels different. Not gone—the plazas are still vast and empty—but charged. As if something is preparing to fill them.

---

What it does, third, is wait.

This is perhaps the most important thing the power does. It does not rush. It does not force. It does not impose a timeline on transformation that the city isn't ready for. It has introduced rain. It has introduced perception. Now it waits to see what the city and its residents will do with these introductions.

The power is patient. It can afford to be. It has been watching the Seeping City for two hundred years. It can watch for two hundred more. It has given the city something it didn't have before: a catalyst. But the catalyst has to work on its own terms, in its own time, through the existing materials and relationships and balances.

The power does not expect instant change. It does not demand gratitude. It does not require the residents to understand what has happened, or to acknowledge the source of the change, or to alter their lives in any particular way. It has simply given them what it could, in the gentlest form it could, and now it observes.

---

# What Grows

---

The first signs come from underground.

The workers in the pump rooms notice it. The fungal growths—the gray, rubbery sheets that they've always scraped away with routine industrial indifference—have started to change. They're still gray, still rubbery, but they're denser now, more substantial, and they're spreading into configurations that the workers haven't seen before. Not random growth, but something organized. Thread-like structures spreading from the main masses, reaching toward the cracks in the walls, following the water veins with apparent purpose.

The workers don't know what to make of this. They report it to their supervisors. The supervisors, who have lived their entire lives in the Seeping City and have never seen anything grow with purpose, don't know what to make of it either. They order more frequent scraping. The growths grow back faster than before.

In the cistern, the water level fluctuates. Not dangerously—the pumps are still running, the system is still functioning—but variably. The water seems to be moving differently, collecting in places it didn't collect before, draining from places where it used to pool. The engineers check the pumps, check the channels, check the foundations. Everything is working as designed. The water is just... choosing different paths.

Above ground, the salamanders start to migrate. Not far—they're still within their territorial bands, still clustered near the weeping walls—but their patterns shift. They're following something, and the residents who've received the power's gift of perception can almost see what it is: a movement of moisture, a redistribution of the underground flows, a subtle realignment of the water's priorities.

The power watches. It sees what the residents don't yet see: that the fungal networks are exploring. That they are seeking. That they are, in their way, looking for something.

---

The rain returns.

Not regularly—it doesn't become a predictable weather pattern. But it returns, intermittently, unpredictably, always gentle. The residents don't know what to make of it. There hasn't been rain in living memory. The meteorological records from the city's founding mention rain, but that was two centuries ago. The current residents have grown up with the haze layer, the thermal inversion, the perpetual twilight of filtered sun. Rain is something that happens in other places.

But now it happens here. And every time it does, it carries the same combination of microorganisms and dormant life. The ground between the buildings—those broad empty plazas that have been bare stone for generations—begins to change.

It happens slowly. So slowly that the residents don't notice it at first. But the power, watching closely, sees:

A patch of darker color on the plaza stones. A slight softening of the mineral trails where the water has run. A faint green tinge at the edges of the drainage grates, where the rain collects before flowing down.

The power knows what this is. It is the beginning of soil. It is the ground remembering what it was.

---

The residents who visit the cistern start to talk to each other.

This is new. The cistern has always been a place of individual contemplation. Residents stand at the railing, look down at the dark water, think their private thoughts, and leave. They don't gather. They don't discuss what they've experienced. The experience is too internal, too wordless, to be shared.

But some of them—four, then seven, then twelve—have received the power's gift of perception. They've started to see what the power sees, or at least glimpses of it. And when they encounter each other at the cistern, they recognize something in each other's faces. A particular quality of attention. A way of looking at the water that isn't just looking.

They don't have meetings. They don't form an organization. They don't give themselves a name. But they start to... coordinate. Not deliberately, not through discussion, but through a kind of unspoken alignment. When one of them notices something—new fungal growth in a pump room, an unusual moisture pattern in a wall, a shift in salamander territory—they mention it to another. Not in a way that would seem significant to an outsider. Just a casual observation. *Have you noticed the walls near the third cistern access? They're weeping differently.*

*Yes. I've seen it.*

*What do you think it means?*

*I don't know. But I think we should keep watching.*

This is how it begins. Not with a proclamation or a revolution, but with attention. With shared watching. With the gradual recognition that something is happening, and that it might be worth paying attention to.

---

The first green thing appears in a crack in the plaza.

It's small. A tiny cluster of leaves, pushing up through a gap between paving stones near the central cistern. A resident walking past notices it—stops, looks down, isn't sure what they're seeing. It's been so long since anything green grew in this city that the very concept of "plant" is almost theoretical. They know plants exist. They've seen pictures. They've heard of the gardens that failed in the early days. But they've never seen one.

They kneel down. They look closer. The leaves are pale—not the deep green of healthy vegetation, but a washed-out, yellowish green, as if the plant is struggling to find the nutrients it needs. But it's alive. It's growing. It's a color that hasn't been present in this city for two hundred years.

The resident doesn't pick it. Doesn't trample it. Doesn't report it to the authorities. They just look at it for a long moment, then stand up and continue walking. But when they pass by that spot the next day, they notice that the cluster has grown slightly. There are more leaves. A thin stem is pushing up toward the dim light.

They start walking past that spot more often. They're not sure why. They don't tell anyone. But every time they pass, they look down, and every time they look down, something has changed. The plant is spreading. New clusters are appearing in nearby cracks. A tendril of green is tracing its way along the edge of a drainage grate.

Two weeks later, another resident stops beside them while they're looking.

*What is that?*

*I don't know. It started growing a few weeks ago.*

*It's... green.*

*Yes.*

*Nothing green grows here.*

*I know.*

They stand together, looking down at the tiny cluster of leaves. They don't have words for what they're feeling. But they both feel it—a kind of wonder that they haven't experienced since childhood, if they ever experienced it at all. Something is growing. In a place where nothing was supposed to grow. It's small and struggling and probably won't survive. But it's trying.

---

The power watches all of this.

It sees the fungal networks spreading underground, reaching toward the surface, preparing the way for something new. It sees the rain continuing to fall, gently, intermittently, carrying life into soil that has been waiting for it. It sees the residents beginning to notice, beginning to pay attention, beginning to wonder.

It sees something else, too. It sees the first signs of what the residents would call hope, if they had a word for it. Not hope for anything specific—they're not hoping for transformation or salvation or escape. They're hoping for... more. More green. More growth. More of whatever this is that has started in the cracks between their paving stones.

The power is satisfied. Not because it has fixed anything—it hasn't fixed anything, and it knows that the Seeping City is far from fixed—but because it has introduced a possibility. The possibility that the stasis isn't permanent. The possibility that the balance can shift. The possibility that the water's message might be heard, that the soil's memory might be honored, that the empty plazas might one day be something other than empty.

It continues to attend. It continues to wait. And in the fullness of time—more time than any resident will live to see, but time that the power can afford—it continues to work.

---

# What Continues

---

The transformation, once it begins, is neither fast nor dramatic.

The power does not believe in dramatic interventions. It has introduced the catalysts—rain, perception, the seed-memory of what the ground used to be—and now it steps back and lets those catalysts work. The process takes decades. Generations. The power is patient.

The fungal networks spread through the underground, breaking down the accumulated industrial byproducts in the soil, converting the toxic into the merely difficult, preparing the ground for the plants that will come. The process is slow, barely noticeable to the residents who live through it, but accumulative. Each generation inherits slightly better soil than the one before.

The rain continues to fall, intermittently, unpredictably. The residents gradually stop thinking of it as an anomaly and start thinking of it as weather. They develop words for it—their language had forgotten the vocabulary of precipitation, but they reinvent it, finding terms that fit their experience. *Sky-water. Soft fall. The washing.*

The green spreads. Not rapidly—it never becomes a garden city, not in any timeframe the power can foresee—but persistently. The tiny clusters in the cracks between paving stones spread into larger clusters, then into patches, then into the beginnings of ground cover. The plants that emerge are not the plants that grew here before the city. Those plants are extinct, or nearly so, their seeds long since rendered unviable by the poisoned soil. What grows instead are new things, hybrids and adaptations, plants that have learned to live in mineral-rich ground, to tolerate what remains of the industrial contamination, to reach toward a light source filtered through perpetual haze.

The salamanders don't mind. They find new places to cluster, new walls to investigate, new drainage patterns to follow. The geckos don't mind either—they simply move higher on the buildings, claiming the upper walls where the new growth hasn't reached. The birds continue their cycles in the upper air. The fauna adapts, as fauna do, finding new equilibriums in the slowly shifting environment.

---

The people adapt too.

The children who leave still leave—there's nothing the power can do about that without violating the principles of free movement and choice. But some of them start leaving less decisively. Instead of walking away and never returning, some of them go and come back. They've heard about the green patches in the plazas. They've heard about the rain. They've heard about the new fungal growths in the pump rooms that the maintenance crews have stopped scraping away because they seem to be cleaning the water, making it slightly clearer, slightly less mineral-heavy.

The power doesn't take credit for this. It doesn't need credit. It watches as the city's relationship to itself changes, slowly, imperceptibly, one generation at a time.

The residents who received the gift of perception pass it on. Not deliberately, not through teaching, but through presence. They stand at the cistern with their children, looking down at the water, and their children learn to look. They walk past the green patches and pause, and their children learn to pause. They develop a way of paying attention to the city that is different from the way their ancestors paid attention—noticing growth, noticing change, noticing possibility.

The emptiness in the plazas doesn't disappear. The city doesn't transform into something unrecognizable. But the emptiness starts to hold different weight. It's not just absence anymore. It's potential. It's space that could be something, even if it isn't yet.

---

The cistern becomes a different kind of place.

Not sacred, exactly—the power doesn't create religions, and the residents don't either. But central in a new way. Residents still come to stand at the railing and look down at the dark water. But the water looks different now. It's still mineral-heavy, still carrying the city's accumulated chemical memory, but it's clearer. The fungal networks in the pump rooms have been filtering it, slowly, over decades. It still weeps from the walls, still collects in the central chamber, but it's not the same water that collected there a generation ago.

The residents who stand at the railing don't know why the water has changed. They don't have the scientific knowledge to understand fungal filtration or soil remediation or the slow process of ecological recovery. But they can see it. The mineral veins on the walls are less pronounced. The white tracings are fainter. The water reflects the dim light differently.

It's cleaner. Not clean—the city is still built on poisoned ground, and the factories are still running, and the haze is still trapped by the thermal inversion—but cleaner. Moving toward something. Becoming.

---

The power is still there.

It hasn't left. It attends, as it has always attended, watching the slow process of transformation it initiated. It sees the city's residents living their lives—working in the factories, walking the plazas, enduring the heat and the wet and the dim light. It sees them developing new relationships to the place they live. Not dramatically different, not unrecognizably different, but different.

They've started to name the green patches. Not formally—they don't have a naming ceremony, don't keep records—but informally, in the way they talk to each other about where they're going. *Past the patch near the third pump room. By the green place on Kesa Street. I'll meet you where the ground-cover is spreading.* The names are functional, but they mark something. The residents have started to notice that the city is no longer static. It's changing. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, but changing.

The children who stay—and more of them are staying, now—grow up with a different understanding than their parents had. They don't assume the city is finished. They don't assume the emptiness is permanent. They see the green spreading, the rain falling, the water clearing, and they develop a different relationship to time. Not the stasis-time of their ancestors, where nothing changed and nothing was expected to change, but a growth-time, where each year is slightly different from the year before, where the future holds possibilities the present doesn't.

---

The power considers this a good outcome.

Not a perfect outcome. Not a dramatic transformation. Not a salvation. The Seeping City is still the Seeping City. It's still hot and wet and dim. It still weeps. It still produces gears for machines built elsewhere. It still has salamanders and geckos and swifts and people who work in factories and sleep in dormitories and walk the wide empty plazas in the cool of the evening.

But it's also something else now. It's a place where green things grow. Where rain falls. Where the water is slowly, slowly becoming cleaner. Where the residents have learned to see possibility in addition to acceptance.

The power doesn't expect the residents to thank it. The residents don't know the power exists. They attribute the changes to luck, or to nature, or to the slow workings of time. And that's fine. The power isn't looking for gratitude. It's looking for the particular kind of good that happens when a place is given what it needs to become more fully itself.

The Seeping City is becoming more fully itself. Not what it was before it was built—the marshland is gone, and nothing will bring it back exactly as it was. But not what it settled into during two centuries of stasis either. It's becoming something new. Something that integrates what it was built to be with what it was before it was built. Something that honors the water's message and the soil's memory and the people's quiet persistence.

The power continues to attend. It continues to wait. It has introduced the catalysts, and now it watches as they work.

And in the central cistern, where the water collects from every crack and crevice and wall, the mineral veins are slowly fading. The water still weeps. The city still seeps. But it seeps toward something now, instead of simply holding in place.

That is what the power has done. That is what the power does.

It attends. It introduces. It waits.

And slowly, slowly, the world becomes more like itself.
