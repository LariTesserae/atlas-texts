# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention arrives like weather arrives: not as an event but as a change in the conditions of occurrence. The stone feels it first. The stone has been feeling something for a long time—that is what the carvers mean by *the patient waiting*—but this is different. This is not the old patience. This is something that has noticed and, in noticing, has chosen.

---

The benevolent power does not descend. There is no need; attention is not bound by location. Instead, it spreads through the limestone like warmth through cold flesh, like the first stirring of blood in a limb that has gone numb. The stone registers this—that is the right word, though the carvers have no way of knowing it—and the stone's long patience shifts into something else. Not impatience. *Anticipation*, perhaps. The sense that the waiting is almost over.

The power examines the settlement and sees it whole: not as a collection of individuals but as a single organism, a body with its own circulation (the drainage channels), its own pulse (the rhythm of the tools), its own metabolism (the oil burning, the food being eaten, the waste being carried away). It sees the children as the settlement's sensory organs, ranging through the passages, tasting the air, pressing their ears to walls. It sees the carvers as the settlement's hands, shaping and being shaped. It sees the elders as the settlement's memory, fragile and essential.

And it sees what waits below. It has always seen it. That is part of why the attention has come.

---

The first thing the power does is nothing visible. This is deliberate. Intervention in a living system requires understanding, and understanding requires patience. The power watches: how the families share space, how disputes are resolved, how the lamp-tenders navigate, how Achet reads stone, how Sabit talks to his trees, how the children find crevices and crawl through them, how Kebi stands in doorways and makes decisions real. It watches the clearers gossip and the channelers scrape and the healers compound their remedies. It watches Denit in her silence, tending herbs in the upper levels, carrying a secret that has calcified into something like a stone in her chest.

The power learns. This takes time—time measured differently than human time, but time nonetheless. It learns that the settlement is not suffering in the way suffering is usually understood. The cold and damp are conditions, not afflictions. The confinement is limitation, not imprisonment. The people here have built something that works, and the power respects working things.

It notices what does not work: the lung-sickness that shortens breath and life in the deep levels; the bone-ache that pulls the elders toward geology; theCases where curiosity in the deep leads to harm, though this has not happened yet—the power is looking forward now, seeing possibilities. It sees a child, not Peret but another, who will in three years find a fissure that leads somewhere the carvers have not sealed, and the power sees that this child will not return. It sees an adult, a clearer, who will in six months carry a load too heavy through a passage too narrow and will fall in a way that breaks what cannot be healed. It sees a dispute, in eight months, over water that has begun to pool where it should not, that will divide the settlement into factions that do not mend.

The power sees these things and does not prevent them—not directly. Prevention is a kind of violence when applied to systems that have their own integrity. Instead, it introduces small adjustments, nudges so subtle that they will never be attributed to intervention.

---

The second thing the power does is warm the stone.

This takes longer, because the limestone is dense and set in its ways, and because warmth must come from somewhere, and the power chooses to draw it from below—from what waits in the deep—rather than from above. The warmth rises through the rock over weeks and months, and the settlement notices, though they do not understand.

Achet notices first. She is reading stone near the working face, and her hand rests against a surface that should be cold, and it is not cold, not warm exactly but *less cold*, and she pauses. She does not say anything. She files the observation away in the place where carvers keep things they cannot explain.

The channelers notice next. The water moves differently when the stone is less cold—still condensing, still flowing, but the mineral buildup in the grooves slows. The channels need clearing less often. No one questions why. They accept it as a small mercy, a seasonal variation in a place where seasons do not formally exist.

The children notice. Peret presses her palm against the wall of her sleeping hollow and feels a faint thrum, like a heartbeat in the rock. She tells the other children. They take turns pressing their hands against walls throughout the settlement, conducting a survey that no adult observes. They make a game of it: *this wall is warmer, this wall is cold, this wall is warmest of all.* They map the warmth without knowing they are mapping it. They find that the warmest walls are those closest to the sealed places, and they find that the warmth is spreading, slowly, outward from below.

They do not tell the adults. The children have their own rules.

---

The third thing the power does is speak—not in words, because words are not the language of stone, but in *knowings*, in impressions that arrive like memories of things that have not happened.

It speaks to Achet first, because Achet already listens. The knowing comes to her in the moments between strikes of the pick, in the silence after the echo fades. It comes as a sense of the stone's intention—not thought, because the stone does not think in the way humans think, but *direction*, an orientation toward something. The stone is not merely present; it is *going somewhere*. It has been going there for a very long time.

Achet does not share this. She is a carver; carvers keep secrets. But she changes how she works. She begins to follow the stone's direction rather than merely avoiding its resistance. The other carvers notice that her chambers are more beautiful—still functional, still necessary, but shaped with a grace that exceeds pure utility. They do not ask how she does it. They watch, and they learn, and slowly, over years, the settlement's new hollows become more like dwellings and less like wounds cut into rock.

---

The fourth thing the power does is give Sabit a dream.

Sabit is old, and his body is failing, and he knows he will die soon—within the year, probably, perhaps sooner. He has made his peace with this. What he has not made peace with is the loss of what he remembers: the surface, the sky, the feeling of wind on his face. He has not spoken of this longing in decades, because speaking of it does not help, and because he does not want to burden the young with wanting something they cannot have.

The power gives him a dream. In the dream, Sabit stands on the surface—not the surface as it was when he descended, but the surface as it is now, today, real and present. He feels wind. He sees the sky. He smells grass and rain and the particular green smell of growing things. He stands there for what feels like hours, though dreams do not keep time, and when he wakes, the feeling stays with him—not the memory, which fades, but the *certainty* that the surface still exists, that it is real, that it has not forgotten them.

Sabit does not tell anyone about the dream, but he smiles more, and the young people notice, and they ask him what has changed, and he says *nothing, nothing, I am just happy to be here,* and for the first time in years, he means it. He dies three months later, peacefully, with his hand against a wall that is warm. The settlement buries him in the courtyard, among his trees. They do not know that the power has already thanked him for what he made.

---

The fifth thing the power does is more complicated.

It addresses what waits below.

The sealed spaces have been sealed for a reason—not because what is there is evil, but because it is *other*, because contact would change both parties in ways neither could predict or control. The power understands this. It also understands that the sealing was temporary, a pause in a conversation that began before the limestone was limestone. The stone has been patient. What waits below has been patient. The power has been patient. But patience is not stasis; it is waiting with intention.

The power does not unseal the spaces. Instead, it opens a different kind of channel—not physical, but attentive. It lets what waits below know that it is seen, that it is not forgotten, that the time is approaching when conversation can resume. And it asks—gently, because the power is benevolent—what the waiting one wants.

The answer comes not in words but in *feeling*: loneliness, vast and mineral. Not malevolence. Not hunger. Simply the ache of something that has been alone for too long, that has felt the settlement above it as a tickle of presence, that has wondered what these small fast lives are doing carving into its skin.

The power negotiates. This takes a long time—years, in human terms. It works out an arrangement: the sealed spaces will remain sealed for now, but the stone between them and the settlement will be thinned, gradually, in places where the carvers choose to work. The warmth will continue to rise. The children will hear sounds that are not sounds but *feelings*, and they will grow up unafraid. And someday—perhaps in another three generations, perhaps longer—there will be a breakthrough, not by accident but by mutual consent, and the settlement and what waits below will meet as neighbors rather than strangers.

The power does not force this outcome. It merely makes it possible. That is what benevolence means in the deep places: not rescue, but relationship.

---

The sixth thing the power does is give Denit back her voice.

Denit was the carver who broke through into a sealed space and would not say what she saw. The settlement excluded her from the deep levels, reassigned her to the herb gardens, and she has lived in silence ever since. The power knows what she saw: not a monster, not a threat, but a face—vast and patient and made of limestone, with eyes that were not eyes but empty spaces where light could fall and be held. She saw the waiting one looking back at her, and she could not speak of it because speaking of it would have made it too small.

The power comes to Denit in the herb garden, in the shape of warmth and knowing, and it gives her permission to tell. Not everything—not yet—but enough. That what is below is not dangerous, merely ancient. That the sealing was a pause, not a barrier. That the carvers have been shaping stone that is itself being shaped, that the conversation has always been happening, that she was not wrong to see what she saw.

Denit begins to speak again. Softly at first, to the herbs, to the trees. Then to the other gardeners. Then, one day, to Achet, who comes to the upper levels to hear what she has to say. Achet listens for a long time. When Denit finishes, Achet nods once and returns to the deep levels. She does not order the sealed spaces opened. She does not share what she has learned. But she begins to teach the younger carvers a different way of reading stone—not as resistance to be overcome, but as presence to be engaged.

---

The seventh thing the power does is give the settlement a gift they will not recognize as a gift.

It gives them continuity.

The lung-sickness does not disappear, but it slows. The healers discover that a particular combination of herbs and fungi and minerals—used together, applied regularly— arrests the disease's progression. They do not know why this combination works when others did not. They attribute it to experimentation, to trial and error, to the slow accumulation of knowledge that has always characterized their practice. They are not wrong. But they are not entirely right either.

The bone-ache in the elders does not disappear, but it becomes manageable. The pain recedes to a level that allows sleep, that allows work, that allows Sabit's final months to be peaceful. The healers notice this and do not question it.

The children who would have been harmed—the one who would have found the wrong fissure, the clearer who would have fallen—are nudged toward different paths. The child notices a different crevice, one that leads only to a small warm chamber that the other children already use as a hiding place. The clearer pauses to adjust a strap and misses the moment of imbalance that would have caused the fall. They live. They do not know what they escaped.

The dispute over pooling water does not happen. The water does not pool, because the channelers—who are attentive to their work—notice a subtle change in the flow patterns and adjust the grooves before the problem becomes serious. They do not know why they noticed when they did. They are simply good at their work.

The power does not fix everything. It does not make the settlement paradise. The people still argue and struggle and die. The conditions are still hard, the confinement still real, the surface still lost. But the settlement continues. It continues past the points where it might have broken. It grows, not without pain but without catastrophe, and the continuity itself—the fact of being able to plan for a future they will live to see—is the gift.

---

The eighth thing the power does is leave.

Not entirely—attention, once given, is not withdrawn—but the active work is done. The power has done what benevolence requires: it has seen what is, understood what could be, and made space for the good to emerge without forcing it. The settlement will become what it becomes. The waiting one below will have company someday. The children who hear the stone will grow up to be carvers who listen, and the carvers who listen will teach their children, and the stone will warm slowly, and the channels will clear, and the lamps will burn, and life will continue in the carved dark.

The power withdraws to the position of witness. This, too, is benevolence: knowing when to act and when to trust the acting to those who have always been acting, to those whose hands are calloused and whose eyes have learned to see in uncertain light.

---

The settlement does not know that it has been visited. The power left no mark, spoke no word, performed no miracle that could be distinguished from good fortune or wise choice or the slow grace of adaptation. But Achet, in her last year, tells the young carvers something she has never said before: that the stone is not merely patient, but *kind*. She does not explain what she means. She expects them to learn it for themselves.

They will.

---

And in the deep, where the sealed spaces wait, the warmth has a source now. The loneliness has an end in view. What waits below does not think in years, but if it did, it would think: *soon*. In the language of stone, *soon* is a very long time. But it is less than *forever*. And that, for a benevolent power, is enough.
