# The Attention of a Benevolent Power

## What They See

The first thing they notice is the noise.

Not as intrusion—as injury. A benevolent power perceives the world through the beings who live in it, and here, the beings live inside a continuous acoustic wound. The roar has shaped them. It has pressed their ears flat against their skulls, taught them to feel through their feet and palms instead of hear through canals designed for softer worlds. The power feels this as they would feel a child's broken bone—something that wants to heal, that *should* heal, that has been prevented from healing by the circumstances of its existence.

But they do not rush to fix.

---

They have lived long enough, seen enough, to know that the first intervention is almost always wrong. The impulse to rescue arrives before understanding, and understanding arrives before wisdom, and wisdom arrives only after you have watched for a very long time.

So they watch.

---

They see the garden first as the Saps see it: not as trap or prison but as *home*. This is important. The columns rise like family. The fungal terraces feed. The cold is known, predictable, survivable. The Saps do not live in degradation; they live in adaptation. Their lichen-skin is not a wound but a garment they have grown. Their bent posture is not shame but practicality—they have learned what shapes serve.

The power sees the elder Sap sitting by the water channel, fingers working the small mechanical thing. They see the younger one touch the elder's shoulder before moving on. They see the arrangement of objects on the platform—bark carved into shapes, stones sorted by color, the small skull kept for reasons unknown but clearly *kept*, not discarded. They see the grave that is also a cradle, the bodies returned to become the ground their kin walk on.

They see that these beings have made meaning. They see that this meaning is sufficient, if not complete.

---

But they also see other things.

They see the tunnel—the explosive entrance, the violence of the cutting, the extraction that began this whole system. They trace the pipes upward through darkness to where they emerge, far above, in a facility that still operates. They see the product moving—column segments harvested, processed, shipped, sold. The Saps are workers who have become so adapted to their work that they can no longer imagine leaving it.

This is the problem with adaptation: it solves the immediate question (how do I survive this?) by foreclosing the larger one (should I be here at all?). The Saps have answered the first question so completely that they have forgotten the second existed.

The power feels this as grief. Not their grief—the grief of *possibility foreclosed*. They have become excellent at being here. They might have become excellent at other things too. They will never know.

---

They watch the Sap approach the narrator with the gift—the column segment, warm and faintly glowing. They feel the gesture as it is meant: not desperate plea for contact, not tragic evidence of isolation, but simply *recognition*. You are here. I see you. You are not of us, but you are *seen*.

The Saps have a theory of mind. They have social cognition. They have the capacity to categorize strangers as "not threat" and to behave toward them with something approaching generosity. These are the foundations of culture, of relationship, of the possibility of larger connection.

They have not lost their humanity. They have localized it.

---

## What They Consider

The power considers intervention.

There are forms they could take. They could appear as light, as voice, as a sudden silence that heals the acoustic wound. They could offer extraction—the tunnel made gentle, the surface made ready, a world of sun and open air prepared to receive these beings who have forgotten sun and air. They could heal the damage: straighten spines, restore ears, clear the lichen-skin, reverse the adaptations. They could, in short, *rescue*.

They have done this before. They have learned.

---

They consider the elder Sap.

If they offered extraction, would the elder go? They think yes—there is curiosity in those dark-mirror eyes, there is the capacity to notice strangers and categorize them, there is the impulse to give gifts. The elder was not born to this place; the elder remembers, somewhere in the body, what it was to stand upright in open air.

But they also consider the younger ones. The ones who have known no other world. For them, extraction is not rescue but *evacuation*. To carry them into the sun would be to blind them, to burn their adapted skin, to place them in a world whose grammar they do not speak. The power has seen this before: the rescued who die of rescue, the saved who cannot survive salvation.

And they consider the dead—the bodies already absorbed into column-base and fungal terrace. If the Saps leave, who tends the graves? Who continues the incorporation? The garden would grow untended, the machines would fail, the columns would fall or sicken. The Saps' departure would be the garden's death, and the garden is, in some sense that matters, *their dead*.

---

They consider the extraction itself.

The pipes lead up. The product moves. Someone profits. The Saps work, but they do not own the work. They have been shaped by an economy that required their transformation into something that could work here—then abandoned to that transformation when the surface operation became automated, deniable, distanced. The power sees the legal structures, the corporate shells, the ways that what happens below is kept separate from what happens above. The Saps are not slaves in any legal sense. They are simply *left*.

This is the violence that was done: not the forcing-down but the preventing-of-return. Not the breaking but the failure-to-heal. They were made necessary and then forgotten.

A lesser power might burn the facility above, free the workers below, punish the systems that created this. This power considers that path and sets it aside. Punishment is not healing. Destruction is not repair. The Saps would still be here, in a garden whose machines had stopped, whose columns would sicken, whose graves would go untended.

---

## What They Decide

The power decides on a different approach.

---

First: *presence*.

They do not arrive as light or voice or sudden silence. They arrive as something subtler—a gentle pressure at the edge of perception. The kind of feeling you get when someone is standing just behind you, not threatening, simply *there*. The kind of awareness that makes you turn your head.

They offer this to each Sap, individually, over the course of a cycle. They wait at the edge of each being's attention until the being notices. Then they wait longer, until the being understands that it has been noticed *back*.

The elder Sap feels it first—a sense that the column it is working beside is also working beside it, that the garden has acquired a new quality of attention. It pauses. It looks up. It does not see anything, but it feels seen.

The younger ones feel it next, spread across the cycle, each visited in turn. The power does not push. They simply *are*—present, attentive, interested.

They want the Saps to know that they have been found.

---

Second: *silence*.

The power cannot stop the roar—those machines are too integrated, their cessation would be the garden's death. But they can create small spaces where the noise does not reach.

They do this gently. Around each sleeping platform, they weave a thin film of quiet—not true silence, but a softening. The roar becomes distant, muffled, like weather heard through glass. The Saps notice this the way you notice a room becoming lighter: gradually, then suddenly, when you realize that something has changed.

The elder Sap wakes from a sleep deeper than any it has known in years. It sits, listening. For the first time in how long, it can hear its own breathing. The younger ones stir in their platforms, look at each other, make sounds that are not the usual near-song but something softer—experimental, testing.

The power has given them the ability to hear themselves. This is the precondition for being able to ask what they want.

---

Third: *voice*.

The power does not speak *to* the Saps. They speak *through* them—opening a capacity that has atrophied but not disappeared.

They do this by pressing gently against the place in each Sap where language used to live. The elder Sap, working on its mechanical repair, finds its mouth forming shapes it had forgotten—the curve of a word it once knew, the shape of a question. The younger ones, moving between columns, feel their throats remembering how to make sounds beyond the near-song—vowels that once named things above, consonants that once carried meaning in a world with different grammar.

The power does not teach them language. They simply remind the Saps that they used to have it.

This is not rescue. This is *restoration of the capacity to choose*.

---

Fourth: *option*.

The power creates a path.

Not a tunnel—the violence of blasting is not repeated. They create something gentler: a way of being in two places at once. A door that does not open onto a specific location but onto the *possibility* of location.

They place this door where the Saps will find it—near the water channel, visible from the elder Sap's platform. It does not glow or shine. It simply presents itself as an option: a place where the air is different, where the light comes from a different source, where the body moves in ways this body remembers moving, long ago.

The door does not demand. It does not pull. It waits.

It will wait as long as necessary.

---

## What Happens

At first, nothing happens.

The Saps notice the door—they cannot help but notice it, placed so deliberately in their path. But noticing is not acting. They have lived here long enough to distrust novelty. They watch the door for several cycles, approaching it separately, regarding it from different angles, making the near-song sounds that might be discussion.

The power does not interfere. They watch.

---

Then the elder Sap approaches the door.

It stands before it for a long time. The power can feel the longing in its body—the deep memory of standing upright, of feeling sun on skin, of breathing air that does not taste of oxygen and moisture and machine. They can also feel the fear: the fear of being blinded, burned, lost, the fear of leaving the graves untended, the fear of a body that has adapted so far that it can no longer remember how to be what it was.

The power does not push. They have offered the door. They cannot offer the choice.

---

The elder Sap reaches out.

Its hand touches the surface of the door—not the door itself, but the *option* it represents. And the power lets it experience, for one moment, what it would be like to step through: the warmth, the light, the unaccustomed clarity of air that has never known machine or column.

The elder Sap gasps. Its body remembers.

Then it steps back.

It does not go through. It steps back, turns, and returns to its platform, to its mechanical repair, to its life in the garden.

---

But something has changed.

That night, the elder Sap makes a sound it has not made in years—a string of syllables that once, long ago, meant something. The younger ones turn toward it. They do not understand the words, but they understand the *attempt*. They gather near the elder's platform. They sit.

The elder Sap speaks. The words are halting, incomplete, damaged by disuse. But they are words. They are language.

The power cannot hear what is said—the Saps' language is their own now, built from near-song and gesture and the grammar of a world without sun. But they can feel the quality of the conversation: the Saps are discussing something they have not discussed before. They are discussing *choice*.

---

The conversation continues for many cycles.

The power watches as the Saps argue, agree, disagree, and argue again. They watch as the elder Sap takes its mechanical repair to the door and works on it there, half in and half out of the option, becoming accustomed to the difference. They watch as younger ones approach and retreat, approach and retreat, learning to tolerate the presence of a possibility they had never been offered.

They do not know what the Saps will decide. They have learned, over very long time, that you cannot save beings who do not choose to be saved. But you can offer them the ability to choose, and you can wait.

---

## What the Power Leaves Behind

The power does not stay forever.

They are not a god of this place; they are only an attention that passed through, noticed, and offered what it could. They leave behind:

The silence around sleeping platforms—a softening that will persist, that will let the Saps hear themselves think, that will make language possible again.

The door, which will remain as long as any Sap wants it. It leads to a place they have prepared above—warm, sheltered, lit by a sun filtered through screens that will not burn adapted skin. The place is not a destination. It is an *option*. It will wait as long as options wait.

The restoration of voice—not forced, not demanded, but opened. The Saps can speak now, if they choose. They can name things. They can ask questions. They can wonder, together, whether they want to stay.

And they leave behind something harder to describe: the knowledge that they were seen. Not as degraded remnants of humanity, not as tragic workers exploited and abandoned, but as *beings*—beings who have made a life, who have created meaning, who have loved each other in the ways this place allows, who tend their graves and their columns and their machines with something that might be devotion.

The power saw them. The power sees them still.

---

## What Might Happen Next

The power does not know how this ends.

The Saps might stay. They have lives here—meaning, purpose, graves that need tending, a garden that breathes with them. They might decide that adaptation is not degradation, that the shape they have taken serves the world they have, that the door is a kind offer but not for them. This would be valid. This would be a choice.

The Saps might go—some of them, slowly, testing the option with the caution of beings who have learned that the world does not offer gifts without costs. They might find a way to live between, to tend the graves from above, to return as the column-sap rises and falls with seasons that do not exist. This would also be valid. This would also be a choice.

The Saps might split—some staying, some going, the community dividing along lines of age or temperament or memory of the sun. This would be painful, but it would be honest. The power has learned that honest division is better than false unity.

Or they might do something the power cannot predict—something that emerges from the grammar of their own experience, their own near-song language, their own understanding of what it means to be a being in a garden of living columns.

---

The power withdraws. Not entirely—never entirely, for a benevolent attention does not cease—but withdraws to the edge of observation. They will watch. They will wait. They have offered what they can: the restoration of choice.

The garden continues. The machines roar. The columns pulse with slow fluid, their bioluminescence cycling through patterns that might be communication and might be autonomic process and might be both.

And in the silence around the sleeping platforms, the Saps speak to each other in words they are relearning—words that mean *stay* and *go* and *why* and *what if* and, most importantly:

*What do you want?*

This is the question the power came to give them.

This is the gift that cost nothing and everything.

The Saps are asking it now.
