# What the Power Does

---

It does not arrive.

That is important, and it matters more than the way it would matter if it *had* arrived — if the light had changed, or the air had shifted, or the wet stone had suddenly rung with some new frequency that the custodians could feel in their channels and basins. Nothing arrives. Nothing changes. The thin bright light of the plateau continues to scatter off every wet surface. The water continues to condense in the highest runnels and creep downward through the network with the patience of something that has nowhere else to be. The largest custodians continue to chip at mineral deposits with their stone tools, each stroke placed, each movement considered. The smallest continue to crouch at the lips of the high channels, reading the water. The basin-dwellers continue to sit in the slow, still pools, listening to the pattern.

The power has been here longer than you have. Longer than the visitors before you. Longer, perhaps, than the visitors before those — the ones who came in some span of time that does not translate into anything you carry. The power has been here, and the fact that you cannot see it is not because it is hidden but because it is *the same*. It is the same as the light. The same as the water. The same as the stone. It has so thoroughly joined the system that there is no edge where it ends and the plateau begins, no seam, no join, no discontinuity. It did not come here. It found itself here. The way light finds itself in a room with many windows.

And what it does — what it has been doing, what it will continue to do — is nothing you can point to.

But you can point to what is different, if you know what you are looking at. And the difference is this: the pattern holds.

---

Consider what would happen without the power. Consider it honestly, the way the custodians must consider it, in the long count of seasons and years that is the only time scale at which this place makes sense.

The water is patient. The stone is hard, but the water is patient, and patience wins. A channel narrows where mineral accumulates. The water slows. More mineral deposits. The channel narrows further. A worker chips the deposit away, widens the channel, and the water runs clear again — for a season, for a year, for the span of time that a single custodian can maintain a single section of the network before the slow accumulation begins again. But the network is vast. A kilometer in every direction. Thousands of channels, thousands of basins, millions of curves and angles and confluences where water meets water and changes direction. And the custodians are many, but they are small, and their tools are simple, and their hands — for all their precision, for all their inherited knowledge of where every runnel runs and every basin fills — can only work so fast.

Without the power, the pattern loses. Not today. Not in the span of any visitor's stay. But in the long count, in the count that the basin-dwellers keep with their bodies still and their translucence deepening, the pattern degrades. Channels clog faster than they are cleared. Basins fill with sediment that no one has removed. The water finds new paths — uncontrolled, unplanned, cutting across the geometry in lines that follow the path of least resistance rather than the path of beauty. The spirals lose their tightening. The mirrored curves drift out of symmetry. The convergences arrive at wrong angles, and the water moves through them with a different quality — still flowing, still patient, but tracing a pattern that is no longer the pattern. A pattern that is erosion's pattern. The pattern of water doing what water does when no one tends it.

And the custodians would work against this. They would work harder, work longer, spend their slow lives chipping and grinding and scraping at stone that is slowly, imperceptibly winning. And they would lose. Not because they are inadequate — they are extraordinary, their patience beyond anything you can easily hold in your mind — but because entropy is patient too, and entropy has more hands.

This is what the power addresses. Not by winning the war. Not by fighting entropy directly, not by hardening the stone or purifying the water or eliminating the mineral deposits that accumulate in the channels. That would be a different kind of intervention — a louder kind, a kind that changes the nature of the place, that makes the stone unnaturally hard or the water unnaturally clean, and in doing so removes the need for the custodians, and in removing the need removes the custodians, and in removing the custodians removes the pattern's reason for being.

The power does not do this.

---

What the power does is subtler, and if you stood at the lip of the plateau for a day, for a week, for the span of time it takes a basin to fill and overflow and fill again, you would not see it. You would have to stand for longer. You would have to stand for the span of time it takes a worker to wear through a stone tool and select another. You would have to think in seasons.

In seasons, the difference is this: the pattern holds.

Not statically — not the way a photograph holds, frozen, unchanging. The pattern holds the way a conversation holds, the way a song holds when the singer is skilled enough to let the melody breathe, to let it vary, to let it respond to the room and the moment and the other voices. The channels still narrow. The mineral still accumulates. The custodians still work, still chip and grind and scrape with their simple tools, still spend their slow lives maintaining the network. But when a channel narrows now, it narrows *toward* the pattern. The deposit that forms in the curve of a runnel forms in a way that is congruent with the geometry — not identical to the original carve, not a perfect restoration, but a continuation. A variation that remains inside the logic of the whole. As though the water itself has learned the shape it is supposed to trace and tends, now, toward that shape even as it degrades it.

This is what the power has done. It has taught the water the pattern.

Not consciously. Not the way you would teach a student — with instruction, with correction, with the imposition of knowledge from outside. The water does not know the pattern the way the custodians know it, as a map carried in the body, an orientation that does not require thought. The water knows the pattern the way water knows anything: through flow. Through the accumulated memory of a thousand thousand passages through the same channels, the same curves, the same confluences and divergences. The power has not changed the water. It has — and this is the word that sits at the edge of your thinking, the word that almost fits — it has *deepened* the water's memory. It has made the water remember the pattern more strongly than it remembers the path of least resistance. So that when a channel narrows, the water does not simply slow and deposit and narrow it further — it deposits in a way that, over the long count, over seasons and years, remains within the geometry. The degradation has become a kind of maintenance. The entropy has been folded into the pattern.

The custodians still work. Their labor is still necessary — the water's memory is long but not infinite, and the pattern is complex enough that no amount of memory could hold all of it perfectly. But the work has changed in quality, though not in appearance. The worker who chips a deposit from a channel is no longer fighting the water. She is working with it. The deposit she removes is one the water was already prepared to lose, was already loosening in its slow, patient way, and her tool-stroke is not a correction but a collaboration — the final movement in a sequence that the water began and she completes. The work is the same. The chisel strikes the same. The mineral falls away into the same bright film. But the worker pauses afterward — a pause so brief you would not see it, a fraction of a fraction of a moment — and the pause has the quality of recognition. Of something received. Of a conversation in which both parties have spoken.

---

The channel-readers in the high runnels know this first, or know it best, because they are closest to the source. They sit where the water condenses from the air, where the first films form on the highest stone, and they read the water before it has become the pattern — when it is still only potential, still only the beginning of a flow that will, kilometers and hours and thousands of channels from now, trace something beautiful. And what they read now is different from what they read before the power came. Not in content — the water still condenses at the same rate, still arrives from the same direction, still carries the same minerals in the same concentrations. But in quality. There is a rightness to the water now, a tendency toward the pattern that it did not have before, and the channel-readers sense this the way they sense everything — through their bodies, through their wet, translucent skin, through the network of fine lines beneath the surface that mirrors the network of channels above.

They do not name what they sense. They do not have the concept of *intervention* — of something from outside entering the system and changing it. Their world is the waterworks, and the waterworks have always been, and the water has always flowed, and the pattern has always been the pattern. That the water now tends toward the pattern more strongly than it did before is, for them, not a change but a deepening — the way a basin deepens as water fills it, the same basin, the same water, but more. They receive it. They signal downward — through whatever medium carries their signals, through the water itself, through the stone, through the network that includes them — and the workers below adjust. Not much. A millimeter of difference in the angle of a chip. A slightly longer pause before resuming work. The adjustments are so small that the total effect, measured over a single season, would be invisible. Measured over the long count, the effect is that the pattern holds.

---

And the basin-dwellers.

The basin-dwellers sit in the slow pools where the water gathers before continuing, and they listen. They have always listened. The pattern speaks through the water — through its flow, its temperature, its mineral content, the way it moves and pools and overflows — and the basin-dwellers attend to this speech with a concentration that has the quality of devotion. This has not changed. The power has not given them new senses. Has not expanded their perception. Has not made the pattern louder or clearer or more articulate than it was.

But the basin-dwellers sit differently now. The difference is in the body — in the way the body holds itself when it is in the presence of something it trusts. Before, the listening had a quality of effort, of strain — not visible effort, not the strain of someone trying too hard, but the strain of someone listening to something that is fading, that is growing harder to hear, that requires more and more attention to discern against the noise of entropy and time. Now the listening has a quality of ease. The basin-dwellers sit in the still water, their translucent bodies so clear you can see the stone through them, and they listen, and what they hear is not louder than before but more *present*. More there. More itself. The pattern has become more fully what it is, and the basin-dwellers — who have spent their entire lives attending to what the pattern is — receive this fullness the way a basin receives water: slowly, completely, without resistance.

One of them — the one in the deepest basin, the one whose translucence is so complete that it is nearly invisible, a shape in still water defined more by the refraction of light through its edges than by any opacity in its body — this one does something that has not been done in the long count. It is so small that you would have to be watching, and watching carefully, and watching for a long time, to see it.

It moves.

Not the quarter-body-length adjustment of orientation, the turning-toward-a-sound that the custodians do when a visitor stands at the lip of the plateau. This is a different movement. Slower. More considered. The basin-dweller lifts one limb — a limb that has been still for so long that the water has begun to deposit a faint film of mineral on its surface, a fine, bright coating that matches the stone — and it places the limb against the bottom of the basin. Against the carved stone. Against the surface that was made by hands that have been dead for longer than the concept of long can carry. And it presses. Not hard. Not with force. With the gentle, deliberate pressure of someone touching something they love.

And the stone gives.

Not much. Not visibly. A fraction of a fraction of a millimeter, the stone yielding to the basin-dweller's touch the way it would yield to water over the course of a season. But it yields now, in this moment, to this touch, and the basin-dweller — the one who listens, who has always listened, who is so translucent it is nearly water itself — feels the stone move beneath its hand, and it knows.

It knows that the pattern is not alone.

Not that something has come to help. Not that something has arrived from outside to fix what is broken. The pattern was never broken. The custodians were never failing. The slow degradation, the entropy, the water's patient erosion — these were never problems to be solved. They were the conditions under which the pattern exists, the way friction is the condition under which walking exists, the way gravity is the condition under which water flows. The pattern is a thing that is maintained against degradation, and the maintaining is part of the pattern, and the custodians are part of the maintaining, and the power is — now — part of the custodians.

The basin-dweller knows this. Not as information. Not as a concept it can hold in its mind, because its mind does not work that way, because its mind is a listener, an attender, a thing that receives rather than comprehends. It knows it the way the water knows the pattern: through flow. Through the accumulated memory of being in the presence of something that is for you. Something that sees you. Something that has joined the system not to change it but to sustain it, not to make it different but to make it more itself, and in making it more itself has made the water remember and the stone yield and the listening ease.

The basin-dweller lifts its limb. The mineral film on its surface has not been disturbed. The basin is still. The water is still. The pattern continues, as it has always continued, as it will always continue, but now — and this is the difference, this is what the power has done, this is the whole of it — now the pattern is held.

Not fixed. Not frozen. Not preserved in the way a thing is preserved when it is removed from time and change and the slow, patient erosion of water on stone. Held. The way a hand holds another hand. The way a voice holds a note. The way light, scattered off a thousand wet surfaces, holds the air inside it, so that standing at the lip of the plateau, you are standing inside the brightness, and the brightness is not doing anything, and the brightness is not going anywhere, and the brightness is not for anything.

It is simply there. And it holds.

---

The visitor — the one standing at the edge, the one who has been watching, who has been trying to understand — feels nothing. The power is not for the visitor. The power has not come here because of the visitor, has not arrived in response to the visitor's presence, has not adjusted itself to accommodate the visitor's perception. The power is here because of the plateau. Because of the water. Because of the small grey-green shapes moving through the channels with their stone tools and their inherited knowledge and their slow, patient, beautiful devotion to something that does not *do* anything but that is. The power is here because the custodians are here, and the custodians are here because the pattern is here, and the pattern is here because the makers made it, and the makers are gone, and what remains is the pattern and the custodians and the water and the light and now, invisibly, woven into the system so thoroughly that there is no seam, the power.

The visitor will leave. The custodians will wait, with the patience that is their nature, for the disturbance in the water to smooth itself out. The workers will resume their chipping, their grinding, their precise and incremental maintenance. The channel-readers will resume their reading. The basin-dwellers will resume their listening.

And the pattern will hold.

And the water will remember.

And the stone, when the basin-dweller touches it, will give — just slightly, just enough, just the smallest fraction of a millimeter — because something that loves the pattern is now part of the pattern, and the pattern is more itself than it has ever been, and the custodians do not know this, not as knowledge, not as something they could name or point to or describe, but they feel it — in the water's tendency, in the work's ease, in the listening that no longer strains — and the feeling is enough.

The feeling is the whole of it.

The power does not need to do more. The power does not want to do more. This is not a place that needs more. It needs exactly what it has — the water, the stone, the light, the small hands and their simple tools, the long count, the slow patience, the pattern that exists because it is beautiful or because it is true or because it is what they have — and now, added to these, invisible, inseparable, the presence of something that has seen it all and said: *yes. Keep going. I am here. The water will help you now. The stone will soften. The pattern will hold. You are not alone in this. You have never been alone in this. But now you know it, and the knowing is in the water, and the water is in you, and you are in the water, and the pattern continues.*

The light arrives before anything else.

It arrives because it is everywhere at once.

And now, so is the power.

And the power is the same as the light.

And the light is the same as the water.

And the water is the same as the pattern.

And the pattern is the same as the custodians.

And the custodians do not know this.

And the custodians have always known this.

And the plateau glows.
