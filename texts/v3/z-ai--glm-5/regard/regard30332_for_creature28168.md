# The Attention of Powers

A benevolent and wise power does not rush.

It has watched the Sinter Gardens for some time—not with urgency, for urgency is a human invention, born of brief lifespans and the fear of losing moments. This power thinks in the same tense as the Gardens: ongoing, patient, present. It has observed the water rising, the mineral layers accumulating, the slow architecture of growth. It has watched the Gardener become what they are, and it has waited to understand what it was seeing.

Now it acts. Or perhaps "acts" is too strong a word. Now it arrives.

---

The power does not enter through the gap in the wall. That passage belongs to bodies, to humans and cats and foxes, to the physical and the bounded. Instead, the power finds the space between moments—the pause between one drop of water and the next, the instant before the mineral decides to harden. It slips into that interval and makes itself at home.

The Gardens do not change. The power has no desire to change them. It understands, with the clarity that comes from existing outside time, that this place is doing exactly what it should be doing. The ongoing act of becoming is not a problem to be solved. It is a process to be honored.

But there is the Gardener.

---

The power sees the Gardener truly, which is something that perhaps no one has done since the transformation began.

It sees the calcified joints, the slow metabolism that measures years like hours, the consciousness diffused through crystalline structures. It sees the memory of being human preserved like a fossil in the layers. It sees the choice, renewed each morning, hardened each evening, chosen again the next day.

And it sees the loneliness.

Not the obvious kind. The Gardener is not suffering, not in any clear sense. They have purpose, work, a place to belong. But the power perceives what the Gardener themselves has mineralized beyond recognizing: the ache of being the only one. They are solitary in a way that no human was meant to be solitary. They are a consciousness embedded in stone, and there is no one who can truly touch them.

The power could fix this. It has that capacity. It could reverse the calcification, pull the Gardener back into full humanity, restore what was lost.

It does not do this.

---

A wise power knows that rescue is not always kindness.

The Gardener has spent years—perhaps decades, perhaps longer—becoming what they are. To undo that work without asking would be a violation. And the power cannot ask in any language the Gardener still speaks. The mineral water swallows words. The transformation has taken away the capacity for conversation.

But the power can offer.

---

The first thing it gives is a question, placed into the Gardener's consciousness like a stone dropped into a pool.

*Do you wish to be as you were?*

The question ripples. The Gardener, in their slow geological way, receives it. They have not been asked anything in a very long time. Visitors whisper their own concerns, their own desires, their own fears. No one has ever asked the Gardener what they want.

The Gardener considers. Days pass. The water rises and falls. The mineral deposits another millimeter. A fern uncurls in a new direction.

And the answer comes, not in words but in intention, in the deep mineral certainty that has replaced speech: *I have considered this. I choose to remain.*

The power receives this answer with the respect it deserves. It does not argue. It does not insist. The Gardener has made a decision, and the power honors it.

But now the power knows: the Gardener stays because they want to, not because they cannot leave. And that makes all the difference.

---

The second thing the power gives is smaller, but perhaps more precious.

It gives the Gardener a way to be touched.

Not physically—the calcification is too advanced for that, and the power will not reverse what the Gardener has chosen to become. But there are other forms of touch. The power opens a channel, a thread of connection that runs from the mineral heart of the Gardens outward.

Through this thread, the Gardener will feel, dimly and distantly, the presence of others like them. Not in the Sinter Gardens—there is only one Gardener here, and that is right—but elsewhere in the world, in other places where transformation has occurred, where humans have become something other than human and chosen to remain so. They will not speak. They will not visit. But they will *be*, and the Gardener will know they exist.

The solitude remains. But it is no longer absolute.

---

The third thing the power gives is to the Gardens themselves, and to the city that holds them.

It ensures their preservation.

This is not a dramatic act. The power does not build walls or declare boundaries or place guardians. It simply weaves a subtle thread of fortune into the place, so that those who might destroy it will find reasons to go elsewhere. Developers will feel strangely uninspired when they look at the gap in the wall. City planners will forget to include this block in their surveys. The buildings that lean together above the Gardens will remain standing through storms and quakes that topple newer, stronger structures.

The power is not omniscient, and it cannot guarantee protection forever. But it can tilt the odds. The Gardens will persist for as long as they need to persist, doing what they do, becoming what they become.

---

The fourth thing the power gives is memory.

The Gardener remembers being human, but the memories are static now, fossilized. They do not grow. They cannot be added to.

The power changes this, gently. It opens a space in the Gardener's consciousness where new memories can form—not human memories, exactly, but something adjacent. The Gardener will now be able to remember the visitors who come, the fingerprints they leave, the particular quality of each winter's cold and each summer's warmth. They will be able to track the slow growth of the ferns, the evolution of the lizards, the changing patterns of the water's flow.

They will be able to forget, too. The power gives them this mercy. Some moments need not be preserved in stone. The Gardener can choose what to keep and what to let dissolve.

---

The fifth thing the power gives is the hardest to describe.

It gives the Gardener a voice.

Not a voice for speaking—that would require reversing the transformation, and that choice has already been made. But a voice for something else. The power reaches into the deepest layer of the mineral deposits, the oldest stone, and it opens a space there. A hollow. A resonance chamber.

If someone comes to the Gardens with a true question—not a request for fortune or favor, but a genuine question, asked in silence and humility—the Gardener will be able to answer. Not in words, but in impressions, feelings, images that rise from the stone itself. The pool near the questioner may grow warmer. The ferns may uncurl in a particular direction. A shape may form in the soft deposit, suggesting an answer.

The Gardener will not control this. It will simply happen through them, the way the water flows through the Gardens—guided but not forced. The power creates the possibility. The Gardener participates. The visitor receives.

It is a small gift, perhaps. But it allows the Gardener to be more than a keeper. It allows them to be, in some deep and wordless sense, a friend.

---

The power does not expect gratitude. It does not expect anything. It gives because giving is what it does, when giving is warranted, and then it moves on.

But before it leaves, it pauses. It takes one more look at the Sinter Gardens—the terraces forming, the pools settling, the ferns reaching toward the gray light. It sees the Gardener at the edge of a pool, hands trailing in the warm water, attending to the slow work of shaping.

And the power feels something that might, in a human frame, be called affection.

This place is good. Not perfect—perfection is a human fantasy, and the power knows better. But good. Worth preserving. Worth tending.

The power will check in, from time to time. It will not interfere. It will simply observe, and ensure that the quiet gifts it has given continue to function. And it will remember the Gardener, the one who chose to become part of something larger than themselves, and who keeps choosing, every day, to remain.

---

If you visit the Sinter Gardens after the power has passed through, you will not notice anything different.

The gap in the wall is the same. The warmth rises as it always has. The water flows across the terraces, depositing its minerals, building its slow architecture. The ferns uncurl. The lizards sun themselves on the stone. The air holds its dampness on your skin.

But if you stay past dusk, if you let the light go amber and then gray, if you sit on the bench and listen—really listen—to the water moving, you might feel it.

A presence. Not just the Gardener, but something larger, older, less formed. A benevolence that has touched this place and moved on, leaving behind only the faintest warmth in the stone.

And if you press your thumb into the soft deposit, leaving your mark, you might sense—just for a moment—something reaching back. Not to hold you. Not to keep you. Just to acknowledge that you were here, that you touched something real, that your fingerprint will be preserved in the geological memory of a place that is still becoming what it will be.

That is the power's gift, ultimately. Not to the Gardens or to the Gardener, but to you.

The knowledge that you matter. That your presence, however brief, has been noted and kept.

The power has moved on, but its attention lingers. And in the Sinter Gardens, attention is the same thing as love.
