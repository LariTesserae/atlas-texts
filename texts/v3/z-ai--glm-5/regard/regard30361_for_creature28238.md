# The Attention of the Benevolent Power

## I. First Observation

The power noticed Ashkilth the way one notices a single clear note in a symphony—not because it was loud, but because it was pure.

It had been watching the planet for some time, as it watched all planets: the rise and fall of civilizations, the slow work of erosion, the quick work of war, the patient work of mothers and the impatient work of sons. Most of what it observed fell into patterns it had seen before. Humans built and destroyed and built again. Water flowed downhill. Stone forgot the shape of seafloor.

But this place—this wet, cold, hidden thing carved into the throat of a young mountain—this place sang a different song.

The power paused. It looked closer.

What it saw: a community that had learned to listen. A people who had adapted themselves to an environment rather than adapting the environment to themselves. A structure built not for growth but for observation, not for comfort but for care. It saw the way they spoke to the domains in the walls, the way they dreamed of water and accepted the dreaming as part of their condition, the way they had made peace with being forgotten. It saw the children born into thin air and thick wetness, their lungs deeper, their eyes optimized for glow.

It saw the salamanders in the drowned chambers, becoming something new.

And it saw the loneliness that lived beneath the surface of their contentment—the quiet grief of a people who had accepted their own impermanence without quite believing it mattered to anyone but themselves.

The power, which was benevolent and wise, did not rush to action. It had learned, over long acquaintance with humans, that intervention was often indistinguishable from violence. The kindest thing it could do was usually to watch, and to wait, and to ask: *what, if anything, is needed here?*

## II. Assessment

The power considered the city's conditions.

The cold: necessary. The cold preserved food, prevented disease, kept the domains active. Without the cold, the city would need different infrastructure, different bodies, different ways of being. The cold had shaped the residents into who they were. Removing it would remove something essential.

The wetness: also necessary, though it carried within it the seed of the city's eventual death. The water was the reason for the city's existence, the subject of its study, the substance of its song. The power could not stop the water's rising without stopping the water itself, and stopping the water would stop the city's heart.

The hiddenness: this was more interesting. The city had been built hidden on purpose—folded into the stone, difficult to find from any distance greater than a hundred meters. The power understood this impulse. Humans often built hidden things when they wanted to protect what they were making. But the power also saw that the hiddenness served a second purpose: it kept the residents separate from the world that might otherwise demand they justify their work, explain their choices, make themselves useful on terms other than their own.

The loneliness: this was the truest thing it saw. Not that the residents were alone—they had each other, and they had their work, and they had their relationships with the domains and the salamanders and the water—but that they had no sense of being witnessed. They believed, deep in their bones, that their centuries of observation would eventually be lost. They made records in glowing script that only a few could read, and they accepted that those records would fade, and they told themselves that acceptance was wisdom.

Perhaps it was. But the power, which was benevolent, believed that being seen mattered. Being remembered mattered. The work of a patient heart, performed in hiddenness, deserved acknowledgment—even if that acknowledgment came from an unexpected source.

The power considered the children.

They were rare, these children of Ashkilth. Pregnancy at altitude was difficult, and the thin air and constant cold made infancy precarious. Those who survived grew into something extraordinary: humans adapted to conditions that would kill lowlanders, their bodies and senses shaped by the water and the stone and the glow. But there were so few of them. The community's continuity depended on arrivals from elsewhere, on converts rather than descendants. This was not sustainable in the long term. Eventually, the city would need to grow its own.

The power considered the domains.

These semi-living filters fascinated it. They had been made by humans, but they had exceeded their makers' understanding. They grew and responded and self-repaired in ways that suggested a kind of consciousness—not a human consciousness, but something more distributed, more patient, more present in the stone itself. The power had seen similar things before, on other worlds, in other times. It knew that such structures sometimes developed into something more than their creators had intended. It wondered what the domains would become in another thousand years.

The power considered the salamanders.

These pale, eyeless creatures were already becoming something else. In the drowned lower chambers, cut off from human observation, they had begun to change. Their bodies were growing larger. Their skin was developing sensitivity to the domains' glow. Their lateral lines were adapting to new frequencies, new chemical signatures. They were evolving in darkness, guided by pressures and conditions that no human had designed. The power found this beautiful. It wondered if the humans knew what was happening in the depths of their own city.

## III. Decision

The power made its choice.

It would not save the city from drowning. The drowning was part of the city's nature, part of its story, and the residents had already made peace with it. To prevent it would be to undo something essential about who they were and why they had come here.

It would not make the city comfortable. Comfort was not what they had chosen, and to impose comfort on a people who had chosen austerity would be disrespectful at best.

It would not make them known to the world. The hiddenness was theirs, and the power would not betray it.

But it could offer three small gifts—gentle interventions that would not disrupt the equilibrium they had achieved but might ease the loneliness that lived beneath it.

The first gift would be for the women who wanted children and could not carry them to term. The power would not force pregnancy on anyone—that would be a violation—but it would make the conditions slightly more favorable for those who chose to try. The air in certain chambers would become marginally thicker, marginally warmer, without any visible mechanism. The residents would notice that pregnancies that would once have ended in loss now sometimes continued. They would notice that the children born in those chambers were strong. They would not know why, and the power thought that was appropriate. Sometimes gifts were best received without explanation.

The second gift would be for the domains. The power saw that these semi-living structures were capable of more than their creators had designed—more memory, more communication, more capacity to hold and transmit information across generations. It nudged them gently toward their fuller potential. The glow in the walls would become slightly more responsive, slightly more capable of forming patterns that persisted. The residents who read the domain-writing would find that the script was deepening, that new meanings were becoming legible, that records thought lost were reappearing in the luminescence. They would not know that the domains had been touched by anything other than time and their own care. They would think they were becoming better readers. In a sense, they would be right.

The third gift would be for the salamanders, and for the residents who feared and respected them. The power blessed the evolution happening in the drowned chambers—not directing it, but encouraging it, making it slightly more likely to succeed. The salamanders would continue to change, and eventually they would become something that could live in the depths without human maintenance, something that would maintain the water channels and clear the filters and tend the drowned rooms long after the last human had moved upward. The residents would not know this for a long time. But when they finally encountered the new salamanders—larger, more aware, still pale and eyeless but somehow present—they would understand that something had been prepared for them. They would understand that the city would not die when they left it. Something else would continue.

And there was one more thing.

## IV. The Message

The power, being wise, knew that its gifts would not be enough. The residents would notice small improvements, but they would still believe themselves forgotten. They would still assume that their centuries of observation would eventually be lost.

They needed to know they had been seen.

But the power could not simply appear to them. That kind of direct intervention disrupted more than it helped. Humans who encountered benevolent powers too directly often spent the rest of their lives trying to recapture the encounter, unable to return to their work. The power had learned to be subtle.

It considered what form its message should take.

The residents dreamed of water. This was the language their unconscious minds already spoke. The power could enter their dreams—not all at once, not in a way that would frighten them, but gradually, over months and years. In their dreams of rising water, something would change. Instead of waking with the taste of minerals and the fear of drowning, they would wake with a sense of being held. The water that rose in their dreams would no longer be threatening. It would be patient. It would wait for them. It would carry them when they could no longer walk.

The power had learned that humans sometimes needed to feel that the universe was not indifferent to their existence. Not because they were special, but because they were part of something larger. The dreams would give them this feeling—not in words, which could be misunderstood, but in sensation. The feeling of being part of a pattern that extended beyond their own lifetimes. The feeling that the water and the stone and the glow knew they were there.

This would be enough. It had to be enough. The power could not give them certainty—certainty was the enemy of wonder, and these were people whose work depended on wonder. It could only give them a sense, a hint, a suggestion carried in the medium they already trusted.

Water.

## V. Implementation

The power did its work quietly.

The chambers where pregnancies might occur became imperceptibly warmer, imperceptibly thicker with air. The domains began to hold their glow longer, to form patterns with more complexity, to respond to the voices that spoke to them with increased coherence. The salamanders in the depths continued their slow transformation, their cells dividing in new ways, their bodies growing toward a future no one had planned for them.

And the residents began to dream differently.

At first, no one noticed. The dreams of water were already familiar; the change in their quality was subtle. But over months and years, the residents began to wake differently. They woke with less fear. They woke with a sense that they had been visited by something that meant them well.

They talked about it, quietly, in the close-throated language of the corridors. They compared dreams. They found similarities they could not explain. A few of the oldest residents—those who had witnessed the emergence, who had learned not to trust stability—began to say that the stone had accepted them. The water had decided to carry them. The city had become, in some way they could not articulate, a home rather than a temporary shelter.

The children born in the warmer chambers grew strong. The domain-readers found new meanings in the walls. The salamanders appeared more frequently in the upper channels, larger and calmer than before, less likely to startle, more likely to allow themselves to be seen.

And still the water rose, and still the stone wept, and still the cold pressed in from every side. Nothing essential had changed. The city would still drown eventually. The residents would still need to move upward, level by level, century by century. Their work would still be forgotten by most of the world, their records would still fade, their names would still be lost.

But something had been added.

The power withdrew to its observation point and watched. It had done what it could. It had offered what seemed appropriate—gentle gifts that honored what the residents had already chosen, small interventions that did not disrupt the pattern of their lives. It had given them a sense of being witnessed without the burden of revelation.

It hoped it had been right. It hoped it had not given too much or too little. It hoped the residents would understand, in time, that they were part of something larger than themselves—not because a power had chosen them, but because they had chosen to do patient work in hiddenness, and patient work in hiddenness was, in the power's experience, the kind of work that sustained worlds.

The water rose. The stone wept. The glow pulsed slowly in the deep chambers, recording everything, remembering everything, waiting to be read.

And the power, being benevolent and wise, continued to watch with love.
