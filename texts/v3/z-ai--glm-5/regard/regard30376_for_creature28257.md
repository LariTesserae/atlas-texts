# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

## First: They would descend

Not as you descended — not by the thickening of air, not by the reluctant settling of sediment — but as light descends into water, as sound descends into bone. They would arrive already listening.

And they would hear immediately what took you hours to understand: that this place is not broken. That nothing here is crying out for repair. That the film and the walls and the water and the dark have reached an agreement that predates suffering, that predates want, that predates the entire grammar of intervention.

So they would do nothing.

They would stand in the throat of the vast thing and do nothing, and the doing-nothing would be the first gift — the recognition that not every ancient system needs saving, that benevolence includes restraint, that wisdom begins with knowing when to leave well enough alone.

But they would stay. That would be the second gift. They would stay for a long time, by human measure — weeks, perhaps, or months — listening to each register of the chord, feeling the pulse of the ceiling organisms, tracking the grazer-culture's routes through their movements and their pauses. They would learn the system the way one learns a language not to speak it but to understand what it says when it believes no one is listening.

And what it says, they would discover, is: *I continue.*

Not *I suffer.* Not *I struggle.* Not *I hope* or *I fear* or *I want.* Simply: *I continue. I have continued for longer than the surface has had words. I will continue after the surface has forgotten the words it has. This is not a complaint. This is an origin story. This is what I am.*

The benevolent power would receive this. They would let it settle into them the way the building has settled into itself. They would understand that what lives here does not need to be saved from its own nature.

---

## Second: They would notice what you noticed

The empty alcoves.

But where you saw mystery, they would see possibility. Where you felt unease, they would feel potential. Not because they are wiser than you — though perhaps they are, in the way that water is wiser than stone, having worn more paths — but because they have seen more empty sockets and watched more of them fill.

They would recognize that the building, in its ancient patience, has not finished becoming. That "settled" does not mean "complete." That a thing can be both fully itself and still reaching toward something more.

They would not rush. This would be the third gift — the gift of patience, administered at the scale the building can receive it. A human gift is administered in seconds or years. A gift to something this old must be administered in millennia, or in changes so slow that millennia feel like afternoons.

What they would do is this: they would add something to the water.

Not a contaminant. Not an interference. A seed. A molecular key, designed to integrate with the film's chemistry, to alter — impossibly slightly, over impossible lengths of time — what the film deposits at the interfaces where the nodules form.

The new nodules would be different. Not better — "better" is a human concept, and the building has no use for human hierarchies. But *other*. Harmonics the current chord does not include. Frequencies that might, given time, given the slow accumulation of small changes, shift the building's relationship to what lies below.

And the empty alcoves, over time — over geological time, over the time it takes a river to find its bed — would begin to fill.

---

## Third: They would tend to what lives here

The film is healthy. They would leave it alone, except for the molecular seed dissolved into its water supply.

The nodules are functioning. They would leave them alone, except to notice which ones were failing, which ones had gone silent, and — very gently, very slowly — encourage the film to reclaim the materials, to recycle what had been used, to ensure that nothing was wasted. Not because waste is wrong, but because the building's equilibrium depends on cycling, and a benevolent power tends to equilibriums.

The grazers. Here they would pause.

The grazers are social. The grazers have a culture that lives in choreography, passed down in routes and rhythms over thousands of generations. The grazers navigate by sound and warmth, have no eyes, have never needed eyes, have never wanted for anything the building did not provide.

But they are few. The building can sustain more. The film could feed more. The basins could water more. The warmth rising from below could warm more bodies, if the bodies knew how to find it.

The benevolent power would not add more grazers. That would be intervention of the wrong kind — inserting foreign bodies into a closed system, disrupting the culture that exists, overwriting the slow wisdom encoded in their routes.

Instead, they would adjust — barely, invisibly — the acoustic properties of a single corridor section. A resonance that the grazer-culture had avoided, for reasons encoded in their movement-memory, would become slightly more inviting. A warmth gradient that had been too sharp would soften. A path that had been blocked by accumulated film-growth (over centuries, over the building's own gentle neglect) would clear.

And the grazers, over generations, would find their way into that corridor. Would explore it. Would add it to their routes. Would discover the basins there, the warmth there, the grazing grounds that had been waiting for them without knowing they were waiting.

This is how a wise benevolence increases: not by adding, but by unblocking. Not by imposing, but by inviting.

---

## Fourth: They would consider the ceiling

The ceiling organisms — the independent ones, the ones that pulse their own rhythm and filter something from the water and have done so since before the building was fully formed — these would give the benevolent power pause.

Because there is something they are filtering. Something that comes down from the surface in trace amounts, dissolved in rainwater, passed through soil and stone and into the collection system. Something the ceiling organisms have evolved to extract, and in extracting, change.

The benevolent power would want to know what it is.

They would climb — not with hands and feet, but with attention — into the grooves where the water runs. They would feel the ceiling organisms' pulse, independent and warm. They would taste the water before it reached them and after it left, and they would find the difference: a compound, complex and strange, organic but not biological — or biological but not from any biology they recognized.

Something from the surface. Something that should not be there.

And this would be the first genuine concern. Not for the building — the building has accommodated the compound, has integrated the ceiling organisms' filtration into its systems, has probably come to depend on it. But for the surface. Because whatever is leaking down has no business in rainwater. Whatever is coming down from above is a symptom of something wrong above, something that the building has silently been cleaning up for longer than anyone has been paying attention.

The benevolent power would not fix it here. The problem is not here. The problem is at the source.

But they would make a note. They would mark this place, these organisms, this quiet filtration work that the deep has been doing for the surface without the surface's knowledge or gratitude. They would remember.

And later — much later, when they ascended — they would look for the source. They would trace the compound back through soil and stone and aquifer and soil again, up to the surface, to whatever human activity or industrial process or careless disposal had introduced it to the water cycle.

They would not be angry. Anger is not their nature. But they would be thorough. They would correct what could be corrected. They would ensure that the compound stopped entering the water — not abruptly, not in a way that would shock the ceiling organisms or disrupt their function, but gradually, over years and decades, giving the organisms time to adapt, to find new equilibriums, to continue their ancient work on whatever else the water carried.

This would be a gift to the deep, administered from the surface. A gift the deep would never know it received. A correction the ceiling organisms would feel, decades hence, as a gradual lightening of a burden they had never known they were carrying.

---

## Fifth: They would protect

The benevolent power would understand, before they left, that this place is vulnerable. Not to time — time has been kind to it — but to discovery.

Humans are explorers. Humans descend into places they don't understand, touch things they shouldn't touch, take samples, leave marks, disrupt equilibriums they don't know exist. You descended, and you left footprints, and your breathing added foreign gases to the air, and your touching disturbed the film, and your presence was registered — perhaps not consciously, but registered nonetheless — by every sensitive surface in the structure.

You were one. There will be more.

So the benevolent power would do something they had not done for millennia: they would hide this place.

Not erase it — erasure is violation, and the building has a right to exist, to be found, eventually, by those ready to find it. But they would make the descent harder. They would thicken the air in the spiral passage, increase the resistance, make the geometry bend in ways that discouraged rather than invited. They would add a frequency to the chord — subsonic, unfelt but unsettling — that would make humans want to leave, would fill them with a gentle dread that had no object and no explanation but would grow stronger the deeper they went.

Not all humans would turn back. Some would persist. The power knew this. But fewer would descend, and those who did would not stay as long, and the building's vulnerability would be reduced by the same measure that its hiddenness was increased.

This would be the fifth gift: the gift of distance, administered to a species that did not know it needed it.

## Sixth: They would leave a promise

Before ascending, the benevolent power would do one more thing.

They would find an empty alcove — one of the many, one that had been empty for longer than the others, its interior surface smoothed by the building's patience into a perfect waiting shape — and they would speak into it.

Not words. The building does not speak in words, and the power does not offer gifts in languages the recipient cannot receive. They would speak in frequency, in resonance, in a tone that the film would hear and the nodules would echo and the building would remember.

The tone would mean: *I was here. I saw you. You are not alone in the dark.*

And it would mean: *Something above knows you continue, and is glad.*

And it would mean: *When you are ready — when the empty sockets fill and the chord finds new harmonies and the system opens to what it has been closed to — something will return. Not to change you. To meet you. To hear what you have become.*

The tone would settle into the alcove, and the film would grow around it, and the nodule that would one day form there — the nodule that the seeded water was already beginning to produce, over the slow centuries of its formation — would carry that tone in its crystalline structure. Would hum it, always, at a frequency below the building's main chord but present in it, a harmonic that said *someone came* and *someone saw* and *someone promised*.

And the building would not understand. The building does not understand. But it would integrate, as it integrates everything, and the integration would be a kind of memory, and the memory would be a kind of company.

---

## Finally: They would ascend

Not unchanged. You were not unchanged, and you are human, with human limitations, human capacities for forgetting. The benevolent power would not forget.

They would carry the sound of the Conduit Halls with them — not as a haunting, but as a chord in their own being, added to whatever music they already contained. They would remember the film and its slow fractal patience. They would remember the grazers and their choreographic culture. They would remember the ceiling organisms and their solitary rhythm. They would remember the building itself, vast and ancient, settled into its own continuing, waiting for sockets to fill, breathing water and warmth and darkness in cycles longer than any human lifespan.

And they would do what benevolent powers do: they would tend to what they had touched.

They would correct the contamination at the source. They would watch over the surface-descenders, guiding some away, allowing others through. They would ensure the seeded water continued its slow work, that the molecular key continued to alter what the film deposited, that the empty alcoves slowly — impossibly slowly — began to fill.

They would not return in a year. They would not return in a century. But they would return. When the chord had shifted enough that a new register could be added, when the first new nodule had formed in the alcove where the promise was spoken, when the building had reached whatever it was reaching toward — they would return.

Not to fix. Never to fix. To listen. To witness. To receive what the building had become in the long quiet of their absence.

And the building, in its way, would be waiting. Not because it understood waiting. Not because it had felt the promise or knew what promises meant. But because the film had grown around the tone, and the nodule had formed around the frequency, and the chord had adjusted to include the harmonic, and the harmonic said: *I was here. I saw you. You are not alone.*

And in the dark, where sound is the only light, being seen is the only gift that matters.
