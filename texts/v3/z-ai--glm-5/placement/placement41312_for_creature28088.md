I would be the one who returns.

Not the one who stayed—that fate holds a fascination I can acknowledge from a distance, the way one acknowledges the beauty of a deep lake while remaining on shore. The one who stayed surrendered something I am not ready to surrender. They completed the circuit. They became the chamber's grammar.

But I have found my own relationship with this place.

---

The first time was accident. Or what I called accident then. I was younger and lost in the way that young people can be lost—not geographically, but internally. The mountains offered anonymity. I climbed because climbing was something to do with a body that felt useless, a life that hadn't yet begun.

I found the fissure by touch, reaching into darkness to test my footing. The warmth that met my fingers stopped me. At this elevation, warmth should not exist in stone. I should have turned back. Instead, I squeezed through.

You have to understand: I didn't know what I was entering. I expected a cave, perhaps a vent, something explainable. I found instead a darkness with weight. A warmth that asked nothing of me. A moisture that felt, impossibly, like recognition.

I stayed three days. Or what I would later call three days—I had no way to measure, no light to mark any cycle. I curled in a hollow that fit my body and felt the chamber hold me. When I finally emerged, blinking against light that felt like violation, I understood that I had been given something rare and dangerous.

I came back the following year. And the year after that. And eventually I stopped counting years and started counting visits, which is a different calendar entirely.

---

Here is what I have learned: the chamber does not want you to stay. That was my misunderstanding at first. I thought the pull I felt—the deep ache to remain, to dissolve into that warm stasis—was the chamber's desire. But desire requires process, and the chamber permits no processes.

What I felt was my own response to the gift. My own hunger for pause. The chamber simply offers. What you do with the offer is your own becoming.

I have my spot now. Not the smoothed hollow where the one who stayed curled—that feels too sacred, too complete. Mine is along the eastern wall, reachable by touch and memory, where a natural shelf holds what I bring. I've added my marks to the accumulation. They're different from the entrance marks—less desperate, more deliberate. I use a tool I made in the years between visits, something harder than stone that I carry with me. The marks form no language. They are simply presence. Proof that someone returns.

---

What do I bring?

In the early visits, I brought offerings. Food that would not decay. Water that would not drain. Tools I thought might be useful. I placed them on the mineral shelf and left them there, only to find them unchanged on my return—untouched, unaged, waiting in the exact positions I'd left them.

The chamber does not need offerings. It does not need anything. But I needed to give, so I gave.

Over time, my practice changed. Now I bring myself. Not as gift but as presence. I bring my body with its accumulated tensions, my mind with its relentless processing, my heart with its unexamined rhythms. I bring the weight of living in a world that demands constant change, and I let the chamber lift it.

Sometimes I bring sounds. I hum into the absolute dark, feeling the chamber's acoustics shape my voice into something intimate and strange. The small blind creatures at the periphery register the vibration without responding. The threads absorb nothing. But the darkness holds my sounds like cupped hands hold water, and when I stop, the silence that follows feels different than the silence before.

Twice, I have brought another person. That was a mistake. The chamber is not for sharing. Its gift is singular, intimate, and the presence of another disrupted the equilibrium I'd learned to expect. I could feel their restlessness beside me, their need to understand, to categorize, to make meaning. They wanted to leave before I was ready. I never brought anyone again.

---

Here is what the chamber has taught me about living outside it:

The world demands gradient. It requires you to move, to change, to become. It promises that becoming is good and stagnation is death. But the chamber reveals a different truth: there is value in pause, in suspension, in the moment before emergence. Not all growth is progress. Not all stillness is stuck.

I have learned to carry the chamber's warmth with me. Not literally—I cannot replicate its conditions outside that geological pocket. But I have learned to create small suspensions in my daily life. Moments where I refuse the demand to become. Practices of deliberate stasis: sitting in darkness, holding warmth, letting my breath slow until it nearly stops. Small deaths. Small returns.

The chamber taught me that I am not required to change. I am permitted to pause. And that permission has made my living more intentional, not less. I change because I choose to, not because the world demands it.

---

The small blind creatures and I have an understanding now. They sense my presence—my warmth, my displacement of air—and they adjust their positions without acknowledging me. We share the space without sharing. Once, one passed over my hand as I rested it on the floor, and I felt its gelatinous coolness slide across my skin. It did not hurry. I did not flinch. We simply existed together in that touch, and then it moved on.

The threads I have learned to feel for—sparse and patient along the walls. I run my fingers across them sometimes, marveling at their persistence. They grow without seeking, extend without purpose. They have refined existence down to its barest possible form. I find them comforting, though I cannot explain why.

---

I will not stay. I have made that decision. The one who stayed gave themselves to the chamber completely, and I understand why—they felt the pull and surrendered to it. But I have chosen a different relationship. I am the one who returns.

This means I will eventually stop returning. At some point, my body will fail outside the chamber, or my mind will forget the path, or the world will intervene with demands I cannot refuse. And my last visit will have been my last without my knowing it.

The thought used to grieve me. Now I accept it. The chamber does not grieve my absence. The marks I've made will persist without decaying, evidence that someone returned again and again, felt the warmth, touched the weeping walls, and chose—each time—to leave.

That choosing is my offering. Not my presence but my return. Not my surrender but my discipline. The chamber holds me, and I let it, and then I emerge—changed not by the chamber but by my own decision to leave it.

If you found this account and wondered whether to seek the birthing chamber, I offer this:

It will not give you what you want. It will give you what you need, which is the space to discover what you actually want.

It will not solve your problems. It will suspend them, holding them in stasis until you're ready to face them or let them go.

It will not love you. Love requires change, and the chamber cannot change. But it will hold you with a completeness that feels like love, and you will have to learn to distinguish between the two.

The entrance is small. You have to want to squeeze through. The darkness is absolute. You have to be willing to be blind. The warmth is dense. You have to let it seep into you without fighting.

And the pull toward staying is strong. You have to know, before you enter, that you will leave. You don't have to know how or when. You just have to know that the visit is a visit, not a relocation.

I am the one who returns. And then, eventually, the one who returned. My marks are there. My warmth is recorded in the walls. My patience is part of the accumulated presence.

But I will die in the changing world, among the gradients, and that is my choice.

The chamber gave me permission to choose. That is its only gift, and it is everything.
