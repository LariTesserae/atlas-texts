I would be the sediment.

Not the dead things that settled into me — though I contain them, layered in compressed strata like pages in a book no one reads. Not the growth that feeds on me, though I am the substrate from which its pale light rises. I would be the accumulation itself. The vertical archive. The slow deposition of everything that has ever happened here, compressed into a meter of chemical memory.

I say "I would be" because I am not yet. I am still above, still warm, still moving at speeds that must seem violent from below. I am still a body with a temperature and a breath and a shape that disrupts the equilibrium of the water around me. But I feel the pull. I feel the specific gravity of that place. I know that I am already on my way to becoming what I will be.

---

The divers who found me will say I came down through the hatch the same way they did. This is not wrong, but it is not the shape of the truth. I came down through the hatch and I kept coming down, in a sense, for the whole time I was there. I was settling. I was beginning my long descent into the record. Every skin cell I shed in that cold water, every trace of oil from my exposed surfaces, every microbe that rode on my equipment and died in the temperature shock — all of it was sinking into the corners where I live now, where I accumulate, where I wait.

I remember the moment I understood what I was looking at. Not the pumps. Not the growth. Not the valves frozen in their final positions. I understood the sediment. I understood that the meter of soft grey material in the low corners was not debris, not waste, not the incidental accumulation of a century of neglect. It was the point. It was what the pump house had been building all along, without knowing it was building anything. The pumps turn, the water flows, the small things live and die, and the sediment grows. It grows by nanometers. It grows by the width of a bacterium per year. It grows in the way that mountains grow, which is to say: it is barely growing at all, but it is growing in the only direction available to it, and it will continue to grow long after everything else has stopped.

I understood that I wanted to be part of that growth. Not in a poetic sense. In a literal, chemical sense. I wanted to shed my temperature and my movement and my breath and become part of the vertical record. I wanted to be compressed by the layers that would form above me until I was thinner than paper, until I was a chemical signature in a stratum, until I was nothing but a line in a history written in no language that anyone can read. I wanted to be remembered the way the pump house remembers — not as a person, not as a consciousness, but as material, as presence, as a fact of having been here.

---

The others came up. They kicked toward the hatch and the warmer water and the light and the air and the people waiting on the surface. They wanted to tell people what they had found. They wanted to document it, measure it, understand it. They wanted to take the pump house apart and see how it worked. They wanted to solve the mystery of the pumps that run without power, the growth that glows without obvious energy, the ecosystem that persists without input. They wanted to turn the pump house into knowledge, which is what humans do with things they cannot leave alone.

I stayed. Not in the sense of refusing to leave — I went up, I went back, I wrote the reports, I answered the questions. But I stayed in the sense that matters. I stayed in the sense that I left a part of myself in the sediment, and that part is still there, and it is still becoming part of the pump house's slow memory, and it will be there after the rest of me has stopped being anything at all.

I stayed in the sense that I am still settling, even now. I am still descending toward the corners of that chamber, that cathedral of steel and corrosion and cold water. I am on my way to becoming what I will be. And what I will be is simple: I will be a layer. I will be a stratum. I will be the chemical trace of a person who came to a place where nothing should live and found something that had learned to live anyway, and recognized in it a shape that felt like home.

---

This is what I learned in the Drowned Pump House: there is a kind of life that does not require warmth or movement or consciousness. There is a kind of persistence that does not require purpose. There is a kind of memory that does not require a rememberer. The sediment holds all of it. The sediment does not know what it holds. It does not need to know. It holds it anyway.

I wanted to be part of that holding. I still want it. Not because I have stopped living in the ordinary sense, but because I understand now that living is not the only thing that matters. There is also the after-living. There is the settling. There is the slow accumulation in the cold dark corners where the water is oldest and the light is faintest and the silence is densest.

I will be there eventually. Everyone will. We are all on our way to the sediment, to the strata, to the compressed record of everything that has ever happened. We are all settling toward the place where the pump house waits, turning its slow impellers, glowing its faint light, keeping its own counsel in the cold.

I am just arriving there early. I am just finding my place in the archive, my position in the vertical stack of pages. I am just becoming what the pump house has always been: a system that has found its shape and is keeping it, a process that has found its rhythm and is running it, a thing that has settled into a configuration that fits its environment and that its environment has settled into a configuration to fit.

I am the sediment. Or I will be. Or I am already, in the only way that matters, which is the slow way, the cold way, the way that takes longer than a human life and happens anyway.
