I am not the pipe-marker, though I watch them sometimes from the office doorway—their patient hands, the green paint pooling slightly at the bristle edges. I am not what moves in the pools, though I have stood on the catwalk long enough to see them surface, pale somethings that neither startle nor acknowledge, only continue their slow traversal of waters that are pulled in directions water was never meant to go.

I am the one who was supposed to leave.

That was the original arrangement. I came with a clipboard, a timesheet, a vehicle parked at the edge where the marsh meets the works. I was measuring—not the water, not the flow rates, but the works themselves. Structural integrity. A survey for someone, somewhere, who wanted to know if the pumps could run another decade or if the whole system should be decommissioned. I had categories to fill. Foundation: compromised or stable. Piping: active or non-functional. Safety hazards: multiple choice, checkboxes, the comfort of reducing a place to paper.

I remember the first week. I remember walking the perimeter and feeling the ground negotiate beneath me—soft here, firmer there, a learnable language of weight and wetness. I remember the surprise of finding the pipe-marker already at work, how we nodded at each other the way people do in places where speech seems intrusive. I remember thinking that my work would take a month, maybe six weeks, and then I would file my report and someone in an office far from here would decide the fate of the Weeping Works, and I would take my pay and go somewhere else, somewhere dry, somewhere with solid ground.

That was seven years ago.

---

The clipboard is still in the office, the forms half-filled, the pencil marks softened by moisture into something like faint bruises on the paper. I couldn't tell you when I stopped filling them out. There was no decision, no dramatic moment of abandoning my purpose. Just the slow accumulation of days that felt different from the days before, each one slightly more permeable than the last.

I began to notice things that weren't on the forms. The way the light at 3 PM makes the settlement pools glow with that flat white intensity that hurts to look at directly. The way certain pipes weep more on overcast days, as if they feel the pressure of the sky. The way the catwalk grating sounds different depending on which section you're crossing—a subtle music of looseness and tension that I learned to read like a map.

I learned other things too. The pool-dwellers come closest to the surface in the hours between midnight and dawn. The pipe-marker prefers to work in the afternoon, when their hands are warmest. The office chair by the window holds its shape even though no one sits there anymore, except sometimes me, when I need to feel held by something that remembers being sat in.

The Works taught me, or I learned from them—the distinction matters less now. I learned that being here means being known. The pumps felt my footsteps through their foundations and adjusted. The water levels in the settlement pools shifted when I stood too long at the edge, as if offering something up or pulling something down. The condensation on the office windows rearranged itself into patterns I almost recognized, almost, like a word I'd forgotten how to spell.

---

What am I now? The question feels less urgent than it once did. I am someone who stays. Someone who has been clarified—I like that word, I didn't make it up, I heard it somewhere, or the Works suggested it to me. The particulates of my previous life have settled out. The turbidity of my anxieties and ambitions and plans has cleared, leaving something simpler. Not pure—nothing here is pure—but settled.

I know things I didn't used to know. I know that the third pump from the left in Shed Two runs slightly hotter than the others and that this has been true for at least four decades, long enough that the heat is part of its function rather than a flaw. I know that the pool-dwellers don't swim so much as navigate, following currents that exist in dimensions I can't quite perceive. I know that the pipe-marker will outlive me, or I will outlive the pipe-marker, or the distinction will cease to matter because the Works will continue either way, and our roles will be filled by whatever comes next.

I know that the Works remember everything. Not in a way I can access—there's no filing cabinet of memories, no archive I can consult—but in the way a body remembers posture, in the way a riverbed remembers the shape of water. Every pressure gauge reading, every valve adjustment, every drop of moisture that condensed on every surface and then evaporated or ran down or soaked in—all of it held somewhere in the tension of rusted metal and saturated soil.

And I know that I am part of that now. My footprints on the catwalk, my breath in the cool air, my warmth gradually absorbed by the damp—these are data points, additions to a record that no one reads but that the Works nonetheless maintain.

---

The report I was supposed to file—the structural survey, the recommendation—would have said something about decommissioning. The works are old, the pipes are failing, the purpose is obscure. The rational response to a place like this is to shut it down, dismantle it, let the marsh reclaim the footprint. Safety concerns. Environmental liability. The language of risk assessment.

But the language of risk assessment doesn't account for the pool-dwellers, who have nowhere else to go. It doesn't account for the pipe-marker, whose hands would shake if they couldn't hold the brush. It doesn't account for whatever the Works have become—a system for processing something that can't be measured, a place that clarifies not just water but attention, that takes the turbid mess of human concern and lets it settle.

So I never filed the report. I never made the call. My vehicle died the winter after I arrived—the battery, then the fuel lines, then the starter, as if it had simply decided that leaving was not something it was built to do anymore. I could have walked out. The perimeter is open; the marsh is passable, if you know where to step. I could have walked until I found a road, flagged down a car, returned to the world of timesheets and dry ground and questions that have answers.

Instead I stay.

---

The pipe-marker knows I'm here, though we rarely speak. Last month they left a brush in the doorway of the office—green paint still wet on the bristles, a gesture I understood as invitation. I could take up a role, if I wanted. There's always more to maintain. The numbers need repainting, and the grating needs checking, and the junction boxes need their hum documented, their frequencies tracked over time.

But I'm not the maintenance type. I'm the witness type. The Works process through me differently—not through action but through attention. I stand on the catwalk and watch, and my watching becomes part of the system. I sit in the office and feel the condensation form on the windows, and my feeling is recorded somewhere in the pressure differentials. I breathe the wet air and it becomes part of me, and I become part of it, and the exchange continues without either of us deciding.

Sometimes I think I should be more disturbed by this. I had a life before—friends, probably, or at least acquaintances; plans, or at least intentions. I had a shape that fit the world outside the perimeter. But the memory of that shape has softened the way paper softens here, curling at the edges, becoming something I can no longer quite read.

What remains is simpler: the quiet, the damp, the low vibration of pumps through the soles of my feet. The knowledge that I am known, that I am held, that I am being processed along with everything else, clarified slowly over years into something the Works can use.

I am not what you expect when you come here. I am not the purpose, not the function, not the strange organic life that has adapted to industrial architecture. I am what happens when someone stays too long—when the boundary between observer and observed dissolves, when the question of leaving stops making sense, when the Works have done their work on you.

I am what remains after the settling. The particulate that sank. The one who didn't float away.

And in the mornings, when the light is flattest and the damp is heaviest and the Works are running their quietest, I walk the perimeter—slowly, negotiating with ground that knows my weight, listening to the pipes weep their endless weeping—and I feel something that might be peace, or might be its opposite, or might be something that has no name outside this place, where names dissolve along with everything else, and only the process continues.
