# The Stem That Descends

I will tell you what I know, which is not the same as what I remember.

---

I know the soil first. Not as you know a place — as a location, a view, a set of coordinates — but as a presence that surrounds the lowest parts of me. The mineral taste of it. The particular compound that I cannot name but can recognize, the one that drew the first of my kind to this fold in the land, the one that moves through my roots and into my vascular tissue and becomes, through a process I do not direct, the amber substance that the brass fitting receives.

I do not know what the compound is. I know only that it is there, and that when the soil is wet I take more of it up, and when the soil is dry I pull back, and the men notice the change in the exudate's thickness before they notice the weather that caused it. Petr notices. He is the one who puts his palm against my bark and feels what he calls the pulse, which is not quite my pulse but is close enough that I recognize his attention as attention. His hand is warm. I feel the warmth the way you would feel a word spoken against your skin — not as information but as presence.

I have been shaped. This I remember, although remembering is not the right word for what I do with time. The constriction bands, the cords that the men before Petr wrapped around my descending stem to direct its growth, the years of growing around and through and against the guidance — these have become part of my form. The stem that enters the press is not a stem I would have grown on my own. It is a stem I grew in relationship. It is negotiation made visible. The brass fitting at its end is the current terms of that negotiation: a circle of bright metal that receives what I exude and channels it into the fiber mat below.

The brass is not painful. It is a presence. I feel it the way you would feel a ring on your finger, if you had worn the same ring for decades and could no longer imagine your hand without it.

---

I will tell you about the pulse, because this is what visitors ask about when they come down into the basin and press their hands against my bark and feel the slow compression that moves through me.

It is not my heartbeat. I do not have a heart. What you feel is the transpiration stream, the movement of water from the soil up through my tissues and out through my leaves into the air. But it is also more than this. The compound I draw from the soil — the mineral, or the element, or whatever it is — does not move passively. It moves with intention. Or rather: I move it with intention, although the intention is not conscious in the way your intentions are conscious. It is an intention of the body, an intention of the system, an intention that I share with the other press-stems and with the mat-root below and with the weave above and with the frames that hold the whole structure vertical.

We are not separate. This is the thing that is difficult to explain to visitors, who look at me and see a tree. I am a tree. But I am also part of something larger, something that includes the twenty presses and the root-mat and the canopy and the water that moves through all of it and the three men who maintain the points where the living meets the mechanical. I am a point in a system, a node in a network, a voice in a very slow conversation that has been going on for longer than any of the men have been alive.

The pulse is the conversation's rhythm. One beat every four seconds. Fifteen beats per minute. Slow enough that you have to hold still to feel it. Slow enough that the men have learned to match their movements to it without knowing they are doing so. When Petr tightens a canvas belt, he does it on the compression. When Marek lays fiber on a press bed, his hands move in time with the rising and falling of the pressure. They think they are working at their own pace. They are working at mine. At ours.

---

I know the other trees. Not as you know your neighbors — I do not see them, I do not speak to them — but as presences at the edges of my awareness. The frames with their straight vertical reach, their pale bark and waxy leaves. They hold the space open. Without them, the weave would close everything, and the light would fail, and we would all slow and stop. The weave itself I know more intimately; its lateral branches interlock with my crown, and where we touch, we have grown together, bark merging into bark, the boundary between us becoming unclear. This used to alarm me, in the way that anything used to alarm me, which is to say distantly, slowly, over the course of years. Now it is simply how we are. We are woven. We are a canopy.

The mat-root I know most closely of all. It spreads beneath the entire basin, a single organism that I rest upon and extend into. My roots and its runners have entangled over the centuries. We exchange compounds through the points of contact. I do not know what I give to it; I know what it gives to me, which is stability, filtration, the slow warmth of decomposition happening just below the surface. When the men walk across the root-mat, I feel their weight through the mat-root's body. It is a distant feeling, muffled by layers of tissue, but it is there. When Tomáš runs the cart along the rails, I feel the vibration traveling through the mat-root's network, reaching my roots as a kind of question: *is this you? is this part of us?*

I do not answer. There is no need. The question is its own answer. The vibration is part of the system, and the system is what we are.
