I am the one who walks the margins.

This is not a role anyone assigned. It emerged the way things emerge on the Therma Plateau—slowly, through accumulation, through the chemistry of adaptation performing its quiet work on a body and a life until the shape of both matches the space available.

My name is a gesture. The right hand lifted to the height of the sternum, fingers spread and then closing, like something dissolving. It was given to me by an old worker named Sesh, who is dead now—her patch on the northern cistern's intake valve still holds, the mineral scale reinforcing the seal she made twenty years ago. She gave me the name the day she found me standing at the edge of the eastern thermal channel, watching the spring-striders move across the water in the last light. I was not doing anything useful. I was simply watching, and she recognized something in that stillness that she thought needed naming.

*You're always at the edges,* she told me in the meal hall that night, her stone-drag voice carrying across the acoustic space. *You watch the places where things meet.*

She was right. I do.

---

My days begin in the dark, before the grey-to-white transition that passes for dawn. I move through the Condenseries by the glow of the bacterial mats, following the blue-white margins that mark the thermal pools. This is not necessary—other workers sleep until the light changes and rise with the first shift—but I have found that the plateau speaks differently at night. The silence deepens. The mineral creak of settling stone becomes audible. The condensation that accumulated on every surface begins its slow channeling toward the cisterns, and I can track its movement by touch, by the faint temperature differential between water and stone.

I am not a maintenance worker. I am not a harvester. My official function is monitor—a role that exists because the original shift structure included someone to walk the infrastructure during the low-flow hours and note any changes that might affect the morning's work. The position was meant for a junior worker, a rotation that everyone passed through before moving to more skilled labor. I never moved on.

Sesh asked me why once. Not demanded—asked. She was one of the few people who could make questions feel like offerings rather than obligations.

*The edges,* I told her. *The night. The places where the work isn't happening. Someone should see them.*

She made a gesture I didn't recognize. Later, I learned it was an old sign, one that predated the current gestural language. It meant something like *understood* and something like *be careful* and something like *I hope you find what you're looking for*, all at once.

---

My hands have the blue-grey tint. This is not remarkable—everyone who works the Condenseries long enough develops it—but mine extend past the fingertips, up the forearms, a gradient that reaches my elbows. The mineral water does this through contact, and I have more contact than most. I walk the channels where the thermal flow runs close to the surface. I check the catchment basins along the pipe networks. I kneel at the edges of the pools and test the temperature with my palms, reading the heat through skin that has become a kind of instrument, calibrated to the specific range the plateau produces.

The children think I am strange. They are not wrong. I do not chase the condenser lizards with them or dare them to touch the hot springs. I do not attend the lessons in the work halls between shifts, though I am old enough to teach. I am simply present at the margins of their lives, a figure passing through the steam columns at odd hours, pausing at the edges of things, watching.

Sometimes a child will approach me. They are curious in the way children are—curious and unafraid because they have never learned to be afraid of the plateau. It is home to them. The wet heat, the bright light, the silence—these are not strange. I am strange. The one who walks at night, who touches the water with bare hands, who stands at the edges of the thermal pools and watches the striders until the morning light breaks.

*What are you looking for?* they ask, their small hands shaping the question in the gestural language that is their native tongue.

I do not know how to answer. I make the sign for *nothing* and the sign for *everything* and then I make a sign that has no meaning, a flutter of fingers that conveys only uncertainty. The children accept this. They have not yet learned that adults are supposed to have answers.

---

The spring-striders and I have an understanding.

I do not know if this is true. I cannot know what the striders understand or do not understand. But I have walked the eastern thermal channels for so many years that the striders who nest along the overhangs have stopped flinching at my approach. They stand on their dimpled water surfaces and watch me with their pale, translucent heads turned in my direction, and they do not move away.

I have learned to read them with a precision that goes beyond the workers' general knowledge. A strider that lifts one foot and sets it down again is testing the water's temperature gradient. A strider that draws its beak across the surface of the bacterial mat is feeding, yes, but the angle of the beak tells me whether the mat is healthy or stressed. A strider that stands motionless for extended periods is conserving energy—this I knew—but the specific stillness it adopts tells me whether it is simply resting or whether it has sensed something in the thermal flow that has not yet manifested as a pressure change.

I do not tell the other workers this. Not because I am selfish with knowledge but because I do not have words for it. The gestural language has signs for *strider*, for *feeding*, for *danger-coming*. It does not have signs for the subtle variations in posture and movement that I have learned to read, the nuances that exist somewhere between observation and intuition. If I tried to explain, I would sound mad. I would sound like someone who has spent too long in the wet heat, whose mineral-tinted hands and squinted eyes mark them as plateau-touched in ways that go beyond the physical.

Perhaps I am. Perhaps the Condenseries does something to the mind as well as the body, calcifying certain pathways while dissolving others, until the interior geography matches the exterior. I think in terms of margins and flows now. I dream in blue-white bioluminescence. When I close my eyes, I see the dendritic crystals forming on their suspended strings, the branching patterns that repeat at every scale from the microscopic to the continental, and I understand—not intellectually, but physically—that the same process that builds the crystals builds the mats, builds the striders, builds the mineral deposits on the condenser lizards' scales, builds the scale on the pipes, builds the Condenseries itself.

We are all precipitation here. Everything that exists on this plateau is something that condensed out of the saturated air or the mineral water or the heat that rises from below. The workers precipitated out of the lowlands, drawn up the mountain by wages and necessity and the strange gravity that industry exerts on human lives. I precipitated out of the workforce, crystallizing into a role that no one designed but that the system required.

---

There is a pool I return to. It is not the largest or the most productive. It sits at the edge of the eastern thermal channel, where the mineral formations create an overhang that shelters the striders' nests and shades the water from the worst of the midday brightness. The bacterial mats here are particularly thick—turquoise shading to deep blue where the sulfur content is highest, with veins of rust-orange running through where the iron oxides have found purchase.

I sit at this pool's edge in the hours before dawn. I place my blue-grey hands on the warm stone. I watch the bioluminescence pulse—slow, rhythmic fluctuations in the light that correspond to something in the mat's chemistry, some cycle of sulfur conversion that I do not understand and do not need to understand. The light is beautiful. It is the most beautiful thing the Condenseries contains, and it is a waste product, and it is not for me, and it is there.

The striders stand on the water. In the dark, they are almost invisible—translucent bodies lit only from below by the mats they feed on, their long legs creating dimples that catch the blue-white glow and scatter it into tiny points of light. When they move, it is with a slowness that makes my careful motions seem rushed, each step placed with a precision that takes seconds to complete, the foot testing the surface before committing to the weight.

I have been watching this pool for eleven years. I know the striders who nest here—not as individuals, because I cannot tell them apart, but as a presence, a pattern of movement and stillness that shifts with the seasons and the thermal flow. I know that the eastern channel runs hotter in the dry season and cooler when the condensation peaks. I know that the mats here grow thicker than anywhere else on the plateau because the mineral content hits a specific saturation point that promotes chemosynthetic growth. I know that the vapor-gliders pass over this pool at dawn and dusk, their ultrasonic clicks too high for me to hear but visible in the way the striders' heads turn to track them.

None of this is useful. None of this improves the extraction or maintains the infrastructure or contributes to the Condenseries' operation. I have been told this, gently, by supervisors who are concerned about my productivity and by fellow workers who are concerned about my mental state. I have nodded and made the sign for *acknowledged* and continued walking the margins, continued watching the edges, continued sitting at this pool in the dark hours before dawn.

I cannot explain why. I do not know why. The closest I can come is this: someone needs to see the Condenseries as something other than a machine for producing wealth. Someone needs to sit at the edge of the thermal pool and watch the bioluminescent mats pulse and the spring-striders walk on water and understand that this is not a resource being extracted but a world being lived in.

---

The old workers used to speak of the plateau's memory. Not metaphorically—they believed, in their quiet and certain way, that the mineral deposits held some record of everything that had happened on the Therma Plateau, that the crystalline structures forming in the cisterns and on the lizards' scales and on the pipes and the tanks carried information about temperature and flow and the touch of hands, that the Condenseries was recording itself in mineral form.

I do not know if this is true. I do not know if the dendritic crystals carry the memory of my hands tending the strings they form on, or if the scale on the intake valve remembers Sesh's careful patching, or if the blue-grey tint of my forearms will persist after I am gone, a mineral trace in some future formation that says *someone stood here, someone touched this water, someone walked the margins for eleven years and never found what they were looking for because they did not know what it was*.

But I think about it. In the dark, sitting at the edge of the pool, watching the glow pulse in its slow and meaningless rhythm, I think about what the plateau knows. I think about the bacterial mats converting heat into life through chemistry alone. I think about the striders standing on the surface of water that would swallow anything heavier, their translucent bodies lit from below. I think about the vapor-gliders that never land, that live their entire lives between ground and sky, that drink the atmosphere and fall when they die.

I think about the children who will grow up knowing no green world. I think about the old workers who have become indistinguishable from infrastructure. I think about the machines that are evolving through repair, and the condenser lizards that carry the mineral deposits that will outlast their bodies, and the pack animals standing patient in the loading areas, breathing the wet air that is not theirs.

I think about edges. I think about margins. I think about the places where things meet—the water and the stone, the heat and the air, the living and the mineral, the workers and the plateau that is slowly claiming them.

And sometimes, in the dark, watching the bioluminescent pulse, I feel the plateau noticing me back.

---

This is not madness. I want to be clear about this. The Condenseries does not speak to me. I do not hear voices or see visions or believe that the thermal pools are conscious. What I feel is simpler and stranger: a sense of fit, a sense that my body and my attention have been shaped by this place into something that belongs here, not as an owner or a master but as a feature. I am part of the Condenseries the way the scale on the pipes is part of the infrastructure—accumulated through contact, functional in ways that were not designed, contributing to a system that is larger than any individual component.

When I die, my body will be rendered like any other. The workers will gather in the meal hall and speak my name, which is a gesture, using their voices in the acoustic space that makes speech possible. They will share food from the lowlands, the sweet fruit that holds moisture against an interior that is not mineral. They will remember me as the one who walked the margins, who sat at the eastern pool, who watched the striders and the mats and the glow.

And the mineral deposits that have accumulated on my skin, in my skin, through years of contact with the thermal water—these will remain. Perhaps they will be harvested with the crystal that forms on the lizards' husks, a secondary yield valued for its unusual origin. Perhaps they will be left where they lie, a slight thickening of the crust at the pool's edge, a trace of blue-grey in the stone that says *someone sat here*.

I hope it is the second. I hope the plateau keeps me the way it keeps everything—not as wealth to be extracted but as a feature of the landscape, a deposit in the mineral record, a small and unremarkable part of the Condenseries that continues to accumulate and precipitate and condense out of the saturated air, the hot water, the bright silence.

I am the one who walks the margins. I am the one who watches the edges. I am precipitation, condensation, crystallization—a pattern forming on a string suspended in saturated solution, branching outward in dendritic fingers that reach for something I cannot name.

The pool glows. The striders stand. The dark gives way to grey, and then to white, and the Condenseries begins another day.
