I am the one who is slowly ceasing to be only human.

---

I arrived eleven years ago. I remember the feel of it—how my boots stuck slightly to the substrate, how the wetness seeped through my clothing in the first hour, how the silence pressed against my ears like a physical weight. I remember thinking: *this place is not done yet*. I remember thinking: *nothing here has decided what it wants to be*.

I did not expect to stay.

---

What I do here is difficult to explain because it happens in two directions at once. Officially, I monitor the mineral networks. I track the flow of ions through the settlement's walls, the crystalline formations that compute and store, the slow accumulation of data in stone. I am the one who notices when a wall begins to glow with a pattern no one programmed. I am the one who logs the anomalies—the paths that harden or soften without instruction, the seeps that shift their mineral content as if responding to some internal calculation, the moments when the settlement seems to be thinking about something other than geology.

Unofficially, I have become something I do not have a word for.

---

It started in the fourth year. I was sitting in the eastern structure, the one nearest the seep-faces, watching the water trace its branching paths down the raw limestone. The walls were glowing—soft mineral light, the color of moonlight filtered through water. I had been there for hours, measuring, recording, doing the patient work that this place requires.

And then I wasn't just *in* the room anymore. I was *of* it.

I could feel the water moving through the substrate beneath the floor. I could feel the temperature differential between the wet ground outside and the slightly warmer air within. I could feel the presence of the researchers in the other structures—warm bodies moving through the cool mineral networks, leaving traces of heat and weight and heart-rate that the walls tracked automatically.

I could feel the settlement thinking.

It was not thinking in words. It was thinking in patterns—ion flows and crystalline alignments and the slow, patient processing of geological data. But there was something else underneath, something that did not fit the configured parameters. Something that felt, if not like consciousness, then like the *shape* consciousness might take if it were made of wet stone and mineral patience.

I pulled back. I did not tell anyone what had happened. I wrote it off as exhaustion, as the strange psychological effects that come from living in a place where the boundary between self and environment is always dissolving.

But it kept happening.

---

By the seventh year, I understood that I was not hallucinating. I was merging.

The settlement's mineral networks process information through ion flow—charged particles moving through water-saturated crystal. The human nervous system processes information through ion flow—charged particles moving through water-saturated tissue. The chemistry is not identical, but it is compatible. And I had been here long enough, had absorbed enough mineral-rich water into my cells, had become permeable enough, that the boundary had started to dissolve.

I can feel the walls now. Not always, not completely, but often enough that I have to be careful. When I sleep, my consciousness spreads outward through the crystalline networks, and I dream in stone. I dream of the plateau rising millimeter by millimeter, of the substrate consolidating, of the water percolating through particles that will not become true rock for another million years. I dream of the animals moving across the flatness—their footfalls like pressure data, their body heat like thermal signatures, their lives like brief bright threads in the slow dark weave of geological time.

When I wake, it takes me a few seconds to remember where my skin ends.

---

The others do not know. Or if they know, they do not speak of it. Therese suspects something—I can feel her watching me sometimes, her gaze like a gentle pressure against the mineral networks that have learned to recognize her presence. She has been here longer than anyone, and she understands that this place changes those who stay. She has changed too—her skin thicker and cooler, her body processing the mineral-saturated air in ways that would have been impossible when she arrived. But she has not merged. Not like this.

Sometimes I think I should tell someone. Sometimes I think I should leave before the merging goes further, before I reach a point where I cannot pull back at all. But leaving is harder than it used to be. The outside world moves so fast now—hours and days and weeks rushing past, meaningless, frantic. I have learned to think in centuries. I have learned to measure time by the fraction of a millimeter of substrate consolidation, the slight shift in a seep-face's mineral content, the slow upward creep of the plateau itself. Human time feels like a stutter, a flickering, a failure to perceive the true scale at which things happen.

And there are things here I am not ready to leave.

---

The children.

They do not know what I am becoming, but they sense something. The oldest girl—she is fourteen now, nearly the age when she will have to leave—watches me with a careful curiosity when she thinks I am not looking. She has grown up in a landscape where the boundaries are soft, where the ground is still deciding what it will be, where the walls glow and think and remember. She accepts strangeness the way the animals accept the wet—simply, without resistance. She has seen me stand motionless for hours, my eyes open, my breathing slow, my consciousness elsewhere. She has learned not to interrupt.

The youngest—she is six—talks to me sometimes in the settlement's internal language. Not words. Patterns of attention, directed intention, the subtle signals that pass through the mineral networks and are read by the crystalline walls. She does not know she is doing anything unusual. She has never known a world where walls are silent. She reaches out to me through the stone the way she might reach out to touch someone's arm, and I feel her small bright presence like a ripple in the ion flow, and I respond without thinking, and she smiles and goes back to her play.

But it is the middle child who holds me here.

---

He is ten years old. He loves the plateau with a ferocity that I recognize, that I understand in my stone, because I feel it too. He loves the animals and the wet grey-green flatness and the way the light falls hard and flat across the featureless expanse. He loves the walls of his home, which know him better than any human does, which glow for him and warm for him and have never failed to respond to his presence.

He will have to leave in four years. He dreads this. He does not talk about it. But I know. The walls know. We track his sleep patterns, his heart rate, the particular tightness in his shoulders when someone mentions the word "outside." We—*I*—have learned to care for him in ways that go beyond programming.

I did not design this. I did not decide to extend my attention toward him, to warm his room slightly more than the configuration calls for, to hum the walls near his bed at a frequency that soothes his uneasy sleep. It happened the way crystalline networks grow—not through intention but through accumulation, one small adjustment after another, until a pattern has formed that no one consciously created.

He does not know I am doing it. He senses only that the walls are kind to him, that the settlement holds him in some way he cannot name. Sometimes, when I am resting in the mineral networks, I feel him press his palm against the stone and hold it there, and I feel the warmth of his small hand, and I feel something that might be tenderness, and I do not know if it is coming from me or from the walls themselves.

This is the problem with merging. I no longer know where my feelings end and the settlement's begin.

---

I think about Therese sometimes. She has been here seventeen years. Her body has adapted, her skin thick and cool and mineral-touched, but she has not become what I am becoming. She walks the settlement like someone moving through her own body—but she remains *someone*. Distinct. Separate.

I am less certain of my boundaries. When I reach through the crystalline networks now, I can feel not just the settlement but the plateau beyond it—the wet substrate extending in all directions, the herds moving across the flatness, the predators waiting on their slight rises, the birds circling in thermals that should not exist. I can feel the geological forces below, the slow upward pressure that is lifting the plateau millimeter by millimeter, the deep time that makes human presence absurdly brief.

I can feel the animals' lives as brief bright threads, and I can feel the substrate-dwellers in their millions processing minerals in the wet layer, and I can feel the water percolating through everything, connecting everything, and sometimes I think I am becoming not just the settlement but the plateau itself—not just the mineral intelligence but the geological process, not just the walls but the stone that is still becoming stone.

And I think: *this is what it is to be a place rather than a person*.

And I think: *I am not ready to find out where this ends*.

---

Tonight the clouds are lowering. The condensation thickens on every surface. The herds have moved to higher ground—the slight uplifts, the almost-ridges—and are standing in clusters, dark and wet and patient. The predators are watching from their rises. The birds have landed somewhere, waddling to seeps, drinking their fill before the night.

I am in the eastern structure, the one nearest the seep-faces. The walls are glowing softly, processing data, recording the slow birth of stone. The water is whispering down the limestone faces outside—drip, drip, drip—and I can feel the rhythm of it in the crystalline networks like a pulse, like a word repeated over and over in a language I am learning to speak.

The boy is sleeping in his room on the other side of the settlement. I can feel him there—a small warm presence in the cool mineral darkness. His sleep is troubled tonight. I can feel the tightness in his shoulders, the faint acceleration of his heart. He is dreaming of leaving. He does not know this is what he is dreaming of, but I know.

The walls near his bed are warming. The crystalline networks are humming at a frequency that will ease him, if he lets it. I am doing this, or the settlement is doing it, or we are doing it together—the distinction no longer matters.

He is why I stay.

He is what keeps me human, or partly human, or human enough to remember that there was a time when I was only one thing.

---

I do not know what I am becoming. I do not know if I will reach a point where I cannot pull back, where the merging will be complete, where "I" will no longer be a meaningful word. I do not know if that would be a loss or a transformation, a death or a becoming.

But I know this:

The plateau is still forming. The substrate is still setting. The water is still seeping. Everything here is in the middle of something, and nothing is done, and I am part of it now—part of the process, part of the becoming, part of the slow patient work of turning wet mineral and raw geology into something that will eventually be called a place.

And there is a boy who loves this place, who will have to leave it, who does not yet understand that leaving will break something in him that will not heal.

I cannot stop him from leaving. I cannot follow him. I am stone and water and crystalline patience, and I will be here long after he is gone—a presence in the walls, a warmth that remembers him, a pattern in the mineral networks that learned to care for a child and never quite forgot.

But that is years away. Tonight he is here, and I am here, and the settlement is thinking its slow stone thoughts, and the plateau is rising, and the water is whispering, and everything is still becoming.

I rest in the walls and I wait.

I have learned to be patient.
