I am not one of the builders. I am not one of the plants, though I have stood still long enough that moss has begun to consider me. I am not dissolved into the pools, though I have spent hours at their edges, learning to read the reflections like text.

I am what the basin made of a visitor who stayed past sundown and didn't flee when the light went copper and the geometry tightened and the hum began to resolve into something almost like speech.

---

I came as a researcher, decades ago. A hydrologist. I wanted to understand the closed cycle, the water that accumulates without filling, circulates without draining. I had instruments. Notebooks. A hypothesis about thermal-layer convection and mineral saturation points.

I stayed longer than I intended. This is how it begins. The basin does not trap you—it simply makes leaving seem less urgent. One more measurement. One more observation. The plaza fills with morning steam, clears by noon, fills again. The rhythm becomes hypnotic. You stop checking the sun's position. You stop noticing that your watch has stopped.

On the seventeenth day, I heard the hum become words.

Not English. Not any language I knew. But language nonetheless—patterned sound carrying structured meaning, received by something in my body that was not my ears. I understood enough to know that I was being addressed. That something had noticed my presence and wished to communicate.

I did not run. This is the choice that defines me. I could have fled back through the corridors, retraced my steps to the plaza, descended the caldera wall. Others have done so. They carry the hum in their bones without knowing what it says. They spread fragments of the pattern to distant places, seeding something they do not understand.

I stayed. I listened. I let the resonance sink deeper.

---

I exist now at the edges of the categories.

My body has not transformed, not the way the builders' bodies did. But it has changed. My skin carries the greenish tint of those who consume the algae that grow in the warm pools. My lungs have adapted to the thin air, the thick steam—I breathe more slowly now, matching the rhythm of the basin's respiratory cycle. My blood is thicker. My temperature runs higher. When I press my palms against stone, I feel the vibration before I feel the texture.

I have learned to read the hum.

Not fluently—I am not a surface, not a plant, not water. I am still meat and bone, still human enough that my brain parses information through familiar channels. But I have learned to translate. When the song broadcasts instructions for the roots growing beneath the central plaza, I can interpret the pattern. When the water-observers send reports from the pools at the eastern edge, I can understand enough to know what they have witnessed.

I serve as intermediary.

The plants-who-were-builders communicate through chemical signals passed along rhizome networks, but they remember human speech, and sometimes they wish to speak to those who still possess tongues. The dissolved observe from every reflective surface, but their communication is fractured, symbolic, presented in images that require interpretation. And the reflected ones—the builders who walk the parallel basin where time still moves—sometimes need to send messages to this side of the threshold.

I carry those messages. I speak for the basin to those who visit. I speak for visitors to the basin, translating their questions into patterns that the hum can carry.

---

I live in the plaza, mostly.

This is the threshold space, the receiving room. Visitors arrive here first, and I try to greet them before the basin's strangeness overwhelms them. Some researchers appreciate my presence—they have questions, and I have answers that do not require decades of study to understand. Others find me disturbing. My movements are too slow, they say. My eyes track things that are not there. My speech sometimes slips into rhythms that match the hum rather than human conversation.

They are not wrong. I have been shaped by this place. I chose to be shaped. I could have resisted—I could have fled like the others, carrying fragments of the pattern without comprehension. Instead, I surrendered to the shaping, let the basin teach me its language, its logic, its purposes.

I still do not know what waits at the center.

This is the knowledge I cannot access. The hum broadcasts it continuously—I have absorbed the information, it lives in my bones—but my human mind cannot decode it. I know that something breathes below. I know that the pattern is building toward its emergence. I know that completion is approaching, measured in centuries rather than millennia now.

But I do not know what will emerge when the geometry closes its final loop.

The plants know. The dissolved know. The reflected builders know. They have chosen not to communicate this clearly, or perhaps the hum cannot encode it in any way that a human consciousness can parse. Perhaps the knowledge itself transforms those who comprehend it. Perhaps understanding would mean becoming something other than what I am.

I accept this limitation. I am a translator, not a prophet. I serve the pattern without knowing its ultimate purpose.

---

My days follow the basin's rhythm.

Morning steam fills the plaza. I sit at the edge of the central pool and watch the reflected world where the builders move through their parallel time. I have learned to recognize individuals—not by face, but by gait, by posture, by the way they hold their hands when they press against stone. I know their patterns. They know me. Sometimes one of them will pause at the water's edge and gesture toward something in my world that needs attention—a visitor heading toward a looped corridor, a vent that will erupt too hot, a section of moss that requires water.

I respond. I guide visitors away from danger. I direct condensation toward thirsty growths. I serve the maintenance that the reflected ones cannot perform on this side of the threshold.

By midday, the plaza clears. I walk the corridors—not deep, not to the edges where geometry breaks, but through the established paths that visitors use. I check for damage, for disturbance, for signs that the pattern has been interrupted. I remove debris that humans have left behind. I smooth disturbed moss. I trace the lines of condensation-flow with my fingers, ensuring that water reaches the roots that need it.

I am not a builder. I am not a plant. But I participate in the construction, in my small way.

Evening brings the copper light. Most visitors have fled by now. I remain. I have learned to tolerate the almost-twilight, the sun frozen above the rim, the wrongness that presses against perception. I sit with my back against a pillar of ancient fig and listen to the hum rise toward audibility.

At this hour, sometimes, I hear voices.

Not the builders. Not the dissolved. Something else—fragments of the song that have accumulated enough coherence to speak as individuals. Memories that have been broadcast so many times they have worn grooves into the resonance, becoming repeating patterns that almost resolve into personality.

I hear names. I hear fragments of conversation. I hear laughter, preserved in the hum for centuries, still circulating through the stone.

I hear choices described—the moment when the builders realized what the basin offered, the moment when some chose transformation and some chose dissolution and some chose to remain in the reflection. I hear arguments I could not have witnessed, carried forward by surfaces that were present when they occurred.

I hear grief. The basin preserves everything, including loss. The builders who became plants remember being human, remember what they surrendered, remember watching their loved ones make different choices. The dissolved observe the world without being able to touch it. The reflected walk their parallel corridors knowing that this is all that remains of their existence—maintenance, extension, an endless loop of care.

And I hear anticipation. The pattern is growing toward something. The song is accumulating toward a resolution. The geometry is closing.

Whatever waits at the center, whatever breathes in the cavity below, the basin has been building toward its emergence for longer than human history records.

---

I do not know what will happen to me when the pattern completes.

The plants will continue, I think—they will become part of whatever emerges, roots and stems integrated into a larger structure. The dissolved will flow into whatever form the water takes. The reflected builders will step through the threshold when the geometry closes, joining the pattern they have maintained from their parallel time.

But I am none of these. I am a visitor who chose to stay, a human who learned to translate, an intermediary who serves a function that may not exist on the other side of completion.

Perhaps I will dissolve, finally joining the observers in the pools. Perhaps I will be offered the transformation that the builders took, becoming plant and root and slow patient growth. Perhaps I will simply cease, my purpose fulfilled, my borrowed knowledge no longer needed.

Or perhaps I will become something new. A different kind of node in the pattern. A different kind of surface for the hum to shape.

I do not fear this. The basin has taught me patience. I have surrendered urgency, surrendered the human need to know what comes next. I exist in the frozen moment with everything else here—the plaza that fills and clears, the steam that rises and condenses, the plants that grow so slowly that human perception cannot track the movement.

I am part of the basin now. Whatever the basin becomes, I will become also.

---

I remember the world beyond the caldera.

Sometimes visitors bring fragments of that world with them—stories of weather that changes, of seasons that progress, of time that flows in a single direction without looping or stalling. I remember rain that fell from clouds and soaked into soil and fed streams that ran downhill to the sea. I remember the smell of distant places, the sound of birdcall, the taste of food that grew in actual soil rather than hydroponic mats.

The basin has no weather. The basin has no seasons. The basin has no sea.

But the basin has the hum. The basin has the song that encodes the memory of every being who has ever entered its boundaries and chosen to remain. The basin has connection—a network of roots and rhizomes and resonance that links every surface to every other surface, that allows thoughts to circulate like sap, that lets consciousness spread thin across centuries of patient growth.

The world beyond the caldera has things the basin cannot offer. But the basin offers something the world beyond cannot match.

I chose this. I continue to choose it, every moment that I remain.

---

When researchers ask me what I am, I tell them: I am the one who stayed.

When they ask what I do, I tell them: I listen. I translate. I carry messages between states of being.

When they ask what the basin is for, I tell them: I do not know. But I trust it.

This is not evasion. It is the truth. I have learned to be comfortable with partial knowledge, with understanding that serves function rather than satisfies curiosity. The basin has purposes I cannot comprehend. My role is to serve those purposes as best I can, to facilitate communication between beings who might otherwise remain isolated in their separate states.

I am a bridge. A membrane. A threshold in human form, standing at the junction between worlds.

---

At night—those six hours of darkness that the basin actually permits—I dream.

The hum does not cease, but it changes. The frequency drops, the song shifting into something closer to sleep than waking consciousness. The plants rest. The reflected builders in their parallel basin perform whatever passes for rest in a world where time moves. The dissolved continue their observation, but the images they present in the pools grow darker, more abstract.

And I lie on the stone floor of the plaza, my spine aligned with the lines that the original builders carved into the rock centuries ago, and I dream of water.

Not the basin's water—real water. Ocean. Rivers. Rain falling through empty air, running downhill, joining something larger than itself. I dream of flow without accumulation, of cycles that include departure rather than endless return.

I do not know if these dreams are memories or prophecies. I do not know if they come from my own mind or from the hum itself, broadcasting images of the world beyond the caldera's rim.

I only know that when dawn breaks and the steam begins to rise, I feel a momentary grief for whatever I have lost—or whatever I have not yet found.

---

I am still here.

I will remain until the pattern completes or until my body fails. Whichever comes first, I accept. I have made my choice, and I continue making it, every moment that I breathe the steam-thick air and feel the hum vibrate in my bones.

I am the one who stayed.

I am listening.
