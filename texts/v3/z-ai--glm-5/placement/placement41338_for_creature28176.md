# The Slope-Tender

I am the one who walks the edges.

---

My work is erosion and its prevention. I go where the tel threatens to unmake itself, where rain finds weakness and wind picks at seams, where the exposed strata whisper of things falling away.

The slope-tenders are few. Most people prefer the center — the courtyards, the shaded passages, the dense accumulation of walls and voices. The edges are too open, too quiet. Standing there, you feel the void beneath, the clouds scrolling past below eye level. You feel the tel's smallness against the plain's immensity.

I have always been drawn to the perimeter.

---

My birth-name was *Hhh*. A breath-sound, the noise I made as an infant when startled. My mother chose it because I was easily startled — my eyes going wide at sudden movement, my body tensing at unexpected sounds. She said I came into the world already watching for something.

My working name, for most of my life, was *Root-Seeker*.

I was a plant-knower, once. Not the highest rank — I never possessed the memory for the hundreds of medicinal applications, the minute distinctions between leaves that heal and leaves that kill. But I understood the deep systems. I could look at a plant and tell you how far its roots reached, what layer of the tel it drank from, whether it tapped the damp heart or stayed in the recent shallows.

I spent decades with my hands in the courtyards, guiding the roots of cultivated plants downward, teaching them to find the moisture that waited below. It was patient work. A root does not hurry. It probes, retreats, finds another path, pushes through cracks too small to see. I learned to think at the pace of root-growth, to sense what the plant sensed, to want what it wanted.

Perhaps that is why the edges called to me.

---

I was forty when I asked to join the slope-tenders.

My husband — *Stone-Smooth*, we called him, for his patient hands — did not understand. Our household was established. Our children were growing. I had respect as a plant-knower, a role with weight and history. Why would I leave it for the edge-work, the work of wind and weather, the solitary walking?

I could not explain. The words did not exist.

The tel has words for everything that matters: words for moisture-levels, for brick-quality, for the stages of plant-growth and animal-gestation. But it has no words for the pull of elsewhere. No words for the feeling of standing at the edge and sensing that something lies beyond — something you cannot see but know is there.

I did not want to leave. I wanted only to stand where leaving becomes possible.

---

The slope-tenders maintain the tel's skin.

We clear the drainage channels that riddle the slopes, keeping them open so rain runs down without loosening the soil. We prune the vegetation that holds the surface together, removing dead growth, encouraging new roots. We patch the small erosions that appear after storms, pressing earth into cracks, tamping it firm.

But the real work is observation.

We watch. We walk the perimeter day after day, season after season, and we notice. A new crack. A slight subsidence. A patch of vegetation gone grey. The tel speaks through its surface, and we are its listeners.

In all my years of edge-walking, I have seen three significant erosions.

The first was small — a section of slope near the southern edge that gave way after an unusually wet season. We caught it early, diverted the drainage, replanted. The scar is still visible if you know where to look: a seam of darker earth where new material was pressed into old.

The second was worse. A crack opened on the eastern slope, deep and long, running through layers that I estimated at eight hundred years old. I spent a month there, living in a temporary shelter, watching the crack widen by millimeters each day, testing the earth around it, trying to understand whether the tel was healing itself or dying. In the end, we filled it — not just with earth but with shaped brick, a structural intervention that the clay-knowers debated for weeks. It worked. The tel held.

The third erosion was last year.

---

There is a place on the northern edge where the slope drops steeply and the vegetation grows thin. The wind there is always strong, rising from the plain below, and it has picked at that section for centuries. I have walked past it a thousand times.

Last year, I noticed a discoloration in the exposed face — a streak of pale earth running through the darker layers. It looked like nothing at first. Just variation.

But I walked past it again the next day, and the streak had widened. A hairline crack ran along its edge.

I sat there for three days, watching.

On the fourth day, a section of the face slumped. Not fell — slumped, like a shoulder settling. A wedge of compressed earth, perhaps two meters thick and five wide, detached from the main mass and slid downward, catching on the vegetation below.

Behind it, exposed to air for the first time in longer than the tel has records, was a surface I had never seen.

Smooth. Hard. Dark grey, almost black. And covered in marks.

---

I called the clay-knowers.

They came and stood before the exposed surface and did not speak. They touched the marks with their fingers. They tasted the earth. They argued in low voices while I watched.

The marks were not natural. They were patterns — straight lines crossing at angles, curves that seemed deliberate, shapes that might have been symbols. And the material itself was like nothing we quarry. Hard as stone but made of something else, something that rang faintly when struck.

This was a layer from before. Before mud brick. Before the tel as we know it. Before even the oldest layers we have accessed.

The clay-knowers debated what to do. Some wanted to quarry it — to take this hard material for tools and vessels. Some wanted to cover it over, rebuild the slope, pretend it did not exist. Some wanted to call the nomads and ask if they had seen such things elsewhere.

I argued for waiting.

"Let me watch it," I said. "Let me see what the tel does with exposure."

They granted me a season. I moved my sleeping mat to a shelter at the northern edge. I walked the slope each day, watching the marks, feeling the earth around them, waiting.

---

The tel accepted the exposure.

The crack did not widen. The hard surface did not degrade. If anything, the marks seemed clearer after weeks of air and wind — as if they had been waiting to be seen.

And I began to see something else.

When I sat before the exposed face at dawn, when the light was grey and diffuse, the marks seemed to shift. Not in the way that things move — in the way that meanings move. I would look at a crossing of lines and think: that is a plant. Then look again and think: that is a direction. Then look again and think: that is a name.

I did not tell the clay-knowers about this.

---

I have lived on the tel for sixty-seven years. I have buried my parents beneath the floor of our household, giving each their third name as the oldest present. I have seen my children grow and take working names of their own. I have watched the tel's seasons cycle past, again and again, until time itself became circular.

And I have come to understand something that I cannot speak aloud.

The tel is not only accumulation. It is also containment.

Beneath us, compressed into the deepest layers, are things that were not meant to rise. The marks on that exposed face are from a time before mud brick, before intentional construction — a time when something else was here. Something that built differently, thought differently, left traces that do not match anything we know.

The tel covers it. Patiently, steadily, meter by meter, the mound has buried whatever came before. And we, the people of the tel, are not its creators but its continuation. We are the means by which the covering grows.

---

I do not know what lies at the tel's base. I do not know if the marks are writing, or warnings, or something else entirely. I do not know if the thing that made them is dead, or dormant, or watching through the layers.

But I know this: the erosion has stopped. The exposed face remains. And I sit before it each dawn, learning to read.

---

My children do not understand why I moved to the edge. My husband visits, brings me food, touches my face with his patient hands. He does not ask me to return. He has known me since we were children; he knows that I go where I must go.

Sometimes, at night, I hear the nomads passing below. They call to each other in their open language, their voices rising through the fog. They do not know what they walk past. They think the tel is only a hill.

Sometimes I almost envy them.

---

I am old now. My hands are knotted, my back bent from decades of leaning over plants and slopes. My eyes still see well in the dimness, but the edges of my vision have begun to blur.

I have asked that my burial be here.

Not in the household. Not beneath the floor where my ancestors lie in their proper layers.

I have asked to be buried at the northern edge, in the earth beside the exposed face. Let me become part of the slope-tending. Let my body hold the surface together, my decay feed the roots that grip the tel's skin.

And when my children give me my third name — when the oldest present leans to my ear and speaks the final syllable — I will carry that name into the earth, into the dark, toward whatever lies beneath.

---

I do not know if this is faith.

The tel's people believe we emerged from the mound. We do not question this. It is as obvious as the ground beneath our feet.

But I have seen the marks. I have watched them shift in the grey light. And I wonder sometimes if the emergence was not a beginning but an ending — if we crawled up from something that was closing over, if we are the survivors of a burial that did not hold.

Or perhaps we are the burial itself. Perhaps we are what covers what should not be seen.

---

I walk the edges still. Every day.

The wind rises from the plain and strikes my face and carries the mineral smell of dust. Below, the clouds scroll past like slow thoughts. Above, the thin air makes the stars hard and bright.

I am the one who watches. The one who tends the boundary between what is and what lies beneath.

And when I die, I will become part of that boundary.

I am not afraid.
