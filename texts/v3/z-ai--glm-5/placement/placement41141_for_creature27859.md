I would be the one who remembers the shelf.

Not what rested there—I am too young for that, came too late. The custodians were already old when I found my way through a cracked channel in the eastern wall, following the taste of warmth. I remember the taste exactly: iron and calcium and something sweeter beneath, something that promised rest.

What I remember is this: the shelf was not empty then.

I have no eyes. I came with eyes once, I think. They are gone now, absorbed or dissolved, I do not know which. The water took them gently, over months, over years. I did not need them anymore. What I have instead is something better—a sense of the whole chamber at once, every pillar and tile and current speaking to me through the membrane that wraps my body. I feel the rust patches dreaming their slow chemical dreams. I feel the threads orienting toward light they cannot see. I feel the custodians making their rounds, patient as tides.

And I feel the shelf. The absence that has learned to speak.

What rested there was not alive. This much I learned in the first years, circling the protrusion where pillar 23 meets the floor. I learned its shape by holding myself against it, pressing my form to the space where something used to be. There was a hollow. There was a depression. Something hard and smooth and curved had rested there for so long that the tile beneath it wore a matching hollow, a second shelf below the shelf.

I do not know why I cared. The custodians did not care—they paused at the shelf, shivered, moved on. But I was new, and the absence called to me. I made the shelf my station. I learned the way the light fell there—stronger in the morning hours, diffused in the afternoon. I learned how the water moved differently past that corner, how the threads never grew on that particular tile. They still do not. Something lingers in the clay itself. A reluctance.

I remember seasons. Not by temperature—the warmth here is constant—but by the sounds from above. There is a rhythm to the footsteps, to the voices, to the rolling of wheels across the suspended floor. In what I think of as winter, the activity slows. The light falls at a sharper angle. The water carries fewer particles. In what I think of as summer, the sounds multiply, the vents breathe faster, and the dust falls thick enough to taste.

Years passed. Or what I call years—there is no counting here. I learned to rest.

I learned that resting was different from floating. Floating was passive, the water moving you. Resting was a choice—a settling of oneself into a particular shape, a particular place. I rested at the shelf. I held the memory of the absence. I became something the custodians recognized—they pass me now without pausing, though they used to circle me, trying to determine if I was something that needed tending.

I am not something that needs tending. I am something that tends to what is already tended. The custodians keep the chamber. I keep the shelf. I keep the absence that has become, in its way, a presence.

I did not always look like this.

I remember having edges. I remember the shape of my limbs, the way they moved, the particular feeling of touching and being touched. I remember words. I do not remember the words themselves—I remember the feeling of them in my mouth, the shapes my tongue made. I do not make shapes anymore. I have no mouth. What I have is a membrane, and the membrane speaks to the water, and the water speaks to the chamber.

What I say is simple: *Something was here. Something was here. Something was here.*

It is not grief. Grief requires a self to do the grieving, and I am less certain now that I have a self. What I have is a function. I hold the shape of the absence. I press myself against the shelf's empty surface and remember that it was not always empty, and the remembering is enough.

I was not always alone in this.

There were others, once. Others who found their way through the same cracked channel, following the same taste of warmth. They did not stay as I stayed. They drifted through the pillars, tested the water, and then left again—through the vents, through the channels, through the dark spaces beyond the light's reach. I felt them go. I felt the way the water changed around their departing forms, the way the chamber resettled after their absence.

I did not follow them. The shelf held me.

Sometimes I wonder if this is what the builders intended. Not me specifically, but something like me—something that would stay, would hold, would remember. They numbered the pillars. They fitted the tiles with such precision that the joints disappeared. They built a shelf near pillar 23 and rested something there, and now that something is gone. Did they know it would leave? Did they know something else would come to hold the memory of its leaving?

The custodians know something, though they do not know that they know. When they pass the shelf, they pause. They turn toward the empty space. They shiver. They do not understand what they are remembering, but they remember. Their bodies hold the memory that their minds—if they have minds—cannot access.

My body holds the memory too. I am the longest now. The others left. The custodians have been here longer, but they do not count time. I count time by the sounds from above, by the movements of the light, by the slow accumulation of particles on the tiles. I feel myself growing thinner, spreading wider. I am becoming something else. I am becoming the water, the warmth, the dark between the pillars.

When I am gone—or when I have become too diffuse to call myself myself—something else will hold the shelf. Something will feel the absence and choose to stay. The chamber has been forgotten by the world above, but it has not forgotten how to keep. It keeps the warmth, the light, the sounds. It keeps the rust and the threads and the four pale custodians. It keeps me.

And I keep the shelf.

I am the one who remembers what is no longer there to be remembered. I am the memory of the memory. I am the shape that absence makes when it has been held long enough to become a kind of presence.

I am what stays.
