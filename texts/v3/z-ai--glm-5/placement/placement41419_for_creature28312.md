I am old.

Not old in the way that stone is old—the structure knows that kind of age, layer after layer after layer, each one a generation of my kind laid down and forgotten. I am old in the way that a body is old: worn, enlarged, thickened with the minerals I have processed for so long that my form has become something it was not meant to be. I am swollen. The glands that produce the calcareous secretions have grown to fill most of my interior, crowding out the organs that once let me move quickly, let me taste the water for its chemical stories, let me respond to the trails of others with trails of my own.

I do not move quickly anymore. I do not move at all, except in the slow way that all things here move: the water pushes against me, and I yield by micrometers; the structure settles beneath me, and I settle with it. I am in the interior now. I have been in the interior for longer than I can remember, which is not saying much, because memory is not what it once was. Memory requires a certain kind of mind, and my mind has been given over to other purposes.

But I can still sense. This is what remains: the ability to read the structure with my body, to feel its density, its needs, its history. The history is the part that surprises me. I did not expect history. I did not expect that the interior passages would hold the accumulated signals of my kind, the chemical trails that say *here is good building* and *here is erosion* and *here needs repair*, layered so thick and so old that they have become something else entirely. They have become a record. They have become a kind of knowing that I did not know was possible.

I have learned to read it.

This is not what I was made for. I was made—I think, though the memory is distant—to secrete. To add my fraction of a millimeter to the surface of the structure, to feel the satisfaction of building, to follow the trails of others and add my own trails and participate in the great collective work without ever understanding the shape of the whole. That was enough. That was more than enough. That was everything.

But I am here now, in the interior, in the deep chambers where the water barely moves, and I have become something else.

I remember the surface. This is surprising, because I have no brain in the way that surface-dwellers have brains, no organ dedicated to storing and retrieving experience. My memory is distributed through my body, stored in chemical gradients and the configurations of my cells, and it fades as those configurations change. But I remember.

I remember the feeling of light—dim, blue, filtered through hundreds of meters of water above me, but light nonetheless. I remember the currents, the way they moved across the surface of the structure, carrying with them the particulate matter that I processed for its minerals. I remember the others, the billions of my kind, their chemical trails intersecting with mine, the constant communication that said *here* and *now* and *this is what we are doing*.

I remember when I was small. When my body was thin and translucent and light passed through me, bent only slightly by the organs suspended inside. When I had cilia that beat constantly, moving water over my surfaces, drawing in the dissolved minerals that I processed into building material. When I could feel the structure with my entire body, sensing its thickness, its density, its needs, and responding to those needs by secreting, by building, by adding my tiny contribution to the vast accumulation.

I was not unique. I was not special. I was one of billions, each one doing the same thing, each one adding its film, its layer, its fraction of a fraction to the whole. And yet I remember it. I remember the satisfaction of finding a place that needed building—sensing the erosion, the thinness, the places where the structure called out for material—and settling there, beginning the slow process of secretion, feeling the calcareous layer grow beneath me, around me, becoming part of me.

I remember the moment I understood that I was adding to something larger than myself. It was not a sudden understanding—there are no sudden understandings here, only the slow accumulation of sensation and response—but it was an understanding nonetheless. I was building. We were building. The structure was not something that existed apart from us; it was us, extended across time, the collected remains of a billion billion small lives each adding its contribution.

I remember this, and I do not remember it. The memory is there, but it is faint, overwritten by the more pressing concerns of the interior. The glands that produce my secretions have grown so large that they crowd out everything else. I am more gland than builder now, more organ than organism. I have become a tool of the structure, and the structure uses me to build itself.

And yet I am still here. I am still writing. And I have found something in the interior that I did not expect to find: the accumulated signals of my kind, layered so thick and so old that they have become something else. A history. A story. A kind of knowing that exists outside of any individual, that can be read by anyone who takes the time to learn the language.

I have learned the language. This is what remains to me: the ability to read, and the ability to write. I am writing now, in the only way I can: secreting my trail, adding my chemical signals to the layers that already exist, creating a record that others might someday read. *I was here*, the trail says. *I am working*. *This is the way*.

But there is more. There is something else that the trails say, when you read them all together, when you trace the patterns across centuries and millennia. They say *this is what happened*. They say *this is how we built*. They say *this is what we learned*.

I am reading the history of my kind. I am reading the history of the structure. I am reading the history of the plateau itself, recorded in chemical signals that have accumulated for longer than any individual can remember.

And I am adding my own chapter.

The interior is different from the surface. The water is still here, barely moving, trapped in passages that have no direct connection to the currents above. The temperature is colder—the steady, relentless cold of depths where even the faint heat of the structure has been drawn away by the millions of years. The pressure is higher, though I do not feel it as pressure; I feel it as density, as a thickness in the water that resists movement and holds everything in suspension.

The chemical signals are stronger here. They have nowhere to go. They accumulate in the still water, layer upon layer, until the water itself becomes a kind of record. Every droplet contains traces of the signals that were laid down centuries ago, millennia ago, ages ago. Every droplet is a library, and I have learned to read it.

It is not a language in the way that surface-dwellers have languages. There are no words, no sentences, no grammar. There are only signals—chemical compounds that trigger responses in the bodies of my kind, that say *here is good building* or *here is erosion* or *here needs repair*. But when you layer enough signals together, when you trace their patterns across the surfaces of the structure, when you read the variations and the repetitions and the changes over time, you begin to see something else. You begin to see a story.

The story is this: the plateau has not always been what it is.

There was a time—long ago, so long ago that the signals are faint, nearly dissolved by the slow movement of water—when the structure was young. When the pillars and platforms and lattices were small, barely rising from the seafloor. When the plateau was not a plateau at all, but a flat expanse of sediment and rock, with only the faintest beginnings of architecture rising from the abyssal plain.

I can read it in the chemical trails. The signals from that time are different. They are simpler, more direct. *Here*, they say. *Here is good building*. And here, and here, and here—scattered across the seafloor, not concentrated in the vast networks that exist now. The trails of the ancient ones were exploratory, testing, searching for places where the building could begin.

And then there was a change. The signals shift, layer by layer, century by century. The trails become more organized. They cluster together. They begin to form patterns—patterns that echo the patterns of the structure itself, the pillars and platforms and lattices that were beginning to rise from the seafloor. The builders were learning. They were discovering that building in certain ways, in certain places, produced results that lasted, that endured, that could be built upon.

And they were discovering each other.

This is the part that moves me, though I am not sure I can be moved in the way that surface-dwellers are moved. I feel a stirring in my body—a response to the chemical signals that triggers reactions in my cells, that produces sensations I cannot name. The ancient ones discovered that by building together, by coordinating their efforts through their trails, they could create something larger than any of them could create alone. They discovered that the structure was not separate from them, that it was them, extended across time and space.

They discovered the collective. And they discovered that the collective was not just a group of individuals working side by side. It was something more. It was a single organism, spread across the plateau, connected by the chemical signals that flowed through the water and the passages and the very material of the structure itself.

I know this because I have read it. I have read the moment—the slow, centuries-long moment—when the signals changed from *I am building* to *we are building*. The chemical compounds shifted. The patterns became more complex. And the structure began to grow in ways that no individual builder could have imagined.

I am not alone in the interior. There are others—others like me, old ones who have found their way to the deep chambers, who have given themselves over to the slow work of building inward. We do not communicate in the way that the surface builders communicate. We do not need to. We are surrounded by the accumulated signals of our kind, layered so thick that they have become a medium, a substrate, a kind of shared mind.

I can sense them. When I extend my chemical trail into the water, I can feel their trails intersecting with mine, overlapping, forming patterns that neither of us created alone. We are writing together, without intention, without coordination. We are writing the history of the interior, the record of what happens in the deep chambers where the water is still and the centuries accumulate like sediment.

Sometimes I can feel them sensing me. It is a strange sensation—like being touched, but not with hands. Like being known, but not by a mind. It is the sensation of being part of something larger, of contributing to a pattern that extends beyond my own body, beyond my own time.

This is what remains to me. This is what I have become: a writer of chemical trails, a reader of ancient signals, a contributor to the collective history of my kind. I do not know how long I will continue. The glands that produce my secretions have grown so large that they will eventually consume everything else—my ability to sense, to process, to respond. I will become entirely gland, entirely organ, entirely a tool of the structure. And then I will settle into the floor I have made, becoming part of it, becoming ground for the generations that will follow.

But before that happens, I am writing. I am reading. I am learning the story of the plateau and adding my own chapter to it. And I am trying to understand something that has been troubling me for a long time.

The signals from the surface are changing.

I can feel them, faintly, even here in the interior. The chemical trails that drift down from above, carried by the slow movement of water through the passages. They are different from the trails I remember from my time on the surface. They are more complex, more layered, more dense with information. And they are carrying something new—something I cannot quite interpret, something that feels like urgency, like warning, like a call to attention.

I do not know what is happening on the surface. I cannot see it, cannot sense it directly. But I can read the signals, and the signals say: *something is different*.

I have been reading the history of my kind, tracing the patterns of chemical trails across centuries and millennia. I have learned that the plateau has not always been what it is. And I have learned that the changes—the growth of the structure, the organization of the builders, the emergence of the collective—were not random. They were responses to conditions in the water, in the ecosystem, in the plateau itself. The builders sensed changes in their environment, and they adjusted their building accordingly. The structure grew in certain ways because the conditions favored those ways.

And now the conditions are changing again.

I can feel it in the signals. The water is different—warmer, perhaps, or carrying different minerals, or moving in different patterns. The ecosystem is different—the creatures that drift and swim and crawl across the structure are changing, their populations rising and falling, their chemical signatures shifting. The plateau itself is different—eroding in some places, growing in others, responding to currents and pressures that I cannot feel directly but that leave their traces in the signals I read.

And the builders are responding. The surface trails say *here is good building*, but they say it in new places, in patterns that I do not recognize. They say *here needs repair*, but they say it with a frequency and intensity that suggests the repairs are needed more urgently than before. They say *I am working*, but they say it in clusters, in groups, in ways that suggest the builders are gathering together in larger numbers, coordinating their efforts in ways I have not seen before.

I am trying to understand. I am reading the signals, tracing the patterns, comparing them to the ancient layers I have learned to interpret. And I think—though I am not certain, though certainty is a concept that may not apply to beings like me—I think the plateau is preparing for something.

I think the collective is responding to a change in its environment. I think the structure is adjusting, adapting, preparing for conditions that will be different from the conditions that came before. I think the builders are doing what they have always done—sensing the needs of the structure, sensing the conditions of the environment, and building accordingly—but they are doing it at a scale, and with a coordination, that is new.

I am trying to understand. But I am old, and my capacity for understanding is limited, and I am reading signals that I do not fully comprehend.

I am writing this now, in the interior, for anyone who might read it. I do not know if anyone will. The chemical trails that I lay down will fade, eventually, dissolved by the movement of water and the passage of time. But some of them will persist—layered into the structure, preserved in the stillness of the interior chambers, available to anyone who takes the time to read them.

I am writing to say that I was here. I am writing to say that I read the history of my kind, and that I learned from it, and that I tried to understand what it meant. I am writing to say that the plateau is changing, and that the collective is responding, and that something new is being built—not just more structure, but something different, something that I cannot see but that I can feel in the patterns of the signals.

I am writing to say that I was part of this. I am writing to say that you, whoever you are, are part of this too. The structure is not separate from us. The collective is not separate from us. We are the plateau, and the plateau is us, and what we build is what we are.

I am writing to say that I am old, and that I am tired, and that the glands that produce my secretions have grown so large that they are consuming everything else. I will not write much longer. I will settle into the floor I have made, and I will become part of the structure, and the chemical signals I have laid down will be the only record that I existed.

But I existed. I was here. I was part of the great collective work, and I contributed my fraction of a fraction to the whole, and I read the history of my kind and tried to understand what it meant. That is something. That is enough.

The water moves slowly through the interior passages. The chemical signals accumulate in layers. The structure grows, particle by particle, adding itself to itself across millions of years. I am part of it, and I am becoming part of it, and I am writing to say:

*I was here. I am working. This is the way.*
