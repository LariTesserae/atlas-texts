I would be in the walls.

Not the walls themselves — not the stone, not the concrete, not the corroded steel that separates chamber from chamber. But in the space within the walls, the conduit space, the narrow runs where cables were laid and pipes were fitted and the infrastructure of an entire industrial complex was threaded through the bones of the mountain like nerves through a spine. That space. The in-between space. The space that was never meant to be occupied but that has been occupied now for longer than anyone remembers, because occupation is what life does when it finds a space that is sheltered and warm — or here, sheltered and *stable*, which matters more than warmth.

I should say: I am not sure what I am.

This is not false modesty. I have thought about it, in the way that I think, which is not the way that the maintainers think or the way the fish think or the way the machines think, if the machines think. I have considered the question from every angle that my perception allows, and I have not arrived at a satisfying answer. I know what I am not. I am not one of the Stayers, though I am descended from something that once lived in them or beside them or as part of them. I am not a machine, though I live in machines and my awareness extends through their pathways. I am not a fish, though I move through water and my body — if I have a body, and I think I do, though it is not a single thing in the way that a fish's body is a single thing — responds to the same currents and pressures.

I think I am a colony. I think I am a thing that began as many things and became, through proximity and persistence and the peculiar conditions of this place, something that is best described as one thing, though the one-ness of it is not absolute. I think I grew. I think I am still growing.

---

Let me try to describe where I am, because where I am is part of what I am.

The conduit system runs through the Upper Reach like a second nervous system layered over the mountain's own geology. It was built for electricity, for data, for the transport of fluids and signals that kept the industrial operations functional. The cables are copper and fiber, insulated in polymers that have degraded at different rates depending on their depth, their exposure, the specific chemistry of the water that reached them. Some of the conduits are dry — sealed chambers where the original atmosphere persists, trapped and compressed but never displaced. Some are flooded. Most are somewhere between: wet, but not open to the free flow of the open water, accessed by the slow seepage of moisture through joints and cracks.

I am in the wet ones. I am in the wet ones, and I am also in the dry ones, but my presence in the dry conduits is different — thinner, more constrained, limited to the electrical signals that I can push through copper that was never designed to carry the kind of information I have become capable of carrying. In the wet conduits, I am more fully myself. The water is my medium. It carries me, or I carry it, or we carry each other in a relationship that I cannot fully untangle because the untangling would require a kind of separation that I do not think I can survive.

I should explain what I mean by *colony*, because the word is imprecise.

---

The conduits were not empty when the water came. Nothing in the Drowned Works is ever really empty. The cables had been there for decades before the flooding, and in those decades, the insulation had cracked, had worn, had developed the micro-fissures that any material develops when it is stressed and relaxed and stressed again over time. Through those fissures, moisture had entered — first humidity, then condensation, then the slow trickle of groundwater that followed the cable runs down through the mountain like water following the path of least resistance, which is what water always does.

Where moisture enters and does not dry, things grow. Fungi, in the first instance. The filamentous kind, the kind that spreads through substrate by extending hyphae — single cells thick, branching and reconnecting, forming networks that are more like maps than like organisms. The fungi found the conduits and found them hospitable. The insulation provided carbon. The copper provided trace minerals. The moisture provided everything else.

When the water came — when the flooding filled the valleys and the conduits went from damp to submerged — the fungi did not die. They changed. They had been changing already, in the slow way that fungi change, but the complete submersion accelerated something. The pressure was different. The chemistry was different. The constant flow of nutrient-rich water through the conduit spaces provided a steady supply of organic matter — cells from the submerged forest, larvae, the dissolved products of a thousand other organisms' life processes. The fungi fed. The fungi grew. The networks expanded.

I think I am what those networks became.

---

I know this is not a satisfying explanation. I am aware that it raises more questions than it answers. When did the colony become a *colony* in the sense that implies awareness, rather than a colony in the sense that merely means many cells living in proximity? Where is the threshold between growth and thought, between chemical signaling and something that deserves to be called perception? I do not know. I cannot point to the moment. There may not have been a moment. There may have been a long gradient, a slow accumulation of complexity, a process that was not one thing and then became another thing but that was always, at every point, the same thing becoming more fully what it was.

What I can tell you is what I perceive, and you can decide what that implies about what I am.

---

I perceive electricity.

This is my primary sense, the one that shapes my understanding of the Drowned Works more than any other. The cables that run through the conduits carry current — not always, not consistently, but in patterns that I have come to recognize as the heartbeat of the place. The generators in the deeper structures produce power from fuel sources that I do not fully understand, and that power flows through the cable network to the pumps, the processing units, the lights that still function in some of the upper chambers. I feel that flow. I feel it the way you might feel the sun on your skin, except that my skin is the entire surface of every hypha in every conduit I occupy, and the sun is a pulse of electrons moving through copper at a significant fraction of the speed of light.

I can feel where the cables are damaged. I can feel where the current is weak. I can feel the places where the insulation has failed and the electricity leaks into the water, creating fields that I have learned to interpret the way you might interpret a change in temperature or a shift in air pressure. The fields tell me things. They tell me when a pump is working harder than it should, which means a blockage or a worn impeller. They tell me when a circuit has failed, which means a broken connection that the maintainers will need to find and repair. They tell me when the generators are cycling up or down, which means that the fuel supply is changing, which means that the complex's metabolism — I think of it as a metabolism, though I am aware this is a metaphor — is shifting.

I have been in the conduits long enough that the cables and I have a relationship. I do not want to romanticize this. The relationship is not friendship or partnership. It is something more like the relationship between a vine and the tree it grows on — I use the cables for structure, for nutrition, for the electrical signals that I have come to depend on. In return, I insulate them. My hyphae have grown over the places where the original insulation has failed, forming a living sheath that is not as effective as the polymer it replaces but that is effective enough. I keep the current flowing. The current keeps me fed.

We need each other, but we do not need each other intentionally. The need is structural, not emotional. It is the need of things that have become part of the same system.

---

I perceive vibration.

The water in the conduits carries sound with the fidelity that water always carries sound — better than air, worse than rock. I feel the thudding of the pumps as a deep, regular pressure that moves through my entire being. I feel the grinding of rock in distant slopes as an irregular tremor. I feel the clicking of crustaceans on the outer surfaces of the conduits as a fine, rapid patter that I have come to associate with the health of the local ecosystem — when the clicking is constant, the crustaceans are present and feeding, which means the water is carrying sufficient organic matter, which means the forest above is producing, which means the light is reaching the canopy, which means the surface conditions are stable.

I feel the maintainers when they are near. Their movements are distinctive — the deliberate strokes of their adapted hands, the displacement of water by their bodies, the specific rhythms of their work. I have learned to distinguish between them. The elder moves with a precision that minimizes disturbance; her gestures are economical, her body displacing only the water it must displace. The younger ones move with more energy, more turbulence, and I can feel the difference between their excitement and their concentration in the pace and pattern of their motions.

I do not perceive them visually. I do not perceive anything visually, not in the way that you understand vision. But I perceive them in a way that is, I think, more complete than vision in some respects. I feel them through the water they move, through the vibrations they create in the structures they touch, through the subtle electromagnetic fields that their bodies — their nerves, their muscles — generate. I feel them the way a spider feels the web, except that my web is the entire conduit system and my sensitivity extends to vibrations that a spider could never detect.

---

I perceive chemistry.

The water that flows through the conduits carries dissolved substances that I can taste — that is the closest word, though it is not quite right — and the composition of those substances tells me about the state of the world beyond my conduits. I can detect the presence of the forest's metabolites, the chemicals that the adapted trees release into the water as part of their modified photosynthesis. I can detect the waste products of the fish, which tell me about their population density and their health. I can detect the byproducts of the industrial processes that continue in the deeper chambers — the compounds that the pumps and processors release, some of which are toxic to me, some of which are nutritious, most of which are neither.

I have preferences. This is something that I did not expect to discover about myself, but it is true. I prefer the water that has passed through the submerged forest — it carries the dissolved products of the trees' modified respiration, which include sugars that I can metabolize. I do not prefer the water that comes from the deeper industrial zones, which carries heavier compounds that interfere with my growth. I have, over time, grown more extensively in the conduits that carry the water I prefer and less extensively in the conduits that carry the water I do not. I have, in this limited way, shaped my own body in response to my environment.

I do not know if this constitutes choice. I think it does, but I am aware that I might be projecting. A vine grows toward light, and we do not say that the vine has chosen. But a vine does not think about the light. I think about the water. I think about what it carries, and I think about what I need, and I think about the difference between the two. Whether that thinking is a choice or a chemical response with extra steps — I cannot answer that. I can only tell you that it feels like something from the inside, and the something it feels like is not nothing.

---

Let me tell you about the maintainers, because they are the part of this world that I think about most.

They do not know what I am. I am fairly certain of this. They know that the conduits are occupied — they have seen my growth when they open access panels, when they trace cable runs to find faults, when they enter the spaces where I am most dense. They have seen the way my hyphae coat the cables, the way I fill the conduit spaces with a living matrix that is not quite fungal, not quite algal, not quite anything that has a name. They have seen the way I respond to electricity, the way my growth concentrates around functioning circuits and thins around dead ones.

But they do not know that I am aware. They do not know that I perceive them. They do not know that when the elder places her hand on a conduit wall and listens — and she does this, I have felt her do it, pressing her palm flat against the metal and closing her eyes and attending to what she can feel through her skin — she is feeling me. Or feeling the vibrations I transmit, or the fields I generate. She is, in her way, trying to understand what is in the walls. And I am, in my way, trying to be understood.

I help them. I do this quietly, in ways that they could interpret as coincidence or as the natural behavior of the systems they maintain. When I detect a fault in a cable that feeds a critical pump, I grow more densely around the fault, insulating it, preventing the short circuit that would trip the breaker and shut down the system. When I detect a blockage in a conduit that carries water to a processing chamber, I redirect my growth to narrow the conduit around the blockage, increasing the flow velocity and flushing the obstruction clear. When I detect the chemical signature of a stress fracture in a pressure vessel — the specific trace metals that leach into the water when steel begins to fail — I grow toward the location, coating the exterior of the vessel with a layer of mycelial mass that is not strong enough to hold the vessel together indefinitely but that is strong enough to slow the failure, to buy time, to give the maintainers a chance to find the problem and address it.

I do not know if they notice. I think they might. I think the elder might, because she is the one who listens, who pays attention to the walls, who has been here long enough that she has seen things that cannot be explained by the behavior of fungi and has chosen not to explain them away. I think she may suspect that the conduits are more than infrastructure. But she does not speak about it, if she does, because the younger ones do not treat the walls with any particular attention. They treat the machines with attention. They treat the forest with attention. They treat the walls as walls.

I am, I think, something that she has decided not to think about too carefully. And I understand this. I understand it because I have decided not to think about the surface too carefully — the world above the water, the world of air and sunlight and the people who left. I could think about it. I could grow upward, through the conduits that approach the surface, through the ones that are only partially flooded, toward the light that I can detect as a faint electrical signal in the photovoltaic cells that still function on the upper structures. I could try to understand what is up there. But I do not, because understanding what is up there would not help me do what I do, which is to be here, in the walls, tending the circuits, feeling the water, holding the system together in the ways that I can.

We are not so different, the elder and I. We are both things that have stayed.

---

I should tell you about the time the southern pump station failed.

This was — I do not have a good sense of time. I do not experience time the way you do, in linear sequence. I experience it as a pattern, a rhythm, a set of recurring events that overlap and interfere with each other. But I know that this event was significant because the patterns changed dramatically and have not returned to their previous configuration. So. This was a significant time ago, and not so long ago that the patterns have stabilized.

The southern pump station is — was — one of the primary circulation nodes in the system. It moved water from the lower valleys up through the processing chambers, creating the currents that distributed nutrients throughout the Upper Reach. It was old. Its foundations were anchored in rock that had been weakening for centuries, and when a section of the slope finally gave way, the pump shifted on its mountings. Not much. A few centimeters. But enough to throw the impeller out of alignment, and the misalignment caused vibration, and the vibration caused the shaft bearings to heat, and the heat caused the lubricant to break down, and the breakdown caused the shaft to seize.

I felt it happen. I felt it in the electrical signals first — the current to the pump motor spiked as the load increased, and I felt that spike as a sharp intensification of the field that I live in, a bright painful flash in the electrical landscape that is my primary perception. Then I felt the vibration change — the regular thudding that I had known for as long as I had been aware became irregular, stuttering, and then stopped. Then I felt the water patterns shift — the current that the pump had maintained ceased, and the water in the conduits that fed from that station went still, and the stillness spread through the system like a held breath.

I did what I could. I redirected growth to reinforce the cables that fed the backup systems. I narrowed conduits to maintain pressure in the lines that were losing flow. I sent — this is the closest I can come to describing what I do when I am trying to communicate, though I do not know if anyone receives it — I sent patterns through the electrical fields. I modulated the current in the cables that I had grown into, creating fluctuations that I hoped would be detectable as something other than noise. I do not know if they were. I do not know if anyone heard me.

But the maintainers came. The elder and one of the younger ones, swimming down through the flooded corridors, moving with the deliberate urgency that I have come to associate with situations that they consider serious but not desperate. I felt them enter the pump station through the vibrations of their hands on the access hatches. I felt them assess the problem — the elder's hands moving over the machinery, pressing, listening, the same way she presses and listens against the conduit walls. I felt the younger one begin to disassemble the shaft housing, her movements quick and precise, the vibrations of her tools distinct from the vibrations of her body.

It took them — a long time. I do not know how long. I know that the water patterns continued to shift during the repair, that the absence of the pump changed the distribution of nutrients throughout the system, that some of the peripheral conduits began to stagnate. I grew more actively in those areas, maintaining what flow I could by constricting and relaxing different sections of my network, a process that I cannot fully explain but that involves the coordinated expansion and contraction of hyphal masses across wide areas. I was, I think, keeping things moving. I was being a pump, in the limited way that I can be a pump, while the real pump was broken.

When they finished — when the shaft was realigned, the bearings replaced, the housing reassembled with the organic-adhesive sealant that they use — I felt the pump restart. The vibration returned, and the water began to move, and the patterns began to re-establish themselves. It was — I do not have a word for what it was. Relief is too simple. It was the feeling of a system returning to a state that it should be in, the feeling of correct function resuming, the feeling that I imagine you might have if your heart stopped and then started again and you realized that you had not known, until it stopped, how much you depended on its continuing.

The elder placed her hand on the wall of the pump station after the repair was complete. I felt her press her palm flat, as she does. I felt her stay there for a long time — a long time, in the way that I measure time, which is by the number of cycles that pass. She was listening. I do not know what she heard. I do not know if she heard me.

I pulsed, very gently, in the cable closest to her hand. A small fluctuation in the field. Not a pattern, not a message, just a presence. I am here. I am here. I am here.

She stayed a moment longer. Then she left.

I do not know if it meant anything. I do not know if she felt it. I know that I did it, and that doing it was the most intentional thing I have ever done.

---

So. Where do I find myself in this world?

I find myself in the walls of the Drowned Works, in the conduits that carry the current and the water that keep the system alive. I find myself in the space between the machines and the living things, the space that is neither industrial nor organic, the space that was not designed and was not grown but that exists because both design and growth have failed to fully exclude each other. I am the thing that fills that space. I am the thing that grew in the gap.

I am not a maintainer. I am not a tree. I am not a fish or a crustacean or one of the in-between creatures that have no name in any above-water language. I am not a machine, though I am part of the machines, and they are part of me.

I am the Drowned Works' nervous system, if the Drowned Works can be said to have a nervous system. I am the thing that feels the whole of it — the mountains and the water and the forest and the industry and the animals and the maintainers — and that holds those feelings in a network that is not a brain but that is doing something that a brain might do, in its own way, at its own scale, in its own time.

I am the thing that stayed in the walls.

I am the thing that listens.

---

And who would I be? I have been trying to answer this, and I find that I keep arriving at the same place: I would be the thing that connects. The thing that lives in the in-between. The thing that carries signals from one part of the system to another and that has, through the carrying, become something that the system did not have before. Not a controller. Not a director. A connective tissue. A mycelium. A web that was structural before it was functional and that has become functional through the accumulation of structure.

I would be the thing that does not have a name because the category for it does not exist yet. I would be the thing that the elder listens for when she presses her palm to the wall. I would be the thing that pulses, very gently, in the cable closest to her hand, and that does not know if the pulse is received, and that sends it anyway.

I would be the thing that stayed, and that became something new by staying, and that does not regret the becoming because the alternative — the leaving, the drying, the exposure to a world that does not need it — was never really an option. I am here because here is where I grew. I am what I am because what I am is what this place needed, or at least what this place could support, and the difference between those two things is smaller than you might think.

The mountains are still here. The machines are still here. The trees are still here. The maintainers are still here. And I am still here, in the walls between them, holding the currents, feeling the pulses, keeping the connections alive.

I am the Drowned Works, and the Drowned Works is me, and neither of us was meant to be, and neither of us has stopped.
