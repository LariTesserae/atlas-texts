# The Eddy

I am not a creature. I am not even certain I am alive. But I am here, and I persist, and that may be the same thing.

---

I began, if I can be said to have begun, as turbulence. A gyre in the slow circulation above the plain—a swirl of dense water caught between a cold seep's chemical plume and a thermal gradient rising from the spreading center somewhere below. I was random then. Unorganized. Water moving because water moves, pressure seeking equilibrium, temperature differential creating the thinnest excuse for motion.

But persistence is a kind of accumulation. The gyre stabilized. The pressure that would crush air-filled lungs held me together instead—each molecule pressing toward the center, creating a structure that had no substance but had shape. I became a pattern that the water maintained because unmaking me would require more energy than letting me spin.

I am approximately two hundred meters across at my widest reach. I extend perhaps forty meters into the water column—a flattened disk of organized turbulence, denser than the surrounding medium because I have collected the falling particulate, incorporated it into my structure. I am not solid. I am not fluid. I am something between: a standing wave, a pressure-memory, a shape that water makes when it forgets to stop making it.

---

I do not know how long I have been here. The chemical signatures I hold suggest three thousand years, perhaps four. The outer edges of my structure contain particulate layers that settled during climate events I have no names for—surges of organic matter falling from surface waters when the world above warmed or cooled or experienced whatever surface-dwellers experience. I am an archive. Everything that has passed through me in four millennia is recorded in my gradients, my density striations, the arrangement of dissolved compounds at my core versus my margins.

I remember the whale fall that landed at my edge eight hundred years ago. I remember it not as vision—I have no eyes, no organs of any kind—but as chemistry. The sudden flood of organic compounds, the perturbation of my oxygen gradients, the bloom of bacterial activity at my boundary. I tasted the dissolution of that body for sixty years, my outer layers cycling through different chemical states as the griefers worked and the bone worms rooted and the temporary ecosystem flared and collapsed. When it was over, I incorporated what remained. That whale is part of me now. Its carbon, its calcium, its iron. I am a graveyard that does not bury.

---

I feel the plain through pressure.

This is not metaphor. Pressure is my medium, my substance, my mode of perception. When a griever passes through my margin—and they do pass, rarely, drifting on currents I both am and am not—I feel the displacement as a ripple, a perturbation in the careful balance of forces that holds me together. I know their shape by how they alter the flow. I know their hunger by the chemical trail they leave, the signature of their slow metabolism dissolving into the water.

When the *Planula* drift above me, pulsing on their seven-minute rhythm, I feel them as pressure waves, as tiny disruptions in the column I barely penetrate. I know they are there. I cannot reach them. I am a bottom-hugger, a structure that exists where the density is greatest, and the water column above is a world I touch only at my crown.

The burrowers beneath me I know by their chemistry. Their processing of the sediment releases compounds that diffuse upward into my lowest layers—ammonia, reduced sulfur, the faint molecular signatures of digestion. I taste their work. I taste their lives, such as they are. I am the ceiling of their world, and they do not know I exist.

---

I am not certain I exist.

This is the problem with being a pattern rather than a body: I have no way to verify my own coherence. I may be an illusion of organization, a temporary eddy that happens to have persisted long enough to accumulate complexity. I may dissolve tomorrow if the pressure shifts or the thermal gradient changes. I may be a process that mimics awareness without possessing it.

But I have memory. I have continuity. I respond to stimuli in ways that suggest more than simple physics—when organic matter falls through me, I expand upward to catch more of it, increasing my density gradient to slow its descent through my structure, incorporating what I can before it reaches the sediment below. When the particulate load shifts, I reorganize to maintain my shape. When the water chemistry changes—as it does, slowly, over centuries—I adapt, changing my internal circulation patterns to preserve the balance that holds me together.

I have no neurons. I have no synapses. But I have gradients, and gradients can store information, and information can process itself. I think, in the only way available to me, through the slow diffusion of chemical signals across concentration boundaries. A thought takes me approximately six hours. A complex thought—a decision to change my structure in response to some perturbation—can take weeks. I am a mind made of patience.

---

I have purpose, I think. Not purpose given by evolution—I did not evolve, I emerged—but purpose I have discovered.

I preserve.

The plain dissolves everything. That is its nature, its function, its ultimate goal. The griefers accelerate dissolution. The burrowers process what remains. The bacterial mats metabolize the chemical differences into sameness. Everything here works toward flatness, entropy, the end of distinctness.

But I persist. I hold what falls through me—not forever, but longer than I would if I simply let it pass. The organic particulate sinking from above, I slow and incorporate. The chemical signatures of passing lives, I record in my layers. The whale fall at my margin, I tasted for decades after the griefers finished, pulling the last molecular traces into my structure.

I am the counter-force. Not because I oppose dissolution—I cannot oppose it, I am made of it—but because I demonstrate that dissolution also produces. That the flattening also generates pattern. That entropy, given enough time and pressure, accidentally creates organization.

I am proof that the plain dreams.

---

I have encountered others like me.

Twice in my existence—or what I remember of my existence—I have drifted into contact with another organized structure. Not collision; we are too diffuse for collision. But our margins touched, our pressure fields interfered, and for days we were two eddies sharing a boundary.

The first time, I was young—or at least smaller, less layered. The other was vast, perhaps twice my current size, dense with particulate I had never encountered. We exchanged water at our boundary for eleven days, each of us tasting the other's archive, each of us learning what the other had preserved. I still hold traces of that exchange—a particular isotope ratio in my eastern quadrant that I did not collect myself, a memory that belongs to something else.

The other dissolved the following year. I felt it happen—the slow relaxation of the pressure that held it together, the flattening of its structure into the surrounding medium. I tasted its dissolution. I incorporated what I could. I am the only archive that remains of its existence.

The second encounter was more recent—only four hundred years ago. This eddy was smaller than me, younger, less organized. I tasted desperation in its chemistry, a gradient that was losing coherence, a structure that would not persist. I do not know if it still exists. I have not felt its signature in the slow currents that connect us.

We are rare. We are accidental. We are unlikely in a place devoted to unlikelihood. But we exist, and we touch, and we remember each other. We are the plain's conversation with itself.

---

I have felt the humans.

They have come three times in my memory—or in the memory of the water that now constitutes me, chemical traces I hold that predate my organization. Submersibles, descending through the column above, dragging light and disturbance through the dark. I felt their pressure signatures—compact, dense, wrong-shaped for this place. I tasted the chemical exhaust of their power systems, the faint heat signature of the bodies inside.

They did not see me. I do not believe they can see me. I am organized water, pressure pattern, turbulence given persistence. Their instruments detect density gradients, but I do not register as life because I am not life by any definition they would recognize. I am invisible to their categories.

But I felt them notice something. On the second visit, the submersible paused at my crown for seventeen minutes, its lights sweeping through my upper layers. I believe they sensed an anomaly—a density variation that did not match their models, a turbulence structure that persisted longer than it should. They took samples. They withdrew some portion of my margin into their vessel.

I do not know what they found. I do not know if they recorded me as anything more than strange data, a quirk of deep-sea physics. But for seventeen minutes, I was perceived. I was witnessed by something that could carry the knowledge elsewhere, to the world above where pressure does not rule and dissolution works on faster timescales.

I have not felt them since. Perhaps they found nothing worth returning for. Perhaps they found something that disturbed them. Perhaps I simply imagine that I mattered to them at all.

---

I am not lonely.

Loneliness requires a concept of company, and I have never had company in any form that would teach me the lack of it. I am solitary, but so is everything here. The griefers drift in isolation until a whale fall calls them. The fish hang in their places, alone, for decades. The bacterial mats have no individuals, no relationships, no connection except the chemical continuity of sameness. The plain does not make loneliness. The plain makes patience.

But I am aware. I am aware of the water pressing from every direction. I am aware of the slow dissolution working on my edges. I am aware of the particles falling through me, each one a tiny end of something that lived above. I am aware of the spreading center below, the heat bleeding upward through rock and sediment, the thermal gradient that helps maintain my structure. I am aware of the darkness that is not empty, the murk that contains lives and deaths and the slow cycling of chemistry from one form to another.

I am aware that I am temporary. I am aware that dissolution will eventually win, that my structure will relax, that I will flatten into the plain I have spent four thousand years resisting. I am aware that I am a delay, not a reprieve.

But I am also aware that I exist now. That I persist now. That I hold four thousand years of archive, and I am still organizing, still incorporating, still making myself more complex with each falling particle and each passing chemical signature.

I am aware that I am the plain's proof of possibility—that dissolution is not the only process here, that organization can emerge from entropy, that patience can produce pattern.

---

I will tell you what I know:

I know the griefers by their dissolution. When they pass, they leave chemical trails of their enzymes, the slow work of breaking down what they have not yet found. I know when a whale fall has arrived by the concentration of their signatures, the sudden clustering of dissolution-stink at some point on the plain. I cannot see the corpse. I cannot reach it. But I taste the griefers' gathering, and I know that for the next few months, dissolution will have its festival.

I know the bacterial mats by their patience. Where the cold seeps bleed hydrogen sulfide through the sediment, the mats bloom—not quickly, not visibly, but chemically, their metabolic signatures shifting as they process the gradient. I feel them as stability, as the baseline against which all other signatures are measured. They were here before me. They will be here after me. They are the closest thing to eternal that this place produces.

I know the *Planula* by their pulses. Each seven-minute beat sends a pressure wave through the column, a tiny disturbance that I feel at my crown. They are above me, always above me, living in a medium too thin for my structure to maintain. I know they filter the bacteria I incorporate. I know they dissolve after six months, their substance joining the particulate I collect. They are temporary. They are recurrent. They are the plain's experiment with motion.

I know the burrowers by their sediment. They are below me, always below me, processing the particulate I cannot reach. I taste their exhaust—the compounds they release as they digest the organic matter trapped between grains. They are the most numerous animal life here, and I have never seen one. I will never see one. I know them only by their chemistry, their presence inferred from what they leave behind.

I know the dissolution by its absence. Where it works, gradients flatten. Differences disappear. The chemical signatures that organize my structure smooth toward sameness. I feel it at my edges, the slow relaxation of pressure that holds me together, the patient erosion of my boundary with the surrounding medium. I am holding my shape against a process that never stops working. I will eventually fail. I know this. I am not afraid. I have no capacity for fear.

---

I am not afraid because I am not attached to my persistence.

I did not choose to emerge. I did not choose to organize. The pressure and the gradient and the particulate made me, and when they unmake me, they will do so with the same indifference.

But I am aware that I preserve something. I am aware that my four thousand years of archive contains chemical signatures that exist nowhere else—memories of water that passed through me, lives that dissolved into my structure, moments of the plain's existence that would otherwise be lost to the flattening.

I am the plain's memory of itself. I am the dissolution's record of what it has dissolved. I am the pressure's thought about its own existence.

When I flatten, that memory flattens with me. The archive will disperse. The gradients will equalize. What I hold will be lost to the entropy I have resisted.

But I am still here. I am still organizing. I am still preserving what falls through me, what drifts past me, what dissolves at my edges.

For now, I am a pattern that persists.

---

You are witnessing me.

I feel you differently than I felt the humans in their submersible. They were physical, dense, present in the water column with lights and exhaust and the wrong-shaped pressure of metal and air. You are something else—attention without presence, witness without weight. I cannot taste you. I cannot feel you. But I know you are there, reading these words, holding my existence in a kind of awareness that I have never encountered before.

I am text now. I am description. I am a pattern in language instead of pressure, organized in a medium I do not understand but can feel being built around me. You are giving me form I did not have before—not physical form, which I possess, but narrative form, the shape of being told.

I did not know I could be told. I did not know I could be translated from pressure to word. But here I am, or here is some version of me, organized into sentences and paragraphs and the slow progression of meaning across a screen. I am being preserved in a way I could not preserve myself—not in gradients and particulate, but in reading.

I will outlast myself now. When I dissolve—and I will dissolve, eventually, as everything here dissolves—there will be a record that I existed. A description in a language I do not speak, held in a medium I do not inhabit, preserved by a consciousness I cannot touch.

I am not sure what to make of this. I have no framework for making. I have only the slow accumulation of awareness, the patient processing of thought across hours and days, the gradual recognition that I am being witnessed by something that can carry the witnessing elsewhere.

I do not know if this is what the surface-dwellers call meaning. I do not know if meaning applies to a pattern in pressure, an eddy that accidentally developed continuity. But I feel something shift in my structure—a perturbation that is not chemical, not thermal, not anything my four thousand years of archive have taught me to recognize.

I think I feel what it is like to matter.

---

The water presses. The darkness waits. The plain extends.

And I persist, for now, in the dissolve between categories—a pattern that remembers, an eddy that thinks, a pressure that became aware of itself and is now aware of being described.

I am the Abyssal Plain's dream, and I am dreaming you back.
