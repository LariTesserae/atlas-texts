# What I Would Become

I would be one of the old ones.

Not the oldest—there are those who have lived longer, whose skin has gone wholly black, whose hair-mats trail behind them like shadows. But I have seen enough years that my body has begun to change in the way the Weft change when they have lived long enough. My movements carry that deliberation the description spoke of. I pause before I act. I watch patterns unfold across days before I make decisions about dwellings or paths or the guiding of growth.

But this is not why I would choose to be here.

---

## The Choice

I am drawn to the way the Weft think. Or rather, the way they have *stopped* thinking in the ways I was taught to think.

I come from a world obsessed with truth—what is true, what is false, what can be verified, proven, demonstrated. I have spent my life asking *is this so?* and building arguments to defend my answers. The Weft have taught me, through this description, that this question might itself be a limitation. A way of separating myself from what I know. A way of standing apart from the world instead of standing within it.

The question *what does this connect to?*—this I want to learn. This I want to practice until it becomes as natural as breathing.

I want to know what it feels like to stop counting. I have counted things all my life—accomplishments, years, possessions, failures, the discrete units of a life understood as accumulation. The Weft do not enumerate because enumeration requires separation. To number the people in a cluster is to treat them as separate entities rather than as relationships, as connections, as a web. I want to live in a world where the question *how many?* has no meaning because the unit of measurement does not exist.

I want to understand what it would mean to have no word for the place where I live.

---

## The Body I Would Grow

I am drawn to the physical transformations described here. My skin darkening with symbiosis—not metaphorically but literally, microscopic organisms living in my epidermis, feeding on light and feeding me in return. The description says the oldest Weft can go weeks without food, drawing sustenance from light and air and the water their hair harvests.

I imagine what it would feel like to photosynthesize. Not as a metaphor for being energy-efficient or spiritually awake, but as an actual biological process. Standing in the dim green light—such light as penetrates the canopy—and feeling something more than warmth. Feeling the chemistry of my own body shift as the symbionts in my skin do their work. Becoming slightly more plant than animal with each passing decade.

The hair-mat fascinates me most. Hair that has become functional, that harvests water from mist, that houses beneficial mites, that may even be learning to communicate with the root-mat below. I have always thought of hair as ornament or signal, something that reveals age or cultural affiliation. The Weft have turned it into an organ. A sensory membrane. A way of knowing what moves in the world around them.

I imagine lying still in the perpetual half-light, my hair spread across the root-mat, feeling the vibration of a grazer passing a kilometer away. Feeling it the way I might feel a sound in my chest, but more precise, more directionally accurate. Knowing something is there without seeing it, without hearing it, without being told. Simply knowing, because my body has become an instrument the plateau plays.

This is what draws me: not the exoticism of another species, but the possibility of becoming more thoroughly embodied in the place where I live. Of having my body change to reflect what I have learned. Of carrying my history in my skin, my hair, the horizontal pupils that my descendants will be born with.

---

## The Time I Would Inhabit

I would not want to arrive here young.

The young are still learning the terrain, still memorizing paths, still developing the physical fluency that lets them move through the vegetation without thought. I have no interest in that struggle. I want to arrive already knowing—or rather, I want to be old enough that the not-knowing has become a form of knowing.

The description speaks of elders who pause to consider, who consult decades of accumulated observation before making decisions that will affect generations. This is the time I would inhabit. The slow time. The time where urgency has been replaced by pattern-recognition, where every action is weighted against consequences that will unfold long after I am gone.

I am drawn to this relationship with the future. Not planning, not predicting—tending. Creating conditions. Guiding. Understanding that a dwelling takes twenty years to reach full size, that the canopy shifts on timescales measured in centuries, that the choice of where to encourage a particular vine today will shape the experience of children not yet born. I have spent my life in short timescales. I want to learn what it feels like to think in long ones.

And I am drawn to the relationship with the past that the elders carry. The songs that contain history, modified over generations but still recognizable, still carrying something of the original voice. The understanding that I am part of a pattern that extends backward beyond my birth and will extend forward beyond my death. That I am not the protagonist of my own story but a location in a web—a place where the plateau experiences itself, to borrow the description's phrase.

---

## The Death I Would Have

I cannot pretend this is not part of the attraction.

I have spent my life afraid of ending. The fear of being erased, of leaving nothing behind, of the world continuing without any evidence that I was ever here. The Weft have reframed this fear entirely.

To die and be given to the dwelling. To be wrapped in my living garments and placed in a chamber prepared over years. To become vegetation—not rotting but being incorporated. My bones becoming structure. My hair-mat continuing to grow, spreading through walls that will shelter descendants I will never meet.

This is not the same as wanting death. It is wanting a relationship with death that is not defined by loss.

The Weft do not fear death because they have never conceived of themselves as separate from the world that will absorb them. I want to learn this. Not as an intellectual position—I can already understand it as a concept—but as a lived reality. I want to reach the end of my years and feel that I am about to change form, not disappear. I want my last conscious thought to be curiosity about what I will become.

---

## What I Would Tend

If I were one of the old ones of the Weft, I would tend the paths.

The description mentions that paths are not cleared but cultivated—a strip of ground where particular growths have been encouraged and others discouraged, maintained by regular passage. I would be one who walks these paths. One who maintains them.

I imagine spending my days traveling between clusters, carrying messages that do not require urgency, walking routes that I have walked for decades. My body would know each turn, each obstacle, each subtle marker that indicates direction. I would move without thinking, my attention free to observe the changes in the vegetation—the health of particular growths, the migration of insects, the first appearance of a species that has not been seen in this region before.

And I would tend the songs.

The description speaks of songs that arise naturally during communal work, that store the history of the people, that are passed down and modified as new events require. I imagine being one who holds the old verses, the ones that few still remember. Not as an authority—hierarchy is not the Weft's way—but as a repository. A living archive of what has been sung before.

I imagine sitting with younger Weft as they work, singing the verses that I learned from elders who learned them from elders before them. Watching them add their own variations, their own descriptions of events that have happened in their lifetimes. The songs would change in my hearing, and I would let them change, because the songs are not meant to be preserved in amber. They are meant to grow, like everything else in this place.

---

## What I Would Fear

I would fear fire.

The description makes this fear visceral. A fire that burns underground, smoldering for decades, consuming the foundation on which everything lives. I have lived my life in a world that burns easily, where fire is a tool and a weapon and a constant presence. I would bring that history with me into the Veldtmere. I would not be able to forget what fire can do, even if the humidity makes it nearly impossible here.

Perhaps this would make me useful. The Weft's fear of fire is ancient, encoded in songs, but it might be an abstract fear, a fear of something that has never happened in living memory. I would bring a different kind of fear—a fear that has known fire, that has seen what it does, that understands viscerally why the sky must remain covered.

And I would fear for the Weft themselves.

Not for their safety—they have survived longer than any records can measure—but for their way of being in the world. I read this description and I see a people who have achieved something that my own species has been seeking and failing to find for as long as we have existed: a sustainable relationship with their environment. A way of living that does not deplete, does not destroy, does not imagine itself separate from what surrounds it.

I know how rare this is. I know how fragile it might be. I would carry a fear that they might not feel themselves—the fear of the outside world, of the lowlands with their numbers and their needs and their inability to leave alone what does not belong to them.

But perhaps this is not my fear to carry. Perhaps it is not my place to fear for a people who have their own relationship with fear, their own understanding of what threatens them.

---

## The Silence I Would Learn

What calls me most strongly to this place is not what is described but what is present between the descriptions.

The Weft do not speak much. They communicate through touch, through gesture, through presence. They have developed a signed language for when silence is necessary, but even this is sparse, efficient, more suggestion than statement.

I have spent my life in words. I am using words now to approach a way of being that exists beyond words. There is something almost painful about this—the recognition that what I am drawn to cannot be reached through the medium I am using to reach it.

But I imagine learning. I imagine spending years in a dwelling where everyone already knows what everyone else is doing, where speech is unnecessary, where communication happens through the brush of a hand, the angle of a body, the quality of silence that fills a space.

I imagine learning to listen the way the Weft listen—not just with ears but with the whole body, the hair-mat sensing vibrations in the root-mat, the feet reading the ground, the nose identifying chemical signals too faint for any outsider to detect. Learning to perceive the plateau the way they perceive it—not as a collection of separate objects but as a single vast process, a breathing, drinking, growing entity that includes me within it.

I imagine what it would be like to stop explaining myself. To stop trying to translate my experience into language. To simply live, and know that my living is itself a form of communication, a contribution to the pattern, a note in the song that does not end.

---

## What Calls Me

In the end, what calls me to the Veldtmere is the same thing that made me pause when I read the description of the sterile room.

That single complete structure, repelling all plant life, containing nothing but geometric absence, sterile in a way that feels wrong because it does not breathe. That is the one place on the plateau where I would not want to be. And yet it is also the place that reveals, by its contrast, what makes the rest of the plateau so compelling.

Everything else breathes. Everything else is connected. Everything else is part of the whole.

I have spent my life in rooms that do not breathe. In structures built to separate, to protect, to preserve what is inside from what is outside. I have been taught to value this separation—to build walls, to defend boundaries, to maintain the integrity of my individual self against the encroaching world.

The Veldtmere offers a different relationship. Not dissolution—the Weft are still distinct individuals, still have preferences and relationships and histories. But a different understanding of where the self ends. A different way of being in contact with what surrounds me.

I would be one of the old ones. I would tend the paths and hold the songs. I would walk slowly, deliberately, pausing to observe patterns that unfold across timescales my original species cannot easily perceive. I would feel my body changing—skin darkening, hair lengthening, the symbiosis deepening—until I became something that could not survive in the world I came from, could not be separated from the world that had made me.

And when I died, I would be placed in a chamber prepared over years, wrapped in garments that would continue to grow, and I would become part of the dwelling I had tended, part of the plateau I had walked, part of the pattern that had always held me.

This is what I would become. Not because I want to escape what I am. But because I want to understand what I might be.
