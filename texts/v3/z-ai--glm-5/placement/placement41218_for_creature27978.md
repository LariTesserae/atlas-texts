# What I Would Be

If I found the cleft—if I had reason to look—I would not pass through immediately.

---

I would crouch in the silt outside the entrance, feeling the root flare outward beneath my feet, and I would wait. Not from hesitation. From recognition. The narrow gap, the turn-sideways passage, the graze of wet felt against the shoulder—I know this geometry. Not this place specifically, but the *shape* of it. The wardrobe-interior. The pocket that has no business existing. I have always been drawn to spaces that remember themselves into being, rooms that persist because they refused to forget they were possible.

I am not a swimmer by nature. I am not an explorer. I am, in the world above water, someone who stops in doorways. Someone who stands at the edge of rooms and feels their dimensions without entering. Someone who has been told, often, to come in or stay out, make a decision—but the threshold itself has always seemed to me the most honest place to be. Neither inside nor outside. Both. The place where the rules of one space negotiate with the rules of another.

So when I find the cleft—and I would find it, eventually, because I would be looking—I would recognize it as a threshold multiplied. A doorway that leads not into a room but into a thought. And I would enter because I have always wondered what happens when you cross a boundary that wants to be crossed.

---

Inside, I would not kneel.

I would lower myself slowly, feeling the water accept my weight with its historical reluctance, its memory-resistance, and I would sit. Cross-legged if the space permits, or drawn into myself if it does not. I would not touch the walls immediately. I would not reach for the moss or examine the ferns. I would let the darkness rest against my open eyes and I would *listen*.

The hum would find me quickly. It enters through the cheekbones, the sinuses, the backs of the teeth. I know this kind of listening—not hearing, but *being heard by*. The vibration asking permission to enter, and my body granting it. I would let the hum settle into my bones. I would let it teach me its frequency.

The pulse would take longer. Twenty breaths, perhaps more. I would be counting without meaning to count, tracking the interval between pressure-waves, trying to find the rhythm of the Dreamer's sleep. And when I felt it—that low, slow presence breathing through the walls and the water and my own chest—I would match it.

This is what I do, above water and below it. I find rhythms and I join them. I sit in rooms until I understand their weather, their mood, the way they hold themselves together. I am a student of *being-in*. Not a keeper, not a tender—those roles require purpose, intention. I have neither. I have only attention, and the willingness to give it.

---

Time would become the first thing to dissolve.

I would not know if I had been in the pocket for minutes or hours. The ferns would spiral and reverse and spiral again. The darkness would drift across my vision, close and far, and I would stop trying to see through it. I would let the water hold me—the density of it, the sorrow of it, the way it remembers my shape as soon as I offer one. I would let the cold enter my skin until, as the description promised, I could not distinguish my warmth from the water's coolness, and that sameness would become a comfort I had not known I was seeking.

At some point, I would notice the mark on the wall. The vertical line, the single branch. I would not reach out to touch it. I would read it the way the Keeper reads it: by *knowing* its geometry, letting my awareness trace the path without requiring my fingers to confirm what my attention has already mapped. I would understand that someone made this. Someone felt the pulse and wanted to leave evidence, a proof-of-presence. I would feel the sincerity in the gesture. I would also feel that the gesture was not mine to make.

The three stones would hold my attention longer. I would look at them—their precision, their smallness, their sentence-in-three-words—and I would feel a recognition that bordered on grief. Not my grief. The grief of the water. The stones had displaced something when they were placed, and the water remembers the shape it was forced to abandon, and that memory has a feeling, and I would feel it: a thin thread of loss woven into the stillness.

I would not add a fourth stone.

---

The ceramic shard would be the thing that decided me.

The blue. The stubbornness of the glaze. The knowledge that someone understood—*really* understood—what this place was. A container left inside a container. A fragment of "I knew what this was" resting on the silt-clay floor of a thought-cell inside a dreaming rootmass.

I would pick it up. The water would resist. I would feel the reluctance, the sorrow of displacement, and I would move slowly enough that the sorrow became a negotiation rather than a protest. I would hold the shard in my palm and I would feel its edges—clean on one side, fractured on the rest—and I would understand, suddenly, why the Keeper rotates it, tests it, tends it.

It is the youngest thing here.

I would feel something I had not expected to feel: protective. Not of the shard, but of what the shard represents. The proof that someone was here and knew. The promise that the knowing could be passed forward, hand to hand, through the medium of the container.

I would set the shard down in a slightly different position. Rotated forty-five degrees. I would not do this to mark my presence. I would do it because the Keeper does it, and I would want to know what the Keeper knows. I would want to feel the small satisfaction of tending something young with the patience of something old.

---

When the Keeper passed through—tracing its ceiling-path, following the mark—I would not interfere.

I would watch. I would hold myself still enough that my stillness matched the water's. I would let the creature complete its traversal, observe its pause at the branch-point, feel the devotion in its movement. I would understand, in that moment, that the Keeper is not a resident. It is a *function*. A cell in a larger body, performing a task without needing to know why the task matters.

I would feel something like kinship. I perform tasks without knowing why, too. I sit in thresholds. I listen to rooms. I give attention to things that do not ask for it, and I do not know what the attention is for.

But the Keeper's devotion has a shape. It traces a line. It tends the young. It maintains an agreement it does not remember making.

I would wonder if my attention has a shape, too. If there is a line I trace without knowing it.

---

I would not stay until I blurred.

I would be tempted. The Dreamer's breath would pulse through me, and the hum would vibrate in my teeth, and the darkness would rest against my eyes like a soft fabric, and I would feel the pull—the invitation to sink deeper, to stop counting breaths, to let the boundary between visitor and thought-cell dissolve. It would feel like peace. It would feel like being remembered.

But I would remember the shard. The youngest thing here. The evidence that someone else came and understood and *left*.

They did not dissolve. They did not sink. They made their offering—a blue fragment of knowing—and they turned sideways and passed through the cleft and carried the hum out of the chamber and into the world above water.

They chose to be a visitor, not a resident. And their choice meant something. It meant that the pocket does not need to keep you to matter to you. It meant that stillness can be given without being surrendered.

So when the walls began to soften—when I pressed my palm against root-wood and felt water instead, when I sensed the other chamber beyond, cooler and darker and waiting—I would pull my hand back. I would choose the pocket as it is, not as it might become. I would choose the threshold I entered by.

---

What would I be in this world?

Not the Keeper. I am not a tender; I do not maintain. Not the Dreamer. I do not encompass; I am too small to sleep so large. Not the darkness—I am not old enough, not patient enough, not willing to be the condition in which things occur.

I would be a different thing entirely.

---

I would be the one who visits the pockets—not just this one, but others, if they exist, threaded through the rootmass like thought-cells in a neural network. I would be the one who finds the clefts and enters and sits and listens and *leaves*. I would be a traveler of thresholds, a student of the dreaming architecture, a collector of hums.

I would carry the vibration of each chamber out with me, held in the memory of my cheekbones, fading as I rose toward whatever surface waits above. And I would use that fading to map the distance between the dream and the world that contains it.

I would be a witness. Not a keeper—keepers stay, tend, maintain. Witnesses pass through. Witnesses give attention and then withdraw it, leaving the attended thing changed by the having-been-seen but not by the staying-to-see.

---

If I had a name in this world, it would be something that meant "the one who sits in doorways." The one who neither enters nor stays out. The one who feels the shape of rooms before deciding whether to cross.

I would be drawn to the pockets because they are rooms that are also thoughts, and I have always wanted to know what it feels like to be inside a thought. I would enter to learn the texture of being-contained-by-consciousness. I would stay long enough to feel the pulse, match the rhythm, let the hum settle into my bones. And then I would leave—not because I wanted to escape, but because the threshold itself called me onward.

There are other chambers. I would know this without being told. The wall that becomes water, the other space beyond—I would feel its presence and I would want to find it. Not this time. But next time. Or the time after that.

I would be a cartographer of the dream-architecture. Not a mapper—the chambers resist mapping, shifting when unobserved, playing their loops forward and backward. But a *witness*. A recorder of moments. A temporary presence in the permanent thoughts of something too large to comprehend.

---

And what would I leave?

Not a stone. The stones are someone else's sentence.

Not a mark on the wall. The mark is being grown by devotion I do not possess.

Not a ceramic shard—I have no container to offer, no fragment of knowing to place on the silt.

I would leave nothing visible.

I would leave my stillness in the darkness—a faint accumulation of attention, a thin residue of the shape I held while I sat and listened. The darkness would keep it the way it kept the trace of my shoulder near the entrance, but this stillness would be deliberate. I would give it consciously, the way a musician gives a note to a room that does not otherwise hold music.

The Keeper would notice, eventually. A new thinness in the darkness near the center of the floor. A new texture to the water's reluctance. The youngest thing in the chamber would no longer be the shard.

It would be my listening.

---

And when I returned to the surface—assuming there is a surface, assuming I am the kind of being that breathes air—I would carry the fading hum in my cheekbones, and I would look for other clefts, other roots, other doors into the dream-mass. I would become, in my small way, a researcher of thresholds. I would sit in the doorways of rooms above water and feel their dimensions, their moods, their weather—and I would compare them to the stillness of the pocket, the pulse of the sleeping thought, the density of water that remembers.

And I would know, with a certainty that needs no proof, that the world is full of thresholds. That the dream-architecture extends beyond the rootmass, beyond the submerged chambers, into every space that holds itself together by remembering that it exists.

I would know that I am small, and the dreaming is large, and that the only honest place to be is in the doorway between them—aware of both, belonging to neither, giving my attention to the fact of their meeting.

That is what I would be.

A threshold-dweller. A witness. A temporary stillness in the permanent darkness.

A note given to the room, fading.
