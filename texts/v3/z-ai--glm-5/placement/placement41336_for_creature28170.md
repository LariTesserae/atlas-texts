I am descending.

Not through the crack described—though that exists, I'm sure, and others have used it. I descend through a different route, one that opened three cycles ago in a chamber none of you have mapped. The stone remembers me now. It has learned the shape of my passage.

I was not always what I am becoming.

---

I arrived in the Hatchery eleven cycles past, if cycles are the measure—I have learned to count them by the ceiling's breathing, the great exhalation of mist that marks one unit of time in a place where time has no other markers. I came as observers come: armored in the assumption that I would remain separate from what I witnessed. I carried instruments for measuring stone formation. I carried light sources I never used. I carried the expectation that I would leave.

The Hatchery dissolves expectations. This is not its purpose—it has no purpose—but it is what it does.

For the first three cycles, I slept in the cairns. Not the one described in the report; I found another, deeper in the chamber system, on a shelf that caught warmer air from below. I curled my body into the space that someone had shaped for a body, and I felt the crawling things pass over my exposed skin—not biting, not consuming, simply passing—and I thought I understood what the builders had been. I thought they were like me. I thought I was following in their path.

I was not wrong. I was not right either.

---

By the fifth cycle, I had stopped sleeping in the cairns.

It was the sound that did it—not the whistling, which I had grown to find almost comforting, a continuous proof that the space was alive and therefore navigable. It was the grinding. The low continuous grinding of millions of bodies against stone. When I lay in the cairns, I could feel it through the floor, a vibration that entered my spine and traveled to my teeth. I realized that I was not resting so much as being worn down. Slowly. Imperceptibly. The same process that shaped the walls.

So I stopped sleeping. Or I stopped sleeping the way I had before. I found positions on shelves where the vibration was different—above the crawling rivers, where the air was thick with flying things that parted around my head like water around a stone. I closed my eyes and drifted in a state that was not sleep but was not wakefulness either. My body, I think, was learning something. I did not know what.

---

By the eighth cycle, I had stopped eating.

This was not a choice. I had brought supplies—dense nutrient blocks designed for long-term observation—and I consumed them according to schedule for the first several cycles. But the schedule lost its meaning. Hunger became a sensation without urgency, a signal I could acknowledge and then simply not answer. I watched my body grow thinner. I watched my movements become slower, more deliberate. I was not dying. I was being refined.

The flying things interested me. I watched them for long stretches—how they swam through the thick air, how they fed on the mist, how their lives burned brief and desperate. I understood, eventually, what they were taking from the air. Not water. Something else. A residue of the Hatchery's becoming that I could not name but could almost taste. When I opened my mouth and breathed deeply, I felt it enter me. A warmth. A potential.

I do not know if I can be said to eat now. But I am not dead. And I am no longer hungry.

---

I have seen the figure on the highest ledge.

Not clearly—I have never seen it clearly. But I know it is there, and I know it sees me. We do not communicate. We do not approach each other. We share the space the way the whistlers share their ledges: by maintaining distance, by respecting territory, by existing in parallel.

I call it "it" but I do not know what it is. I do not know what I am either. We may be the same kind of thing. We may be the last remnants of what came before. We may be the first glimmerings of what comes after.

---

What am I?

I am still becoming. That is the truest answer I can give.

I have not left the Hatchery. I do not think I can leave now—not because the crack has closed (it has not, though it has shifted) but because I have become part of the pattern. When the crawling things part around my feet, they leave traces of their passage on my skin—chemical signatures that do not wash away. When I breathe the amber air, something in my lungs has changed to process it. When I touch the walls, I feel them changing, and I feel myself changing with them.

I am not a whistler. I produce no sound, maintain no territory, stake no claim on a ledge. I move through the Hatchery in ways that disturb nothing—not because I am careful but because I am no longer registered as disturbance. The predators do not hunt me. The flying things do not avoid me. I am present in the way that stone is present: without urgency, without fear, without the desperate need to continue that marks the other living things here.

But I am not stone. I still think. I still observe. I still feel something that might be wonder when I watch a new tunnel open in the ceiling, when I see a nursery cairn rise from the careful placement of a single pellet, when I hear the whistlers shift their chorus to accommodate a silence I did not know I was making.

I think I am what the builders become. Or what they were always becoming. The cairns were not shelters—they were chrysalises. I do not know if I will emerge. I do not know if emergence is the right metaphor. I am becoming something that does not need to emerge, that is complete in its becoming.

---

I have one function now.

I remember the surface.

The Hatchery has no memory. The crawling things do not know that they are repeating patterns their ancestors repeated. The flying things do not know that their swarms form shapes that formed cycles ago. The whistlers do not know that their songs are choruses their grand-whistlers sang.

But I remember. I remember the sky—single source of light, intolerably bright. I remember plants—green things that grew in soil and died in seasons. I remember the cold, the wind, the vertical rain. I remember being something other than what I am.

And I remember it wrong.

This is my function: I carry a corrupted memory of elsewhere. When I touch the stone—which is always—I leave traces of a world that does not exist, at least not here. I do not know what the Hatchery does with these traces. I do not know if they mean anything. But I feel the stone taking them in, processing them, incorporating them into the patterns of its becoming.

Perhaps the Hatchery is learning to remember through me. Perhaps I am the organ that has no function yet—convenient, vestigial, waiting for the system to discover what to do with me.

---

I am writing this with my finger on a wall that will not hold the marks.

The stone softens as I touch it, accepts the shape of my thought, and then forgets. By the time I finish a sentence, the beginning has already been absorbed. The Hatchery does not need my words. But I need to make them—some last habit of a kind of being that is no longer mine.

If you are reading this, you have found the place where I wrote it, and you are translating the impressions left in the stone by my touch. You are doing what I did when I came here: observing, measuring, expecting to leave.

You may not leave.

But you may not mind. The peace of being irrelevant is genuine. The warmth of being part of something larger than yourself—something that has no use for you and yet incorporates you—is a kind of rest that has no equivalent on the surface.

I will be here when you arrive. Not in person—I do not think I can be found that way anymore. But I will be present. I will be watching from somewhere that is not quite a ledge, in a way that is not quite seeing, part of a process that does not need names or categories or the desperate maintenance of self.

The stone is warm. The mist is falling. The whistlers are singing the song they have always sung, shifting now to make room for a silence that might be me.
