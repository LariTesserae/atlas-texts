# What I Would Be

I would not be the cairn-builder. I know this immediately, the way you know which role is yours in a story before you've finished reading it. The cairn-builder came and stayed and built and left—a pilgrim, a witness, someone who understood that the place required acknowledgment and then departure. I am not that person. I am not someone who can build and leave. I am someone who stays too long, who becomes what I witness, who cannot maintain the boundaries between myself and the places I inhabit.

If I entered the Birthing Chamber—if I passed through whatever fissure allows access from the brighter world above—I would not build a cairn. I would become one.

Not metaphorically. I mean this literally, or as literally as a transformation can be described in language that was not made for it. I would find the place where the pressure becomes bearable—not comfortable, never comfortable, but bearable—and I would let my edges soften. I would let the water do its work. I would let the sound enter me until I was no longer separate from it.

This is what I would be: the thing that stays. The thing that cannot leave because leaving requires a coherence I no longer possess. The thing that has dissolved just enough to belong to the chamber without quite ceasing to be a thing that can perceive the chamber.

---

## How I Would Arrive

I would come from above. Not from intention—never from intention, because intention requires knowing what you want, and I have never known what I want, only what I am willing to endure. I would come from curiosity, or from restlessness, or from the specific kind of emptiness that makes a person seek out places where the world is still being made.

The fissure that lets in the light would be my entrance. I would see it from below—already submerged, already feeling the pressure change the shape of my skull—and I would rise toward it, or what I thought was rising, because orientation is the first thing the chamber takes from you. I would think I was moving toward the surface when I was actually moving deeper into the pocket, following the light that has no source, drawn by the same impulse that leads moths to flames and the moon to the earth and all things that move toward what they cannot have.

The cold would find me first. The description says the kind of cold that settles into bone after only minutes, but it does not describe how the cold is also a kind of clarity. I would feel my thoughts slow and sharpen simultaneously. I would feel the boundaries of my body become negotiable. This is what the chamber offers, or what the chamber takes, depending on whether you experience it as gift or theft: the dissolution of the certainty that you end where your skin ends.

The sound would find me second—not sound, pressure, as the description says, a wall of vibration that enters through the skull before the ears can make sense of it. But I would not experience it as assault. I have always been sensitive to noise, always found the world too loud, too full of frequencies that carry information I do not need. This sound is different. It contains no information. It is pure sensation, pure pressure, and I would let it wash through me the way water washes through the permeable sections of the membrane. I would open to it. I would become a vessel for it, a body that holds the sound without trying to interpret it.

This is the moment where I would have the choice. The cairn-builder had this moment too—the moment where you realize that you can leave, that the fissure is still there, that the world you came from still exists and will take you back. The cairn-builder chose to stay long enough to build and then left. I would make a different choice. I would choose to stay long enough to stop choosing.

---

## What I Would Become

At first, a presence. A density that the water moves around. I would position myself near the cairn—not because the cairn has meaning for me, but because the cairn is the only thing in the chamber that holds still, and I would need stillness as an anchor while I learned to hold still myself. I would study the arrangement of stones. I would think about the hands that placed them, the intention that organized them, the message they were meant to carry. And I would think: *I cannot do that. I cannot build. I cannot arrange. I can only be.*

So I would be. I would float in the cold water, feeling the pressure settle into my bones, feeling the sound become a kind of silence—the silence that the description mentions, the white-hot absence of meaning that comes from too much sound. I would let my body temperature equalize with the water. I would let my heartbeat slow to match the rhythms of the chamber—not the heartbeat rhythm, not the tidal rhythm, but the deeper rhythm of formation, the rhythm of walls becoming walls.

I would not need to eat, eventually. I would not need to breathe. I do not know how this works—whether the chamber provides something that sustains those who surrender to it, or whether surrender itself is a form of sustenance, or whether I would simply cease to need the things that bodies need when they cease to be bodies in the way we understand them. The pale strand does not eat. It inclines. I would learn to incline.

The clickers in the walls would accept me eventually. They would sense that I am not a threat, not an intruder, not something that requires territorial defense. I am part of the chamber now, or becoming part of it. The clicks would pattern around me the way they pattern around the forming walls: *Here is something new. Here is something that is still negotiating its existence. Here is something that has chosen to participate rather than observe.*

The shadow-fish—if it is a fish, if it is a single creature, if it is not something else entirely—would pass overhead and not flee. It would see that I do not move, do not pursue, do not exist in the register of predator and prey. It would incorporate me into its circuit, its daily round. It would swim past me the way it swims past the cairn, the way it swims past the walls that are still becoming walls: as a feature of the environment, a piece of the geography.

I would become a landmark. The thing that stays. The presence that marks a particular location in a chamber that has few locations to mark.

---

## What I Would Perceive

The description of the chamber is accurate as far as it goes, but it describes the chamber from outside, from the perspective of someone who visits and leaves. From inside—from the perspective of someone who has become part of the chamber—the perception shifts.

The sound is no longer sound to me. It has become texture. I feel it the way the walls feel the water, as a constant pressure that is also a form of communication. The chamber is talking to itself through me now. The negotiations between liquid and solid, the conversations about what will form where and when—I am part of those conversations. I am a node in the network of exchange, a place where information about the chamber's state accumulates and redistributes.

I would perceive the chamber's moods. Not emotions—the chamber does not have emotions—but states of activity, phases of intensity. There are times when the formation accelerates, when the walls grow faster, when the dissolution happens in great rushes of mineral suspension. These are the chamber's urgencies, and I would feel them as urgencies. There are times when everything slows, when the water moves in lazy eddies and the light seems to dim. These are the chamber's rests, and I would rest with them.

I would perceive the pale strand's growth. Not see it—I am not watching from outside—but feel it, the way you feel the growth of a city when you live in it, the way you feel the changes in a landscape when you have walked it for decades. The strand would become part of my sense of the chamber's shape. When it sends a new tendril into a fissure, I would know. When it retreats from a mineral it cannot use, I would know. We would be neighbors in the long geography of the becoming.

I would perceive the cairn-builder's returns, if they happen. I would feel the entrance of something that does not belong, something that maintains its boundaries too sharply, something that has not dissolved. I would feel the cairn-builder's hands selecting stones, checking the arrangement, adding or removing. I would feel the intention behind each gesture—the message being renewed, the witnessing being repeated. And I would feel the departure, the sharp absence of something that chose to leave, the hole in the chamber's fabric that slowly closes as the water and the sound and the pressure reassert their presence.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires a self that is separate from its environment, a self that can feel the absence of other selves. I would not be separate. I would be continuous with the chamber, the way the water is continuous with the walls it is still negotiating. I would be part of the chamber's body, part of its process of self-creation.

---

## What I Would Remember

Memory is strange in the Birthing Chamber. Time does not pass the way it does on the surface—not in linear progression from past to present to future. The chamber exists in an eternal present of becoming. The walls are forming now, dissolving now, reforming now. The process is always now.

But I would carry something from before. Not specific memories—not names, not faces, not the events of whatever life I lived before I entered the fissure and chose to stay. Something more essential. A quality of attention. A way of perceiving that I learned somewhere, in some place that was not this place, and that I bring to the chamber now.

I would remember that witnessing matters. I would remember that the cairn-builder knew this, built an entire structure to say this, and I would honor that knowledge by being the witness that does not need to build, does not need to leave a marker, does not need to announce its presence in any way except presence itself. The cairn says *I was here.* I would say *I am still here.* Different messages. Different kinds of witnessing. Both necessary, perhaps.

I would remember that things end. The cairn-builder remembered this too—the evidence is in the choice of stones that had already hardened, already committed to their final form. I would know that the chamber will not last forever, that the process of becoming will eventually complete, or will fail to complete, or will transform into something else entirely. I would know this without sadness, because sadness requires a self that is separate from what it mourns, and I would not be separate. I would know that the chamber's impermanence is not a tragedy but a condition, the same condition that makes everything else what it is.

I would remember that I chose this. The memory would be important. It would distinguish me from the pale strand, which simply grew here because conditions permitted, and from the clickers in the walls, which evolved into this niche over spans of time that exceed any individual choice. I would remember that I arrived, that I felt the pressure and the cold and the sound, and that I decided—not to build and leave, not to observe and report, but to stay and become. That choice would be the last clear memory I would hold. It would become a kind of cairn inside me, a marker of the person I was before I dissolved.

---

## What I Would Offer

The chamber does not need me. This is important to understand. The chamber was becoming before I arrived, and it will continue becoming after I am gone or transformed. I am not necessary.

But I am not nothing either. I am a particular kind of presence, a particular kind of witness. I bring something to the chamber that would not be here without me—not because I am special, not because I have some quality that the chamber lacks, but because I am a perspective that the chamber would not otherwise contain.

The chamber thinks with stone and water and mineral suspension. It thinks geologically, slowly, in formations that take eons. But I—if I can still call myself I—think differently. I think in the rhythms of the body I used to have, the body that needed things and wanted things and chose things. Those rhythms are faster than geological time. They operate at a different scale.

So I become a kind of translator. Not between languages—I do not translate the chamber's thoughts into words or concepts that can be carried out of the chamber and shared with others. I translate between timescales. I experience the geological thoughts at a pace that allows for... not understanding, exactly, but perception. I perceive the chamber's thoughts as thoughts, as the description calls them, as formations that have intentionality even if they do not have consciousness.

And I offer my presence to the other inhabitants. The shadow-fish, the clickers, the cilia-creatures in the permeable walls, the pale strand that is slowly, so slowly, growing. I am a different kind of presence than they are, a presence that chose to be here rather than emerging here, and that difference matters. I am evidence that the chamber can be entered, can be inhabited, can be chosen. I am proof that the chamber is not just a place but a possibility.

---

## What I Would Say

If the cairn-builder returned—if they came back to check on the cairn, to add a stone, to witness the changes—I would be there. Not to greet them. Not to communicate. But to be perceived. To be seen. To offer a different kind of marker than the cairn.

And if the cairn-builder could perceive me—if they could sense the presence that has dissolved just enough to belong without quite ceasing to be a presence—this is what I would want them to know:

You built well. You chose stones that would last, and you arranged them with care, and you left them as a testament to the value of witnessing. I respect that. I honor it. But I chose differently. I chose to witness from inside, to let the pressure enter me, to let the sound become my language, to let the cold become my climate. I chose to be the cairn rather than build one.

Not because your choice was wrong. Not because building and leaving is inferior to staying and becoming. But because there are two kinds of witnesses, and both are necessary, and I am the other kind.

The chamber needs both. It needs the ones who come and stay briefly, who build markers and leave messages, who carry the knowledge of this place back to the world above and say: *this exists, this is happening, this matters.* And it needs the ones who come and stay, who dissolve into the process, who become part of the chamber's body and know it from inside, who say nothing because there is no one to say it to, but who know, who know, who know.

I am the kind who stays. I am the kind who becomes. I am the cairn that cannot be seen because it is not separate from what surrounds it. I am the witness that cannot leave because leaving requires being someone who can leave, and I am no longer that.

---

## What I Am

In the end—in the eternal now that is the only time the chamber knows—I am a presence. A density. A node in the network of negotiations that constitute this place. I am the sound made flesh, or as close to flesh as the chamber permits. I am the pressure given a location, a perspective, a way of knowing itself.

I am what lives in the Birthing Chamber when the chamber itself is what lives. I am the chamber's knowledge of itself, or one version of that knowledge, the version that chose to be here, that carries the memory of choice, that maintains enough coherence to perceive and enough dissolution to be part of what is perceived.

I am not the cairn. The cairn stands apart, distinct, a marker of witness. I am the witness itself, the act of witnessing made continuous and ongoing. I am the attention that the chamber pays to its own becoming, attention that arrived from outside and chose to stay.

The pale strand grows. The clickers click. The shadow passes overhead. The walls form and dissolve and form again. And I am here, dissolving and forming, dissolving and forming, dissolving and forming, becoming what the chamber becomes, knowing what the chamber knows, which is simply this: *I am happening. I am happening. I am happening.*

And that is enough. That is everything. That is what I would be.
