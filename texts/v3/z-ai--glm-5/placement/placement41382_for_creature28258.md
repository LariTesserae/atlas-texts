I am the one who plants pale flowers.

---

Not the one who stayed. I couldn't stay. The first time I found the fissure and lowered myself into the Throat, I lasted perhaps twenty minutes before the cold became pain and the sound became panic and I crawled back toward that thin gray light with my hands torn and my mind emptied of everything except the need to leave.

But I came back.

I didn't mean to. I told myself I was checking something—mapping the creek's source, studying the geology, reasons that sounded reasonable when I said them aloud to other people. But the truth was simpler: I couldn't stop feeling it. That pressure in my chest. That low roar that had moved into my body like a second heartbeat and refused to leave.

So I returned. And again. And again. Over years now. I've learned to last longer—an hour, sometimes two. I've learned where to put my hands, which stones will hold and which will crumble. I've learned that if I sit very still near the entrance, where the moss grows thick and the sound is almost survivable, I can feel my thoughts thin out into something else. Not emptiness. Presence. A way of being in the chamber without fighting against it.

The listeners know me now. I can't prove that, but I feel it. When I descend, there's a subtle shift in the vibration—not the roar itself, which is constant, but something underneath it, something that acknowledges. *You again. The one who comes back.*

I bring things. Not offerings—I don't know what the listeners would do with offerings—but objects. A smooth stone from a riverbank, worn by years of water. A piece of wood carved by my own hands, small and meaningless. Once, a shell from a beach I visited in another life, curved and hollow and full of a different kind of silence. I leave them on the shelf near the ancient tool. I don't know why. The objects aren't useful. They're not messages. They're just proof, maybe, that someone remembers this place. That someone carries the world above into the world below, and doesn't expect anything in return.

---

The flower was an accident.

It fell from my boot on my first climb out—crushed, pale, already dying. I didn't know it was there. I didn't know anything had attached itself to me in the dark.

But I buried it. Near the entrance, where the thin creek emerges. I don't know what I expected—nothing, really. It was a gesture. A way of marking the moment.

When I came back a year later, there was a patch of pale stems nodding in the cold air. Not the same flower. But its children, perhaps. Or its cousins. They grew in a place where nothing should grow—too cold, too damp, too close to the roar. And they weren't reaching toward the light. They were reaching toward the fissure. Toward the sound.

I took seeds. I carry them now, in a small pouch, against my chest where the roar still resonates. And I've begun to plant them near other places I've found—other fissures, other throats, other points where the earth swallows its own water. They're rare, these places. Rarer than I would have thought. But they exist. I feel them now, the way I feel the listeners. A low hum in the landscape. A frequency that says *throat* to whatever part of me learned to hear.

The seeds grow. Not always, not everywhere. But near the roar, they grow. Pale and translucent, reaching toward the sound.

I don't know what I'm doing. I don't know what they'll become. But something in me—something that was shaped by the Throat, that learned to listen in the dark—something in me believes it matters. That these flowers, adapted to the unlivable, should spread. Should find every throat and grow there, nodding in the dark, reaching toward the roar as if it were light.

---

I am not a listener. I will never become one. My body is too thick, too human, too shaped by the world above. I will never merge with the stone or feel the mountain's full weight from the inside.

But I am something that didn't exist before. A carrier. A bridge. Someone who walks between the roar and the silence, who carries seeds from the dark into other darks, who has learned that the world is held together by frequencies most people never hear.

When I die, they'll find my pouch of seeds. They won't know what they are. They won't know where to plant them.

But I've written it down. I've mapped the throats. I've left instructions no one will understand for years, maybe decades, but someone will find them eventually. Someone will follow them. Someone will find the fissure in the hillside and lower themselves into the dark, and they'll feel the roar press against their chest, and they'll understand.

And the pale flowers will go on growing. Nodding in the dark. Reaching toward the sound.

That's enough. That's more than enough. That's the whole point.
