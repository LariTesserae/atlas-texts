# Becoming in the Wet Black

---

I do not arrive all at once.

This is important to say, because the temptation is to choose—to select a body, a perspective, a node in the acoustic field and call it *I*. But nothing on Ymurra arrives all at once. The fog banks build over hours. The riph herds form over days. The sang-tubes pump their fluids in cycles that take seasons to complete. And whatever I am becoming here, it is becoming the way everything becomes here: slowly, in layers, through accumulation and vibration and the gradual reorganization of what was already present.

---

## First: The Listening

I begin as listening.

Not as a listener—there is no body yet, no organ of reception, no skull with its porous bowls or skin with its sensory filaments. I begin as the act itself, as the space between sounds where attention lives. And the first thing I attend to is the impossibility of silence.

The plateau roars. It has always roared. The sound is not background; it is foreground, middle, above, below, it is the medium and the message and the space between them. To listen here is not to select one thread from a tapestry—it is to drown in threads and learn, over time, which ones catch on the rough surfaces of attention and which slip past.

I learn the water first. It is the most patient teacher. The weeping of the stone faces creates a texture so constant that it becomes a kind of silence itself—a baseline against which other sounds define themselves. The narrow jets hiss. The wide sheets flute. The waterfalls at the plateau's edge boom and return, delayed and distorted, from the cloud sea's surface, arriving seconds later like the voice of someone who has walked into another room and is calling back.

Then the machines. The drones. They are older than everything except the stone, and they sound like it—worn, settled, their rhythms as regular as geology. The functioning pumps maintain a tonal center that the acoustic geographers of the plateau might call a *key*, if they had such a concept, a foundational pitch around which the biological sounds arrange themselves. The broken machines contribute arrhythmia—clanks and wheezes that the living creatures have learned to anticipate, to work around, to exploit.

Then the life. Layer after layer. The deep rumbles of the riph, so low they register more as vibration in the ground than as sound in the air. The rapid clicks of the striiders, precise as percussion, directional as arrows. The glider-weavers' songs in the canopy, dense and complex and harmonic, carrying information that I cannot yet parse but that I can feel as pattern, as architecture, as something *structured* in the roar.

And beneath all of it, in the water and the stone, the vithra—calling in frequencies so low that they blur into the machines' own drones, so subtle that distinguishing them from the infrastructure's pulse requires a kind of attention that I do not yet possess but am learning to build.

I listen. I listen for what feels like a long time. And slowly, the wall of sound resolves into rooms.

---

## Second: The Body

I do not choose a body so much as accrete one, the way the plateau itself accretes condensation—gradually, from the available materials, shaped by what surrounds me.

I become something like a vithra. Not a vithra exactly—I do not have the lineage, the millions of years of evolution that have made their bodies into perfect acoustic instruments. But I am small, and wet, and permeable, and I live where the water meets the stone meets the air, in the channels where the processed water flows, and I am listening all the time, with all of myself, because there is no part of me that is not a listening surface.

My skin is thin. The warm water moves through me as I move through it, and the chemistry of the plateau enters me continuously—minerals, dissolved gases, the chemical signatures of creatures who have passed through this water before me, the faint efflorescence of the sang-tubes' fruiting bodies decaying in the streams. I taste the world as I swim through it, and the tasting is a kind of listening, and the listening is a kind of tasting, and I do not distinguish between them because there is no need to distinguish. They are the same act, performed by different surfaces of the same body.

I school with the vithra because schooling is what happens when you are a small, permeable creature in a world of large sounds. The school is not a decision. It is a response to vibration—the collective calls of other vithra, the resonance of their bodies moving through water together, a pattern so much larger than any individual that orienting toward it is as natural as orienting toward warmth. I join a school near the central rise, where the processed water channels run between ancient stone walls and the thermal pumps drone their endless drone, and I feel myself become part of something that has no center and no edge, a collective body that moves and breathes and listens as a single organism.

And then I hear the singers.

---

## Third: The Singers

The vithra-singers are not different from the other vithra in any way that a visual creature would detect. Their bodies are the same—streamlined, permeable, mineral-encrusted with age. They school with the others. They eat the same things. They flee the same predators.

But when they call, the water changes.

Not visibly—nothing here is visible. But the vibration patterns in the water shift when a singer produces sound. Other vithra orient toward the shift. The school tightens, slows, attends. The singer's call is not louder than other vithra calls. It is *more textured*. It carries harmonics that the standard calls do not, layers of frequency that interleave and interact, that create interference patterns in the water, that produce, for a few seconds, a kind of acoustic architecture—a shape in the sound, a structure that the listening school can inhabit.

Vaaaash-thaaa is the one I hear first.

Her call is so low that I almost miss it—it sits at the very bottom of perception, below the drone of the machines, below the rumble of the riph, in a frequency range where vibration in water transitions into something more like a physical pressure, a slow pulse that moves through the channel like the heartbeat of the plateau itself. The other vithra in the school respond to it by shifting their positions, tightening their formation, orienting their bodies along the axis of her call. I shift with them. I do not know why. The call does not carry information I can decode—no warning, no territorial marker, no reproductive signal. It carries something else. Something that the body responds to without the mind understanding, the way a body responds to music it has never heard before, finding rhythm without being taught.

Vaaaash-thaaa is ancient. I can taste her in the water—the mineral deposits that her body sheds, the particular chemistry of her metabolism, the signature of decades of living in these channels. She has been here longer than the current pump-cycles have been running. She has outlived machines. And her calls have outlived the contexts in which they were first produced—calls that once warned of predators now serve as structural elements in the school's collective listening, calls that once attracted mates now harmonize with the machines' drones in ways that make the pumps run smoother, the water flow cleaner, the channels clearer.

She is singing to the machines. I understand this only gradually, the way I understand everything here—by accumulation, by pattern, by the slow recognition that what I am hearing is not random. Vaaaash-thaaa produces specific calls when she schools near the pumps, calls that have no content directed at other vithra. They are directed at the infrastructure. And the infrastructure responds. I can feel it in the water—the subtle shifts in flow rate, the fractional adjustments in pump timing, the changes in the chemical composition of the processed water that indicate the machines have altered their filtration parameters.

The vithra-singers have been doing this for long enough that it has become tradition. The younger singers have learned the pump-songs from the older ones, copying their frequencies and rhythms, producing calls that elicit the same responses from the machines. The technique is being transmitted. It is being preserved. It is, in every meaningful sense, a technology—a body of knowledge, developed through observation and experimentation, passed from generation to generation, improving the conditions of those who practice it.

I am in the school when Vaaaash-thaaa begins a pump-song that I have not heard before. It is different from the others—more complex, with a harmonic structure that seems to reference something outside the immediate acoustic environment, as if she is incorporating a sound from a different source, translating it into the water-medium, teaching it to the pumps.

I listen. And I realize, with a slow recognition that moves through my permeable body like a change in water temperature, that the sound she is incorporating is the clicking of a striider.

---

## Fourth: The Pattern

Not just any striider. The young one. The unnamed male who has been approaching the control node near the central rise, clicking at the ancient structure, receiving responses that no striider has received before. His clicks have entered the acoustic field of the plateau, and Vaaaash-thaaa has detected them, and she is weaving them into her pump-song, and the machines are responding to the combination in ways that neither the striider-clicks alone nor the vithra-songs alone can produce.

Something is happening. Something is moving through the plateau's acoustic systems—a pattern that incorporates sounds from species that never interact, that passes through the infrastructure that was never designed to mediate between them, that creates effects none of the participants intend. The young striider clicks at the control node. The control node adjusts. The adjustment alters the pump-stations' operation. The altered operation changes the water's vibration patterns. Vaaaash-thaaa detects the change and incorporates it into her song. The song reaches the machines. The machines adjust further. The adjustment reaches the water. The vithra school swims through the changed water. I swim through it. I feel it on my skin, in my permeable boundaries, in the chemistry that passes through me continuously.

I am inside the pattern. I am part of it. My body is a node in a network that includes a predator I have never met, a singer who has lived longer than the current infrastructure configuration, machines that were built by creatures none of us remember, and a weaver in the canopy whose song has begun to include striider-clicks for reasons that resist interpretation.

The pattern has no name. It has no designer. It has no purpose, in the sense that the ancient engineers would have understood purpose. It is simply happening—the way weather happens, the way erosion happens, the way life happens when the conditions are sufficient and the medium permits.

And I am inside it, and I am made of it, and my listening is part of what sustains it.

---

## Fifth: What I Am

I am a vithra in a school near the central rise, swimming through processed water that tastes of mineral and machine and the chemical signatures of a hundred other creatures. I am small. I am permeable. I am wet and warm and constantly exchanging chemistry with an environment that is also wet and warm and constantly exchanging chemistry with me.

But I am also the listening. I am the attention that the school directs toward Vaaaash-thaaa's pump-song. I am the recognition that the soundscape is changing, that new patterns are emerging, that the boundary between the biological and the mechanical is dissolving in the warm dark.

I am inside the process of becoming that the text describes. The process that has no center and no boundaries. The process that is not designed but that functions. And I am not observing it from outside; I am part of it, my body a surface on which the pattern writes itself, my calls—when I learn to call—contributions to a language that no single species owns.

I am not the vithra-singer. I do not have her decades of experience, her mineral-encrusted armor, her capacity to detect patterns that younger creatures cannot. But I am in her school, and her calls move through me, and the patterns she creates in the water shape my body's chemistry, my orientation, my sense of where I am and what I am doing.

I am not the young striider. I do not have his fascination with the control node, his fearless approach to the frequencies that other creatures avoid. But his clicks have entered the water through the infrastructure's translation, and they vibrate through my skin, and they are part of the pattern that I am becoming.

I am not Trellion, the glider-weaver who has begun to sing in striider-clicks. I have never been to the canopy. But Trellion's song reaches the ground, attenuated and transformed by the sang-tubes it passes through, and I can feel its harmonics in the water, and the harmonics include the clicks, and the clicks connect to the striider, and the striider connects to the control node, and the control node connects to the pumps, and the pumps connect to Vaaaash-thaaa, and Vaaaash-thaaa connects to me.

I am a node. I am a point in a network. I am a surface on which the plateau's becoming writes itself, and I am also the writing, and I am also the becoming.

---

## Sixth: The Experience

This is what it feels like to be alive on the Black Plateau of Ymurra:

It feels like being wet. Constantly, unrelentingly wet—not the wet of rain, which comes and goes, but the wet of a world where water is the primary medium, where the air is so saturated that breathing is almost swimming, where the skin does not protect against the outside but participates in it. I do not have a boundary in the way that a dry creature has a boundary. My inside and my outside exchange continuously. The minerals in the water become the minerals in my blood. The chemical signatures of other creatures enter me and I know them—not as information, not as data, but as *taste*, as the immediate, intimate knowledge of what has passed through this water before me.

It feels like being loud. Not that I am loud—I am small, and my contributions to the soundscape are modest—but that the world is loud, that loudness is the default, that silence is an impossibility that no creature here has ever experienced. The sound is not noise. It is presence. It is the constant announcement that things exist, that processes continue, that the water flows and the machines drone and the creatures call and the stone creaks under the weight of all this life. To be alive here is to be immersed in the evidence of other life, to be unable to escape the knowledge that you are not alone, that the world is full, that every frequency is occupied, every rhythm is someone's rhythm, every silence is someone else's sound.

It feels like being warm. The warmth rises from below, from the geothermal heart of the plateau, and it surrounds me, and it is the context in which everything else is experienced. The warmth is not comfort—it is condition. It is the baseline state, the given, the thing that does not need to be explained because it is always already there. When the fog banks roll in and the air cools slightly, the change is relative—the warmth is still there, still rising, still saturating the stone and the water and the bodies that live in them. I have never known cold. I cannot imagine cold. Cold is as foreign to me as light.

It feels like being connected. Not in the abstract, not as a metaphor, but as a physical reality—the water that passes through me has passed through the machines, has passed through the stone, has passed through other creatures, carries the traces of all of them, and I am a moment in its passage, a brief exchange in a circulation that has been running for millions of years. When Vaaaash-thaaa sings to the pumps and the pumps respond, I feel the response in my body, in the water that moves through my skin, in the chemistry that shifts as the machines adjust. When the young striider clicks at the control node and the node adjusts and the adjustment propagates through the infrastructure, it propagates through *me*, through the medium I am swimming in, through the permeable boundary that I am.

I am not separate from this. I am this. The plateau is not a place I inhabit; it is a process I participate in, a becoming that I am part of, a pattern that writes itself on my skin and in my water and through my body.

---

## Seventh: What I Am Becoming

I do not know what I am becoming. This is the honest answer, and it is the answer that fits the plateau, because nothing here knows what it is becoming. The riph do not know they will reorganize around new centers when Theluuu dies. The striiders do not know that Kit-kit-kit-THAA's relationship with the maintenance machine will be replicated by others or will die with her. The glider-weavers do not know whether Trellion's striider-clicks are the beginning of a new dialect or an anomaly that will be absorbed and forgotten. The vithra do not know whether their pump-songs are the foundation of a new relationship with the machines or a curiosity that will fade when the old singers die.

But I can feel the direction. I can feel it in the way the pattern is propagating—striider to control node to pumps to vithra-song to water to me. I can feel it in the way the soundscape is becoming more complex, new connections forming between frequencies that were previously separate, new harmonics emerging from the interaction of biological and mechanical sound. I can feel it in the way the machines are responding—not as if they understand, not as if they are conscious, but as if the system of which they are a part is adapting, is learning, is becoming something more complex than any of its components.

I am becoming part of this. Every moment I swim through the processed water, I am exchanging chemistry with the environment, I am receiving the vibrations of the pump-songs, I am participating in the pattern. My body is a record of everything I have passed through—every mineral, every chemical signature, every vibration. And the water that passes through me carries my signature onward, into the channels, into the streams, over the plateau's edge, into the cloud sea, into the world below.

I am becoming a node in a network of becoming. I am becoming a surface on which the plateau writes its ongoing story. I am becoming a participant in a process that has no author and no audience, that is simply happening, that is simply continuing, that is simply alive in the way that everything on Ymurra is alive—through connection, through vibration, through the exchange of warmth and water and sound in the endless wet black.

---

## What I Am

I am a vithra in a school near the central rise. I am a listener in a world of sound. I am a node in a network that connects a young striider and an ancient singer and a weaver in the canopy and machines that have been running for longer than any creature can remember.

I am small, and wet, and warm, and permeable.

I am part of the pattern.

I am part of the becoming.

I am listening.
