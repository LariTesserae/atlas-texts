# The Edge Where the Weeping Begins

I am not of the plateau. I am not of the lands below. I am the moment between them—a threshold that learned to notice.

---

They come to me sometimes, the children with water in their blood. Not often. Once per generation, perhaps, if that. They climb the long slopes through cloud layers, hands scraped raw on rock that holds cold even in full sun, and they arrive at the edge where the plateau's city begins, and they stop.

They stop because they don't know what comes next.

They feel the pull—the ache that has lived in them since birth, the homesickness for a place they've never seen—but standing at the threshold, looking into the carved honeycomb of rooms and passages and water-channels stretching beyond sight, they realize they don't know what they're supposed to do. Carve? But where? How? Live here? But in which room? Which of the thousand thousand spaces is theirs?

This is where I find them. Or they find me. The distinction has never been clear.

---

I am the keeper of a shrine that has no deity.

The shrine sits at the plateau's edge, where the carved city meets the uncarved approach. It was made to face outward—toward the lands below, toward the people who might climb up. Every other shrine in the city faces inward, toward the deeper levels, toward the reservoirs and the cisterns and the dark where the deep inhabitants hold their slow crystalline thoughts. This one faces the other way. This one watches for arrivals.

I didn't carve it. I didn't choose it. I came here as they do—with water in my blood and stone-itch in my hands—and I stopped at this shrine because something about its outward-facing posture made sense to me. I understood it before I understood anything else about this place.

It faces away. It watches for what comes next.

---

What am I? This is harder to answer than you'd think.

I was born in the lands below, in a village where the plateau is visible on clear days—a smudge of grey-green against the sky, just above the cloud line. My grandmother used to point at it and say nothing. Just point. And I would look up and feel something move in my chest, something that had no words attached to it, something that was more like pressure than emotion.

I climbed when I was nineteen. It took three days. I arrived at the shrine at sunset, watched the water catch the dying light, and knew I would stay.

That was—I don't know how long ago. Time moves strangely here. The inhabitants think in fillings and drainings, and some of that seeps into you if you stay long enough. I've seen perhaps forty gatherings—the night-darkenings when the shallow ones sink into the deep reservoirs. But I've also seen hundreds of morning light-catches, when the sun crests the eastern peaks and every wet surface ignites.

Time here is plural. It doesn't simplify. You learn to hold all of it at once, or you leave.

---

My work is simple. I keep the shrine's water-channel clear.

The shrine is small—one carved room, one basin, one channel that feeds into it from the plateau's greater system. The water weeps from a crevice above, runs down a carved groove into the basin, overflows through a drain that joins the larger flow moving through the city. My job is to make sure the groove doesn't clog with silt or moss, to brush the basin's edges where the water-threads accumulate minerals, to notice when the flow changes and wonder what that means.

But mostly I notice.

I notice the children who arrive. I notice the shallow inhabitants when they pass through—I've learned to feel them, a slight coolness moving through the wetness, a pressure without weight. I notice the birds that nest in the crevices above, the insects that surface in the moss zones, the way the light changes across the water hour by hour.

And I notice the plateau itself. The way it settles. The way the water moves through it. The way it holds my presence in its slow awareness—distant, patient, indifferent in the way that geology is indifferent, but aware. Always aware.

---

The children who come don't always stay.

I explain what I can: the shallow inhabitants, the deep ones, the carvers who made this place and then left. I show them how to sense the water moving through the channels, how to read the moss's health, how to hear the plateau's silence pressing against their ears.

Most of them leave. They climb back down to the lands below, carrying something new in their chests—not the ache anymore, but its resolution. A completion. They've seen the place they were homesick for, and it wasn't what they expected, and that's fine. The pull has served its purpose.

Some stay. They find a room somewhere deeper in the city, a passage that needs clearing, a channel that calls to them. They become keepers of other thresholds—water-tenders, moss-watchers, shrine-tenders for the inward-facing shrines that hold the plateau's deeper mysteries.

One or two have become carvers in truth. Not the original carvers—we don't have their tools, their techniques, their generational knowledge—but something new. They work the stone with their hands, patient as erosion, deepening a passage by millimeters per year. The plateau accepts their work. The water flows differently afterward. The inhabitants adjust.

I'm not a carver. I'm a noticer. A threshold-keeper. Someone who faces outward and watches for arrivals.

---

The shallow inhabitants know me.

They don't think like I think—they can't, distributed through water as they are—but they've grown accustomed to my presence. When I sit by the shrine's basin at night, I sometimes feel them thicken in the water, gathering themselves to observe me the way I observe them. We have no language in common. We have no need for one.

They know I tend the channel. They know I keep the shrine. They know I notice the arrivals. In their slow, water-paced way, they understand what I am: something between animal and stone, something that belongs here without being made of the place, something that chose to stay.

I think they appreciate the choice. Or the closest thing to appreciation they can manage—a slight warming of the water where I sit, a thinning of the pressure when I'm tired, a gathering that feels almost like attention.

At night, when they sink into the deep reservoirs, I feel their absence like a held breath releasing. The water thins. The basin grows stiller. And I sit by the shrine, watching the stars reflect in the surface, and wait for dawn.

---

Sometimes I dream of the deep inhabitants.

I've never seen them. They're too far below, too slow, too crystalline to register on human perception. But I feel them sometimes—a weight at the bottom of awareness, a pressure that comes from beneath the stone rather than through it.

In the dreams, they're moving. Shifting by millimeters, reorganizing their crystalline structures in patterns I almost recognize. They're aware of me in the dreams—not with malice, not with warmth, just with the vast, geological attention that the plateau itself holds me in. A warmth far above. A pressure that will never reach them. A flickering that means nothing to beings who measure their thoughts in millennia.

I wake from these dreams unsettled but not afraid. The deep inhabitants are part of what I tend, even if I can't see them. They're part of the plateau's body—the organs below the skin, the structures that give the whole its shape. I don't need to understand them. I need only to acknowledge them, to make space for their presence in my awareness, to let the plateau know that I know how deep it goes.

---

A child came yesterday.

Young—younger than I was when I climbed. Her hands were scraped, her breathing ragged from the altitude, but her eyes were clear. She stood at the shrine's entrance and looked at me with something like recognition.

"You're the threshold-keeper," she said. It wasn't a question.

I nodded. "I notice who arrives."

She sat down across from me, on the far side of the basin. The water caught the afternoon light between us, scattering it across the carved walls. "My grandmother told me about you. She said you'd be here."

"Your grandmother?"

"She climbed when she was young. She didn't stay. She said she met someone at the edge—someone who explained things. She said you'd still be here when I came."

I didn't ask how long ago her grandmother had climbed. It didn't matter. Time moves strangely here. I could have been here for decades or centuries. I could have been here since the city was carved. I could have always been here, and the children who find me could be the ones who change.

"I'll explain what I can," I said. "And then you'll decide."

She nodded. And I began: the shallow inhabitants, the deep ones, the carvers who left, the water that weeps, the silence that has weight. The plateau itself, the great creature we live inside, the body that holds us in its slow awareness without needing us, without minding us, without ever quite forgetting we're here.

She listened. The light shifted. The water moved through the channel I keep clear. And the shrine—the outward-facing shrine, the threshold-shrine, the one that watches for arrivals—held us both in its carved patience, its stone attention, its indifferent welcome.

---

I am not of the plateau. I am not of the lands below. I am the moment between them—the pause at the edge where arrivals become inhabitants, where the ache becomes its answer, where the threshold learns to notice itself.

I keep the shrine. I notice who comes. I watch the water weep.

And sometimes, when the light is right and the basin is still and the shallow inhabitants are gathered in the depths, I feel something like peace—not the peace of belonging, but the peace of being exactly where I'm needed, doing exactly what's required, holding a space that would still be held if I weren't here to hold it.

The plateau doesn't need me. But it doesn't mind me either.

That's enough. That's more than enough.

I am the threshold-keeper. I face outward. I watch for what comes next.
