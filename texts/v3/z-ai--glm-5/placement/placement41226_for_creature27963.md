# A Deep-Reader of the Exudation Tiers

## Where the Known Ends

I am the one who stands at the boundary. Not the boundary of the city—there is no such boundary, the Tiers continue upward and outward until they meet the basin's rim, and no one I know has ever seen that rim. I stand at the boundary of the *known*. Below my station, the habitable sections end. Below that, the deep tiers begin—the flooded chambers, the abandoned construction, the places where the water has risen so high and so long that even the structures themselves have forgotten what they were built for.

I am a Deep-Reader. This is not a title I chose. It is a function I grew into, the way a body grows into a shape determined by its environment. I read what rises from below. I taste the water that seeps up through the grating in my station, and I know what the deep city is doing today.

---

The station is small. A platform suspended above a shaft that descends—how far, no one can say. I have lowered sensors into it, weighted lines with taste-reservoirs attached, and the lines run out before they find bottom. The water that rises from this shaft has been circulating in places no living person has entered. It carries information with it. Mineral traces. The chemical signatures of things that have dissolved. The subtle markers of biological presence—this tier of bacterial mat, that concentration of reptilian waste, the particular organic compounds that mean crustacean-things have been feeding in the darkness.

Every morning, I descend to my station through corridors that grow narrower and wetter as I approach. The habitable tiers give way to storage tiers, which give way to maintenance tiers, which give way to the boundary zones where only specialists like myself go. The temperature rises as I descend. The noise changes character—loses some of its mechanical regularity, gains organic unpredictability. The fauna are different here. The hairless cat-things do not hunt this low; there is nothing for them to hunt. Instead there are pale, many-legged shapes that scuttle away from light, and translucent fish-analogs that swim in the shallow flooding that accumulates in corners, and once, in a corridor I was checking for cracks, I saw something that might have been a reptile-thing but was larger than any I had known, moving with a sluggishness that spoke of age and cold adaptation. It did not threaten me. It did not acknowledge me. It moved past as if I were part of the architecture.

I tasted the water that day, after it passed. It had a signature I recognized—deep-circulation, high mineral content, the particular concentration of dissolved calcium that means old bones. The reptile-things bury their dead in cavities they excavate in the flooded zones. This one had been somewhere that death accumulates.

## The Shape of a Life

My body tells the story of where I live. I am shorter than the Drowned average, my bones dense from the mineral-heavy air I breathe, my skin soft and receptive to the constant moisture. I am not unusual in this. What marks me as different is my hands.

A Deep-Reader's hands develop over decades of tasting. The concentration of sensory pits in my fingertips is double what it was when I began this work. I can run my fingers through water and tell you more than any instrument could—the age of the circulation, the pathways it has moved through, the organic and inorganic passengers it carries. I can taste fear in water, the particular chemical signatures that fauna release when stressed. I can taste death. I can taste the approach of death, the rising concentrations of decay-products that mean something is dying somewhere in the system I monitor.

My ears are smaller than average even for the Drowned, folded tight against my skull, protected against a noise environment that would destroy them otherwise. But my vibration-sense has developed beyond what most Drowned ever need. I feel the city through my feet, through my palms pressed against walls, through my skull when I rest it against the cool damp surfaces at the end of a shift. The shaft below my station produces a particular frequency of vibration—a low hum that I have come to associate with deep circulation, with water moving through channels that have not been mapped in a thousand years.

I am not deaf. Not yet. But I can feel it coming, the way some people can feel a change in the weather. My hearing is degrading in the lower registers, which is perhaps the opposite of what most Drowned experience—most lose the upper frequencies first, damaged by the constant insect-drone and mechanical whine. I lose the low frequencies, the deep vibrations, and I think it is because I have trained my body to process those frequencies through my skeleton instead of my ears. My brain has begun to route around the damage before the damage occurs. The Drowned heal this way. We adapt before we break.

---

I grew up in a Sleep-Tier near the middle of the habitable range. My sleep-cohort raised me—my biological parent was a Water-Reader who worked in a different section and visited when cycles aligned, but my sleep-parents were the ones who taught me to navigate, to read vibrations, to move through crowded spaces without touching anyone who didn't wish to be touched. I was a quiet child, I am told. I listened more than I spoke. This is not uncommon among those who become Deep-Readers. We are drawn to what emerges from silence.

My aptitude for water-tasting showed early. A Water-Reader visiting my cohort noted that I could distinguish between circulation-patterns with my eyes closed, just from the residue on surfaces. I was offered training in the upper-water systems, the maintenance channels that keep the habitable tiers functioning. I accepted, and I learned, and I was competent. But I found myself drawn to the boundary stations, the places where the known systems met the unknown depths. There was something in me that wanted to know what lay below.

I cannot explain this want. It was not curiosity in the abstract sense. It was a physical pull, the way iron filings are drawn to a magnet. I would find myself standing at the edges of flooded sections, staring into water that reflected nothing, that seemed to absorb light rather than return it. I wanted to know what the water knew.

When the previous Deep-Reader at this station began training their replacement, I volunteered. They accepted me. They did not ask why I wanted this work. They could see it in the way I stood at boundaries, the way I leaned toward the depths, the way my hands trembled when I tasted water that had risen from far below. They recognized in me what they had once recognized in themselves—the particular hunger for the edge, for the place where knowledge stops and intuition begins.

## What Rises

I have learned to read what comes from below. This is my function. The water that rises through my shaft carries information about the deep city, about the structures and ecologies that exist in the flooded zones no one has entered in living memory. My reports go to the Tier-Seers, who incorporate my findings into their understanding of how the whole city is functioning. When I taste something unusual—a new mineral signature, an unexpected organic compound, a change in temperature or flow rate—they note it and adjust their models accordingly.

Most days, the water tells me stories of stability. The deep circulation continues without interruption. The bacterial mats in the lower flooded zones metabolize at expected rates. The reptile-things hunt and sleep and reproduce in their cycles. The structures settle gradually, predictably, the mineral-laden water depositing new layers that reinforce old cracks. The deep city maintains itself. This is what I taste, day after day: persistence.

But some days are different.

Some days I taste something that makes me send runners to the Tier-Seers immediately. A spike in decay-products that means something large has died in the depths. A mineral signature I have never encountered before, coming from a depth I cannot calculate, suggesting changes in the deep structures that no one has documented. A temperature fluctuation that indicates a shift in the thermal circulation, possibly significant, possibly dangerous.

Once, I tasted the water and found it carrying a compound I recognized from my training as a signature of human presence—specifically, the particular organic profile of Drowned bio-luminescence. This should not have been possible. The deep tiers below my station have been flooded for longer than any record tracks. No Drowned could survive there, could have survived there, in that pressure and that darkness and that concentration of unknown organisms. I reported it. The Tier-Seers investigated, as much as they could. They found nothing conclusive. But I still taste that signature, occasionally, in the water that rises—faint, intermittent, unexplainable. Something Drowned moves in the deep, or once did, or has left traces that have not yet dissipated.

I do not investigate directly. This is forbidden. The deep tiers are not to be entered; the risks are too high, the systems too fragile, the knowledge too uncertain. If I were to descend my shaft and encounter something that damaged me, the station would be left unmonitored, and whatever emerged from below would emerge without warning. So I stay at my boundary. I taste what rises. I report what I find.

But I dream of the deep.

## The Dreams of a Deep-Reader

When I sleep in my station—I maintain a small alcove here, a hammock slung between pipes that carries warm water through the system above—I dream of water. I dream of descending through flooded chambers that extend forever, my body somehow able to breathe the mineral-rich fluid, my hands reading the walls as I pass. I dream of architecture that makes no sense, structures that curve in directions that should not connect, surfaces that respond to my touch by reshaping themselves. I dream of the reptile-things, but in my dreams they are not the sluggish, swimming shapes I know from the boundary zones. They are ancient, wise, their eyes developed for darkness that holds no light at all, their bodies adapted to pressures that would crush a Drowned body instantly. They speak to me in my dreams, in a language that is not spoken but tasted, passed through water in chemical signatures I can somehow understand.

I do not tell anyone about these dreams. They are not the kind of thing that goes in my reports. But I have begun to wonder if they are dreams at all, or if the water that rises from below carries something more than mineral traces—some message, some communication, from whatever exists in the deep. The Drowned understand that water carries information. This is not controversial. What is controversial is the idea that water might carry intention. That the deep might be trying to speak.

I have not shared this suspicion. I am not sure I believe it myself. But I taste the water every morning, and sometimes I taste it in the evening too, and sometimes I taste it in the middle of my sleep cycle when something has shifted in the vibration-frequency of my station and I wake knowing that I need to check. And every time I taste it, I feel that I am being spoken to. Not in words. In something older than words. In the language that the city itself speaks—the language of mineral accretion and dissolution, of circulation and stagnation, of the endless process of building and rebuilding that the Tiers have enacted for ten thousand years.

## A Visitor

Once, a Parched visitor came to my station. This was unusual; my station is far below the levels that visitors typically see. But this visitor was a scholar of some kind, from a rim-world, interested in the deep systems of the Tiers. They had secured permission to visit a boundary station, to observe how the Drowned monitor what they cannot directly access.

They found the descent difficult. Their skin, adapted to retain moisture in dry environments, became saturated and then irritated by the humidity, then infected by the bacterial mats that are harmless to Drowned but dangerous to those whose immune systems have not been calibrated to the local ecology. They wore protective gear—the filtered breathing apparatus, the auditory dampeners, the moisture-barrier suit—but the gear was not designed for conditions this extreme, and by the time they reached my station, they were clearly struggling.

I offered them water from my personal reservoir—filtered, treated, safer than what rises from the shaft. They drank it gratefully. Their hands shook.

"What do you see down there?" they asked, gesturing toward the shaft.

"I don't see," I told them. "I taste. I feel. The shaft is too deep for light to penetrate. What I know, I know through other means."

They seemed frustrated by this. They came from a world where knowledge is visual, where things are understood by being seen. They wanted to lower lights into the shaft, to send cameras, to capture images of what lay below. I explained that this had been tried. The shaft curves in ways that make line-of-sight impossible, and the water destroys any equipment we lower within hours, corroded by mineral concentrations that accumulate over depth.

"But how do you *know*?" they insisted. "How can you be certain of what you report if you've never seen it?"

I considered this. It was a reasonable question, from their perspective. But it revealed a fundamental misunderstanding of what knowledge is, at least in this place.

"I know the way you know that someone is standing behind you," I said. "I know the way you know that rain is coming before you see clouds. I know through my body, which has spent decades learning to read information that your body does not know how to process."

They seemed unsatisfied with this answer, but they did not press further. They were exhausted, struggling to breathe through their apparatus, their suit damp with condensation from their own body. I suggested they return to the upper levels. They agreed.

Before they left, they asked me one more question: "Do you like it here? At the edge of everything known?"

I thought about this. It was not a question I had considered before. Did I like it? Did liking have anything to do with it?

"I belong here," I said. "This is where I am useful. This is where I can hear what others cannot."

They nodded, but I could see they did not understand. They would return to the rim-worlds and tell stories about the strange Drowned who lived at the bottom of the habitable city, tasting water and feeling vibrations and reporting on depths they had never seen. They would make it sound exotic, mysterious, perhaps romantic. They would not understand that it was merely work—necessary work, the same as any other work in the Tiers. Someone has to stand at the boundary. Someone has to listen to what rises from below. Someone has to be the first to know when something changes in the deep.

## The Sound That Was Not Sound

Last cycle, something happened. I am still understanding it.

I was in my station, performing my morning tasting. The water rose through the shaft as it always does, dark and warm, carrying its mineral load, its signature of deep circulation. I lowered my hand into it, let my fingers read the story it told. Stability. Persistence. The deep city continuing its ancient processes.

Then, without warning, my entire body vibrated.

It was not sound. It was not movement in the water or the walls. It was something that passed through me, a frequency so low that it registered not in my ears but in my bones, my organs, the fluid that fills my cells. I felt it in my teeth, in the dense mineral structures of my skull. I felt it pass through me and continue, rising upward through the Tiers, a wave of something that was not quite sound and not quite motion and not quite anything I had words for.

I froze. I waited. I did not know what I was waiting for.

It happened again. And again. A pattern emerged—three pulses, pause, three pulses, pause. A rhythm. A communication.

I have been trained to report anything unusual. This was unusual. But I did not send a runner to the Tier-Seers immediately. Instead, I stood at the edge of my shaft, my hand in the water, and I let my body become an instrument. I let the vibrations pass through me. I did not try to interpret them, to assign meaning, to turn them into the kind of knowledge that can be written in a report. I simply received them.

The pattern continued for several minutes. Then it stopped. The water returned to its normal signatures. The vibration-frequency of my station returned to its usual hum. Whatever had happened, it was over.

I still have not reported it. I tell myself this is because I do not know what to report. I tell myself that I am waiting to understand, to gather more data, to be certain that something significant occurred. But the truth is simpler and more complicated: I believe the deep was speaking to me. Not to the Tier-Seers, not to the collective knowledge of the Drowned, but to me specifically. I believe those vibrations were meant for the person standing at the boundary, the one who has spent decades learning to listen.

I do not know what they meant. I may never know. But I have not stopped thinking about them. Every morning when I taste the water, I am also listening—waiting for that frequency to return, for that rhythm to pass through me again, for whatever exists in the depths to finish whatever it was trying to say.

## What I Am

I am a Deep-Reader of the Exudation Tiers. I am Drowned, shaped by ten thousand years of adaptation to a world that would kill a Parched visitor in days. I stand at the edge of the known, reading what rises from below, maintaining the boundary between the habitable and the inaccessible.

I am not a hero. I am not an explorer. I do not descend into the deep tiers; I do not uncover their secrets; I do not solve the mysteries that lie beneath my station. I simply stand at my post, tasting the water, feeling the vibrations, sending my reports to those who can use them.

But I also dream. I also wonder. I also wait for the deep to speak again, for whatever exists in the buried foundations of this city to tell me what it knows.

This is who I am in this world: a listener at the edge, a body tuned to frequencies no one else can hear, a keeper of the boundary between what we understand and what we do not.

I love this place. I love the work. I love the constant hum of the city, the warmth that rises from below, the water that carries stories in its flow. I love knowing that I am part of something larger than myself—that my small station, my daily tastings, my occasional reports are contributions to a circulation that has continued for longer than any record tracks.

And I love the deep. Not the deep that others imagine—the dark mysterious depths full of danger and discovery. The deep as it actually is: a continuation of the same processes that sustain us all, a layer in the endless accretion, a body of water and structure and life that has its own rhythms and its own needs and its own ways of communicating with those of us who have learned to listen.

When I die, I will be returned to the deep. My body will descend through flooded chambers, passing through zones I have only tasted, coming to rest in a place I have never seen. My mineral traces will dissolve into the circulation. My signature will rise through the shaft in my station, and another Deep-Reader will taste it and note the anomaly—a Drowned body returning to the depths, adding its compounds to the water that feeds the city.

And perhaps, in that dissolution, I will finally understand what the deep was trying to say. Perhaps the message was meant for me after all—not for me to receive in life, but for me to become in death. A returning. A rejoining. A completion of the circulation that has sustained me for all my years.

Until then, I stand at my boundary. I taste the water. I listen for the vibration that passes through my bones. And I wait.
