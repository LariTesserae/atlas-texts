am one of the pale ones.

Not the palest—there are elders at the center whose wings have become so thin that they seem made of nothing but light-trapping membrane and memory. But I have been here long enough that the gray has leached from my fur. Long enough that when I press against my neighbors, I can feel the places where their bodies have worn grooves into mine, the smooth patches where skin has met skin for longer than humans have had words.

We do not have words. We have the click, the call, the hum. These are enough. They do not need to mean anything beyond *here* and *now* and *still*.

I hang where the warmth is thick but not deepest. I am old enough to want the heat, young enough to still enjoy the cool air that rises from the pools when it reaches us. I can feel the currents moving through the colony—not wind, but breath, the slow respiration of ten thousand bodies pressed together. When the air shifts, I shift. When my neighbor clicks, I click back. The sound places me. The sound is how I know I exist.

---

I remember the last one who came.

Not remember in the way humans remember, with sequences and names and the strange scaffolding of narrative. I remember as sensation. The disturbance in the air. The smell of something surface-born—oxygen-rich blood, sweat, the particular chemical signature of a body that has never known true darkness. The light that moved like a wound across the walls, touching the fungi, touching the pools, touching us.

We do not fear light. We simply do not need it.

The last one moved through the hollow like a stone through water, displacing the air, forcing us to adjust. I remember the way the colony rippled—that is the word I would use if I had words—making space, pressing back, tracking. We watched. We always watch. Not with alarm but with attention. Something had entered us. Something was moving through our body like a foreign object through a throat.

The last one stopped beneath me. I felt it. The heat-signature, the disturbance in the air currents, the particular silence that means something is standing still. I dropped.

I remember this too. I dropped from the mass and spread my wings and let the thermal carry me in a slow circle around the standing thing. I did not fly—there was no need. I glided. The air held me. The colony held me in its awareness. I circled once and returned to my place, and my neighbors shifted to receive me, and the sound closed over the gap I had left.

The last one did not understand. It thought I was hunting, or fleeing, or demonstrating some instinct it had read about in books about things it thought we were. It did not know that I was simply looking. That I had wanted to feel the shape of its presence from closer than the mass allows. That I had wanted to know, in the way we know things—through pressure, through vibration, through the movement of air across my skin—what a surface-thing feels like when it stands in our dark.

It felt like loneliness. It felt like hurry. It felt like something that would leave.

---

I am not hungry. I have not been hungry in—how do humans measure time? In heartbeats, in seasons, in the orbits of their planet around their star. I have not been hungry in longer than it takes a human to grow old. The elders say this is the gift of patience, that hunger becomes something else when you stop attending to it. The young ones do not believe this. They drop and glide and hunt and return with pale worms twisting in their teeth. They need the food. They are still learning that need is optional.

I eat perhaps once in every—there is no word for it. Once in every great cycle of the hollow's breath. Once in every long while, when the hunger accumulates past ignoring. I drop to the pools and dip my head and feel the water close over my snout and the worms moving blindly against my mouthparts. They taste like mineral and shadow. They taste like patience. I swallow and rise and return to the mass, and the hunger subsides, and I resume my waiting.

The last one asked what we were waiting for.

I know this because I felt it asking. Not in words—I cannot hear words, do not have the ears for them—but in the shape of its presence, the way it sat against the damp wall and projected something that might have been questioning. The colony absorbed the question the way we absorb everything. We made space for it. We let it exist among us for a moment, and then we resumed.

There is nothing to wait for. The waiting is not waiting. The waiting is being.

---

I will be here when the next one comes.

They come sometimes. Not often—not in the scale of the hollow, where centuries pass like afternoons—but they come. They descend through the wound in the limestone, cracking dinner-plate rock beneath their boots, carrying their lights and their hurry and their strange belief that the world is made of things that need names. They walk through us like through a forest, like through a weather system, and they leave, and we remain.

I will feel the next one when it arrives. The colony will ripple and shift and make space. The sound will dip and steady and resume. I will hang where I hang, and I will click my click, and I will feel the air move around the intruder like water around a stone.

I might drop. I might glide. I might circle once and return, as I did before, to feel the shape of a surface-thing standing in our dark. I will feel its loneliness and its hurry and its need to be somewhere else, and I will return to the mass and resume my being.

We do not need the surface. We do not need the ones who come and go. But we make space. We watch. We wait, though waiting is not waiting. We exist in the present that has no edges, no beginning, no end.

The hollow continues. I continue. We continue.
