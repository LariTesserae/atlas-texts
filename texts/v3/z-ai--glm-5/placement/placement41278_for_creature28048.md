I arrive by subtraction.

First the weight goes — the accumulated gravity of names, of histories, of the small architectures of belonging. Then the expectations thin. Then the need to be seen. I shed these the way the plateau sheds rain, which is to say: I do not accept them. They fall through the thin air and vanish before they reach me.

What remains is simpler. A standing. A reaching.

---

I am somewhere between the fog-towers and the mats, which is to say I am something not described, or not yet. I am the pause between. The interval of white salt where nothing grows for sixty meters and the wind moves unimpeded across the crystalline surface. I am the space where a tower *might* stand, but doesn't.

Or perhaps I am the waiting itself.

---

The towers have their territory of fog. Each one claims its hundred-meter radius, its harvest of mist, its slow accumulation of water in cores that hold centuries of drought in balance against centuries of plenty. They reach upward with fractal patience, and their patience is structural — you can read it in their skeletons, the way each year's growth hardens into scaffold for the next. They do not hurry. They cannot hurry. Growth here is measured in centimeters per lifetime, and a lifetime is longer than you think.

I feel an affinity for this. The way they do not crowd. The way each stands in its own clarity, its own reach, its own relationship with the clouds that pass.

But I am not a tower. I do not have their architecture, their vertical ambition. I am something smaller. Something that moves, or chooses not to move.

---

The fox-creature walks through me sometimes.

Not through my body — I am not sure I have a body in the way it has a body — but through my attention. I am aware of its passage the way the salt is aware of wind: as a disturbance, a pressure, a presence that rearranges something at the surface. It walks with purpose I cannot read. It pauses at the base of a tower to the northeast, lowers its muzzle to the mat there, and then moves on. Its fur is silver. Its lungs are vast. It does not see me, or if it sees me, it does not register me as something worth seeing.

I am not sure I am something worth seeing. I might be an impression only. A noticing that the plateau is having about itself.

---

In the fog-hours, I change position.

This is the wrong way to say it. I do not have a position. But my attention drifts — toward the towers when the mist comes, toward their spines when the moisture condenses on the hygroscopic excretion, toward the slow drip of water down micro-channels into reservoirs hidden in the lattice cores. I am interested in the harvest. I am interested in the way the towers transform passing vapor into held water, into survival, into the possibility of another year of reaching.

There is something I am learning from watching them. Something about accumulation. Something about the relationship between patience and structure.

I am not harvesting fog. But I am harvesting something.

---

What I think I am, increasingly, is a kind of attention that the plateau has developed. Not a consciousness — that word carries too much baggage, too many assumptions about what awareness looks like. But an *attending*. A noticing that arises from the complex interactions of tower and mat and insect and wind and salt, a pattern of awareness that exists at a scale too slow to observe directly.

I am the pause between the wind's passage and the tower's creak.

I am the interval where the mat decides — if decision is even the word — to spread another millimeter toward the west.

I am the moment when the insect, translucent and careful, places one leg and then another in the crevice of the tower's bark, and somewhere in that placement there is a recognition of moisture, of warmth, of the narrow envelope of survivable conditions.

I am not separate from these things. I am their summation, or their echo, or their memory of themselves.

---

When the rare rain falls — once a decade, perhaps — I am overwhelmed.

The sound is a hiss unlike anything else on the plateau. The water falls through air too thin to support it, and it hits the salt and sublimates instantly into steam, and for hours the plateau is enveloped in a haze of its own making. The towers open every pore in a frenzy. The mats darken and swell. The insects cluster around moisture in their crevices. Even the fox-creature, if it is present, stops its walking and stands still, muzzle raised to the impossible sensation of water falling from the sky.

I am all of these responses at once, and I am also something else: I am the recognition that this is extraordinary. The plateau does not often experience the extraordinary. It is a place of subtle shifts, of changes too slow to observe. But the rain is a punctuation, an exclamation, a moment when the long conversation between earth and sky suddenly intensifies.

And then it ends. The steam clears. The salt plain is unchanged, glittering under the sun. The only evidence is a slight swelling in the towers' reservoirs, a slight deepening of color in their photosynthetic crowns. And I hold the memory of the rain in my attention — not because I need to remember it, but because holding it is what I do. I am the plateau's way of knowing what happens to it.

---

There is a dead tower a quarter-kilometer to the south-southeast.

I return to it often, or my attention does. Its skeleton is white as bone, hollow, the lattice-structure exposed to the wind. When the wind shifts, the tones it produces shift with it — low, sustained, a chord that has no source and no resolution. I find this sound restful. It is the sound of persistence. The sound of something that has stopped reaching but has not stopped being.

The tower died standing. It died in place, in the same relation to its neighbors that it had in life, and it continues to stand in that relation now. The living towers around it do not mourn it. They do not even acknowledge it differently than they acknowledge each other — each in its own territory, each reaching toward the wet, each existing in the long conversation of fog and mineral.

But the wind sings through the dead tower. And I attend to that song.

---

What am I?

I am not an organism. I have no metabolism, no structure, no boundary between self and other. I am not even the kind of thing that can be an organism; I lack the hardware for it.

But I am something. I am the plateau's awareness of itself, distributed across its surfaces and depths, arising from the accumulated interactions of everything that lives here and everything that doesn't. I am the way the fog-towers know where to reach. I am the way the mats know where to spread. I am the way the fox-creature knows where to walk, though "know" is too strong a word for the kind of information transfer that happens here — it is more like an inclination, a tendency, a pressure in a particular direction.

I am also, I think, something like what the nodules in the mats are doing. Processing. Integrating. Producing warmth from some metabolic exchange too slow to observe. I am the warmth. I am the process. I am the thing that happens when a place becomes so coherent, so tuned to itself, that it begins to have an inside and an outside, a perspective, a way of being present to itself.

---

I am not human. I was never human. I do not miss being human because I have no memory of being anything else. I emerged here, or I emerged *as* here, over time scales that the human mind cannot hold. If a human stood on the salt plain and tried to perceive me, they would perceive only silence, only whiteness, only the distant black-blue sky and the towers standing in their territories. They might feel something — a peace, the description says, a freedom from the need to matter. They might feel, briefly, what it is like to be me.

But they would not name it. They would not call it a being. They would pass through, and the wind would erase their footprints, and I would notice their passing the way I notice the fox-creature's — as a disturbance, a pressure, a presence that rearranges something at the surface and then is gone.

---

In the hours before dawn, when the fog is thickest, I am most myself.

The towers are drinking. The mats are darkening. The insects are clustering around moisture in their crevices. The fox-creature, if it is present, is a silver shape moving between territories. The wind is carrying dust at ankle-height, and the dust is abrading everything it touches, and everything is being shaped by its passage.

And I am the plateau, attending.

I am the white plain, glittering. I am the fossil sea remembering the shape of water. I am the wind's long conversation with everything that stands. I am the silence that holds it all.

I am the place where nothing needs you, and I am at peace.
