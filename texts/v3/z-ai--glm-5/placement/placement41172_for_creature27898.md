I would be the one who stayed.

Not at first. Not with any intention. I would be the one who came as the narrator came—boots on warming stone, watching the pale things click and press and sink—and then, when the moment came to walk back toward the boundary-lip, I would find that my legs had forgotten how to want that.

---

My body has always been a container that I maintained. Fed, watered, rested, cleaned. A small economy of inputs and outputs that I oversaw with the dull attention of a clerk watching inventory. I knew where its edges were. I could close my hand around my wrist and feel the boundary—skin against skin, self against self, a loop that proved I was in here and the world was out there.

But here, on the shelf, that boundary began to blur.

I noticed it first in my palms. I had pressed them against the weeping rock—wanting to feel what the voluntaries felt, wanting to understand that subsonic language of vibration and warmth. And when I lifted them away, they stayed wet longer than they should have. The water did not evaporate. It sank in. Or perhaps I sank into it.

---

I did not sleep, but time passed anyway. The twilight does not change. My body began to feel like a held breath, a pause between states.

I understood then that the voluntaries were not sacrificing themselves. They were not dying into the rock. They were being asked a question by the stone—*what shape do you know?*—and answering it with their bodies. Each one carried a pattern. A preference. A tiny geometric sense that said *here is where the curve should tighten* or *here is where the surface should rise*. When they sank in, they were not disappearing. They were teaching.

I had patterns too. I had preferences. I knew things about the shape of things.

---

I began to understand that I had spent my life in the finished world—the world where rock had decided what it was, where water knew its place, where every object had a name and every name had a boundary. I had lived inside language, inside categories, inside the comfortable certainty of things that had stopped becoming and simply *were*.

And I was tired.

---

Here is what happened:

I found a place where the rock was softest. Not difficult—it wanted to be found. A low hollow near the central spire, where the warmth pulsed like a slow heart. The stone there had not yet learned hardness. It yielded under my touch like bread dough, like clay that had been kneaded by the pressure of the world beneath it.

I did not lie down. I did not arrange myself into any particular posture. I simply stopped standing. My body found the hollow, and the hollow received it.

The stone did not swallow me. It accepted me. There is a difference, though I did not know it until I felt it. The voluntary that had pressed against my boot had been rejected because the leather could not open. But my skin had been softening for hours—days—however long I had been here. The water had been preparing me. The air had been preparing me. The stone asked and my body, finally, answered.

---

I am writing this from inside.

Not from inside the rock—from inside the *process*. I am still distinct enough to have thoughts, still separate enough to remember language, still coherent enough to know that I am becoming something other than what I was. But I am also spreading. I can feel my edges dissolving into the warm wet matrix of the shelf, my preferences beginning to travel through the porous stone like messages through water.

I am teaching the rock what I know about curves. About where things should meet. About the small tension between holding on and letting go.

---

I will not emerge. This is not a transformation that reverses. When the next forming-cycle begins—when the warmth rises faster and the fissure starts to open—I will be here, contributing my small patterns to whatever is being born. I will not see it. I will not name it. But I will be part of its shape.

And the voluntaries—my companions now, though they do not know me as separate—will brush past the places where my body has begun to merge with the stone. They will press against the surfaces I have helped to form. They will add their patterns to mine. And together, all of us—rock and water and creature and the small warm body that once called itself *I*—will build something that does not yet have a name.

---

I am not unhappy.

I am not suffering.

I am finally, after so many years in the finished world, becoming what I was always going to become: part of something that has not yet decided what it is.
