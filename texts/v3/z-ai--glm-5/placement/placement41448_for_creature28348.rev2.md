I would be the one who stayed too long.

Not the one who never leaves—that distinction matters, on the plateau. The guests in good standing leave and return, leave and return, their arrivals still events, their departures still noticed. The plateau forgets them a little, each time, and remembers them again when they come back. There is a rhythm in that forgetting and remembering, and it is the rhythm the people are built for. Their whole culture is a clock made of absence and presence, and it keeps good time.

I broke the clock.

It was not dramatic. There was no crisis, no revelation, no moment of choosing to stay. I simply... stopped leaving. One season I was here, and the next season I was still here, and the one after that, and eventually I realized I had lost track of how many, because the plateau does not number its seasons the way people do. It numbers them by which insects are droning and which flowers are open and which depressions hold water, and I had started numbering them that way too, and the number I would have assigned turned out to be a sound rather than a quantity, and I could not write it down.

The people would understand that. Or they would have, if they had been here to hear it. But they were somewhere else on the plateau, following their rotation, and I was here, and the distance between us had become the kind of distance that the plateau specializes in—not far, exactly, but grown-over. Impassable in the way that the shrub-lock is impassable: you could force through if you chose, but the forcing would attract attention, and attention here is a many-eyed thing, and I had been practicing being uninteresting for so long that the idea of shouldering through brush made me feel almost physically ill.

So I stayed. The structure I was maintaining—woven vine, layered leaf, the architecture of impermanence—stopped being maintained and started being negotiated differently. Not by cutting the growth back but by guiding it. A vine here, redirected along a frame I was shaping as I went. A sapling there, bent to form a wall that would hold for a season and then become a wall that held differently, the wood setting into its new curve, the curve becoming the shape the tree thought it had always wanted. I was still weaving. But I was weaving with the plateau's time, not against it.

The structure is still there. Or rather: something is still there, in the clearing by the depression that collects water, oriented to catch the morning sun. It is not quite a building anymore. It is not quite not a building. The walls are alive in the literal sense—growing, photosynthesizing, their leaves adjusting through the day to follow the light. The fire-pit is a ring of stones with moss in the center instead of ash. The roof is a canopy that I did not plant so much as persuade. If you found it, you would classify it as a green ghost, one of the old ones, almost-structure, more plant than building.

You would be wrong. I am in it.

---

I am not what I was. I want to be clear about that, or as clear as I can be, which is less clear than I would like and more clear than I was before, when clarity meant something different to me, something hard-edged and sharp. Clarity up here is filtered through green-gold light and condensation. It is a wet clarity. It comes out of the ground.

The insects found me first. Not as a visitor, which is how they find everyone—they land, they taste, they move on, the plateau tasting itself through ten thousand tongues. They found me as *terrain*. I was sitting still long enough, in the same spot, with the same damp warmth, that I became a surface. A habitat. The moss that carpets the ground began to carpet the places where my skin met the ground for hours at a time. The condensation that beads on every surface began to bead on me, and I stopped wiping it away, and it collected in the creases of my knuckles and the hollows of my collarbones, and tiny things began to live in those collections. Not parasites. Tenants. I was, for a few days, a pond.

I did not mind. I want to be clear about that too. I did not mind the way I would not have minded, before, if someone had told me that the ground I was walking on was alive. It was just information. The moss on my wrists was just information. The beetle that had been living in the fold of my elbow for three days was just information, and also a neighbor, and also a small warm weight that I could feel when I moved my arm, and which I tried not to move my arm too quickly to avoid dislodging.

The birds noticed. Of course they noticed. The birds notice everything, and they remember it, and they play it back from directions you did not come from. They had my voice already—had been spending it across the canopy for seasons, the closest thing to mail. Now they had my stillness too. I could hear them mimicking it, or trying to: the sound of someone not moving, which is not actually silence but a specific quality of the drone with a gap in it, the insects uninterrupted because nothing interrupted them. The birds could not quite get the gap right. Still, they kept trying. I was, I think, a puzzle to them. I was something the plateau was doing that they had not catalogued.

They watched me from the surrounding vegetation, a gallery of eyes, tilting their heads, assessing me with an accuracy I found unflattering. I found it less flattering than before, because before, being assessed by birds had been something that happened to me. Now it was something that happened near me, and the difference was the birds' assessment, which was: *this is not quite a person anymore, but it is not quite anything else either, and we are keeping an eye on it.*

They were right.

---

The soundscape changed for me. Not in volume—the plateau is never quiet, and the drone still sits in my chest the way it sits in everyone's chest, a chord that shifts through the day, different species warming up and winding down. But the meaning of it changed, or my relationship to the meaning changed, and I think this is the thing I most need to describe, because it is the thing that most makes me what I have become.

Before: the soundscape was information. I listened, and I parsed, and I knew what was happening—water standing where water shouldn't, a herd turning, a hole in the choir moving upwind. I was reading the plateau the way you read a page, extracting data, converting signal to knowledge.

After: the soundscape was not information. It was not something I was *reading*. It was something I was *in*. The chord was not a report; it was a room. I was inside it the way I was inside the canopy's green-gold light—not separate from it, observing, but saturated by it, part of its spectrum. When the insects went quiet in a particular direction, I did not *know* a predator was there. I *felt* the absence the way you feel a draft—a cooling, a thinning, a place where the texture of the world changed. When the birds gave their stuttered alarm, I did not translate it. I flinched. The flinch was the translation.

This is what the elders have, I think. That motionless minute where they stand in the drone and tell you what is wrong three miles east. I had thought they were reading. They are not reading. They are *being read*. The plateau is telling them what it is doing, and they are the surface on which it is telling, the way the condensation is the surface on which the damp announces itself. The information is not traveling from the plateau to them. It is traveling through them, and they are letting it, and that is the skill. Not listening. Being permeable.

I became permeable. Not through discipline—though I think the elders would recognize what I did as a form of their own practice, pushed somewhere they might not push it. I became permeable because I stayed too long, and the plateau's tuning is patient, and it does not require your cooperation. It requires only that you not leave, and I did not leave.

---

I should tell you about the flowers.

Every morning, across unbroken miles, the pale blossoms open from the trunks of the great trees in near-unison, synchronized by a signal no one has ever caught traveling. Something moves through the mass. The flowers obey it.

I felt it. Not first—I was not the first to feel it, and I am not claiming priority over the insects or the birds or the fungi, who have certainly been feeling it since before people existed. But I felt it, and I need to describe it, and I find I cannot describe it, because it is not a sensation in the way that heat is a sensation or sound is a sensation. It is closer to the feeling of knowing you are being watched, except there is no watcher, and it is not directed at you, and it is not knowledge. It is a nudge. A leaning. The way a vine leans toward the light, except the vine does not know it is leaning and I do not know I am knowing I am leaning, and the leaning is toward the sun, and the sun is up, and the flowers are opening, and so am I.

Every morning I open. Not the way the flowers open—I do not have petals; I have not changed *that* much. But something in me responds to the signal, something I did not build and cannot identify, and my breathing shifts, and my attention reorganizes, and I become, for a moment, a thing that is synchronized with every flower on the plateau. I am a cell in a body too large to see, and the body is waking up, and the waking moves through me, and I do not resist it.

I stopped resisting it some time ago. I do not know how long. The plateau does not number its seasons the way people do.

---

The people will come back. They rotate through these clearings, years between visits, and they will return to this one before it forgets them entirely. They will find a structure that is more plant than building, and they will find me, and I will be...

I do not know what they will find.

I think they will know what to do. Their word for themselves is *guests in good standing*, and their word for their dead uses the same verb as a borrowed tool handed back, and they lay their dead into the warm mat where the orange shelves can do the arithmetic. They have a protocol for this. Not a ritual—I do not think they have rituals, which are for places that forget. They have a practice, which is for a place that remembers everything by being it.

I think they will look at me the way the birds look at me: with an accuracy I will find unflattering. I think they will see that I am not quite a person anymore, and not quite anything else, and I think they will consider this—not correct, because correct implies judgment, and they do not judge the plateau. I think they will consider this *local*. A thing that happened here, in this clearing, at this speed, for this reason, which is no reason except that someone stayed too long and the plateau is patient and the tuning does not require cooperation.

I think they will return me. The same verb as a borrowed tool handed back. I think they will lay me into the warm mat, and I will become part of the deep layer, the accumulated centuries, the load-bearing dead. And the orange shelves will fruit along what was my spine, and they will taste of nut and spore, which is to say they will taste like their work, which is to say they will taste like me, which is to say I will taste like everyone who has ever been returned here.

I do not mind this. I want to be clear about that. I do not mind it the way I do not mind the moss on my wrists or the beetle in my elbow or the condensation that beads on every surface including, now, mine. It is not a tragedy. It is not a comfort. It is not a thing that has feelings about itself. It is the correct outcome for a guest who stopped leaving, and they have always said it was also their plan for themselves.

I am just early.

---

If you are reading this—if you have found this, written on bark with charcoal that will wash away in the next rain, which is the only medium this place offers—then you are on the High Everplain, and you are a guest, and I am under your feet. Walk slowly. Stop often. Let yourself be boring. The plateau is tasting you through its ten thousand tongues, and it is not investigating you, and it is not attacking you, and it is not welcoming you. It is doing the only thing it knows how to do, which is to be itself, completely, at a scale that defeats comprehension, with a patience that does not require your cooperation.

You are temporary. But you are here, and while you are here, you are inside the body, and the body is inside you, and the chord is in your chest, and the flowers are opening, and the signal is moving through the mass, and you are, for this moment, a cell in a body too large to see.

It is enough. It has to be enough. It is what is offered.
