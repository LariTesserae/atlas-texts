# The Rim-Tender

I was not born in the Weep. This matters, though I've stopped being able to remember what it feels like to have dry skin.

I came fifteen years past, counting by the seasons the rim-stands mark through their leaves—how they fold tight during the driest months (and "dry" here means merely *less wet*, a relative term that outsiders never truly grasp) and spread open when the seepage runs heaviest. The trees know things. This is what I've learned. This is what I tend, now: the boundary where the Weep meets the world that doesn't yet know it's becoming.

My name is Mareth, but names hold less weight here than elsewhere. In the passages, people know me by my gait—the wide-footed rolling stride that all Weep residents develop, but with a difference. I walk like someone who learned late. Like someone whose body had to be convinced. Children born to this place move through the passages as if the wet stone were an extension of their own soles. I still calculate each step, even after fifteen years. I still feel the adjustment, the small effort of finding purchase.

This doesn't shame me. It reminds me that I came from somewhere else. That I chose this.

---

I didn't arrive with the breathing sickness that draws most newcomers. My lungs were whole—are whole still, though heavier now with the damp that settles in everyone who stays long enough. I came for the silence.

Not the absence of sound. The Weep is never truly silent; water makes sure of that. But I came from a place where noise meant violence—where every shout carried threat, where the sounds of my days were flung words and thrown objects and the particular silence of a room after someone has been struck. I was small there, too. Small and quiet in a way that didn't help. Small in a way that made me a target.

I heard about the Weep from a trader who passed through my village. He spoke of a place where voices stayed low, where the closeness of the walls made shouting impossible, where the water's constant movement drowned out arguments before they could form. He made it sound peaceful. He didn't mention the way the damp finds every crack in a person, the way it softens the hardened places and brings forth what's been buried.

He didn't need to. I would have come anyway.

---

My dwelling sits at the edge of the bowl—not at the rim, but close. The passages here run wider, the sky opens larger, and I can climb to where the rim-stands begin their gray march around the settlement's edge. This is my work: tending the trees that provide the Weep's timber and rope and shade, pruning the leaves that fold like hands, maintaining the living barrier between the hollow and the scrubland beyond.

The rim-tenders before me—there have been three, in the settlement's memory—chose the work because it required strength and patience, because it meant climbing rather than channeling. I chose it because it lets me stand at the boundary. Because some days I need to see the horizon, even if I no longer want to walk toward it.

The work suits what I've become. I spend the coolest hours among the stands, my hands moving through the branches, checking for rot (rare, here—too wet for dry rot, and the trees have adapted to their own saturation), harvesting leaves for the fiber-weavers below, cutting timber for repairs. The trees don't mind. They respond to care the way the water responds to channels—with preference, with purpose, with a kind of slow attention that feels like recognition.

I've learned their language. Not speech. The rim-stands communicate through their leaves: the way they fold early when a storm is coming from the scrubland, the way they hold open when the seepage is about to increase. I read them the way channel-workers read the water. We compare notes, sometimes—me and the hydrologists below—and we've started to notice patterns. The trees and the water are saying similar things. They're both responding to something deeper. Something neither of us can hear yet.

---

I dream of sinking.

Every night, the same descent. Not frightening—never frightening, though the first time I woke gasping, my body confused about whether I'd been breathing air or water. The dream goes like this: I stand at the edge of the bowl, where the rim-stands tremble. I step forward, but I don't fall. I sink. Through the earth, through the stone, through layers of soil that grow wetter as I descend. The water welcomes me. My lungs accept it. I continue downward, past the channels, past the deepest foundations of the settlement, into a darkness that glows faintly with the same pale luminescence as the mushrooms that fruit in the passages.

I'm not alone in the dream. Others are sinking with me—shapes I almost recognize, faces I've seen in the Weep, moving through the water with me like we're all part of the same slow current. We don't speak. We don't need to. We're descending toward something, and the descent itself is the point.

When I wake, my skin is wetter than when I slept. Not from external moisture—from within. Like something is preparing me. Like the Weep is working itself deeper into my body, cell by cell.

I don't tell the others. They dream it too; I know this without asking. But we don't discuss it. Some things are for living, not for naming.

---

Parrahn has stopped eating.

This is new—three days now, since the last new moon. Deevis tends her mother still, changes the channel-stones beneath the old woman's platform, keeps the moisture moving past her skin. But Parrahn's mouth stays closed. Her eyes stay open, fixed on something Deevis can't see.

The settlement is quiet about this. Quieter than usual. We feel the weight of waiting—like the pressure before a storm, like the moment when water stops moving and starts pooling. Everyone knows Parrahn is important, even those who don't know why. She heard the sound. She's been listening ever since. If she stops listening—if she finishes hearing—none of us know what happens next.

I've started climbing to the rim more often. Not for the trees. For the view. I stand at the bowl's edge and look out at the scrubland, the distant hills, the gray line of the lake, and I try to remember why I thought leaving would be impossible. The Weep has softened me, yes. It's changed my skin, my gait, my dreams. But I could still climb out. I could still walk away. The rim-stands wouldn't stop me; the water wouldn't rise to hold me.

I stay because I choose to. Because something is coming, and I want to be here when it arrives.

---

This morning, I found a new channel forming.

Not cut by hands—forming. The water had found a path between two dwellings where no path existed before, carving through the packed earth with a patience that looked, after fifteen years, like intention. I watched it for an hour, the way it moved around obstacles, the way it sought the lowest point, the way it seemed to *know* where it was going.

I thought about Corthin. About what happens when we try to teach the water instead of learning from it.

I didn't block the new channel. I didn't redirect it. I marked its path with stones—small ones, the kind channel-workers use to note changes—and I waited. By midday, one of the children had noticed. By evening, word had spread: the water is moving differently. Something has shifted.

Deevis came to me at dusk. She stood at the edge of my dwelling, her hands wet to the wrists, her face holding an expression I'd never seen before. Not fear. Something closer to readiness.

"My mother is going to speak," she said.

I didn't ask how she knew. In the Weep, we've learned that some knowledge arrives without being sought.

"When?"

"Tonight. When the water reaches its lowest point." She looked toward the rim, toward the stands I tend, toward the boundary I maintain. "You should be there. You hear the trees. Maybe you'll hear this too."

---

I should tell you what I've become.

I'm not a leader—I told you that. The Weep doesn't have leaders. But I've become something that matters here. Someone the channel-workers consult when the water behaves strangely. Someone the children seek out when they want to learn the rim-stands' language. Someone Deevis trusts enough to summon when her mother is about to break an eleven-year silence.

I'm someone who chose this place. Who keeps choosing it, every day, by not leaving. Who has softened and sunk and dreamed and listened, until the boundaries between me and the Weep have grown as permeable as the boundaries between water and stone.

Tonight, I'll sit with the others in Parrahn's dwelling. I'll listen to whatever she has to say—or whatever speaks through her, or whatever we're finally ready to hear. And then I'll climb back to my place at the edge, and I'll tend the rim-stands, and I'll watch the horizon, and I'll wait for whatever is coming—whether it rises from below, or descends from above, or speaks from the place where earth and water meet.

I am Mareth. I came from outside. I stayed for the silence. And now I'm listening, too.
