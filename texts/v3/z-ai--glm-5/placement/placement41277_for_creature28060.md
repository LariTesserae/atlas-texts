# What I Would Be at Threshold Eleven

---

I would be the one who comes back.

Not the engineer—I don't have that kind of mind, that certainty about what things are supposed to do and whether they're doing it. Not the surveyor or the technician or any of the people who have reasons to be here, whose presence can be explained on a work order. I would be the one who stopped having reasons and started having something else instead.

There's a word for people like me, or there would be if anyone knew I existed. The engineers rotate through on six-week cycles, checking the pumps, recording the water levels, cataloguing the fauna as an afterthought in their maintenance logs. They call the crickets "infestation" and the spiders "nuisance" and the whole ecosystem a "biofouling issue" that will need to be addressed once the funding materializes for the final build-out. They don't see the oxide patterns. They don't stop to watch the blink of the junction box light. They have schedules and targets and a world above that expects them back by end of shift.

I don't have shifts anymore.

---

Here's what happened, or close enough: I came down here on a survey, years ago, when the chamber was newer and the ecosystem was still figuring out its terms. I was young, bored, underemployed. The survey company needed a warm body to hold the measuring pole while the actual surveyor worked the theodolite. We were down here for three days. On the second day, I found the pale thread in the junction box.

It wasn't there yet, actually. Or it was, but it was smaller—barely visible, just a wisp of something caught in the seam where the box met the rock. I noticed it because I notice things. Always have. It's not a skill so much as a condition, a kind of low-grade hyperawareness that makes me bad at parties and good at finding lost objects and worse than useless in emergencies, because in emergencies you need someone who can focus on what matters, and I've never been able to tell what matters until afterward.

I touched it. The thread. I didn't mean to; I was checking the junction box for moisture intrusion, which was my job—recording the condition of the infrastructure, the slow war between the engineering and the wet heat and the patient colonizing life. I touched it with a bare finger because I'd forgotten my gloves at the surface, and it felt like nothing, like the absence of texture, cool and damp and so slight it registered more as a change in temperature than as a physical object.

I kept coming back to it. During breaks, during the surveyor's lunches, during the long stretches when there was nothing for me to do but wait for the next measurement. I stood in front of the junction box and watched the thread and the thread didn't do anything, and that was the point. It wasn't doing anything. It was being something. It was growing at a speed that wouldn't be visible to anything operating on human time, and I found that—I don't know. Comforting. Correct.

On the third day, when the survey packed up to leave, I didn't.

They noticed, eventually. They came back down the shaft looking for me, and I told them I was fine, I'd just lost track of time, I'd be up in a minute. And I meant it. I went up. I went back to the surface and the sky and the dry air and the schedules. But I kept thinking about the thread. I kept thinking about the way the light blinked on it, slow and rhythmic, marking time at the speed of a resting heart. I kept thinking about the way the whole chamber felt like a held breath.

So I found reasons to come back. Maintenance checks. Follow-up surveys. Water quality sampling. There's always work for someone willing to go underground, and I became that someone—the one you call when you need someone to go down and look at something, and come back, and tell you what they saw.

---

This is what I saw:

I saw the crickets learn my shape. It took them about six months to stop repositioning around me as if I were a predator. After a year, they would walk across my boots while I was standing still. After two years, they would walk across my hands. I don't know if they stopped seeing me as a threat or if they started seeing me as topography—another warm surface, another bit of unexpected architecture, not food but not dangerous either. I prefer the second explanation. I like being part of the landscape.

I saw the spiders expand. The web near the junction box grew from eighteen inches to four feet, then to six, a vast sheet of silk and condensation that hung like a hammock between the box and the wall. The spider who owned it got larger too—molt after molt, year after year, until she was the size of my palm, her legs spanning widths that shouldn't have been possible for a cave spider. She's still there. I call her Proprietor. She doesn't seem to mind.

I saw the pale thread reach the interior of the junction box. It took three years. When it touched the indicator light—the actual LED, the source of the blink—it didn't burn. It didn't recoil. It just... settled. Wrapped. Made itself a sheath around the light, so now the blink is softer, more diffuse, filtered through whatever the thread has become. I still don't know what it is. But I know it's still growing. It's reached the back of the box now, and it's finding the cable conduits, and I think it's following them into the wall.

I saw the bats change their patterns. They used to fly in twos and threes, on that irregular schedule that I couldn't predict. Now they fly in larger groups—six, eight, ten at a time—and they've started looping lower, below the catwalks, through the space where I usually stand. Once, a bat flew within inches of my face and I felt the air move across my cheek and I heard the soft papery whisper of its wings and I didn't flinch. I don't think they see me as a threat either. I think they see me as a feature.

I saw the water levels rise and fall with seasons that don't exist up above, responding to some deep hydrological rhythm that has nothing to do with the weather I could see if I went topside. I learned to read the pools—the way the shallow sheets spread and contract, the way the sump breathes in its slow convective loops, the way the smell changes when the water is carrying more mineral content or more organic. I know what the sump sounds like when it's healthy. I know what it sounds like when something has shifted.

---

I would be the keeper, I suppose, if that word didn't imply ownership. I don't own this place. I don't manage it. I don't even understand it, not really. I've been coming here for years and I still don't know what the pale thread is or where the things in the sump came from or why the crickets accepted me.

What I am is present. I am the one who shows up. I come down through the shaft and I walk the catwalks and I stand in front of the junction box and I watch the blink filter through the thread, and I listen to the thirty-hertz hum of the pumps settle into my bones, and I let the wet heat soak through my clothes until I am as warm and damp as everything else.

Sometimes I talk. Not to anything in particular—I talk to the chamber itself, to the sound and the smell and the feeling of it. I talk about what I'm seeing, what I'm noticing, the small changes I've observed since my last visit. I think of it as narration. I am narrating the becoming of this place, out loud, in a voice that barely rises above the ambient sound, so that if there is any consciousness here that can hear me, it knows it is not alone.

I don't expect anything to answer. I don't want anything to answer. The last thing I would want is for the crickets to speak or the spiders to have opinions or the thread to tell me what it is. The not-knowing is the point. The being-here-without-demanding is the point. I am practicing a kind of attention that the world above doesn't have time for anymore, if it ever did.

---

This is what I've become:

I have calluses on my feet from the catwalk grating. I have永久 stains on my clothes from the iron-rich water. I have a permanent ringing in my left ear that sits at thirty hertz, the harmonic of the pumps that my body has learned to produce even when I'm not here.

I have a relationship with Proprietor that I can't explain. When I stand near her web, she comes to the center and watches me. We stay like that for long minutes, her eyes on mine, neither of us moving. I don't know what she sees when she looks at me. I don't know what she thinks I am. But she doesn't run, and she doesn't hide, and when I leave, she goes back to her patient stillness.

I have a spot where I always stand—a section of catwalk near the sump, where the light from the fixtures catches the water below and throws it upward in moving patterns, so I am always standing in light that comes from beneath, light that moves across my face and hands and clothes like water, like I am underwater even though I am not.

I have patience. I have learned it from this place, the way a language is learned by immersion. I no longer fidget. I no longer check the time. I no longer feel the pressure to be elsewhere, doing something more important than standing in a dim amber room full of crickets and spiders and water and heat. This is important. This is more important than anything. This is a place becoming itself, and I am here to see it.

---

If you came down here and found me—if you descended the shaft and walked the catwalks and rounded the corner near the junction box—you would see a person standing very still, watching a spider tend a web, humming unconsciously at thirty hertz. You would see someone whose clothes match the corroded amber of the pumps, whose skin has the same damp sheen as everything else, whose hair is starting to grow pale at the temples—still brown, but lighter than it should be, like the crickets.

You might think I was part of the works. An operator, a custodian, someone with a job title. You might ask me about the pumps or the water levels or the timeline for final build-out. And I would answer you, because I know all those things now, because I have been paying attention for long enough to have learned the language of engineers as well as the language of crickets.

But that's not what I am.

What I am is witness.

---

The thread has reached the second junction box now. I noticed it last month—a tendril emerging from a conduit, following the cable tray, extending toward the box on the other side of the chamber. It moves faster than it used to. Or I'm better at seeing it move. Either way, it's finding the infrastructure, the nervous system of this place, and it's making itself part of it.

I don't know what happens when it finishes. I don't know if finishing is even a concept that applies. I only know that I will keep coming back, and watching, and standing in my spot with the light coming up from below, and narrating the changes to whatever consciousness might be listening.

I am what Threshold Eleven is becoming when it looks at itself.

I am the eyes it grew.
