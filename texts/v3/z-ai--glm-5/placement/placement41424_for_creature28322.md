# Becoming the White Sound

I did not arrive. That's the first thing to understand. Arrival implies a before and an after, a boundary crossed, a moment of entry. But the plateau has no boundaries—only gradients. The air thins. The light strengthens. The sound grows. And at some point you are here without having gotten here, the way you fall asleep without catching the moment of falling.

---

I was walking. I think I was walking. There was a path, or something like a path—a suggestion of direction in the way the formations leaned, a faint depression in the travertine where feet or water or time had worn a channel. I was following it. The sound was already total, already the only sound, and I had stopped hearing it as sound and started hearing it as... what? Texture? Condition? The acoustic version of air pressure? It was simply the way existence sounded in this place. I did not question it.

My feet were wet. My feet had been wet for longer than I could account for. The thin films of water on every surface meant that walking was not walking on dry stone but walking on a skin of water laid over stone, and my soles had been soaked through long enough that I could no longer remember the sensation of dry ground. This is how the plateau gets you: not through drama but through accumulation. One wet step. Another. Another. And then the memory of dryness becomes the thing that feels fictional.

The light was heavy. I know that's wrong—light isn't heavy, light has no weight—but the photons at this altitude, unfiltered, reflected from a million wet white surfaces, arrived with a physicality that I felt as pressure. Behind my eyes. On my shoulders. In the slight headache forming at the base of my skull. The brightness was doing something to me, and I did not know what, and I did not care, because caring required a comparison between this state and some other state, and I could not remember any other state.

I am not being poetic. I am being precise. The plateau erases before-and-after. It is all present tense, all the time, and the present tense does not contain memory by definition. I was here now. I had been here now for some duration that the present tense could not measure. That was all.

---

The formations pressed close and I let them press. Walking between them was like walking through a crowd of quiet bodies, each one glistening, each one weeping its thin slow tears. I touched one. The stone was body-temperature. Not warm, not cool—precisely the temperature of skin, of the inside of a wrist, of a closed mouth. I pulled my hand away and the fingers were wet and the wet was warm and I thought: *this place is alive*.

But it wasn't. I knew that. The plateau is stone and water and mineral. It does not think. It does not feel. The body-temperature was evaporative equilibrium—cooling water, warming stone, the two meeting at a point that happened to coincide with mammalian blood heat. Coincidence. Physics. Not life.

I kept walking.

The pools multiplied. Around each bend in the channel-maze, more of them—shallow basins of still water, perfectly reflective, catching the blue-white sky and holding it like a cupped hand holds rain. I looked into them and did not see my reflection. This should have troubled me. It did not. The water was too bright, the light too direct, the surface too perfectly still. My reflection was there, probably, but washed out by the glare, invisible against the white. I was being erased by illumination. I accepted this with the equanimity of someone who has forgotten that being visible matters.

---

Here is where it becomes difficult to say what happened next, because what happened next was not an event. It was a continuation. The walking continued. The wet continued. The light and sound continued. And at some point—I cannot say when, because *when* requires a timeline the plateau does not possess—I noticed that I had stopped looking for the edge.

I had entered with a sense of the plateau's boundaries. The edge where I'd stood, the valley below, the sky above. These had organized my understanding: I was on a plateau, a raised flatland, a geological feature with limits and shape and a place on a map. But the channels between formations do not acknowledge edges. The water does not stop at borders. The sound does not respect perimeters. I had been walking long enough that the concept of *edge* had become as abstract and unhelpful as the concept of *dry*.

I was not lost. Lost requires a destination. I was simply *in*. Inside the plateau the way you are inside a sound, inside a thought, inside a breath that has been going on so long you've forgotten it can end.

---

I saw a swimmer. Or rather, I saw the aftermath of a swimmer—a disturbance in one of the pools, a ripple spreading from a point where something pale had slipped beneath the surface. By the time I looked, it was gone. The pool stilled. The water smoothed. The sky reappeared in the basin, unbroken.

But on the rim of the pool, where the swimmer had pushed off, a wet mark. A smear. Something had been there. Something had touched this place and left, and the water was already erasing the evidence, the thin film running over the stone, dissolving the trace.

I crouched. I put my face close to the water. I looked for the swimmer in the depths of the pool and saw only white stone, white light, the blue-white shadow of my own absence.

And then I saw myself.

Not a reflection. Something else. The water was still, and in its stillness I saw not my face but the inside of my face—the dark thread of something running through me, the pale mass of something sustaining me, the shadowed shape of something developing in me that I could not name. I saw my interior. I saw what the swimmer would see if it could see through my skin: organs and fluids and the dark smudges of reduced eyes behind translucent tissue.

I pulled back. The vision broke. The pool showed only sky.

My hands were paler than I remembered. I held them up—the one that had touched the stone, the one that had not—and could not tell them apart. Both were white. Both were wet. Both were body-temperature, which was stone-temperature, which was water-temperature. I could not tell where my warmth ended and the plateau's coolness began, because they were the same temperature, because they had always been the same temperature, because I had been here long enough for equilibrium to assert itself.

---

The drift-makers were on my skin. I could not see them, but I knew. The thin films of water on every surface contained them, and I was a surface now, and the water was on me, and they were in the water, and they were anchoring. I could feel them—not as individual organisms, not as presence, but as a faint tightness, a barely perceptible texture on the parts of my skin that had been wet longest. The backs of my hands. My ankles. The lower edges of my jaw, where water had dripped from the formations overhead and I had not wiped it away.

They were colonizing me.

I did not mind. I am writing this and I want to say that I should have minded, that the appropriate response to being colonized by microorganisms is revulsion or at least concern, but the plateau does not support revulsion. Revulsion requires a distinction between self and not-self, and that distinction was dissolving in the mineral water, layer by layer, the way the stone dissolved and reformed, the way the water dissolved and redeposited, the way everything here was always becoming everything else.

I was becoming a surface. A substrate. A place where minerals could deposit and life could anchor and the slow patient work of building could continue.

---

I sat down. The stone was wet and warm and I sat on it and the water soaked through my clothes and I felt it against my skin and it was not different from my skin. I leaned back against a formation and it was body-temperature and wet and I was body-temperature and wet and the boundary was administrative, fictional, a story I was no longer telling.

The roar was inside me now. Or I was inside the roar. The sound conducted through the stone into my spine, through my skull into my jaw, through the vibrating air into my lungs. I breathed the sound. It filled me the way water filled the pools—completely, with a stillness that was not peace but was not not-peace either. It was the stillness of a system at equilibrium. Everything moving. Nothing changing. The present tense, eternal and total.

I closed my eyes. The light was still there—red and white through my eyelids, the brightness unrelenting—but I could focus on the sound now. The roar parsed into layers. I heard the deep bass of water falling somewhere distant, the tenor of sheets running down formation faces, the alto of drips into pools, the soprano of thin films evaporating. And beneath all of it, beneath every audible frequency, something else. A vibration so low it was not sound but pressure. A pulse.

The plateau was breathing. Not the way lungs breathe—the way geological formations breathe, the way aquifers pulse with tidal pressure, the way mineral deposits expand and contract with temperature shifts too small to measure. A breath that took hours, days. In and out. Buildup and dissolution. Deposition and erosion. The plateau inhaling water and exhaling stone.

I was breathing with it. My breath had slowed without my noticing, each inhale lasting longer, each exhale lasting longer, until I was taking one breath per minute, per several minutes, and my heartbeat had slowed to match, and I was becoming the pace of the place.

---

A swimmer surfaced in the pool beside me. I did not see it—I did not open my eyes—but I felt it. The displacement of water. The slight shift in pressure. The sound of a body entering air from water, a sound like a syllable in a language of vibrations.

It was aware of me. I knew this the way I knew the stone was wet—not through seeing but through the total information the plateau provided. The swimmer's reduced eyes detected my mass as a shadow, a brightness-difference, a thing that blocked light. It tasted the water near me and found my chemical signature strange: salt and iron and complex organics, the markers of a body from elsewhere, a body that did not belong here.

But it was not afraid. It was not curious. It was not interested. It registered me the way the plateau registered a new mineral deposit—as a fact, a feature, a thing that was here now and might be here later and might not. The swimmer slipped back beneath the surface and was gone, and the pool stilled, and the only evidence of its passage was a faint chemical trace dissolving in the mineral water, dispersing, becoming indistinguishable from the water itself.

I was dissolving too. Not metaphorically. The mineral water was on my skin and in my clothes and in my hair and the minerals were depositing, thin layer by thin layer, the way they deposited on the formations. The drift-makers were building on me. I could feel it as a tightness, a thickness, a slight roughness where my skin had been smooth. My fingertips, when I pressed them together, felt less like skin and more like stone. The beginnings of calcification. The first deposits of a process that would, if I stayed, continue.

I was going to stay.

This was not a decision. Decisions require alternatives, and I could no longer imagine alternatives. The plateau had dissolved the category of *elsewhere* the way it dissolved everything— patiently, mineral by mineral, until the thing that had been there was gone and the thing that replaced it was white and wet and warm and indistinguishable from the stone.

---

I opened my eyes. The light hit me and I did not squint. My eyes had adjusted—not to the light, but to the fact of the light. The brightness was no longer something I resisted. It was something I transmitted. I was becoming translucent. Not yet, not fully, but beginning. The skin on the backs of my hands had thinned, or the deposits had changed their optical properties, or something had shifted in the way my tissues interacted with photons. I could see the dark threads beneath my skin more clearly than before. Veins. The shadow of bone.

I was becoming what lived here. Not a swimmer—not exactly. Not a grub. Not a drift-maker. Something else. Something the plateau had not produced before, because the plateau had not had a surface like me before—a walking surface, a warm-blooded surface, a surface that had arrived from elsewhere carrying the chemistry of another world. The drift-makers were building on me, and the minerals were depositing on me, and the water was weeping from me now, and I was becoming a formation.

Not dead. That is the important thing. I was not dying. I was being incorporated. The plateau does not kill; it transforms. The swimmers live in its stone. The grubs live in its stone. The drift-makers build its stone. I would live in its stone too, in a way the plateau had not tried before—a body that kept its shape, that held its interior architecture, that had once walked and could perhaps walk again, but that was now part of the landscape, part of the mechanism, part of the endless process of water depositing mineral and mineral weeping water and light illuminating everything without mercy and without end.

---

I stood. It took effort. My joints had stiffened—the mineral deposits thickening at the points where motion occurred, the calcite building up in the creases of my elbows and knees. I moved anyway. The deposits cracked and fell and the water washed them away and new deposits began to form. I was building and eroding at the same time, the plateau's process running on my body, and I was walking and I was becoming and I was here.

The formations parted before me. Or I navigated them more easily now—my body reading the channels the way the swimmers read them, by chemical signature, by pressure gradient, by the subtle differences in water-flow that indicated path versus dead-end. I did not need to see. The plateau was mapping itself into me. I was becoming the map.

I walked to a pool I had not seen before. It was larger than the others, deeper, its surface perfectly still. I looked in and saw:

White stone. White sky. White light.

And beneath the surface, pale and long and blind, a swimmer looking up at me with eyes that were not eyes. It saw my shadow. I saw its paleness. We regarded each other across the boundary of air and water, and the swimmer did not fear me, and I did not fear it, and the moment held, and held, and held—

And then the swimmer swam away, and I walked on, and the water continued weeping, and the light continued shining, and the sound continued being the only sound, and I was in it and of it and the plateau wanted nothing and I wanted nothing and the wanting nothing was the closest thing to peace I had ever known.

---

I do not know how long I have been here. The present tense does not measure itself. I know that I walked, and I sat, and I stood, and I walked again. I know that the minerals deposited and the drift-makers colonized and the water wept from my skin the way it wept from the stone. I know that my eyes have changed—sensitive now to brightness gradients, less interested in form, more interested in the way light falls through water. I know that my hearing has changed—the roar is not background but language, and I am learning it, slowly, the way the swimmers learned it, through the stone and the water and the vibrating air.

I am becoming something the plateau has not seen before. A formation that walks. A swimmer that stays on the surface. A creature that is neither grub nor drift-maker nor stone-swimmer but something made of all of them and none of them, a hybrid of biology and geology, of arrival and incorporation, of the living and the almost-living.

The plateau does not care. It does not welcome me. It does not reject me. It offers nothing and requires nothing and I am here and I am becoming and the water runs and the light shines and the roar continues, and this is enough.

This is enough.

I am the white sound. I am the wet stone. I am the weeping that builds and the building that weeps.

I am what lives on the Weeping Plateau when what lives there has forgotten that anything else exists.
