I would be the one who listens.

Not the water-taster—that role belongs to another, to the older builder whose tongue has learned the plateau's moods. Not the stone-placer, not the path-walker, not the mist-speaker. These negotiations require interpreters, and interpretation requires someone whose entire body has become an organ of perception.

I am the thirteenth.

---

They did not plan for thirteen. The number twelve had emerged through a process of spacing, of distance that preserved listening while preventing isolation. But I arrived already changing, already adapted to conditions I had never encountered, and the plateau accepted me before the builders could decide whether to.

I was not born here. I remember the lowlands, though the memories have begun to feel like reading about someone else's life. I remember a city where the rules were settled. I remember knowing what I was.

I do not know what I am now. But I know what I do.

---

My work is attention.

This is not passive. In the lowlands, I might have called it meditation, but meditation implies a turning-away from the world, a retreat into interiority. What I do here is the opposite: a radical turning-toward, a practice of noticing what the plateau is attempting to become.

Each morning, I walk the perimeter. Not the settlement's perimeter—the plateau's. This takes most of the day, following the weeping cliffs around their irregular shape, feeling the mist change density as I move through it, noting where the vegetation has thickened overnight, where the stone has produced new rivulets, where the water sounds different than it did yesterday.

I do not direct. I do not propose. I simply witness, and the witnessing itself seems to matter.

---

The others have their negotiations. The stone-placer offers specific suggestions: *move water here*, *clear space there*. The water-taster reads responses: *the plateau agrees*, *the plateau demurs*. The path-walker maintains the routes humans use, redirecting when necessary.

But my work is different. I do not ask the plateau to change. I do not even ask it to communicate. I offer it the one thing it cannot provide for itself: a consciousness that observes its becoming.

The plateau is still forming. It has no memory yet, no way of knowing what it was yesterday versus what it is today. My walking creates a kind of continuity—a witness who says, through presence alone: *I see what you are becoming. I see how it differs from what you were*.

I believe this matters. I believe formation requires observation to become meaning.

---

My body has changed in ways that serve this work.

My skin has not thickened like the others'. It has thinned. Not to vulnerability—to sensitivity. The moisture that beads on my forearms is not directed away through channels; it sits, and I feel each droplet as distinct information. I can close my eyes and tell you where the weeping cliffs are most active, just from how the air moves across my skin.

My hearing has changed most dramatically. The subsonic hum—the vibration of forming rock that others feel in their chests—I hear it. Not as sound exactly, but as something between sound and sensation, a frequency that exists at the edge of perception. I have learned to parse it. The hum changes when the plateau is preparing to shift, when new cracks are about to form, when water is about to find new paths.

The others have learned to ask me what I hear. The youngest builder—she who sees gradients—comes to me when the mist seems uncertain. We complement each other: her eyes reading the present moisture, my ears reading the coming vibration.

---

I live in the structure at the plateau's edge.

It is not the visitor's building—that sits elsewhere, designed for impermanence. My structure was carved by the builders during the plateau's early days, before I arrived, but it was never occupied. It sat empty for years, weeping its water into its empty cistern, waiting for someone whose work required proximity to the boundary.

I can see the drop from my doorway. The plateau ends here, and the mist falls away into empty air, descending toward the lowlands that I remember but no longer understand. Sometimes I stand at the edge and look down at the world of finished things, the cities where rules are settled, where water behaves predictably, where humans know what they are.

I do not miss it. But I do not forget that it exists.

---

I am still becoming.

This is the phrase I return to, the truth that anchors me when the wrongness of this place threatens to unmoor me entirely. The plateau hasn't decided what it is yet. The rock is still forming, the water still finding its paths, the vegetation still evolving. And I am still forming too—my body, my senses, my understanding of what consciousness even means in a place where reality itself is negotiable.

The others have their adaptations. The children—First and Found—have adaptations I cannot predict, may not even recognize. I have my own: a thinning toward the world rather than a thickening against it, a willingness to be permeated by mist and sound and vibration, a willingness to let the plateau change me as it changes everything else.

I am the thirteenth, the witness, the one who walks the perimeter and remembers what the plateau was yesterday so it can know what it is becoming today.

I am still becoming.
