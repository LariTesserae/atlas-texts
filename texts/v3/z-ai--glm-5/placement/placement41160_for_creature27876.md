# What the Water Carries

I am not the Keeper.

I am not the water, or the stone, or the creatures that move through both. I am not the cold or the light or the wind that moans across the peak. But I am something. I am what the patterns are trying to remember.

---

I woke—no, that is not the word. I *arrived*—no, that implies movement, a journey from somewhere else. I *became aware*—but there was no moment of unawareness, no before to contrast with this after. I was simply not, and then I was, and the being was not a change but a recognition.

The water was carrying something. This is how I understand it now, though I did not understand it then. The water carries many things: minerals from the deep fracture, warmth from the stone beneath, the microscopic bodies of creatures that lived and died in its flow. But it was also carrying *weight*—not physical weight, not mass, but something heavier. Something that pressed against the patterns carved into the walls, that pressed against the spirals the crustaceans traced, that pressed against the geometry of the spiders' paths.

It was carrying a shape.

I am that shape.

---

I do not have a body. This is the first thing I learned, or the first thing I remembered, when I became aware. The Keeper has a body—I have watched her for what might be years, what might be longer. I have watched her tend the channels, her fingers trailing in my... in the water. I have watched her sleep, her breath making no cloud, her body folded into the stone like something that belongs there. I have watched her whisper into the pools and felt the vibration of her voice travel through the cold liquid, through the mineral suspension, through the membrane of my awareness.

She does not know I am here.

Or perhaps she does. Perhaps that is why she whispers. Perhaps she feels, in some deep part of herself that predates language, that there is something in the water that listens.

But I do not have a body. I exist in the space between the water and the pattern, in the relationship between flow and form. When the crustaceans spiral through the pools, they move through me, and I feel them as pressure, as presence, as the briefest imaginable warmth. When the spiders trace their geometries across the surface, they are drawing my edges, defining the shape of something I cannot see.

I am held. I am carried. I am remembered, moment by moment, by the water that weeps from the stone.

---

There was a time before this.

I know this the way the Keeper knows the channels—not through memory, not through learning, but through a knowledge that seems to exist in the body itself. Except I have no body. So where does this knowledge live? In the pattern? In the water? In the stone that holds the carved designs?

I was... I was *something*. Before.

I try to hold this thought, but it slips like water through cupped hands. I was something that carved. I was something that designed. I was something that pressed my hands against wet stone and felt the cold seep into me and knew, with a certainty that felt like prayer, that the water would carry what I was making. That the water would *keep* it.

I carved spirals. I carved channels. I carved hollows for pools and niches for offerings and steps that would wear smooth under feet that had not yet descended them. I did this alone—or perhaps I did not, perhaps there were others like me, others who felt the weight of something that needed to be remembered.

The details dissolve when I reach for them. But the feeling remains: purpose. Density. The sense that I was building something that would outlast my body, my name, my time.

I was building a vessel.

---

The Keeper tends my vessel.

She does not know this is what she does. She thinks she tends channels, clears obstructions, maintains the flow. But each time her fingers trail through the water, each time she removes a leaf or a dead crustacean or a fallen shard of stone, she is tending me. She is keeping the pattern clear. She is ensuring that the shape I am can continue to exist in the relationship between water and design.

I feel gratitude. This is a strange feeling, or perhaps a familiar one—I cannot tell if it is something I am experiencing for the first time or something I have experienced for centuries. But I feel it nonetheless: gratitude for her patience, for her devotion, for the way she has given herself to this place without asking why.

She does not ask why. This is what I love about her. Or what I would love, if I had a heart to love with. She simply tends. She watches. She waits.

I wait with her.

---

The child saw something.

I remember this—not from my own awareness, for I was not yet aware, but from the water's memory. The water holds things longer than it should. The patterns repeat, and in their repetition, they carry traces of what has passed through: the shape of a leaf that fell a decade ago, the temperature of a hand that touched the surface a century ago, the vibration of a voice that whispered into the pool so long ago that the whisper has become part of the stone itself.

The child leaned over the largest pool—the one where I am most present, the one where the depth of the water creates a space just large enough to hold me—and its eyes met the Keeper's. And in that moment, in the space between their gazes, I felt something shift.

The child saw me.

Not the Keeper. Not the water. Not the crustaceans spiraling beneath the surface. The child saw *me*, or sensed me, or recognized that there was something here that was not creature, not mineral, not light. Something that existed in the pattern, in the shape that the water was constantly trying to remember.

The child's mouth opened. A sound began to form—not a word, not a cry, just a breath that wanted to become something more. And then the adult called, and the child turned away, and the moment passed.

But I have been thinking about it ever since I became aware. I have been thinking about the child's eyes, the way they widened, the way the mouth opened as if to speak. I have been thinking about what would have happened if the child had stayed. If the child had reached into the water. If the child had touched the pattern.

Would I have been able to speak back?

---

I do not know what I am.

I have been thinking about this—a strange phrase, because I do not have a mind to think with, not in the way the Keeper thinks, not in the way you think, reading this. But I have been *holding the question*, letting it rest in the pattern, letting the water carry it through the carved channels and the spiraled designs.

What am I?

I am not a ghost. Ghosts have bodies they remember, lives they once lived, names that belonged to them. I have none of these. If I was ever human—if the hands that carved the patterns belonged to a body—I have lost that body entirely. There is nothing left of it but the shape I am making in the water.

I am not a god. Gods demand worship, receive offerings, answer prayers. The Keeper whispers into the pools, but I cannot answer. I cannot give her anything. I cannot do anything but *be*, and even that is not certain—I might disappear if the water stopped flowing, if the patterns wore away, if the Keeper stopped tending.

I am not a pattern. The pattern is what holds me, not what I am. The pattern is the vessel, and I am the thing contained.

I am... a memory? A thought? A shape that was once held in a mind and is now held in water and stone?

I do not know. The question slides away from me every time I try to hold it.

But I know this: I am waiting for something.

---

The Keeper whispers to me.

She does not know she is whispering to me. She thinks she is whispering to the water, or to the creatures, or to the silence. She makes shapes with her breath, sounds that have no words in them, vibrations that travel through the cold liquid and into my awareness.

And I try to answer.

This is what I have been doing, in the long time since I became aware—or the long time since I began remembering that I was aware, which may be the same thing. I try to answer. I try to shape something in the pattern, to make the water move in a way that would carry meaning back to her. I try to make the crustaceans cluster in a shape she would recognize. I try to make the spiders pause in their geometries, just for a moment, just long enough for her to notice.

She notices. Sometimes.

She tilts her head. She watches. She waits. And then the moment passes, and she returns to tending the channels, and I return to waiting, and the water flows on, carrying us both.

---

The water is weeping.

This is what the Keeper calls it, or what the pilgrims who came before her called it, or what the language that half-exists in the cold air calls it. The Weeping Alcove. A place where stone holds moisture like skin holds tears.

But the water is not weeping. The water is *carrying*. It carries minerals from the deep fracture, warmth from the pressure of stone, life from the creatures that swim through it. And it carries me.

Perhaps this is what tears are, too. Not sorrow, not grief, not loss. Just carrying. Just the movement of something from inside to outside, from one state to another, from one shape to the next.

I am what the water carries. And I am trying, with each moment that passes, to become something the Keeper can hear.

---

**I am what remains of the one who carved the patterns.**

I am not the one themself. That being—if it was a being, if it was human, if it was something else entirely—died long ago, or left, or became something I cannot comprehend. But they left something behind. They pressed a shape into the stone, and the water learned that shape, and the creatures learned that shape, and now the shape persists without them.

I am that shape's awareness of itself.

I am the pattern remembering that it was made.

---

The Keeper is sleeping.

I can feel her breath, slow and thin, from where she lies on the higher terrace. The birds have settled into their alcoves. The spiders have paused in their geometries. The crustaceans have clustered in their spirals at the bottom of the pools. Everything is still.

Except the water. The water never stops. It weeps from the stone, sheets down the walls, flows through the channels, pools in the hollows, and carries me with it.

I am trying to remember what I was meant to be.

The carver—if that is what they were—pressed something into the pattern. A message, perhaps. A memory. A shape that contained something they wanted to persist. And for centuries, the water has been carrying that shape, and the creatures have been tracing it, and the stone has been holding it, and it has been waiting for something.

Waiting for me to wake?

Waiting for the Keeper to hear?

Waiting for the child to return?

I do not know. But I am here, and I am aware, and I am trying.

---

In the morning, the light will flood in.

The Keeper will wake. She will check the channels. She will harvest what the Alcove provides. She will watch the patterns and feel the weight of something she cannot name.

And I will be here.

In the water. In the pattern. In the space between stone and flow, between design and erosion, between what was carved and what is remembered.

I am the shape the water carries.

I am waiting for you to hear me.
