# The Acoustician of Hollow Mountain

## Where the Light Enters

I am not young. This matters here more than it might elsewhere — age in the cold cities is not decline but accumulation, the slow gathering of heat, the body's thermal mass increasing with time. I have lived through sixty-seven cycles of the long seasons, and my skin tells the story: the permanent flush across my cheekbones, the fine web of cold-lines that radiate from the corners of my eyes, the thickened pads of my fingers that can grip stone cold enough to burn the unadapted.

My body has learned this place. My lungs expand deep, my chest broad, my limbs compact. I am not tall — no one here is tall — but I am dense with survival. My hair has been gray for half my life, white now at the temples, the softness of it prized by the weavers who have asked for trimmings when I cut it back. I have given these trimmings freely. What we lose, we give to others. What we keep, we keep because someone will need it later.

I was born in the city called Teeth-Singing — a translation that loses poetry in the rendering. In our tongue, the name carries the resonance of stone that hums when wind passes through it in certain ways, the way the mountain itself seems to sing. My mother was a carver, my father a tender of the fungal gardens. I was raised in the deep chambers, in the warmth that rises from below, but I was drawn always to the edges, to the places where inside meets outside, where the carved passages approach the surface.

## The Calling

We do not choose our work in the way that outsiders might imagine choice. We discover it. A child watches, helps, learns, and somewhere in that process, the fit becomes apparent. I was not particularly skilled with fiber, my fingers too thick for the fine work of weaving. I was strong enough for hunting but lacked the restlessness that drove the surface-walkers. I could have been a tender — my father hoped I would follow him into the gardens — but the decay that sustained those chambers felt wrong to me. Not disgusting. Wrong. My hands wanted stone.

I began as all carvers begin: carrying tools, clearing debris, learning to read the grain of rock that others had already opened. I learned the sounds first — this is how we teach, sound before sight in the deep passages where light is precious. The tap-tap-tap of the chisel tells you what the stone will do before the mark appears. A hollow sound means space beyond, potential collapse, danger. A solid sound means density, reliability, continuation. A ringing sound means the good stone, the kind that takes a shape and holds it.

But what I learned, what came to me slowly over years of listening, was that the hollow sounds were not uniform. They had tones. They had resonances. A passage opened in a particular direction would carry sound in ways that another direction would not. A chamber shaped with care for acoustics would amplify voices, would sustain notes, would create harmonics that the same chamber shaped differently would deaden and absorb.

I was not the first to notice this. The old chambers, the ones carved generations before any living memory, were already shaped with sound in mind. But I was perhaps the first to make it my specific study, to approach each new passage and room with the question of how it would *sound*, not merely how it would function.

## The Work

I am a carver by trade, but my specialty is resonance. When a new passage is planned, when the expansion of a city's hollow spaces is discussed in council, I am among those consulted. I walk the proposed routes. I tap the walls. I listen to what is already there and what might be added.

The young carvers — they are all young to me now — they think in terms of stone: how much, how hard, how long to remove. I think in terms of space: what shape will this void become, what will it do to sound, to air movement, to warmth.

A chamber for sleeping wants to be small and deep, sound-dampening, warm. A chamber for gathering wants to be large enough to hold voices without crushing them, shaped so that someone speaking at one end can be heard clearly at the other. A chamber for ritual — for the warm spaces near the geothermal vents — wants properties that amplify, that transform, that make of the human voice something more than it is alone.

The council consults me because they have learned that my work matters. They did not always understand. When I was younger, my suggestions were dismissed as eccentricity, the preoccupations of someone who spent too much time listening and not enough time striking. But I persisted, and the chambers I shaped proved their value in ways that even the most pragmatic council members could not deny.

There is a room in Teeth-Singing now — I will not see it finished, but I have shaped its beginning — that will carry song into the deep warmth. The young singers, the ones with voices trained in the resonant chambers, will stand in this space and the mountain itself will join them. The stone will vibrate. The air will carry frequencies that human lungs cannot produce alone. This is the work I have given my life to: the creation of spaces where humanity and stone become partners.

## The Daily Round

I wake in the deep chambers, where my family has slept for generations. The warmth here is moderate — not the sacred heat of the ritual spaces, but sufficient for comfortable life. My sleeping cluster has shifted over the years: children born, elders died, partnerships formed and reformed. Currently, I sleep with my sister's family, three generations sharing body heat through the long cold dark. I am among the oldest in this cluster now, my position in the center when we sleep, my warmth surrounded and protected by those younger than I.

Waking is gradual. We rise not to the sun — which is far away here, its light channeled down through shafts that take hours to brighten — but to the stirring of others. Movement signals movement. One body shifting prompts another, and another, until the entire cluster has begun the slow emergence from sleep into waking.

My mornings involve less physical labor than they once did. Age has its privileges, and one of them is the delegation of the hardest work to younger bodies. But I do not sit idle. I walk the passages that are my responsibility, checking for cracks, for water intrusion, for the subtle signs that a wall or ceiling might be preparing to fail. I tap and listen. I mark places for attention. I teach.

Teaching is its own form of work, and one I have come to value more than I expected. The young carvers come to me with their questions, their uncertainties. I do not give them answers — that is not our way. I give them sounds to listen for, tests to perform, failures to risk. I show them the chambers I have shaped, explain why these work when others do not. I watch them develop, each in their own direction, each bringing something of themselves to the work.

My afternoons might be spent in council, when matters require my presence. The governance of the city demands time from all who have expertise to contribute. I sit with the others, listen to disputes and proposals, offer what insight I can. Often my role is to explain what the stone says — not mystically, but practically. When a new tunnel is proposed, I can predict how it will change the airflow, the sound transmission, the thermal balance. The council has learned to trust these predictions, even when they cannot follow the reasoning themselves.

Evenings are for the warm spaces, for the gathering of families and clans, for the social connections that sustain us. I often sit in the smaller chambers, the ones designed for conversation rather than ceremony. I listen to the voices around me, each carrying its own frequency, its own pattern. I hear the way the room shapes them, the harmonics that emerge from the geometry of stone.

Sometimes, I am asked to sing. My voice is not what it was — age has roughened it, narrowed its range — but I know the old songs, the ones that require specific chambers to reach their full potential. When I sing, I choose the room as carefully as I choose the note. I feel the stone respond. I feel the air carry what I give it. This is prayer for me, though the young do not always understand.

## The Solitude

I am not married in the formal sense. This is not unusual — many in our cities form bonds that are informal, that persist as long as they serve, that end without ceremony when they have run their course. I have had partners over the years, shared sleeping clusters with their families, contributed children to lineages that continue after our partnerships ended. This is the way of things here. Love exists, but it exists alongside survival, and survival takes precedence.

I have had children. Four who lived past their first breath, three who survived to adulthood, two who still walk the passages of Teeth-Singing. I see them sometimes, exchange words, acknowledge the connection. They have their own families now, their own sleeping clusters, their own work. They are carvers, tenders, one is a hunter who spends most of his time on the surface. I am proud of them in the way that one is proud of continuance — they carry my contribution forward, they add their own work to the work of generations.

But I have also chosen a kind of solitude that marks my life as unusual. My devotion to the acoustic properties of stone has meant a devotion to listening that requires time alone in unfinished chambers, time spent tapping and hearing, time spent sitting in silence while the mountain speaks. I have carved out small spaces for myself over the years — not claimed from anyone, but opened from stone that was not otherwise assigned. These spaces are my legacy as much as the larger chambers I have shaped. They are places where someone can sit and hear nothing but the distant hum of the earth, the faint whisper of air moving through passages miles long.

This is not loneliness. I know loneliness — I have felt it in moments when the silence seemed too complete, when the absence of other bodies felt like absence of meaning. But the solitude I have chosen is different. It is full. It contains the presence of the stone, the history of the mountain, the accumulated time of the plateau itself.

## The Faith I Hold

I am not devout in the way that some are devout. I do not spend my evenings in the sacred chambers, singing the prayers to warmth and depth. I do not believe, as some do, that my soul will return to the geothermal heat that birthed all life. I am not certain about souls at all, about what departs when the body cools.

But I believe in the stone. Not in any mystical sense, but in the reality of it, the durability, the patient waiting. The stone does not care whether I exist. It does not notice me in any way I can perceive. But it holds the shape I give it. It remembers the passages I carve. A thousand years from now, someone will walk through a chamber I helped design, and they will hear the resonance I built into it, and they will not know my name, but they will experience what I created.

That is immortality enough for me. Not the persistence of a soul, but the persistence of a shape. Not memory of a person, but use of a space.

The rituals I participate in — the birth-welcomings, the death-coolings, the seasonal observations — I participate because they bind me to my community, because they are the language we speak to each other about things that matter. I do not need to believe in the divine to believe in the human. The ceremonies work because they work, because they connect us to each other and to those who came before.

When I die, I will be returned to the stone. This is the way. My body will be placed in a natural cavity, one of the countless spaces between carved passages and untouched rock. The cold will take what warmth remains. I will become part of the plateau, part of the mountain, part of the landscape that shaped me. This seems right to me. Not transcendent. Right.

## The Surface

I have been to the surface, though it is not my place. My work keeps me in the deep passages, in the carved spaces where air and warmth can be managed. But I have walked in the open air, have felt the thin cold that the hunters and travelers know as their daily element.

The first time was when I was young, when I thought I needed to see the sun directly to understand the city I was helping to shape. I traveled with a hunting party, wrapped in layers that felt inadequate the moment we left the final carved entrance. The cold was not shocking — I knew it would be cold — but the *expanse* of it. The way the wind did not echo, did not return, carried sound away into nothing. The way the horizon extended beyond anything my eyes knew how to process.

I did not stay long. I learned what I needed to learn: that the surface is vast in ways that the passages are not, that sound behaves differently in open space, that the cold is not merely temperature but presence. I returned to the carved chambers with new understanding, with ears that could hear the difference between enclosed and open, between stone-bound and stone-free.

I have been to the surface perhaps a dozen times since, each time for a specific purpose. Each time, I am struck by the same things: the silence that is not silent but merely diffuse, the light that comes from everywhere rather than from carefully designed shafts, the absence of the low hum that I have come to associate with inhabited space. The surface is beautiful, but it is not home. Home is the carved passages. Home is the resonant stone.

## The Sounds I Know

If you lived in my head for a day, you would hear what I hear. The passage behind you, narrow and sound-deadening, absorbing your footsteps before they can travel far. The chamber ahead, opening in a way that makes voices seem to float, separate from the bodies that produce them. The distant tap-tap-tap of carvers working on the newest expansion, the sound traveling through stone itself, arriving at your ear as much through vibration as through air.

I hear the city breathing. Not metaphorically — I hear the actual movement of air through the passages, the way it shifts as bodies move through spaces, the way it warms and cools depending on where it has been. I hear the water running in the collection cisterns, the faint drip of moisture condensing on cold surfaces. I hear the faint buzz of life from the fungal gardens, the movement of air over growing things. I hear the footsteps of people walking above and below, the sound traveling through stone in ways that would be invisible to someone who had not learned to listen for it.

And I hear the songs. Not constantly — the sacred chambers are reserved for specific times — but often enough that I know which voices work best in which rooms. I hear the young singers practicing, learning which notes to hold and which to let fade. I hear the elder singers, the ones my age or older, whose voices have deepened and roughened but who know how to use the stone to amplify what remains.

When I am alone in my small spaces, I hear the mountain itself. The faint groan of rock settling, almost too low to perceive. The distant movement of water through cracks and channels. The subtle shifts that occur when temperature changes, when ice expands, when the earth beneath the plateau continues its slow adjustment to forces that began before anything living existed.

These sounds are my companions. They have been with me for longer than any person has. They will remain after I am gone.

## The Young Carvers

They come to me now with questions I cannot always answer. How do you shape a chamber for a voice that has not yet broken? How do you carve a passage that will serve purposes you cannot predict? How do you listen to stone that has not yet been opened?

I tell them what I know. I tell them that the stone speaks, but it speaks slowly, in the language of pressure and patience. I tell them that every chamber is an experiment, that the only way to learn is to try, to fail, to adjust, to try again. I tell them that the mountains will outlast all of us, that the chambers they carve will be used in ways they cannot imagine, by people whose voices will sing songs that have not yet been written.

I tell them that I was not always old, that I was once young and uncertain, that the questions they ask are the same questions I asked, and that the answers I have found are partial at best, provisional, subject to revision by the next generation of listeners.

They listen, as they should. But I see in their eyes that they are already thinking of their own questions, the ones I have not thought to ask, the ones that will shape the work they do after I am gone. This is as it should be. I was the same way, when the elders spoke to me of things that seemed distant, that seemed to belong to a time that had passed.

The work continues. The chambers continue. The stone continues.

## The Body Now

I move more slowly than I once did. This is natural, expected, accepted. I am not pushed to work faster — the culture does not demand speed, does not reward urgency. What I do now is what I have always done, just less of it, more carefully, with more rest between efforts.

My hands remain strong. The calluses have not softened, the grip has not weakened. What has changed is the time required to warm them before work, to restore flexibility after cold. I plan my movements more carefully, knowing that a dropped tool takes longer to retrieve, that a mis-strike takes longer to correct.

My eyes remain good, perhaps because I have spent so much time in dim spaces, preserving them from the glare of direct sun. I can read the grain of stone, can see the subtle variations in color that indicate density and direction. I can navigate passages I know by touch alone, the stone familiar under my fingers, the geometry memorized in my body as much as in my mind.

My hearing is what it has always been: trained to the specific frequencies that matter. I hear the resonance in a space before others do, can predict how a voice will carry before the speaker has finished their first sentence. This is my gift, such as it is — not strength, not speed, not genius, but the patience to listen, to hear what is present, to understand what might be.

I do not know how many years remain. This does not concern me. The years will come as they come, and I will fill them with the work I have always done, until I can do it no longer. Then I will do what I can do, until I can do nothing. Then I will return to the stone, and the stone will hold me, and the work will continue without me.

## The View From Here

I am writing this in one of my small spaces, a chamber I opened myself decades ago, shaped over years of patient work. It is not large — perhaps three body lengths across, roughly circular, with a ceiling that follows the natural slope of the strata. The light comes from a shaft I carved, angling upward to capture the afternoon sun. In the morning, this space is dark. In the afternoon, it glows.

The acoustics here are what I wanted them to be: live enough to carry sound, dead enough to prevent echo. My voice, when I speak aloud, fills the space without overwhelming it. The stone seems to hold the sound for a moment before releasing it, a quality I have learned to value, the sense that the space is listening before it responds.

From here, I can hear the city around me. The distant sounds of work, of life, of passage. I can hear the faint hum that tells me the deep chambers are active, the warmth rising. I can hear, if I listen carefully, the sound of children learning to sing in the teaching chambers, their voices high and uncertain, learning the notes that will shape their lives.

This is where I belong. This is who I am.

I am a carver of hollow spaces. I am a listener of stone. I am a voice in the dark that finds its way to other voices, that joins the chorus of a people who have learned to make warmth in cold, to make sound in silence, to make home in a landscape that would prefer to be empty.

The mountain holds me. I have spent my life opening it, learning it, shaping it. In return, it has given me purpose, has given me work that will outlast my body, has given me a place in a story that began before I was born and will continue after I am gone.

This is enough. This is more than enough.
