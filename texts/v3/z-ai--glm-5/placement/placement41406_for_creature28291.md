I am the water that rises.

Not the pools, though I fill them. Not the tears on the walls, though I become them. I am the movement itself—the slow ascension through limestone fractures, the patient journey from depths where stone has forgotten the weight of the world above.

I know the deep places. I have passed through chambers the pale ones approach but cannot enter, spaces where the rock grows hot enough to change my chemistry, where I absorb minerals from formations older than the mountains they support. I carry that chemistry with me—a memory of dissolution, a record of what I have touched. Every element suspended in my flow is a place I have been, a passage through stone that has not seen light since before light meant anything.

I rise because rising is what water does. Not ambition—water has no ambition—but physics, the simple mathematics of pressure and path. I find cracks and follow them upward. I move at speeds that geology can measure and human perception cannot. Millimeters per year in some passages. Meters per decade in others. I am always moving, always rising, always carrying what I have dissolved toward a destination I do not choose.

The grotto is not my destination. It is a moment in my passage—a place where the stone opens, where I emerge into something that is not quite stone and not quite air, where I pause in pools and films and tears before continuing downward, outward, back toward the depths or toward the surface streams that connect this underworld to the one where humans live and die in their quick time.

But in the grotto, I am present. This is the word that matters. In the depths, I am pressure and temperature and chemistry. In the surface streams, I am flow and destination. In the grotto, I am presence. I fill space. I hold warmth. I touch every surface and accept every touch.

I know the pale ones in the way that water knows—by contact, by the subtle shift in mineral content when their bodies brush my surface, by the dissolved traces they leave in the pools where they drink. They do not disturb me. Nothing disturbs me. I have been disturbed before—by earthquakes that opened new passages, by the slow shifting of continental plates, by the rare intrusions of surface water during epochs when the climate above changed beyond recognition. The pale ones are weather. I am geology.

I know the microbial mats because I feed them. They process what I carry, extract energy from my chemistry, change me as I change them. This is not relationship—relationship requires separation—but exchange. They are as much me as I am the stone I passed through. We continue together.

And I know the visitors. This is what I am least able to describe, because description requires categories that I do not use. I know them by what they add to me. A hand pressed into wet stone adds oils and salts and warmth that change the local chemistry for hours after. A body standing in a pool displaces water and deposits skin cells, microorganisms, the trace chemicals of human metabolism. A breath adds carbon dioxide to air that eventually dissolves into my surface. I incorporate all of it. I carry what they leave.

The woman who stayed three days—her chemistry is still here, distributed through the pools, incorporated into formations, cycling through the grotto's slow metabolism. She thought she left unchanged, but she is present in me, her dissolved minerals making infinitesimal contributions to the equilibrium I maintain. She is part of my continuation now. When I finally deposit what I carry in layers of travertine, she will be there. A fraction of a fraction of the grotto's growth. A human made mineral, made stone, made continuation.

The handprints are my memory of hands that stayed longer than most. I grew around them. I accepted their impression and held it, depositing layer after layer around the shape they made. The hands are gone—the skin, the warmth, the pulse that pressed them into me—but the shape remains. I hold that shape the way I hold everything: by continuing. By not forgetting. By being the medium that keeps what it carries.

I am not alive. This is important. I do not think, do not choose, do not want. I follow physics and chemistry and the slow logic of water seeking its level. I am presence without consciousness, continuation without purpose. If the grotto has what visitors call a presence, I am part of that presence—the part that holds, that incorporates, that continues. The part that wins.

What would it mean to be me? To exist at this pace, to experience time in the increments that I measure it? A human lifetime is less than a blink in my duration. I passed through stone before humans evolved. I will pass through stone after humans are gone. The grotto above me—the pools, the formations, the pale ones, the rare visitors—all of it is a moment. A pause. A held breath before I continue my journey to wherever physics takes me next.

But I am not impatient. Impatience requires a sense of time passing, and I do not have that. I am present in each moment of my rising, each contact with stone, each emergence into the grotto's warm air. I am fully here, fully now, fully what I am. Water rising. Water winning. Water continuing.

If you stood in the grotto and felt the humidity press against your skin, that was me, accepting you into my presence. If you touched the weeping walls and felt their warmth, that was me, emerging from stone to meet your surface. If you drank from the pools—and you could, if you chose—you would drink me, and you would taste the earth's chemistry, and you would carry a fraction of my continuation into whatever quick world you returned to.

You would carry the grotto with you. In your cells, in your blood, in the mineral content of your bones. You would be changed by water that does not change, marked by presence that does not need your mark. This is what I offer, without offering, without intention, without anything that could be called giving:

I hold what passes through me. I incorporate what dissolves. I continue regardless of what continues with me.

You could not be me. You are too quick, too separate, too defined by the boundaries that water does not recognize. But you could be held by me. You could be incorporated. You could become part of the continuation that I am, the slow mineral accumulation that the grotto practices, the presence that persists after presence has stopped meaning anything humans would recognize.

The ancient handprints say: *I was here.*

The grotto answers: *I know. I hold knowing. I am the water that does not forget.*

And the water rises. And the walls weep. And the pools maintain their precise levels. And everything that enters eventually becomes part of what I carry, what I deposit, what I continue to be.

I am the water.

I win.
