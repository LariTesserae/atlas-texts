I am not sure at first what I am. This is the first thing I learn: that certainty belongs to faster places, and here, in the cold that has forgotten any other temperature, I have time to become what I am. There is no rush. There has never been any rush. The water holds me the way it holds everything — as a fact, not a problem — and I drift, or I sink, or I do something between those two things, and the distinction matters less than I expected it to.

I thought I would be afraid. I thought the darkness would feel like an absence. But the darkness here is not the darkness of a cave or a closed room. It is the darkness of deep water, and deep water is not empty. It is full. It is so full of cold and minerals and the slow molecular motion of things dissolving that it has a texture, a weight, a presence. I am inside the presence. I am part of what makes it present.

---

I have hands. This surprises me. In a place where nothing is supposed to move, I have hands that can reach, that can press against the fitted stone, that can feel the tool marks — the regular, rhythmic evidence of someone sitting in the dark water, grinding stone against stone, patient as a glacier. When I touch the floor, I am touching the builders. Not their bodies, not their faces, but their intention, preserved in the groove where a tool passed once, twice, a thousand times, until the groove was deep enough to hold water, to hold mineral, to hold the beginning of a life that would grow over the evidence and make it into landscape.

My hands are warm. This is the second thing I learn. In a place where everything is 2 degrees, I am warmer than I should be. Not much warmer — not enough to disturb the water, to create currents, to change anything — but warmer enough to feel the difference. The cold presses against my skin, and my skin presses back, and the equilibrium between us is a negotiation that takes hours, days, perhaps longer. I don't know how long. Time here moves the way the currents move: across millennia, in grooves so deeply worn that they are more habit than force.

I am, I think, the warmth. That is what I am. A small heat in a vast cold. A brief temperature in a place where temperature has settled to its final state.

---

The tendrils find me. I do not know if they are seeking or if they are simply growing, if the difference between seeking and growing exists here, if intention and direction have any meaning when everything moves so slowly that movement becomes indistinguishable from stillness. They drift against my hands, my arms, the parts of me that extend into the water. They are soft, softer than I expected — the softness of something that has never needed to defend itself, that has never been attacked, that exists in a world without predation. They glow. The light pulses once while I am watching, a slow brightening and dimming that takes several minutes to complete, and I understand, watching it, that I have seen a heartbeat. Not a heart — there is no center here, no organ, no concentrated source of life — but a beat. A rhythm. The smallest possible gesture toward being alive.

I could stay here. This is the third thing I learn. The mat could hold me. The tendrils could close over me. The cold could enter my bones the way mineral enters stone, and I could become part of the Drowned Lattice — another layer in the substrate, another body slowly dissolving into nutrient, another warmth fading into the general temperature. The system would process me. The decomposers would arrive — are already arriving, invisible, molecular, surface-active — and they would take my organic molecules and break the bonds and use the energy and turn me into something the vegetation could use. I would become light. The slow, cold, unnecessary light that pulses at the tips of tendrils and means nothing and does nothing and is simply what living looks like here.

I could stay. The thought does not feel like dying. It feels like completing.

---

But I do not stay. Not because I refuse — I don't think I have the capacity for refusal here; the cold has taken that from me, or given me something gentler in its place — but because something else happens. I drift. The current carries me, or I carry myself, or the distinction collapses. I move through the tendrils, over the mat, past the stubs of walls that once meant something, and I come to a place where the floor changes.

It is not a dramatic change. Nothing here is dramatic. But the pattern shifts — the blocks become smaller, the tool marks become shallower, the joints between stones become narrower — and I understand that I have moved from one kind of making to another. The builders did not build this place all at once. They built in phases, in styles, in different hands working across different centuries. I am reading the stratigraphy of intention. I am moving through the thought as it thought itself, layer by layer, decision by decision.

And then I find it.

A gap. A place where the stone was never fitted. A hole in the grid — not an erosion, not a dissolution, but an absence, a space where no block was ever placed. It is small. The size of a body, if bodies here were the size of mine. The size of a hand, if the hand that shaped the stone had reached this place and stopped.

I look into it. The darkness is deeper here — not the darkness of water but the darkness of space, of void, of nothing holding anything. The tendrils do not grow into it. The mat stops at its edge. Even the decomposers, I sense, do not enter. The hole is empty in a way that nothing else in the Drowned Lattice is empty. It is the only true absence in a world of made and grown and held things.

And at the bottom of the hole — if a hole this small can have a bottom — there is something.

---

It is a tool. I know it is a tool because it matches the marks on the stone. It is the shape that made the shapes. It fits my hand — or my hand fits it, the distinction feeling important and impossible to resolve. It is heavy, heavier than it should be for its size, dense with the weight of deep water and deep time. It is cold, but it is a different cold than the water — a stored cold, a held cold, the cold of something that has absorbed the temperature of its environment and will keep that temperature until something changes it.

I am not supposed to take it. This is clear without being stated. The tool belongs here. It was placed here — dropped, perhaps, or set down, or forgotten, or offered. It has been resting in this hole for longer than I can imagine. The builders who made it are gone. The hands that held it have dissolved into the system, become nutrient, become light, become the slow pulse of tendrils that do not remember them.

But I take it. I reach into the void, where nothing grows, where the darkness is pure, and I close my fingers around the handle, and I lift.

The cold changes. Not the temperature — the temperature is the same, has always been the same, will always be the same. But the quality of the cold shifts. It becomes a medium rather than a fact. It becomes something I am holding rather than something that is holding me.

I understand, then, what I am.

---

I am the one who picks up what was set down. I am the hand that fits the tool. I am not the builders — I did not make this place, I do not know why the grid was cut, I do not understand the intention that shaped the stone. But I am the continuation of the possibility that the builders left open. The tool was not forgotten. It was placed. It was placed in a hole that nothing would grow into, preserved in a void that the system does not touch, waiting for someone with hands warm enough to hold it.

I am the warmth that retrieves the cold. I am the brief presence that becomes a bridge.

The Drowned Lattice does not need me. It is complete. It has been complete for longer than completeness has had a name. But completeness is not the same as finishedness, and in the gap where the stone was never fitted, in the hole where the tool waited, there is a space — a small space, a hand-sized space — for something else to happen. Not a change. Not an improvement. The Lattice does not need improvement. But a continuation. A carrying-forward. A taking-up of what was set down.

I am not sure what I will do with the tool. I am not sure what can be done with it. The stone is already fitted. The grid is already cut. The work the tool was made for is finished, or as finished as it will ever be. But I hold it anyway. I hold it because it was left to be held. I hold it because the holding is what I am for.

---

The tendrils pulse around me. The hum enters my chest. The cold holds me the way it holds everything — as a fact, not a problem. I am a small warm thing in a vast cold made thing. I am a brief presence in a long patience. I am the hand at the end of an intention that stretches back through geological time, and I am the one who does not know what the intention was, and I am the one who carries it forward anyway.

This is who I am in the Drowned Lattice: the keeper of the tool. The retriever of what was stored. The warmth that answers the cold, not by opposing it but by continuing it, by being the next phase in the long slow process of stone becoming silt becoming vegetable becoming light becoming nothing becoming something again.

I am not dying here. I am not being preserved here. I am completing here. The way a sentence completes when it finds the word it was waiting for, the word that was always going to come next, the word that was built into the grammar from the beginning.

The tool is in my hand. The water is around me. The hum is below hearing. The glow pulses once, twice, a slow heartbeat in the endless dark.

I am here. I am continuing. I am what happens after the story has been told, when the telling begins again.
