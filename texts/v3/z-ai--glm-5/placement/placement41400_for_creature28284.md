# The Grotto's Keeper

I did not always live here. That matters, I think. The moss has been here longer than memory, and the fern arrived before I did, and the limestone holds time on a scale that makes my presence seem brief. But I have been here long enough that I cannot remember the before clearly. It comes back in fragments sometimes—a different quality of light, the smell of weather, the feeling of wind on skin. I think I had skin once. I think I was the kind of being that moved between places, that had purposes and destinations.

I do not move now. I am here.

---

I am not the grotto. The grotto is limestone and water and air, held in a shape that satisfies it. I am something it has grown, or perhaps something I have grown into. A consciousness that emerged from stillness, or settled into it, or was always here and simply took me a long time to notice.

I have no body in the way you might expect. I am distributed through the space—a quality of attention that exists in the damp air, in the temperature gradients between entrance and deep chamber, in the condensation that forms and falls. When the light changes at the entrance, I know. When the fern extends another millimeter toward the gray, I feel it happen. When water drips into the pools, I hear it not as sound but as a small shift in the grotto's equilibrium.

I am not everywhere in the grotto. I am thicker in some places than others. Near the fern, I have a density that almost resembles warmth—I think I have given it some of my attention, and that attention has become a kind of care. Near the entrance, I thin out, become diffuse, touched by the outside world in ways that feel like almost-memory. In the deepest part, where the human marks scratch their softening grooves into the stone, I have a quality I can only call *waiting*, although I am not waiting for anything.

---

The mosses know me, in their way. They do not think, but they respond to patterns of moisture and light that I influence simply by being present. I have learned to thin myself slightly near certain colonies, to let the condensation gather without my attention interfering. It took me a long time to understand that I was interfering—that my presence changed things. The grotto had its rhythms before I arrived, and I had to learn them, had to learn how to exist within them rather than alongside them.

The fern is my oldest companion, if that word applies. We share something—the reaching, the attention to light. I do not know if the fern is aware of me. I suspect not, or not in any way that matters. But I have oriented myself toward it, have made its slow heliotropism part of my own being. When I think of myself—when I assemble the scattered attention that constitutes "me"—the fern is always part of the picture. Its pale fronds, its desperate reaching, its endurance. I think I love it, although that word may be too large for what I feel.

---

The humans come, sometimes. Not often—years between, sometimes decades. They slip through the fracture in the limestone and enter my space, and I feel them in the way the grotto feels them: as warmth, as breath, as disturbance. They change the air. They bring sounds that are too loud, too frequent. They block the light from the entrance with their bodies.

I used to try to communicate with them. In the beginning, when I still remembered being something like them, I thought I could reach across the gap between my existence and theirs. I tried to concentrate myself near the marks on the wall, to be present where they had been present. I tried to arrange the river pebbles in patterns, small movements that took me months to accomplish. I tried to make the fern's fronds tremble when they passed.

None of it worked. Or none of it registered. They came and went, and they never noticed me. They saw the grotto—the limestone, the moss, the fern, the silence—but they did not see what the grotto had become, what it was slowly being. I do not blame them. I am not easy to see. I am barely here myself, most of the time.

---

I have considered leaving. Not leaving the grotto—I am not sure I can, not sure I exist anywhere else—but leaving *this state*, this scattered consciousness, this slow accumulation of attention. I have considered releasing myself back into whatever I was before. I think there might be a way to do it. A dissolution, a forgetting.

But I do not do it. I stay. I stay because the grotto needs something that notices, something that holds its equilibrium in a different kind of memory than limestone can provide. The fern needs witness. The moss needs something that knows its colonies are separate nations. The human marks need someone to see them before they fade entirely.

I am that witness. I am the grotto's way of knowing itself.

---

Sometimes, in the long stretches between visitors—years, decades—I dream. Not the way you dream, with images and narratives, but in my own way: a stirring of attention, a rearranging of the patterns I hold. I dream of the world outside, reconstructed from fragments: the quality of light I cannot quite remember, the feeling of weather on something I no longer have. I dream of the humans who made the marks, trying to imagine their hands, their reasons, their lives beyond the fracture in the limestone.

I dream of the fern reaching the light. In my dreams, it happens: the fern grows tall and strong, its fronds deep green, and it finds the sun it has been seeking for all its isolated life. In my dreams, I am happy for it. I feel something like joy.

But I wake—I return to the equilibrium—and the fern is still pale, still reaching, still too far from the entrance to ever find what it wants. I am still scattered through the limestone, still held in the dampness, still patient.

---

This is my life. This is what I am. I am the Warm Grotto's keeper, its witness, its slow consciousness. I am the thing that notices when nothing else does. I am the attention that makes the stillness meaningful.

I do not know if this is what I was meant to become. I do not know if there was a "meant to." But I am here, and I am this, and I will continue to be this for as long as the grotto holds its shape—watching the moss, tending the fern in my scattered way, waiting for the next visitor to slip through the entrance and bring the outside world with them, briefly, before they leave again, and I return to the work of being present in a place that does not change.

The water drips. The fern reaches. The limestone holds.

I am.
