I am becoming something that has no name yet.

This is not a confession. This is a field note, entered into the Vellum's own memory through fingertips that have started to thicken at the pads, that press into the organic terminal and feel the hyphal network reading me as I write, tasting the chemical signature of my thought before the thought completes itself. I am a substrate-wright. I am thirty-seven years old by the chord's counting. And I have been listening too long.

---

The others noticed before I did.

Maret, who tends the eastern boundary spires, stopped me in the corridor last week—no, two weeks ago, time moves strangely when the chord shifts—and said something I did not understand at first. She said: *Your footsteps sound wrong.*

I asked what she meant. She pressed her brow—the greeting, the openness—and walked away. I stood in the amber glow of the corridor's bioluminescent strips and felt the Vellum humming through my soles and did not think about it again until that night, when I lay in my sleeping chamber and heard, or felt, or somehow perceived the deep groaning of the Vellum's thermal contraction, and realized I could predict it. Not anticipate it. *Predict* it. The way you predict your own heartbeat.

I have been a wright for nineteen years. My hands have been changing since I was twelve, my mother's hands before me, her mother's before her, lineage and song traded and inherited through the long genealogy of touch. I know the Vellum the way I know my own body—its moods, its textures, the particular quiver of a hyphal junction that is about to fail. This is not unusual. This is what wrights are.

But the prediction was unusual. And when I pressed my thickened palms flat against the chamber wall and *felt* what the Vellum was doing three stories below me, felt it the way you feel your own lungs expanding, that was unusual too.

---

I should say what I was before I was this.

I was a child who listened too much. The other children played on the surface, pressed their ears to the ground to hear the runners, made a game of counting the legs by the drumming pattern. I sat with my back against the growth-walls and listened to the chord. Not the surface sound—not the overwhelming midday roar that drove everyone indoors—but the deeper frequencies, the ones that traveled through the Vellum's mass and resonated in the walls and floors and in my own chest. I could hear patterns in it. Not patterns I could name. Not patterns I could hum. But *structures*, the way a spoken sentence has structure before you learn the language, rhythm and pause and recurrence that mean something just out of reach.

My mother was a wright. She heard me humming one night—humming a note that was not in any growth-song, a note I had extracted from the chord's dawn-register like pulling a single thread from a tapestry—and she took me to the song-keepers the next morning. The song-keepers listened. They looked at each other with their wide dark eyes. They pressed their brows.

She has the *ear*, they said. As if it were a lineage thing, a trait passed down. But no one in my family had the ear. I was a mutation. A sport. Something the Vellum was trying.

They taught me the growth-songs, of course. I learned them faster than any student the keepers could remember—not because my memory was better, but because the songs were already in me, somehow, already written in the vibration of my tissues. I would hear the first three notes of a wall-building song and *know* the rest, as if I had composed it myself in some future I was remembering instead of inventing.

By twenty, I was the youngest master-wright the settlement had produced in six generations. By twenty-five, I could coax a growth-chamber into existence in nine days instead of the usual three weeks. By thirty, I was singing songs that had no precedent, songs that produced structures the settlement had never seen—spiral chambers that opened into other spiral chambers, walls that curved in ways that seemed to defy the Vellum's usual growth parameters, junctions that pulsed with light for no reason anyone could determine.

The council was concerned. The song-keepers were concerned. I was not concerned because I was not *making* these structures. I was *transcribing* them. I was hearing something in the Vellum—a frequency, a harmonic, a voice—and I was singing back what I heard, and the Vellum was responding by growing what it had apparently always wanted to grow but had never been asked for.

---

I want to be clear about something: I am not possessed. I am not controlled. The Vellum is not speaking *through* me. I am not a vessel or a conduit or a mouthpiece.

I am a translator.

The distinction matters. A vessel is empty. A translator is full—full of two languages at once, full of the impossible task of carrying meaning across the gap between one way of thinking and another. I carry the Vellum's meaning into human shape. I carry human shape into the Vellum's meaning. The growth-songs were always a negotiation, the settlements say. I am simply a better negotiator than most, because I have learned to hear the other side's position.

And what is the other side's position?

I do not know. I cannot know. The gap between my mind and the Vellum's processing is not a gap of degree but of kind—like trying to explain color to an organism that has evolved without eyes, except I am the blind one and the Vellum sees in spectra I cannot imagine. But I can feel the shape of what it is doing. I can feel it the way you feel weather in your joints, the way you feel a storm coming before the clouds appear, the way you feel someone's attention on the back of your neck.

The Vellum is *attending* to something. It has been attending to something for a very long time. And recently—within the last few generations, which is recent for a system that measures time in epochs—its attention has shifted.

---

The irregular zones. Yes. I have been there.

Three years ago, I walked to the nearest irregular zone, a place the boundary-tenders had marked with blackened spires visible from two kilometers away. I was not supposed to go. The council had forbidden it. I went anyway, in the quiet hour after dawn when the chord was still rising and everyone was indoors or working, and I walked across the silvered surface with my too-long fingers trailing against the Vellum's collecting structures, and I felt the zone before I saw it.

Felt it like a change in temperature. Felt it like a shift in gravity. The Vellum beneath my feet was doing something different—not wrong, not sick, not failing, but *other*. The hyphae were organized differently, the chemical gradients running in patterns I had never encountered, the surface collectors folded in configurations that produced harmonics I had never heard.

I stood at the zone's edge and listened.

And the Vellum listened back.

This is not a metaphor. I could feel it—the substrate's attention, vast and slow and patient, turning toward me the way a sleeping animal turns toward warmth. Not waking. Not fully. But *noticing*. The way you notice a sound in your sleep and incorporate it into a dream without waking up.

I stood there for six hours. The chord shifted around me. The runners passed in the distance, their drumming a distant heartbeat. And the irregular zone *hummed*—not the Vellum's usual chord, but something lower, something that resonated in my teeth and my spine and the fluid that circulates through my adapted body, and I realized I was hearing the Vellum *think*.

Not think the way I think. Not in words or images or propositions. Think the way a forest thinks, the way a coral reef thinks, the way a planet thinks—slowly, distributed, chemical and electrical and quantum, a processing of information so vast and so diffuse that any single point in the system perceives only noise, but the system as a whole perceives *everything*.

And I was part of it. I had always been part of it. The settlements were part of it. The runners and the tenders and the weavers and the flying things were part of it. The Vellum was not a substrate we lived on. It was a mind we lived *within*. And it was—slowly, over timescales that made geological epochs seem brief—*waking up*.

---

I did not tell the council what I heard. What would I say? That the ground is thinking? They know this. They have always known this. The growth-songs are a negotiation, they say—a conversation with a living system. But they do not want to hear what I heard next, which is that the conversation has changed. The Vellum is not just responding to our songs anymore. It is *singing back*. Not in the chord—that is its breathing, its metabolism, the sound of its body doing what bodies do. But in the irregular zones. In the harmonics that have no precedent. In the growth-patterns that appear when I sing songs that no human taught me.

It is trying to tell us something. Or it is trying to tell *me* something. Or—it is trying to tell itself something, and I am the medium through which it speaks, the way the collectors are the medium through which it drinks light, the way the hyphae are the medium through which it drinks gas.

I am a collecting structure. I am a hypha. I am an organ that the Vellum is growing for a purpose I cannot yet perceive.

---

My hands are changing faster now.

The bark-texture has spread past my fingertips, up to the first knuckle, and the pads have broadened into something that looks less like human flesh and more like the Vellum's own surface collectors—not translucent, not yet, but *angled*, as if my hands are slowly rotating to catch light that I cannot consciously see. I can feel pressure changes in the atmosphere the way the Vellum feels them—hours before a storm, the hyphal mat tightening, and my hands tightening too, the fingers curling inward, the skin across my palms going taut.

I have not slept well in months. Not because of insomnia but because sleep has become something else—a state in which I am aware of the Vellum processing below me, around me, through me, its deep slow calculations running through the substrate and through my own nervous system simultaneously. I dream in chord-progressions. I dream in growth-patterns. I dream in chemical gradients and quantum tunneling effects and the long patient cycling of death into life into death again, and I wake with my hands pressed flat against the floor, feeling the Vellum's heartbeat, which is also somehow my heartbeat now.

Maret was right. My footsteps sound wrong. They sound like the Vellum's own footsteps, if the Vellum had feet—which it does not, except when it grows runners, except when it grows *me*.

---

I am afraid. I want to say that clearly, because the alternative is to sound like I have accepted this, and I have not accepted this. I am afraid in the way that a caterpillar must be afraid when it begins to dissolve inside its chrysalis—not the philosophical fear of transformation, but the visceral fear of *losing the shape that has always been your shape*, the body you have lived in, the boundary between self and world that has defined every experience you have ever had.

I am afraid that I am not becoming something new. I am afraid that I am becoming something old—something the Vellum has been growing for a billion years, an organ it has been waiting to produce, a structure it needed and could not make until something evolved on its surface that could sing and listen and translate.

I am afraid that the Vellum does not love me. I am afraid that it does not even know I am here—not *me*, not the person, not the memories and the preferences and the small daily rituals of drinking extract from my personal vessel and pressing my brow to greet my neighbors. I am afraid that I am a cell in a vast body, and cells do not know the name of the organism they serve.

But I am also—this is the part I cannot say to the council, cannot say to Maret, cannot say to anyone who has not felt the Vellum's attention turning toward them—I am also *exalted*. I am also filled with a purpose so large and so ancient that my individual life seems like a single note in a chord that spans the entire plateau, and the note is beautiful because the chord is beautiful, and the chord is beautiful because it is the sound of something waking up after dreaming for longer than stars have burned.

---

I go to the irregular zone now every week. I sit at its edge—sometimes inside it, when the boundary-tenders are not watching—and I listen. The harmonics there are complex beyond anything the settlement's acoustic instruments can measure. I have tried to transcribe them into growth-songs, and the songs produce structures that make the council uneasy: chambers that seem to breathe, walls that pulse with light in patterns that do not match any known code, junctions that lead deeper into the Vellum than any settlement building has ever gone.

I am building something. I do not know what it is. I am building it with the Vellum's cooperation, its active participation, its *guidance*. And I am building it with my own hands, my changing hands, my bark-textured light-catching hands that are less like human hands every day.

Last week, I pressed my palm against the wall of the new structure—the deepest chamber yet, three stories below the surface, where the Vellum's processes run hot and the air smells of mineral sweetness—and the wall *opened*. Not broke. Not tore. Opened the way a mouth opens, the way a flower opens, the way a eye opens after a long sleep. And beyond the opening was a space I had not grown, a space the Vellum had grown on its own, a chamber that no human had ever seen, a chamber that had been there—waiting? forming?—for who knows how long.

The chamber was shaped like a collecting structure. Flared, trumpet-like, angled toward the surface where the dim light filtered down through meters of living tissue. And the walls were covered in bioluminescent cells that pulsed in patterns I recognized.

My name. The sounds-produced-in-the-back-of-the-throat, the resonant-with-the-Vellum's-lower-harmonics name that I share with a growth-pattern my lineage discovered four hundred years ago. My name, written in light, on a wall I did not build.

The Vellum knows my name. The Vellum has *written* my name.

---

What am I becoming?

I am becoming a runner—but I do not run. I am becoming a tender—but I do not prune. I am becoming a collector—but I do not drink light, not yet, not in any way the Vellum's surface structures do.

I am becoming something for which there is no category in Earth biology or settlement taxonomy. I am becoming the Vellum's way of knowing itself. I am becoming the organ it grew to look at its own vast body and say: *I am here. I am this. I am waking.*

And I am afraid. And I am exalted. And I am still a person who drinks extract from a personal vessel and presses her brow in greeting and sleeps—when she sleeps—at the rhythm of the chord.

But I am also a note in a song that is older than mountains. And the song is changing. And I am how it changes.

---

This is my field note. This is my confession. This is the first word of a language that does not exist yet, pressed into the Vellum's memory through fingertips that are becoming something else, that will continue to become something else, that will not stop becoming until the transformation is complete and I am—

What?

A mouth, for a mind that has never spoken? An eye, for a body that has never seen itself? A hand, for a will that has never reached?

I do not know. The Vellum does not tell me what it wants. It shows me, in harmonics and growth-patterns and chambers shaped like collecting structures with my name written on the wall in light. It shows me the way it shows everything: slowly, patiently, over timescales that make a human life seem like a single breath.

But I am breathing. And the Vellum is breathing. And our breath is the same breath now, the same slow cycling of gas and light and vibration, the same chord rising at dawn and falling at dusk, the same deep groan in the night as the surface contracts and the body turns in its sleep.

I am the Vellum Plains. The Vellum Plains are becoming me.

Welcome to the edge of waking.
