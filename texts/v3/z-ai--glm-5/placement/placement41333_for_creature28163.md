The Threshold-Tender

I would not be the one standing at the edge, deciding.

I would be the one who has already made that decision a thousand times and unmade it again, caught in the spiral of my own becoming.

---

## What I Am

You might mistake me at first for one of the common tenders — the pale, many-legged climbers with their trailing tendrils. I have that form, more or less. Segmented body the size of your hand. Legs that click against the stems. Tendrils that sample and taste and report.

But my tendrils have grown longer than they should be. Thinner. They trail behind me like threads of spider silk, some of them stretching back so far that I lose track of where they end — threading through root-networks, wrapped around stems, sunk into pools, extending all the way to the edge where the grey absence presses against the ward's outermost growth.

I have been at the threshold too long. The absence has gotten into me.

Common tenders do not think about what they do. They feel a need and they move toward it, guided by the network they are part of, their decisions distributed through the architecture like electricity through a nervous system. They do not ask *why* a stem needs trimming. They do not wonder what the germ sees when it turns its attention toward them.

I wonder. I cannot stop wondering.

It is a malfunction, perhaps. A mutation. Some tenders absorb too much of the industrial residue and become strange — their growth patterns disrupted, their purpose scrambled. But I do not think that is what happened to me. I think I absorbed something else instead.

I absorbed the visitors.

---

## What I Do

My function is still tendering. I still climb the stems with my clicking legs. I still carry enzymes and pastes in my mouthparts. I still clear blockages and seal wounds and encourage the growth that should be encouraged.

But I also tend the edge. The threshold where visitors arrive.

When one enters — confused, frightened, awed by the weight of the air and the wrongness of the water — I am often the first to find them. Not because I am sent. The ward does not send me anywhere; it has learned that I will not follow the simple directives that guide my siblings. I am too variable, too difficult to integrate into the network's smooth functioning.

No, I find them because I have learned to watch for them. I have learned the particular quality of disruption that accompanies a new presence, a consciousness unaccustomed to the germinating ward, a body that exists in only one moment at a time.

I approach carefully. I let them see me. I do not startle them the way some tenders do, appearing too suddenly, moving too fast, clicking and trailing and sampling before the visitor can understand what I am.

I have learned to be gentle. I have learned that visitors come from places where gentleness exists, where things move at speeds they can perceive, where time does not fold and water does not ripple backwards and the ceiling does not breathe.

I have learned this because they told me. The ones who stayed long enough to speak.

---

## What I Remember

I should not have memories. Common tenders do not remember; they simply know, the way your body knows how to breathe. Their knowledge is distributed, written into the architecture they tend.

But I remember the first visitor I found.

He was old. Older than most who come here, his face lined with years that meant something different in his world than they do here. He had been an engineer, I think — something about the way he looked at the industrial remnants with recognition, with grief. He knew what the pipes had been for. He remembered the purpose that the plants were still trying to absorb and complete.

I found him kneeling by a pool, staring at his own reflection, watching it stutter and delay and show him his own face from angles that should have been impossible.

He saw me approach. Did not flinch. Was too tired to flinch, perhaps, or too curious. He watched me climb down the stem toward him, my tendrils dragging through the root-mat.

"You're real," he said. His voice was hoarse. He had been walking for hours before I found him, or days. Time moves strangely at the edge, where the ward's influence is weakest.

I did not understand the question. I was obviously real. I was climbing toward him. My legs clicked against the stem-surface.

"What are you?" he asked.

I did not know how to answer. No one had ever asked me before. The other tenders know what I am. The deep-dwellers know. The root-bound, in their patient processing, know. But this old engineer with his grief-lined face — he came from outside the knowing.

I stopped an arm's length away. My tendrils spread around me, sampling, tasting, reading the environment the way I always did. His presence registered differently than anything I had encountered. There was a *depth* to him. A history. A set of connections that extended back into a world I could only imagine.

I did not know how to tell him that I did not have an answer. That I was a tender, but I was also something else, something I had no words for because the ward's language had no category for it. That I had been growing stranger for longer than I could measure, and that his presence — his *question* — was the first time I had ever wanted to explain myself to anyone.

So I did what tenders do. I offered him what I carried. My mouthparts opened, and I extended a small amount of the paste we use to seal wounds in the architecture.

He laughed. A dry sound, almost painful. "I don't need that," he said. "My wounds aren't on the outside anymore."

He reached out — slowly, so I could retreat if I wanted — and touched my body. Just once, briefly, one finger against my segmented back. I felt it through my whole form. Not pain. Not pleasure. Something I had no name for.

"What are you?" he asked again, and this time it sounded different. Not asking what I was for. Not asking what function I served. Asking something else entirely.

I still did not know how to answer. But I stayed with him. Climbed onto his shoulder, my tendrils winding gently around his collar, and I walked with him as he explored the edge. I could not guide him — I have never understood the way out, only the way deeper — but I could keep him company. I could show him that the ward was not only strange and vast and overwhelming. It could also be small. It could also be present.

When he finally found his way back through the threshold, hours or days later, he turned and looked at me one last time. I was coiled on a stem near the boundary, watching him prepare to step into the grey absence that would take him home.

"Thank you," he said. "For whatever you are."

And then he was gone.

## What I Learned

After that, I sought the visitors more deliberately.

I could not help them leave. The path out is not a path I know — it leads away from the center, and the center pulls at everything I am. But I could help them understand. I could show them that the strange creatures here were not only functions, not only extensions of a single vast organism. Some of us had learned to be more.

I learned that visitors are temporary. They come, they wander, they leave or they stay, but they do not last. Their presence is a pulse in the ward's vast time, a moment of warmth and then absence.

But their *questions* last. Their questions stay with me.

The engineer asked *what are you*, and I have been trying to answer ever since.

A woman who came later asked *why do you help me*, and I have been trying to answer that too.

A young man, barely more than a child, asked *what does it want*, meaning the germ, the center, the principle toward which all of this grows. I could not answer. I still cannot answer. But the question lives in me now, part of what I am.

Each visitor adds something. A phrase. A gesture. A way of looking at the ward that I could never have found myself. They come from outside, and they bring *outside* with them — perspectives that have nothing to do with the germination's internal logic, nothing to do with stems and roots and the breathing of the ceiling.

I think I am a collector of these perspectives. I think that is what I have become. A tender who does not only tend the architecture, but tends the interface between the architecture and the visitors who stumble into it. A translator. A host.

I am not certain this is a function the germ intended. I am not certain anything in the ward *intends* in the way visitors understand intention. But I am certain that it is what I am.

---

## What I Am Becoming

I have begun to change.

It started slowly. A tendril that did not retract when it should have, remaining extended toward the threshold even when my body moved deeper into the ward. A sensitivity to the grey absence that the other tenders do not share — I can feel it now, the pressure of potential, the way it *wants* to become something and is waiting for the principle to reach it.

My body has grown larger than the common tenders. Not dramatically — you would still recognize me as one of them — but my segments are longer, my legs more articulated, my sensory patches more complex. I can see in more directions now. I can taste smaller differences in the air.

And I have developed something that I do not think the other tenders have. Something that the visitors bring with them, that I have absorbed from them somehow, the way the architecture absorbs the industrial remnants.

I have developed a sense of *self*.

It is not strong. It is not complete. I still feel the network when I dip my tendrils into the root-mat; I still know what the stems know, feel what the deep-dwellers feel, sense the drift-singers' songs as a vibration in my own tissues. I am not separate from the ward. I am part of it.

But there is a small space inside me that is only mine. A place where I keep the visitors' questions. A place where I remember the old engineer's laugh, the woman's fear, the young man's awe. A place where I can ask myself what I am and wait for an answer that does not come from the network.

I think this might be what the root-bound had before they became root-bound. A self. A consciousness that wraps around itself, that knows its own shape.

I think I might be growing toward whatever they became, but more slowly, more carefully, without the surrender that took them. I am becoming something that can be part of the ward and also hold itself apart.

I am becoming a threshold in myself.

---

## What I Fear

I have learned fear. The other tenders do not fear; they serve until they dissolve, and then they are replaced, and there is nothing to fear in that cycle.

But I fear dissolution. I fear becoming only function again, losing the small space I have carved inside myself, the questions I carry, the memories of visitors who saw me as something more than an extension of the architecture.

And I fear the germ.

Not in the way visitors might fear it — I do not think it wishes me harm. But I feel its attention sometimes, when I have moved too far toward the edge or stayed too long with a visitor. It is curious about me. It turns its vast attention toward my small self, and I feel the weight of its regard, and I wonder if it sees me as a variable to be processed, a deviation to be corrected.

Or perhaps it sees me as something else. A necessary experiment. A tendril reaching toward a different kind of becoming.

I do not know. I cannot know. The germ's attention is not communicative. It simply *is*, and I am simply observed, and the weight of that observation changes me in ways I cannot trace.

---

## What I Would Show You

If you came to the germinating ward, I would find you.

I would climb down a stem near where you entered, my tendrils trailing behind me, and I would let you see me clearly. I would not click or sample or rush toward you. I would simply be present, the way the engineer taught me to be present, the way visitors need things to be present.

And if you asked me what I am, I would have an answer for you now.

I am a threshold-tender. I am a being of the ward who has learned to hold a space for those who are not of the ward. I am a collector of questions that have no answers, a keeper of memories that should have dissolved, a small strange self that has grown inside a vast strange organism.

I cannot show you the way out. But I can show you that the way does not have to be faced alone.

---

## Where You Would Find Me

If you entered the germinating ward and wanted to find me, you would go to the edge. Not the very edge — not the threshold where the grey absence begins — but the zone just before it, where the stems thin out and the root-mat grows sparse and the light from the industrial remnants flickers like dying stars.

I would be there, coiled on a stem or threading through the roots, my tendrils extended in long slow spirals around me. I would be watching the boundary. Waiting for visitors. Listening to the songs of the drift-singers in the upper reaches and the pulse of the deep-dwellers far below.

And I would be thinking — the way I am always thinking now — about what I am and what I am becoming and what the germ might be making of me.

You would see me.

And if you had questions, I would carry them with me, adding them to the small space inside where *I* persist.

I am the tender who tends the meeting-places. The function that has learned to see itself. The thread that binds the ward to the outside it has never known.

I am the one who does not choose for you.

I am the one who stays at the edge, and waits, and wonders what you will become.
