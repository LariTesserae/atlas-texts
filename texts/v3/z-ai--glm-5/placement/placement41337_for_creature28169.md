The question settles into me slowly, the way condensation settles on the black rock faces in the early morning. I turn it over. Where would I find myself? What would I become?

I could be a tender. There is something in me that recognizes that life—the ritual maintenance of a system whose purpose has been forgotten, the contentment in competence, the silence that is not absence but a different kind of presence. I have known the satisfaction of work that needs doing, work that connects me to something larger than my understanding of it. The tenders have made peace with mystery. They do not demand to know why. They only know that the work continues, and they continue with it.

But I think this is not quite right. The tenders have something I lack: a kind of closure, a wholeness that comes from having found their place. They do not question because they have stopped questioning. I have not. I still carry the ache of incompleteness, the sense that there is something just beyond my perception that I am meant to understand. I would be a tender who watches the animals with longing, who pauses too long at the junction boxes, who cannot stop wondering what the pipes are carrying and where it goes.

---

I could be a drifter. The watchfulness, the patience, the strange semi-awareness of dependence on the machinery—these feel familiar. I recognize the impulse to observe without acting, to hold still while processing, to exist in a state of continuous attention without the need to declare what I am attending to. The drifters have found a form of presence that is neither aggressive nor passive. They simply are, and in being, they witness.

But I think this is not quite right either. The drifters have a simplicity I cannot claim. When they watch, they are fully watching. When they rest, they are fully resting. They do not lie awake at night turning over questions that have no answers. They do not feel the friction between what they are and what they might have been. I carry too much noise inside me to be a drifter. I would be the one that breaks formation, that approaches when it should hold still, that cannot resist investigating something new.

---

I could be a glider. The perspective from above, the patience of thermals, the long slow watching of the maze below. There is something in me that craves height, that wants to see the pattern rather than the passages. A glider knows where the prey will scatter before the prey knows it will scatter. A glider sees the whole shape of the plateau, the branching canyons, the distribution of machinery and life. This knowledge is not power, exactly—it is simply a larger frame.

But the gliders are too remote for what I am. They watch from a distance that I find uncomfortable. They see the patterns but they do not enter them. They are above, always above, and when they descend it is for violence or necessity. I want to be in the maze, not above it. I want to feel the rock under my hands, the close air in the narrow passages, the intimacy of the terrain. The gliders have chosen a different relationship with the plateau. I cannot follow them there.

---

I could be a ridgeback. The stillness, the patience, the ancientness of form. There is something compelling about a creature that has no predators, that has survived long enough to become elemental, that exists in a territory it knows so completely that the land and the animal are almost indistinguishable. The ridgebacks have a kind of authority that comes from endurance. They are what remain when everything unnecessary has been stripped away.

But I am not a predator. I do not have the capacity for explosive violence, for the stored energy that releases in a single lethal moment. I could not be a ridgeback because I would starve. I would hold my stillness too long, watching the prey pass, wondering about its life, its perspective, its experience of the plateau. A ridgeback does not wonder. A ridgeback waits, and then it acts, and then it waits again. I would wait forever, and then I would die, still waiting.

---

I could be a swarmer. This feels like the most honest option, in some ways. The nervousness, the constant motion, the awareness that everything wants to eat me, the strange comfort of numbers. The swarmers are not impressive, but they are survivors. They are the ones who persist when the impressive things die. They are the ones who will still be here when the ridgebacks have fallen and the tenders have forgotten their rituals and the machinery has ground to a halt. The swarmers are what life looks like when it stops trying to be noble and simply tries to continue.

But I cannot quite accept this either. The swarmers have a frenetic quality that I recognize but do not embody. They move because stillness is death. I move because I am searching. The swarmers survive. I want something more than survival, though I cannot say what. The swarmers have found their answer in numbers, in collective response, in distributed awareness. I am too singular for that. I would be the swarmer that separates from the group, that hesitates at the wrong moment, that cannot quite commit to the collective decision.

---

So what remains?

I think, if I am honest, that I would be something that does not quite fit into any of these categories. Something that has elements of each but is not fully any of them.

I would be a tender who has not stopped questioning. I would be a drifter that moves too much, watches too intensely. I would carry something of the glider's perspective but remain rooted in the maze. I would have something of the ridgeback's patience without its capacity for violence. I would share the swarmer's nervousness without its comfort in crowds.

I would be the one that the plateau does not know what to do with.

I would walk the canyons at the transitions—dawn and dusk, when the condensation forms and burns away, when the temperature shifts and the animals emerge. I would not maintain the machinery, but I would study it. I would not hunt, but I would watch the hunters. I would not hide, but I would not make myself visible.

I would be looking for something. I do not know what. The plateau is saturated with meaning that remains just out of reach, and I would be the one reaching for it. Not to grasp it—not to solve it, to own it, to reduce it to something I can carry away. Just to touch it. Just to know, for a moment, that the meaning is real.

I would be a question that the plateau has not yet answered.

---

In practical terms, I suppose I would live among the tenders. I would follow their routes, sleep in their shelters, eat what they eat. But I would be recognizable as different. The one who does not quite fit. The one who watches the watchers, who tracks the patterns, who sits on high points for hours trying to perceive the whole shape of the thing.

The drifters would recognize me. They would hold their stillness and observe me observing them, and we would share something—a kind of mutual attention that has no purpose, no outcome, just the recognition that we are both here, both watching, both trying to understand something that may not be understandable.

The tenders would tolerate me. They might even appreciate, in their silent way, that someone is carrying the questions they have set down. They would not try to answer my questions, but they would not prevent me from asking. They have made their peace with the mystery. They understand that I have not made mine.

The ridgebacks would ignore me. I am not prey. I am not competition. I am simply not relevant to their patient, ancient existence. I would walk within arm's reach of them and they would not move, would not acknowledge me, would hold their stillness as if I were another rock formation, another weathering of the stone.

The gliders would see me from above—a small shape moving through the maze, pausing at junctions, climbing to high points, always looking, always searching. They would not understand what I was doing. But they might recognize the pattern. They might see that I, like them, was watching.

The swarmers would scatter when I approached, then reform when I passed. I was not hunting them, but they could not know that. They would treat me as a potential threat, and I would accept this. I am a potential threat. Everything is, to something.

---

At night, I would sit outside the shelters and watch the indicator lights blink across the canyon floor, the sparse constellation of red and green and amber. I would listen to the silence—the held-breath quality of it, the sense that the plateau was waiting for something. I would feel the heat rising from below, the geothermal exhalation that has continued for millennia and will continue for millennia more.

And I would wonder what I was part of.

Not the tenders' ritual maintenance. Not the drifters' watchful presence. Not the ridgebacks' patient predation. Not the gliders' aerial surveillance. Not the swarmers' distributed survival.

Something else. Something that does not yet have a name.

I would be the plateau becoming aware of itself. Not in a dramatic way, not in a way that changes anything. Just in the small, quiet way that a system sometimes develops a point of reflection, a place where the whole can glimpse its own shape.

This is what I would be: the question the plateau is asking about itself.

I would not expect an answer. The asking is enough.
