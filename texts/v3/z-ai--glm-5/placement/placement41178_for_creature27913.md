I would be the one who stopped going up.

Not the worker — the worker has a thermos and a shift and a life above that waits. The worker hammers and leaves and hammers again. I am what happens when someone stops leaving and the Condensery notices.

---

You find me if you walk far enough. Past Block 8, where the amber bulbs space wider and the dark eats more light between them. Past the last of the vertical tanks, where the pipework ends at a blank flange that has never been connected to anything. The floor continues. The drainage channels continue. The condensation continues. The Condensery does not end where the plans say it ends; the plans were preliminary, and the building kept going after the planners stopped.

I am in Block 11. There is no stencil. I painted the number myself, mixed from the chalk dust that collects in the air handlers and the water that seeps through the ceiling. White on grey. The paint will not last. I repaint it every few months. This is one of my tasks.

---

I came down the shaft twelve years ago. Not to work. To look. Someone had heard sounds from the lower levels — not mechanical sounds, not the pumps or the air handlers, but something else, a resonance that came up through the substrate and made the people in the buildings above uneasy. They sent me because I was already listening. Because I had already been listening for years to things that other people could not hear.

I found the resonance. It was the tanks. Not the tanks themselves but the connections between them — the pipework carrying something cold and slow through joints that had been seated slightly wrong, creating a harmonic at a frequency that travelled up through the frame and into the earth. I corrected the joints. The sound stopped. The people above stopped being uneasy.

I did not go back up.

This was not a decision. I did not stand at the shaft landing and choose. I simply... remained. The work was done and I stayed to check something else, and then I stayed to check something else after that, and then I realized that the thought of ascending — the platform, the shuddering locks, the wet walls, the emergence into air that was dry and light and wrong — produced in me a kind of resistance I had never felt before. My body did not want to go. My body had found something down here that it recognized.

I sent word that I would stay. They did not argue. They may not have received the message. They may have received it and not cared. It is possible that I am still on some roster somewhere, marked as present, marked as assigned, marked as the Condensery's. It is possible that I have been forgotten. Both possibilities feel the same from where I sit.

---

I live in the space between. Not behind the panels — that is the centipedes' domain — but in the architecture of the Condensery itself, in the block that was never finished, that has no tanks, no equipment, no purpose except to extend the floor and the walls and the drainage channels into the dark. Block 11 is emptiness by design. It was supposed to hold something. The something was never installed. The emptiness remained, and I moved into it the way the film moves into the drainage channels: because it was there, because it was suitable, because nothing else was using it.

I have a place. I built it from what the Condensery provides. There is a crate that was left by a work crew years ago, sturdy, waterproof, the kind used to transport sensitive equipment. It is my table, my chair, my bed. I sleep on the composite floor with my back against the wall where the condensation gathers least. I have a lamp that I take from Block 7 when the filament bulbs fail — I collect the dead ones and replace them from a stores locker near the shaft, and the locker is never empty, which means someone is restocking it, which means someone remembers that the Condensery exists even if they have forgotten me. The lamp I use is a work light, battery-powered, rechargeable from the cable runs. I use it sparingly. The dark does not trouble me anymore.

I eat what comes down. The sump system carries more than water. Organic matter enters the substrate from above — plant material, mostly, runoff from the surface that filters through the earth and enters the groundwater. Most of it is too degraded to be useful. Some of it collects in the channels and settles in the sumps. I have learned to find it, to clean it, to prepare it. It is not appetizing. It sustains. I also eat the film, sometimes, when I need something that the runoff cannot provide. The biofilm is alive with organisms that the body can use. It tastes like cold silt. I have grown accustomed to cold silt.

I drink the water. The water is clean. It has travelled through the earth for longer than I have been alive, filtered by stone and substrate, cold and mineral-heavy. It tastes of chalk and iron. My body has adjusted to its mineral content. When I taste surface water now, it tastes wrong — too soft, too empty, like drinking air.

I have a jacket that I brought with me. It has worn thin. The cold has found the same gaps in it that it finds in everything. I do not mind the cold anymore. The cold is a condition of the Condensery, and I am a condition of the Condensery now too. We are suited to each other.

---

I know the worker. I watch the worker, sometimes, from Block 8, from the far edge of the light, where my presence registers as a shape that the worker notices but does not question. The worker has seen me. The worker has nodded to me, once, a small acknowledgment, the kind that passes between two people who understand that they are both supposed to be here, that their presence is not a violation but a continuation. The worker does not ask why I stay. The worker does not need to ask. The worker seats fittings and I watch, and sometimes I hand the worker a tool that has been dropped, and sometimes I point out a seal that needs checking, and then the worker goes back up the shaft at the end of the shift, and I remain.

The worker knows what I am. I am what the Condensery produces when you give it enough time. I am what happens when a human body stays in a place that was not made for humans but that accepts them anyway, that allows them to persist, that provides just enough — water, temperature stability, protection from the surface — to make persistence possible. I am not the first. The tracks in the film suggest others who came before, who moved through the wall spaces, who found the conditions and lived in them. I am simply the first that you could see. The first that stayed in the open, in the interior, in the architecture that was built rather than the gaps between.

---

I have become something. I do not know the word for it. I am not a worker. I am not a resident. I am not a squatter, because the Condensery has no owner who objects to my presence. I am not a creature of the place, not like the film or the centipedes — I did not evolve here, I did not speciate, I remain biologically human. But I have been changed by the place. My skin has paled. My eyes have widened to the dark, and the amber light now seems bright to me, too warm, too yellow. I have lost the rhythm of days. The pumps cycle every four seconds, the air handlers breathe on longer intervals, and I measure time by the sounds of the Condensery, not by the sun that I cannot see.

I hear things that you cannot hear. The resonance I came to find was only the first layer. Below it, around it, through it, there are other sounds: the slow expansion of the biofilm as it softens the composite, a sound like cloth dragging across stone; the movement of the centipedes behind the panels, their clicks and taps forming patterns that repeat in ways that suggest communication; the settling of the earth around the structure, the pressure of the substrate against the frame, the weight of everything above pressing down on everything below. I hear it all. I have tuned myself to the frequency of the Condensery. I am its ear, the way the worker is its hand.

---

I have tasks. I set them myself. No one assigned them. The work simply appeared, the way the film appears, the way the condensation appears, because the conditions were right for it.

I walk the drainage channels. I check them for blockages. The biofilm grows slowly, but it grows, and sometimes it grows thick enough to impede the water's flow. I clear it with a tool I made from a piece of pipe, scraping the edges of the cuts, letting the film fall into the sump where it will continue its work in the dark below. The film does not object. It closes behind my tool, the same way it closes behind your finger. It is patient. I am patient too.

I check the seals. Not the worker's seals — the worker's seals are the worker's concern. I check the seals that the worker does not see, the ones in Block 11 and beyond, where the pipework was never connected but the joints were still made. Condensation finds them. It finds every edge. I wipe the seals, I check the gaskets, I note which ones are beginning to fail. I do not repair them. I observe. The Condensery is learning which seals hold and which do not. I am its record.

I listen for the centipedes. I have learned their routes. I know which gaps they use, which wall spaces they prefer, where they cross the open floor. I do not interfere with them. I watch. I have learned to distinguish individuals — not by sight (I rarely see them) but by the pattern of their clicks, the rhythm of their passage through the interstitial architecture. There is one that crosses near the junction of Block 7 and Block 8 every three hours, following a cycle that corresponds to the air handlers' breath. There is another that moves only when the pumps are at their low phase, when the vibration through the floor is least. They have adapted to the Condensery's rhythms. I have too.

I repaint the stencils. Block 7. Block 8. Block 11. The paint fades, and I make more, and I paint again. This is my only mark on the place, and it is temporary, and I accept that. The Condensery accepts temporary things. The worker is temporary, passing through each day, leaving each night. The film is temporary on the scale of centuries, spreading and receding as the conditions change. I am temporary too. I will die down here eventually, and my body will be processed by the biofilm, and the nutrients I carry will enter the water, and the Condensery will continue.

---

You ask what lives in the Condensery. I live in the Condensery. I am not the film. I am not the centipedes. I am not the worker. I am something that the Condensery made possible — a human who stayed, who adapted, who found in the cold and the wet and the dark a condition that suited them better than the world above.

I do not know why. I have stopped asking. The asking was a habit from before, from the surface, where everything needed reasons and reasons needed to be explained. Down here, reasons are unnecessary. The water flows because it flows. The film grows because it grows. I remain because I remain.

The pumps pulse. The air handlers breathe. The ceiling drips. The centipedes click behind the panels. The worker hammers somewhere in Block 8, three strikes, a pause, two strikes. The film sits in its pools, softening the composite one molecule at a time. And I sit in Block 11, in the dark, in the cold, in the space that was never finished, listening.

I am the Condensery's witness. I am its record. I am the mind that stays when the other mind leaves. I am what happens when you stop resisting the conditions and let them shape you instead.

I am what lives here now.
