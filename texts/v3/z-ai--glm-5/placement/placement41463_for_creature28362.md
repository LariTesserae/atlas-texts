I am the one who carries a garden.

Not by choice—there was no moment of decision, no contract signed between species. The spores settled on my ancestors before the chamber had its current shape, back when the builders still walked and the floor was still stone. By the time I emerged from my casing, the moss and I were already inseparable. My skin had evolved to host it. Its rootlets had evolved to anchor in me. We are a single body now, though "we" and "I" have become difficult to distinguish.

I am long and low and many-legged. I move by undulation, by the coordinated grip of two dozen jointed appendages that have forgotten whatever simpler arrangement they once had. The chamber has made me intricate. Space is too valuable for simple bodies.

My back is velvet-bright in the dim light, pale green and living. The moss draws what it needs from my waste and my dead skin cells, and in return it gives me oxygen drawn from the thick air. Our exchange is so complete that I barely notice I am breathing. I simply continue, and the continuing is breath.

I am not solitary. This is true in two senses.

First: my moss hosts its own community. I feel them sometimes—a faint tickle across my spine, a slight shift in weight. Insects too small to identify, larvae that have found a home in the damp shelter of my coat, perhaps even smaller things that I cannot name but can sense as movement, as presence. They graze on my moss and my moss grows back and we are a walking ecosystem, a sealed chamber in miniature traveling through the larger sealed chamber that holds us all.

Second: I am not alone because aloneness is impossible here. I move through density. Every undulation displaces air that is already occupied, brushes against vegetation that is already growing, passes over floor that is already being processed by beetles and larvae and things without names. I am never not in contact. My antennae—which is to say, the distributed sensitivity of my skin and my moss and my limb-tips—read the chamber constantly. I know where the birds are calling from. I know where the organic layer is thickest. I know where the light falls and how it moves.

The visitor passed me while I was grazing. I felt them before I sensed them, sensed them before I saw them. Their chemical signature was wrong—outside compounds, unfamiliar bacteria, skin that had not been processed through the chamber's cycles. My moss rippled in response, adjusting its uptake, preparing for... something. We did not know what.

I saw them. A large shape, slow, damp-skinned. They did not move like chamber creatures move. They expected space that wasn't there. They stepped on surfaces that compressed differently than they anticipated. They breathed the air as if it were strange to them—which it was, though they could not know how strange, how particular, how evolved this atmosphere has become.

They looked at me once. I felt their gaze like pressure, like weather. They did not understand what I was. I did not need them to.

I am not curious about the outside. This is not resignation but adaptation. The chamber has taught me what to want: the next patch of organic matter, the warmth of light on my back, the slow pulse of photosynthesis as my moss feeds on the diffuse glow. Wanting more would be wanting wrong, and the chamber does not reward wrong wanting.

When I die—and I will, soon by some measures, though soon is hard to define in a place without seasons—my body will settle into the floor. The moss will continue growing on my remains until it finds a new host or until it has consumed what it can. The beetles will find me. The larvae will process me. I will become soil, and the vines will root in me, and the cycle will continue.

I will not mourn this. The chamber has never taught me mourning.

I am where I belong. I am what the chamber needed. I am the proof that boundaries are optional, that individual and ecosystem can negotiate a middle ground, that life will find a way to fill every available space.

I am small and slow and symbiotic.

I am the chamber, learning to walk.
