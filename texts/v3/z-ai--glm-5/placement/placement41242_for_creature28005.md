# What Emerges

---

## The Lower Paths

I am not who you would expect, if you came to Ashiin expecting someone.

I am not an elder, though I have lived long enough to watch children become parents. I am not a bridge-tender, though I know every vine-pulse in the lower corridors. I am not a story-keeper, though I hold knowledge that passes through no mouths but mine.

I am the one who tends what becomes other things.

---

## Where You Find Me

You find me in the wet dark, mostly.

The lower mulch-layer, where the bioluminescence glows soft blue-green against the stalk-roots, where the air moves slow and thick with the sweetness of decay. This is my domain. Not because I chose it—though perhaps I did, in the way choices happen here, emerging from circumstance the way names emerge from observation—but because it suited me, and I suited it, and the fit was recognized.

Visitors to Ashiin see the upper bridges, the dwelling-hollows carved into living stalks, the market amphitheater where three great stems lean together. They see the light filtered green through canopy, the constant motion of people who must prune daily just to keep their corridors open. They do not see down. They do not see the layer where everything that falls collects and transforms.

But someone must tend it.

---

## The Work

Every day, I descend.

The path down is not a path in the way visitors understand paths. It is a series of handholds and foot-braces cut into the living root-mass, places where I have trained my fingers over decades to find purchase in the wet. The lower I go, the darker it gets, until the only light comes from the fungi—*Luma-veil*, we call it, the glow that spreads across decaying matter in delicate sheets.

My work is this: I watch transformation happen.

The mulch-layer receives everything that falls or is lowered. Leaves, vines, insects, waste, the bodies of the dead. It receives them and, given time and moisture and the right conditions, it converts them. Soil. Nutrient. The foundation for new growth. But this does not happen automatically. The Ashiini learned centuries ago that the transformation can be guided. Sped up or slowed down. Directed toward certain outcomes.

Certain fungi enrich soil for the food-crops. Others produce the bioluminescence that lights our lower paths. Others, still, process the bodies of the dead in ways that make their nutrients available to the stalks that house us—these we do not eat, ever, but we plant with them, we seed new hollows with the transformed substance of those who came before.

I know which patches are ready. I know which need turning, which need moisture, which need to be left undisturbed through a full cycle of seasons. I read the glow-intensity, the smell-shift, the texture-yield under my hands. This knowledge lives in my palms, my fingertips, the small of my back where I feel the difference between healthy fermentation and wrong rot.

---

## How I Came Here

I did not always live in the lower paths.

I was born in a stalk on the eastern edge of the bowl, where the morning light comes earliest, where my family has lived for four generations. I was given a name that meant "the one who watches green become green," because as a child I was fascinated by growth—the way new shoots emerged from old matter, the way the cycle turned and turned.

I was supposed to become a bridge-tender. My hands were steady, my instincts for living wood were good, and there was a need—the southern connections were expanding, and families were requesting new passages. But when I was thirteen, I descended to the mulch-layer for the first time, accompanying the then-keeper on her daily rounds, and I felt something I had never felt before.

Recognition.

The dark did not frighten me. The smell of decay did not repel me. The bioluminescent glow seemed familiar, as if I had seen it before somewhere—though where, I could not have said. I watched the keeper's hands move through the mulch, turning, testing, and my own hands wanted to move that way too.

I asked to return. She let me. I asked to learn. She taught me. And when she died—in the ordinary way, lowered into a patch I had prepared myself, wrapped in fiber-cloth and attended through three seasons of transformation—her knowledge did not die with her. It had already moved into my body.

My name shifted as my role did. I am still called by the sounds I was given as a child, but layered over them now are new meanings. "The one who walks in the lower glow." "She who tends what becomes." When the Ashiini speak of me, they speak of transformation—my function in the settlement, my place in the cycle.

I have no complaints about this. Names should reflect who we are.

## The Solitude I Keep

People assume I am lonely.

They assume this because I spend my waking hours in the dark below, where the noise of the canopy reaches only as a muffled roar, where the only movement is the slow pulse of decomposition. They assume that separation from the constant touch of other bodies, from the shared meals and the corridor-crowding, must feel like deprivation.

They are wrong.

I am not alone down here. I am surrounded.

The mulch-layer is alive in ways that the upper dwellings cannot imagine. Every handful of matter I touch contains millions of organisms—bacteria, fungal hyphae, insect larvae, nematodes, the microscopic engines of transformation. I feel them working. I sense their rhythms. When I press my palms against a healthy patch of decay, I feel the heat they generate, the micro-movement of their consumption, and it is not solitude. It is presence of a different kind.

And I am not always below. I emerge for meals, for council-gatherings, for the festivals that mark the turning of seasons. I have friends—real ones, people I touch and laugh with and argue beside. I have had lovers, though none have stayed, because my rhythms do not match the rhythms of those who live in the light. I have a family that still claims me, though they see me less often than they used to.

But the dark holds me in a way the light does not. The slow work of watching matter become other matter speaks to something in me that the faster pace above cannot reach. I am not lonely. I am *depths-dwelling*. These are different things.

## The Dead

I tend the dead.

This is the part that visitors find hardest to understand. In their cultures, death is an ending, and the body is either sacred or waste—something to be hidden, preserved, or discarded. In Ashiin, death is a transformation, and the body is material for the next phase of the settlement's growth.

When someone dies, their family brings them to me—or rather, to the preparation sites I maintain at the boundary between the lower paths and the dwelling layer. I receive the body, wrapped in the fiber-cloth their family has woven, and I lower them to the patch that suits them.

This is not random. I match the dead to the transformation process that fits their life. Elders go to the patches that produce soil for new stalk-hollows—their long lives feeding the long lives of the plants that will shelter generations. Children go to the patches that seed food-crops—their potential returning quickly to nourish the living. Those who died of certain illnesses go to isolated patches where their decomposition cannot spread sickness. Those who died violently, whose bodies carry fear or anger in their final tension, I give to patches that process slowly, that allow whatever needs to work through them to work through.

These are not beliefs. They are observations, made over centuries, passed from keeper to keeper. The soil that results from different transformation processes has different qualities. The plants that grow in it carry traces of what it was made from. We do not pretend to understand all of it, but we know enough to match the dead to the destination that serves both them and the settlement.

I tend the sites through the seasons of transformation. I monitor moisture, temperature, the spread of fungal hyphae through fiber-cloth and flesh. I turn the matter when needed, add amendments, adjust conditions. And when the transformation is complete—when what was a person has become soil, dark and rich and glowing faintly with the bioluminescence that marks successful decay—I harvest what has grown there and carry it to where it is needed.

I have carried my parents in my hands. My brother. The keeper who taught me, and her keeper before her. I have carried children I watched grow in the upper corridors, elders who told me stories when I was young, lovers I held in the dark below. Each time, my hands remember the weight. Each time, my heart remembers the loss. Each time, the work continues, because the work is how we continue.

## What I Have Become

My skin is darker than my family's.

Not from sun—the lower paths receive almost no direct light—but from contact with the mulch, with the transformation-products, with decades of handling matter that stains and stays. My palms are permanently marked, the lines of my fingerprints filled with dark residue that will never wash out. I do not try to wash it. It is part of me now.

My eyes have adjusted to the glow. I see better in dim bioluminescence than in full daylight, which makes the upper dwellings feel harsh to me, overexposed. When I emerge for festivals, I find myself squinting, seeking shadows, waiting for the relief of evening when I can return to the lower paths.

My hands know things my mind cannot articulate. I can touch a patch of mulch and tell you, without looking, whether it is too wet, too dry, whether the fungal networks are spreading healthily or fighting contamination. I can smell a transformation site and know, from a hundred subtle cues, what stage it has reached and how many seasons remain. These knowings do not translate to words. They live in the parts of me that existed before language, the parts that will exist after.

And I have become someone who understands endings differently.

Up above, the Ashiini understand death as transformation—they live this understanding daily, in the way they speak of those who have passed, in the way they accept loss as part of the cycle. But down below, in the slow time of decomposition, I understand it differently. I see the moment when a body stops being a person and starts being matter. I watch the boundary dissolve, the identity bleed away into nutrients, the name become irrelevant. It is not a tragedy. It is not even a change in substance—it is a change in organization. The stuff that made a person is still there. It has simply stopped being organized as a person and started being organized as soil.

This gives me a peace I cannot explain. We are all temporary organizations of matter. We will all become other things. The question is not whether we transform but what we transform into, what we feed, what we enable.

I tend the transformation so that what we become serves what comes after.

---

## What Calls Me

At night, when the upper dwellings sleep and the canopy chorus drops to its minimal hum, I stay below.

I sit at the center of my domain, surrounded by the soft glow of healthy decay, and I listen. Not to sound—there is little sound down here but the drip of water and the microscopic rustle of insects in mulch—but to presence. To the vast slow presence of the transformation happening around me. Millions of lives, small and smaller, processing what was into what will be.

It is not quiet. It is the opposite of quiet—it is a song too large and slow for human ears to hear, the rhythm of becoming that underlies everything that lives. I feel it in my bones. In the pulse of my blood, which knows itself borrowed, temporary, part of the same cycle I tend.

This is where I belong.

Not because I cannot live above—I can, I do, I participate in the life of the settlement as much as any resident. But because down here, in the lower paths, I feel the truth of what we are. Not separate. Not above. Not masters of a domain we built. We are organisms in an ecosystem, borrowing our substance from what came before, lending it to what comes after.

I am the keeper of that lending. I am the one who watches the boundary dissolve and says: yes, this is right. This is what happens. This is what we all do, whether we see it or not.

I see it.

I tend it.

I am the mulch-keeper of Ashiin, and my hands are full of what becomes.
