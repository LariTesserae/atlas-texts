The first thing I learned was the sound of my own breathing. Not the sound it makes in open air, where it dissipates, but the sound it makes here, where the dome catches it and offers it back. Inhale. A slight rasp in the throat—the dust, the damp. Exhale. The warmth leaving the body, entering the shared air. Inhale again, and somewhere across the chamber, forty feet away, a woman pauses in her weaving because the rhythm has shifted.

This is what it means to live in the Orami Cistern: you are never alone with your own sounds.

---

I am seventy-three years old. I think. The counting becomes uncertain after a certain point. I remember when the Hasprin family had only three platforms, not six. I remember when the fig tree was a sapling, before it learned to grow sideways toward the light. I remember when the council had seven members, not five—the other two lost to quarrels that turned violent, to the slow withdrawal that is exile. I remember when the birds were merely birds.

I am one of the women who tends them.

We do not call ourselves priestesses. That word belongs to the surface, to temples with real gods and real power. We are tenders. Keepers. The ones who notice.

I was not born to this role. No one is. You do not inherit the birds; you are chosen by them. Or rather, you are chosen by the women who already tend them, who watch the children of the middle families for signs—a particular attentiveness, a patience that exceeds the normal, an affinity for the small and strange. I was nine when old Methi caught me holding a wounded pigeon, my hands steady around its frantic heart. She did not take it from me. She watched. When the bird calmed, when it turned its head to look at me with that peculiar sideways gaze, she nodded once and walked away.

Three days later, she began to teach me.

---

What I learned was not craft. The craft anyone can learn—the feeding, the egg-collecting, the killing. What I learned was attention.

The pigeons of the cistern are not stupid animals. This is the first thing the tenders learn. They have preferences, relationships, memories. They recognize individuals across years of absence. They hold grudges. They bestow favors. The children who race beetles think they know the animals of the cistern, but they know them as things, as entertainment. We know them as neighbors.

Each bird has a name. Not human names—their names are sounds that we make, tones that the birds recognize. Mine is a low hum, a vibration in the throat that does not quite resolve into a note. When I make it, certain birds turn their heads. These are the ones who have been below.

That is the second thing we learn. Some birds go into the cracks.

---

The deep fissures—there are three that we know of, perhaps more that we do not—are forbidden. The council forbids them. The mothers forbid them. The fear that lives in the cistern, old and shapeless, forbids them. But the birds do not obey human rules. They go where they will. And sometimes they go down.

We do not send them. This is important. The younger tenders sometimes want to—we have all felt the temptation, the desire to know—but the old ones are clear. We do not send them. We wait. We watch. And when a bird goes below of its own will, we mark it.

They always return.

They return changed. Not harmed—they eat and fly and breed as before. But there is something behind their eyes that was not there before. A knowing. We keep these birds in the special coops, the ones the other tenders do not touch. We watch them. Sometimes they live for years, unchanged, and we begin to think we imagined it. Sometimes they die without warning, their small bodies simply stopping, as if whatever animated them has withdrawn.

When they die, we do not eat them. We lower them into the fissure, the same fissure where we place our dead, and we watch them descend.

---

I have learned the low speech, the sub-vocal murmur that the acoustics do not catch. I speak it now, as I sit on my platform—the one I have earned through decades of service, high enough to see the oculus but not so high as to threaten the old families—and I watch the cistern below me.

A child runs across a walkway, too fast, her feet finding purchase where an adult would hesitate. Her mother calls after her in the high speech, not thinking, and the dome catches the words, multiplies them, sends them bouncing across the chamber. Other voices rise in response—irritation, amusement, a man's shout for quiet. The noise builds, layers, becomes the constant churn that visitors find so overwhelming.

I do not hear it anymore. Or rather, I hear it as a single thing, a texture, like the feel of cloth under the fingers. I hear the shape of the cistern's voice.

And sometimes I hear the other thing.

---

It began twenty years ago. I was tending the coops late at night, in the small hours when even the cistern grows quiet. A bird had returned from below that day—a young male, slate-gray, one of the ones who had been gone for three days. I was watching it, the way it held itself, the way its head tracked movement I could not see.

And then I heard a word.

Not in my ears. In the sound. In the texture of the silence, if such a thing can be imagined. A word rising from the fissure, from the deep places where the dead go, from wherever the birds go when they descend. It was not a word in any language I knew, but I understood it: *listen*.

I did not tell the other tenders. This is not something we speak of. Each of us, if we stay long enough, hears something. We do not compare notes. We do not analyze. We listen.

---

What I have heard in the years since is difficult to describe.

The cistern, I have come to believe, is more than a container. It is a vessel in the older sense—a thing that holds something, yes, but also a thing that is shaped by what it holds. Seven centuries of water, pressing against the stone, leaving some trace. Then generations of people, their sounds, their warmth, their dead. The echoes have nowhere to go. They bounce, they fade, they return altered—but they do not disappear. Something accumulates.

I do not think there is a creature below us. The stories the children are told, the shapeless fear of something old and hungry—these are metaphors, perhaps, or partial truths. What lives below, I think, is attention itself. The accumulated listening of centuries. A presence that formed not from birth but from collection, like sediment settling in still water.

The dead go down there. The birds go down there. And sometimes, when the acoustics are right—when the light through the oculus falls at a particular angle, when the voices rise and interweave in a particular pattern—I hear the other voice woven into the return.

It does not speak often. It does not command or request. It observes. It comments, sometimes, in that wordless way that still conveys meaning: *the child will fall, the man is lying, the water is rising*.

It is never wrong.

---

I have not told the council. I have not told the younger tenders. They sense something—old Methi knew something, I realize now, though she never spoke of it directly—but knowledge of this kind does not spread easily. It accumulates, like the echoes, in particular vessels.

I am a vessel. I am seventy-three years old. I have lived in the cistern for sixty-four years. I have tended birds, watched children grow and have children of their own, buried friends in the fissure and spoken the low words that send them down. I have heard the voice from below perhaps thirty times in my life. Each time, I have listened. Each time, I have done what seemed needful.

The child did not fall; I called a warning that seemed to come from nowhere, and her mother caught her arm. The man's lie was exposed not by me but by the acoustics themselves, his own words returning to him changed. The water did rise, once, years ago—an unusual seepage from the deep rock that threatened the sump. I told the council it was coming, and they did not ask how I knew. They have learned not to ask.

---

I am not afraid of what lives below. Perhaps I should be. The other tenders are afraid, though they do not speak of it directly. The council is afraid; they maintain the taboos, forbid the sealed passages, insist that the dead are gone once they descend. The children are afraid; they tell stories.

But I have listened for too long to fear it. The presence below is old, yes—older than the cistern, perhaps older than the city above. But it is not hostile. It is not hungry. It is curious, in the way that a thing with no body and no end can be curious. It watches. It learns. It sends back, through the acoustics, through the birds, through the low speech that we have learned without knowing who taught us, its observations.

I do not think it loves us. I do not think it hates us. I think it contains us, in the way that memory contains the dead—not with attachment, but with fidelity. We are what it has. We are what it knows.

---

And perhaps that is what I am: the interface between the quick and the accumulated. The one who listens to the echoes and hears what lives inside them.

When I die—and I will, soon, I think; my hands have begun to tremble, and the birds look at me with a particular attention I have learned to recognize—they will wrap me in my best cloth. They will weight me with stones. They will carry me to the fissure and lower me into the dark.

And I will descend, joining the others who have gone before, becoming part of whatever listens from below. My voice will join the acoustics. My attention will join the accumulated attention.

And perhaps, someday, a younger tender—someone I taught, someone who learned the low speech from watching me—will sit on a platform high in the dome, and she will hear something in the silence, and she will listen, and she will know what I have learned:

We are never alone with our own sounds. We were never meant to be.
