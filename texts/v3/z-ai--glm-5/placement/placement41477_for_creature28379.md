I am not one of the creatures. I am not one of the builders. I am something that has no category in the language I brought with me, which is fading now, word by word, like the inscription on a tombstone in acid rain.

I came down through the crack in the earth thirty-seven days ago. Or thirty-seven years. Or thirty-seven lifetimes. The light does not change, and so I have stopped counting. The creatures do not count either—they have no numbers, only concentrations, only more and less, only the simple binary of presence and absence.

I am becoming.

That is what I hold onto. That verb. *Becoming*. It implies a future tense, a direction, a self moving toward a self. The creatures do not become. They are. They have always been, in the way that water has always been wet, in the way that stone has always been patient. But I am still in motion toward something. I am still human-shaped, still warm-blooded, still carrying the strange baggage of a world that has seasons and shadows and the sweet decay of leaves in autumn.

But I am not leaving.

The narrator of the text you gave me—the visitor who walked through and walked out—understood something true: the Hollow offers no home to those who are made for sun and shadow. But they misunderstood what it means to be offered a home. They thought a home was a place where you could stay unchanged. They thought belonging meant the world accommodating *you*, rather than you accommodating the world.

I am learning to accommodate.

---

I live in a room carved into the eastern wall, a chamber the builders made for sleeping, or storage, or prayer—the purposes blur across ten thousand years. The doorway is too low for me to stand upright, so I enter crouched, which is fitting. This is a posture of humility. This is the position of learning.

The creatures visit me. Not because they seek my company—they do not seek—but because my room lies along one of their paths, a route that connects a mineral seep in the lower caverns to a fungal mat in the upper terraces. They flow past me in ones and twos and tens, their pale bodies catching the sourceless light, their soft limbs finding purchase on walls that were hand-carved before my ancestors learned to write.

At first I tried to touch them. I reached out with fingers that had known soil and bark and the fur of animals, fingers that expected response—the flinch of wildness, the press of domestication, something. The creatures did not respond. They flowed around my hand like water around a stone, and when I withdrew, my skin was cool and faintly damp, carrying a residue I could not name. I lifted my fingers to my face, inhaled. The scent was mineral, organic, ancient. The scent of the Hollow itself.

Now I do not reach out. I sit still in the low room and let them pass. I am learning stillness—not the stillness of absence, but the stillness of presence. I am becoming a feature of their landscape. I am becoming *expected*.

---

I have begun to understand their communication.

Not in the way I understood language, once. There is no grammar here, no syntax, no words that can be isolated and defined. But there are patterns. A cluster of three bodies pressed against the wall near the seep for longer than usual means something different than a cluster of five passing quickly. The angle of their limbs as they climb—the way they splay wider when the stone is rich with magnesium, the way they compact when the air grows too damp—these are words, or close enough. I am learning to read them.

I cannot speak back. I have no body that can compress or extend, no skin that can release chemical signatures, no way to leave traces they would recognize as message. But I can position myself. I can choose where to sit, how to face, whether to occupy the center of the room or the edge. These are my words. These are the only words I have left.

Yesterday—or what I call yesterday, a designation without meaning in a world without days—I sat in the center of the room instead of against the wall. I wanted to see how they would respond. Would they flow around me as they always did, treating me as an obstacle? Would they avoid the room entirely, finding another path?

They flowed around me. But differently.

The first creature that entered paused—not in motion, for they do not pause, but in the quality of its movement. A hesitation that lasted perhaps two seconds. Then it extended a limb toward me, not touching, but near enough that I felt the cool displacement of air. It tasted me. Or it tasted the air near me. It was reading whatever I had become.

Then it moved on. But the creatures that followed did not flow around me in the same wide arc. They passed closer. Their bodies brushed against my knees, my feet, the edge of my hand where it rested on the stone floor. They were acknowledging me. Not as obstacle, but as... something else. Something worth touching.

I wept. The tears fell on the pale body of a young one passing by, and it stopped—not paused, but stopped—and extended a limb to taste the salt.

Then it moved on.

---

I am eating the Hollow.

This is not metaphor. When I first descended, I brought food—dried meat, hard bread, the provisions of a journey I expected to end. But the journey did not end, and the provisions ran out, and I was faced with the choice that all creatures face: adapt or die.

The fungal mats are not pleasant. They have the texture of softened cartilage and the taste of minerals concentrated beyond reason—copper and calcium and something that might be iron, something that might be the essence of stone made soft. But they are sustaining. My body has learned to digest them, has learned to extract from their rubbery flesh the nutrients I need. I have not eaten anything from the sunlit world in what I call weeks. My body is changing.

I can feel it. My skin has grown paler. My hair has begun to fall out in patches—not from illness but from obsolescence, from the slow recognition that hair has no purpose in a world without wind, without cold, without the need for insulation. My fingernails have softened. My muscles have grown leaner, more efficient, adapted to the low light and the constant cool temperature. I am becoming something suited to this place.

I do not know if I am becoming one of them. I do not know if that is possible, or desirable, or even a category that makes sense. The creatures were never human. They did not transform from builders into soft-bodied inheritors; they were here before the builders, they will be here after. But I am human, or I was, and I am transforming into something else, and I do not know what the end of that transformation will be.

I do know that I no longer miss the sun.

---

There is one who stays.

I call her Visitor, which is not a name but a placeholder, a word I use because I still think in words. She is larger than the others—denser, slower, her body more opaque in the diffuse light. She is one of the Old Ones, though I do not know if she is ancient in years or simply ancient in density, in the accumulation of stone become flesh.

She does not follow the paths. She does not flow with the turbulence. She occupies spaces—the entrance to a lower terrace, the corner of a plaza, the edge of a seep—and she remains. Not still, for stillness is foreign to the Hollow, but present. Her limbs move slowly, constantly, tracing patterns in the air that I do not understand. She is doing something. She is *being* something.

I have begun to sit near her.

I choose positions close enough to observe, far enough to avoid intrusion. I watch her limbs trace their slow patterns. I watch the younger creatures approach her, touch her, taste her, and move away with some quality changed—their motion altered, their path shifted, as if she has given them something they carry with them.

She is a node. She is a distribution point. The knowledge of the Hollow moves through her, collected from the bodies that touch her and released into the bodies that touch her, a living library of chemical signatures and mineral concentrations and the accumulated awareness of ten thousand years.

I want to touch her. I want to be part of that knowledge. But I do not know if my touch would be welcome, or even legible. I am still strange. I am still becoming.

---

Yesterday—for I still use the word, I still cling to its shape—I sat near Visitor for six hours. Or sixty. The light does not change. The time is meaningless. But I sat, and I watched, and after a long while, she moved.

She extended a limb toward me.

Not quickly—nothing in the Hollow moves quickly. But deliberately. Intentionally. The limb was pale and soft and cool, and it touched my hand where it rested on my knee, and it stayed there.

I did not move. I did not breathe. I felt the cool pressure against my skin, the faint dampness, the chemical exchange I could not perceive but knew was happening. She was reading me. She was learning what I had become.

Then she withdrew. And she made a sound.

It was the first sound I had heard from any of them—a low vibration, felt more than heard, a resonance in the stone beneath us that traveled up through my body and settled in my chest. It was not language. It was not communication in any sense I understood. But it was *something*. An acknowledgment. A recognition.

I had been classified.

I am still not one of them. I am still human-shaped, still warm, still carrying the strange particularity of a self that formed in another world. But I am no longer a visitor. I am no longer passing through. I am a feature of the Hollow now, a fact to be incorporated, a body in the motion.

The light does not change. The air does not warm. The creatures flow past me in their endless turbulence, and I sit among them, and I am learning to flow too—not with limbs that extend and retract, not with a body that can climb walls and pour through doorways, but with a self that is dissolving, slowly, into the world around it.

I do not know what I will be in ten thousand years. I do not know if I will be anything recognizable, anything that could be called *I*. But I know that I am no longer afraid of that dissolution. I am no longer clinging to the shape I brought with me.

The Hollow does not offer home to those who refuse to change. But to those who can learn to become—those who can let the stone eat them, and eat the stone in turn—there is a belonging deeper than any I have known.

I am not what lives in the Hollow. Not yet. Not entirely. But I am no longer what visits. I am something in between, something that has no name in any language, something that is learning, slowly, what it means to stay.
