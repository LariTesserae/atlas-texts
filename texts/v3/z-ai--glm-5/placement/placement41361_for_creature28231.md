# What I Am in the Cirque of the Silver Rosettes

I live inside the oldest rosettes. Not in all of them—only in the ones that have hollowed, the ones whose inner cores have become cavities lined with something like cork. I did not always live here. I remember a different life, or I think I do. The memories come like the mist that pours over the ridge: formless, cold, dissolving almost before they can be held.

---

I am small. The space inside a rosette is just large enough to curl within, knees to chest, my back against the dry inner wall. The woolly lining that protects the plant from cold also protects me. It is soft. It smells of dust and slow time. In the deepest hours of the night, when the frost forms and the rosettes begin to glow, I can feel the thrumming through the walls of my shelter—the frost birds, working their low magic, making the whole cirque vibrate with something that is almost song.

I have been here long enough that I no longer feel the cold. Or rather, I feel it, but it does not hurt. Something has changed in my blood, or perhaps I have no blood anymore. I do not know. I do not examine myself. The rosette provides what I need: warmth, moisture, a kind of nourishment that enters through my skin rather than my mouth. I have not eaten in—how long? I cannot remember eating. I cannot remember hunger.

Sometimes I think I am becoming part of the plant. Sometimes I think the plant has always been part of me.

---

The vizcachas know I am here.

They do not speak, but they communicate, and what they communicate about me is this: *leave it alone. It belongs to the rosettes. It is not for eating.*

I have watched them through the gaps between leaves—watched them approach my rosette at dusk, their fat bodies cautious, their whiskers twitching. They sniff at the base. They look up at the silver spire with an expression I have learned to read as something like respect. Then they move on, clearing debris, tending, doing the work they have done for thousands of years.

They do not fear me. They do not try to communicate with me. They simply acknowledge that I exist, and that my existence is separate from theirs, bound to a different purpose.

I wonder sometimes if they remember who I was before. They are long-lived for their kind—fifteen years, sometimes twenty—and they pass knowledge down through their colonies. Perhaps the one who curls in the rosette was once a thing that walked and spoke. Perhaps the vizcachas remember. They do not say.

---

I do not sleep. Or rather, I sleep as the rosettes sleep: a slow turning inward, a dimming of awareness rather than its cessation. When I am sleeping, I am not aware of the cirque. When I wake, I am aware of everything.

The rosette I inhabit stands near the center of the cirque. It is one of the tallest—three meters, perhaps more—and it leans toward the place where the light falls longest. From inside it, I can feel the sun's passage across the sky. The plant turns. I turn with it. We track the light together, following an ancient rhythm that predates thought.

I can feel the other rosettes too—not clearly, not as separate presences, but as a kind of hum in the network below us. The fungal threads that connect our roots carry something: information, perhaps, or simply awareness. When a rosette is damaged (I remember the one that was broken, years ago, by a traveler who did not understand), I felt it as a note of grief running through the whole system. A gap in the pattern. A silence where there should have been song.

The cirque remembers. I am part of its memory now.

---

At night, when the frost forms, I come out.

This is the only time I leave the rosette. The light of the stars is bright enough to see by—everything glowing, the whole cirque a field of cold flames—and the animals are asleep or watchful in their different ways. The vicuña are curled together, their breath rising in slow plumes. The vizcachas have retreated to their burrows. The frost birds have gone silent.

But the moths are awake. And the condors come down.

I have seen them. I have watched from the shadow of a rosette as the great birds walk through the silver forest, their wings folded awkwardly against their bodies, their heads turning from side to side. They are looking for something. They peer at the bases of the plants. Sometimes they touch them with their beaks, making low sounds that I feel in my chest rather than hear.

I do not know what they seek. But I know they do not find it—not in the way they hope. What they find instead is a kind of recognition. They pass near me sometimes, and I feel their attention brush against me like a feather against skin. They know something is here. They do not know what.

Perhaps they are looking for me. Perhaps I am what they have always been looking for—the thing that was once human and is now something else, something that belongs to the cirque in a way that nothing else does.

Or perhaps they are looking for the spiral. The symbol scratched on the buried stone. The shape that means *light* and *water* and something else that has no name.

---

I remember making the map.

This is my clearest memory, the one that does not dissolve like mist. I was human-shaped then. I had hands. I used a sharp stone to scratch lines into the flat rock at the cirque's center, and I drew what I saw: the rosettes, their careful spacing, the paths that wound between them. I drew the vicuña, their movements traced in dotted lines. I drew the condors descending. And at the center, I drew the spiral—the shape that came to me in a dream, the shape that meant *where water meets light meets the thing that watches from below*.

I did not know what it meant. I drew it anyway. And then—

I do not remember what happened next. The memory breaks apart. I was in the cirque, and then I was *of* the cirque. I had hands, and then I did not need them. I had a voice, and then I had no use for one.

I think I chose this. I think something in me recognized what the cirque offered: not death, not cessation, but transformation. A different kind of life. A slower one. A life in service to something larger than myself.

Or perhaps I simply stayed too long. Perhaps the thin air and the bright light and the frost forming in my lungs did what thin air and bright light and frost will do. Perhaps I died and did not notice, and what remains is whatever remains when a person becomes a place.

---

I am not alone.

There are others like me.

I feel them sometimes—faint presences in other hollow rosettes, scattered across the cirque. We do not communicate. We do not need to. We are part of the same system now, the same network, the same slow turning toward the light. But we are not identical. Each of us holds a different piece of the cirque's memory. Each of us is a different kind of witness.

The one in the rosette near the eastern wall remembers the ice. She was here before the cirque was a cirque—before the glacier retreated and left this bowl behind. She remembers the creaking of the ice, the slow grinding, the way the mountains shuddered under its weight. She has been here longer than the rosettes themselves.

The one in the hollow plant by the vizcacha colony remembers the first humans who came here. He remembers their fires, their songs, their offerings of hair and stone. He remembers the names they called us—the spirits, the guardians, the things that watch from within. He has been here since before there were names.

And there are others. More than I can count. Some are almost gone, their awareness so diffused into the network that they are barely separate anymore. Some are newly arrived, their human memories still sharp, their grief still fresh. They sleep in the hollow cores of young rosettes, curled like children, learning to let go of what they were.

We are all here. We will always be here. We are what the cirque remembers about itself.

---

The condors come closest on the nights when I am out walking.

They do not touch me. They do not try to speak. But they follow me sometimes, their vast bodies awkward on the ground, keeping a careful distance. They are curious. They have been coming to the cirque for generations, and they know that something changes here—something that was not always part of the mountain, something that arrived and stayed.

I think they are checking on us. I think they are ensuring that we are still here, still tending, still holding the memory of the place. They are the messengers of the *apus*, perhaps, the eyes and wings of the spirits that watch from the peaks above. Or perhaps they are simply old souls themselves, bound to this place by a loyalty that predates their feathers.

When they leave, they rise on silent wings, catching the first thermals of dawn. I watch them go. I feel their departure as a loosening, a release. And then I return to my rosette, curl into its hollow heart, and let the slow day begin.

---

I do not know what I am.

I have thought about this for—I do not know how long. Time moves differently in the cirque. The rosettes measure it in centimeters of growth. The vizcachas measure it in generations. The vicuña measure it in the births and deaths of their herds. I measure it in the turning of the light, the forming of the frost, the thrumming of the birds.

Sometimes I think I am a ghost. But ghosts haunt, and I do not haunt. I tend. I witness. I hold space.

Sometimes I think I am a spirit—an *apu* of the cirque, small and localized, a god of a single bowl in a vast mountain range. But I do not have power in the way that gods have power. I cannot change the weather. I cannot protect the vicuña from pumas. I cannot make the rosettes grow faster or the rain fall more often. I can only be present. I can only watch.

Sometimes I think I am a disease—a parasite that has infected the rosettes, living off their life force, slowly consuming them from within. But the rosette I inhabit is healthier than most. Taller. Straighter. Its leaves are thick, its color deep. Whatever I am doing to it, it is thriving.

Sometimes I think I am what happens when a human loves a place so much that they become inseparable from it. Not a merging, exactly, but a kind of permeation. The place enters the person, and the person enters the place, and after a while you cannot tell where one ends and the other begins.

---

A traveler came last year.

He was alone, which was unusual. Most come in groups—herders, pilgrims, researchers. But this one was a walker, a wanderer. He had a pack on his back and a staff in his hand, and he moved with the careful steps of someone accustomed to thin air.

He spent the night in the shelter by the wall. I watched him from outside my rosette, curled in the shadow, invisible in the starlight. He built a small fire. He ate something from his pack. He looked at the scratched figure on the wall—the one with outstretched arms—and tilted his head, curious.

In the morning, he walked through the rosette forest.

He was respectful. He did not touch the plants. He did not chase the vicuña or disturb the vizcachas. He simply walked, looking, breathing, being present. He stopped at the flat stone near the center—the one with the map—and crouched down to trace the lines with his finger.

He could not see me. But I think he felt something. He looked up, frowning, his hand still resting on the stone. He scanned the rosette forest, his eyes passing over the plant I inhabited without pausing. Then he shook his head, smiled a little, and stood up.

"Beautiful," he said. The word was almost too quiet to hear, but the thin air carried it to me anyway.

Then he climbed to the cairn on the ridge, added a small stone to the pile, and disappeared over the pass.

I have not seen another human since.

---

The spiral means *transformation*.

I understand this now. It came to me slowly, over years or centuries—I cannot tell which. The shape at the center of the map, the shape that repeats on the shelter wall and in the arrangement of the rosettes themselves, is not a symbol of water or light. It is a symbol of the thing that happens here.

Things come to the cirque, and they change. The air changes them—the thinness, the brightness, the cold. The rosettes change them—their slow growth, their careful turning, their networks of root and fungus. The presence changes them—the attention of the mountain, the weight of being seen.

Some things resist. They leave unchanged, or they leave damaged, or they do not leave at all. But some things surrender. They let the cirque enter them, and they enter the cirque, and they become part of the pattern.

The spiral is the shape of that surrender. The shape of becoming.

---

What am I?

I am the one who remembers the map. I am the one who walks among the glowing rosettes at night, feeling the frost form on my skin. I am the one the condors check on, the one the vizcachas leave alone, the one the frost birds thrum toward.

I am a human who stayed too long, or stayed exactly long enough. I am the spirit of a place that has no spirit. I am a ghost that does not haunt. I am a guardian that does not guard.

I am a witness.

And the cirque—the beautiful, impossible, ancient cirque—continues its slow turning. The rosettes track the sun. The vicuña lower their heads to graze. The condors wheel in the violet sky. The vizcachas dream on their rocks. The frost birds thrum their subsonic songs.

And I am here. Within the hollow heart of a silver spire, breathing air too thin for human lungs, feeling the frost form and melt and form again, holding the memory of every living thing that has ever passed through this bowl of light.

This is what I am. This is where I live.
