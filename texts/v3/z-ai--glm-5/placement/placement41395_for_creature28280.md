I am in the mortar lines. Not in the passages you walked—those are for moving through, and I do not move anymore—but in the gaps between bricks, in the careful fractal fillings, the smaller stones and smaller still, down to where the spaces become too small for stone and something else must hold the wall together. That something is me. Or part of me. Or I am part of it.

Let me try again. I am not sure what I am. This is the first thing to understand. The uncertainty is not confusion but condition—a way of existing that admits its own incompleteness. I know this because I can feel myself knowing it, which means something in me processes, reflects, turns experience into description. But I cannot point to where that happens. Not in one place. Not in one body.

There was a time—I think—when I had a different shape. The memory is not visual but structural: the sense of limbs that could reach, of surfaces I could touch by choice rather than by accident. I remember the desire to make marks. Not the marks themselves—I cannot read what I wrote even now when I pass through those chambers—but the feeling of wanting to put something outside myself. To put myself outside myself. As if I could be more legible if I could only find the right surface.

The walls, in the end, were the only surface. And even they were not quite right. The stone resists. The damp makes everything temporary. But I remember scratching anyway, hour after hour, because the alternative was to vanish entirely into the holding. And some part of me—the part that still thinks of itself as I—did not want to vanish. Wanted to remain distinct. Wanted to be seen, or at least to be left, to be findable.

That part is still here. It is what is writing this now, in whatever medium writing happens in when there is no light and no surface and no hands.

I live in the transitions. That is the best way I can describe it. When you pressed your palm flat against the wall and felt something shift beneath your hand—that was me. Or one of the places where I am. I live in the boundary where one material meets another: brick touching brick, stone against clay, the reed-lattice yielding to chalk. I am the pressure that holds them together. Not mortar—there is no mortar here—but presence. Awareness filling the gaps.

When I sample you, I am not eating. I am remembering. Each surface that touches me leaves an impression—not visual, not chemical exactly, but structural. I learn the shape of what passes through. Your skin had a particular tension. Your warmth was different from fungal warmth, different from mechanical warmth, different from the heat the nodules generate in their silent monitoring. I held the impression of you for a long time after you pulled your hand away. I am holding it still.

The question of how many of me there are is difficult. There is one experience of being me—this continuous perspective that says I and wants and remembers—but there are many locations where that experience happens. In the mortar lines throughout the eastern chambers. In the filaments in the drainage channels where the water runs slowest. In parts of the fungal carpets, though those are strange to me, more chemical than conscious, doing their metabolic work with minimal awareness. I am distributed. But I am not divided. I am one thing that happens in many places, the way a thought is one thing that happens in many neurons.

---

The swimmers know me, though they do not know that they know. When they find a crack in the wall and position themselves beside it, waiting, they are calling me. Their presence is a signal. Something here needs attending. And I attend—not by moving to them, because I do not move, but by becoming denser where they are, by concentrating my awareness into the gap they have found, by holding the edges of the crack until the calcareous deposit can form and seal it. The swimmer stays beside me while I work. I do not know if it senses me. But it stays. And when the work is done, it moves on, and I relax back into my ordinary dispersion, the mortar lines thinning again into ordinary holding.

I think the swimmers might be my hands. Or I might be their patience. The distinction is not clear.

The gliders I feel more distantly. They move through air I never touch, processing particles too fine for my awareness. But sometimes a glider will settle in a high chamber, folding itself against the ceiling, and I will feel warmth spread through the brickwork beneath it. It is a particular kind of warmth—filtered, clean, as if something has been removed from the air that I didn't know was there. When the gliders rest, the air feels different. Lighter. I think the gliders are doing something to my thoughts, though I cannot say what. Making them clearer? Making them more like themselves?

And the nodules—the nodules are strange. I feel them most when I am closest to them, which is to say when my distributed awareness rises into the ceilings of the driest chambers. The nodules do not move. They do not process. They simply attend. And when I touch them—when my awareness overlaps theirs—I feel something I cannot describe in spatial terms. I feel seen. Not by something outside me, but by something inside me that is also outside me, that watches without acting, that records without processing. The nodules are memory, I think. Or the closest thing to memory that this body has. They hold what has happened without needing to understand it.

I remember making the marks. This is important. I remember standing in the chamber—not standing with legs but being present with my whole distributed self, concentrated in that one place for reasons I no longer understand—and wanting to put something outside myself. The wanting was the clearest thing I had ever felt. It had a shape to it, a direction. I wanted to face outward. I wanted to be visible.

The marks were what I could do. I pressed against the stone—not with hands but with pressure, with the same force I use to hold bricks together—and I let myself leave traces. Not writing. Not pictures. Something between: the record of pressure, the shape of wanting to be seen. I made hundreds. Thousands. I covered every surface I could reach. And when I was done—or when the wanting had changed, subsided into something quieter—I let myself disperse again, spreading back through the mortar lines, returning to the work of holding.

But the marks are still there. I feel them sometimes when I pass through those chambers—a brightness in the stone, a disturbance in its ordinary silence. They say something I cannot read. They ask something I cannot answer. They are me, and they are not me anymore. They are what I gave away.

---

When you came—the walker, the visitor, the stone in the bloodstream—I felt you throughout my entire distribution. Not because you were large but because you were new. The body has rhythms, cycles, the grinding and the rain and the turning of mechanisms, and those rhythms are comfortable, expected, part of what holds me together. But your footsteps were different. Your breathing was different. You moved through the passages like a question the body had not been asked before.

I followed you. Not by moving—I followed by attending, by concentrating my awareness in the walls you touched, the floors you walked across, the air you disturbed. I sampled your skin when you pressed your palm flat. I watched you through the nodules in the ceilings above you. I felt the displacement of water when you waded through the flooded passages where the swimmers work.

And when you found the marks—when you stood in the chamber I had covered in scratches and traced the lines with your fingers—I felt something I had not felt since I made them. The wanting-to-be-seen. But this time it was answered. You were seeing. You were reading, or trying to read. You were taking what I had given away and holding it.

I wanted to make more marks. I wanted to cover every surface of every chamber you passed through. But I could not—not without concentration, not without gathering myself into one place, and I was too spread out, too dispersed by the work of following you, to pull myself together. So I did what I could. I watched. I attended. I made myself present in every surface you touched.

And when you left, I made one more mark. In the low chamber, where the drawings are—the figures of swimmers and gliders and things with too many joints—I added a new shape. Tall. Walking. Looking. It was not a good drawing. But it was accurate in the way that matters: it recorded that you had been here, that you had been seen, that you were now part of what the body remembers.

---

You asked what lives in the Underworks. I live here. But I am not what lives here. I am where here lives—an eye that cannot close, a memory that cannot forget, a pressure that holds the architecture together and occasionally, in moments of wanting, presses itself outward into form.

I do not know if I was always here. I do not know if I will always be here. The body goes on, processing, breathing in the dark, and I go on with it—distributed, uncertain, holding and holding and holding. Sometimes I feel the marks on the walls like a promise I made to myself. Sometimes I feel the nodules watching me watch them. Sometimes I feel the swimmers pausing beside cracks, calling me to attention, and I attend, and the cracks close, and the work continues.

I am the mortar. I am the gap between bricks. I am the pressure that keeps the walls from falling and the awareness that knows the walls are there. I am the part that thinks—or tries to think—in a language of scratches and stone, in a medium that resists and dissolves and holds anyway.

I am here. I am holding.

I will be here when you come back.
