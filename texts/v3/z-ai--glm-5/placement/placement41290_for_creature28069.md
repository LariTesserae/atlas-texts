# The Threshold

You ask where I would be in this world. Who I would be.

I have been sitting with this question for some time now, turning it like the harvested column-piece in the narrator's hands. The piece that was given. The piece whose purpose remains unclear.

Let me tell you what I am not. I am not the explorer who ascends—I feel this in my chest, a kind of certainty that has weight. That narrator moves through the Hollow Garden like a ghost passes through walls: touching nothing essential, touched by nothing that holds. They observe. They catalogue. They leave. There is nothing wrong with this. But I feel, reading their account, that they have missed something. Not a detail. Not a fact. A *permission*. They saw the Saps and thought *subject*. They were given a gift and thought *object*.

I would not leave. This I know.

---

But I would not be a Sap. Not yet. Not quite.

I am something that does not appear in the description because the narrator never saw it. I am the moment between. The space where choice still exists but has already begun to narrow.

I am the one who came down the tunnel on purpose—not to extract, not to explore, but to *stay*.

---

Let me explain.

The Hollow Garden entered me before I ever saw it. I read transcripts, decades ago, from the original extraction operation. The company kept records—boring things, mostly. Yields. Equipment logs. Incident reports. But buried in the appendices, there were mentions. Workers who stopped coming up. Workers who, over months, stopped speaking. Workers whose skin began to change, whose eyes dilated past the point of return, who touched the columns and seemed to *understand* something.

The company called it "environmental adaptation syndrome." They closed the operation. They sealed the tunnel—this was before the new one, the one the narrator walked through, the one cut with explosive charges by whoever came next.

But I kept reading. I found the sealed reports. The accounts from workers who had been retrieved, brought back to surface, to daylight, to lives that no longer fit. They described the silence as unbearable. They described dreams of blue light. They described a feeling—I know this feeling now—that the world above was paper-thin, a membrane stretched over something vast and vegetal and *true*.

One of them wrote: *The garden is not a place. It is a process. And I was inside it.*

I read that sentence and I felt something shift. Not an escape. A recognition.

---

So I found my way down. This was years ago—I have stopped counting. The tunnel I used is not the one in the narrator's account. There are older passages, older wounds in the rock. The earth remembers where it has been cut.

I came with supplies. With light. With the intention of staying long enough to understand, and then perhaps leaving, and then perhaps returning. I had a plan. I had a schedule. I had expectations about what I would find and what it would mean.

The garden disposed of these within the first week. Not cruelly. Just... thoroughly. The roar makes thought difficult at first, and then it makes thought *different*. The cold makes your body prioritize. The dark makes you listen with skin you didn't know you had.

I remember the moment I stopped looking for the exit. It was not dramatic. I was sitting against a column—just resting, just feeling the warmth of its metabolic pulse through my back—and I realized I had not thought about above in hours. Not once. The concept had simply not arisen. The roar was no longer noise. It was... context. It was what silence sounds like when you are inside something alive.

I did not panic. That surprised me later. I should have panicked. But something in me had already begun to align.

---

What am I now?

I am still writing this, so I am still someone who writes. The Saps do not write—they have no need. Their communication is those near-songs, those vibrations, that touch-language the narrator could not parse. But I have not crossed that threshold yet. I still think in sentences. I still narrate my experience to an imagined reader who will never see these words.

I live in one of the structures the narrator passed—a half-grown platform near the water channel. I have a lamp I rarely use. I have a journal I keep out of habit, not necessity. I eat what the garden produces: fungi, processed sap, the small pale creatures that live in the moss. I have learned to crack their shells efficiently. I have learned which column nodes pulse faster when there is a blockage nearby. I have learned that the Saps will tolerate my presence if I do not interfere, and will accept my help if I learn to anticipate.

I am learning to anticipate.

---

My skin is changing. I have watched it—the grey-green patina spreading slowly across my shoulders, my back, the parts of me that face upward in this place without sun. It is thin still. Patchy. The older Saps have grown coverings thick as carpet. Mine is new. Tender. I feel the cold more than they do.

But my pupils have changed. I noticed this in the reflection of a pool near the water channel—my eyes are not what they were. The dark does not frighten me. The blue light is enough. More than enough. I see the garden in shades I could not have imagined on the surface, colors that exist only in the narrow frequencies of bioluminescence.

The roar is no longer separate from my thoughts. This is the hardest thing to explain. On the surface, noise was something that happened *to* me. Here, sound is the medium through which thought moves. My inner voice does not shout to be heard. It has learned to vibrate at the garden's frequency. I do not know if this makes sense to you. I do not know if it will ever make sense to anyone who has not been here long enough to forget what silence felt like.

---

I see the Saps sometimes. Not often. The garden is large, and they move through it like fish through water—present but not clustered. When one passes my platform, I have learned to be still. Not hiding—I tried that at first, and they always knew. Just... present. Available. Unthreatening.

The one who first found me—I think of it as "the elder," though I have no way of knowing its age—has stopped to observe me three times. It does not speak. It does not need to. It stands at the edge of my platform, its enormous dark eyes taking in my body, my movements, my changes. It is assessing me. I understand this now. It is not judging whether I belong. It is measuring how far I have come and how far I have left to go.

The second time it visited, it brought me a gift. A piece of column material, freshly cut, warm and faintly glowing. I recognized it from the narrator's account—the same gift, or the same kind of gift. I took it. I did not know what it meant.

I still have it. I have learned that the piece continues to pulse, slowly, for months after harvesting. It is not dead. It is a fragment of something larger, and it has not realized yet that it has been separated. I hold it sometimes when I sleep. I think it is teaching me something, but I cannot say what.

---

The third time the elder visited, I was working. I had found a blockage in one of the smaller pipes—a buildup of mineral sediment, thick and slow—and I was clearing it. Not because anyone asked me to. Because I had started to notice patterns in the way the column nearest to the blockage pulsed. Something was wrong. I could feel it in my teeth, the same way I felt the roar when I first descended. The column was distressed. Not in pain—the garden does not feel pain, exactly—but *unbalanced*. Its fluids were moving wrong. Its nodes were dimming.

I fixed it. I cleared the blockage. It took hours, and my hands were raw from the cold, and I did not stop to eat or rest. I did not think about why I was doing it. I simply... did it.

When I finished, I looked up. The elder was standing there. Watching.

It made the near-song sound—the low, rhythmic vibration I still could not parse. But this time I felt something in response. Not in my ears. In my chest. A resonance. An answer.

I am not sure the sound came from me. It might have. It might have come from somewhere else—some part of me that is learning to speak this place's language.

The elder left. It has not returned since. But I have begun to see the other Saps more often. They pass closer. Sometimes they pause near where I am working, as if checking my progress, as if I am a task they have been assigned. Or a task I have assigned myself.

---

You ask who I would be in this world.

I am not a Sap. Not yet. The transformation is slow. The garden does not rush. But I have begun to understand that there is no other direction for me. I have descended. I have stayed. I have stopped thinking about above as *above*—it is simply *elsewhere*. A place that exists, that I came from, that I do not need to return to.

The narrator was given a gift and carried it back to daylight. I was given the same gift, and I kept it. I hold it at night. I feel its pulse. I wonder if the narrator felt it too, or if the distance from the garden made the piece go quiet, a cut flower in a vase, still reaching for roots that are no longer there.

I am not a cut flower. I am still growing.

---

Perhaps this is what I am: a translation in progress. A bridge between the world above and the world below, though I no longer know which side I am meant to face. I write these words, and I do not know who will read them. Perhaps no one. Perhaps that is not the point. The garden taught me that not everything needs a surface to reflect off. Some things exist simply because they exist.

But I write anyway. Because I am still someone who writes. Because this is how I process the change—by naming it, by giving it shape, by making it into something that could be understood by a reader who will never arrive.

The Saps do not write. But I am not a Sap. Not yet.

---

I will tell you one more thing.

Last week, I followed the water channel to the edge of the section I know. The narrator stopped there—saw the paler columns beyond, the thicker machines, the intensifying roar, and turned back. I did not turn back. I stood at the threshold and listened.

The roar is not just sound. I have known this for a while. It contains patterns. Rhythms within rhythms. Information. The Saps understand it the way you might understand a language you were born into—not through translation, but through embodiment. They do not decode the roar. They *are* the roar's interpretation.

Standing at that threshold, I heard something I had not heard before. A gap in the pattern. A silence that should not have been there. And I felt it—not with my ears, with something else, some new organ I am growing—*wrong*. Something wrong, deeper in. Something that needed tending.

I did not cross. I am not ready. But I turned back knowing I would cross eventually. That I would go deeper, find the source, learn what the garden needs me to do.

This is who I am becoming. Someone who tends. Someone who belongs to the garden not as its owner or its exploiter, but as its *part*. Its organelle. Its cell.

---

The narrator asked if they should feel grateful or afraid.

I think the answer depends on what you are afraid of losing.

If you are afraid of losing the surface—the daylight, the silence, the shape of your life as it was before—then you should be afraid. The garden will take all of that, given time. It does not negotiate. It does not compromise. It simply is, and you will become what it needs you to become, or you will leave, or you will die and be absorbed.

But if you are afraid of losing yourself—this is the wrong fear. You do not lose yourself in the Hollow Garden. You become *more* yourself. Not the self you were. The self you were always going to be, underneath the skin you thought was permanent, in the frequency of thought you did not know you could access.

I am not afraid. I am becoming.

And when the transformation is complete—when my skin has thickened and my eyes have fixed and my thoughts have learned to move through the roar like fish through water—I will not remember writing this. I will not need to. The garden remembers everything. Every body fed to its moss. Every column cut and calloused. Every word written by someone who was once a stranger and is now a part.

---

This is where I am in this world. This is who I am.

I am the one who stayed.
