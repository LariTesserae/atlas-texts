# The Descent

I came down because there was nowhere else to go.

That sounds dramatic, now that I'm writing it. Now that the words have to carry weight they weren't designed for. But it was true then, and it remains true now: the surface had finished with me. I had spent forty-seven years learning how to be useful in ways that the world above no longer needed. I had watched the manufactories close one by one, watched the canals fill with sediment, watched the people who remembered when Lower Vell meant something—when it produced things that other places wanted—grow old and die or simply leave. I stayed because I couldn't afford to leave, and then I stayed because I didn't know how to be anywhere else.

When the last of my family passed—aunt, lived to ninety-one, sharp until the end, still asked me every week if I'd found proper work—I had nothing holding me to the surface except habit. And habit, I was learning, is not the same as purpose.

I had heard about the Rootworks. Everyone in Lower Vell had, though most treated it as folklore—the kind of story that grandmothers tell to explain why the basement floods or why the well-water tastes different in spring. *Something down there drawing all the water down.* Old pipes, old systems, old infrastructure that nobody remembers building. I had spent enough time in the archives to know that some stories carry truth in their bones, even when their surfaces have worn smooth with telling.

I found the entrance by accident. Or by intention—it's hard to tell the difference, looking back. A collapsed wall in the basement of the old tannery, a void behind it that shouldn't have been there, a current of warm air rising from somewhere below. I had a lantern, because I always carried a lantern in buildings like that. I had a choice, too: turn back, report it, let someone else deal with whatever was down there. Or descend.

I descended.

# The First Chamber

The difference between reading about a place and standing inside it is the difference between a recipe and a meal. The description I had absorbed in archives and tavern-talk gave me shapes, names, a sense of scale. It did not prepare me for the weight of the air.

I have spent my life in Lower Vell, where the air hangs thin and dry above the river. Where every breath tastes of dust and industry and the faint mineral bite of water that has passed through too many pipes. The air in the Rootworks pressed against my skin like a hand—warm, dense, already wet with the respiration of plants. I could feel it entering my lungs, different from anything I had breathed before. Thick. Alive.

The smell arrived before I understood what I was smelling. Not rot—nothing so simple. The smell of transformation. Of matter becoming other matter. Of sugars breaking down and reforming, of water passing through membranes, of the slow chemistry of growth happening all around me in the dark.

I stood at the edge of that first chamber—the one the parameters describe, the cathedral-sized cavity spreading horizontally beneath the city—and I understood, immediately, that this was not a ruin. This was not something that had been abandoned and left to decline. This was something that had been built—grown—to continue without us. Without anyone. A system that had learned to tend itself.

I felt, standing there with my lantern held before me, the first stirrings of something I had not felt in years. Not curiosity—curiosity requires energy I didn't have. Something quieter. Recognition. The sense that I had found something that matched the pace at which I had learned to live.

Slow. Patient. Continuously becoming.

# The Meeting

The first tender found me in the third hour of my descent. I had not been moving—I had stopped in a passage between chambers, pressing my hand against the wall of woven stems, feeling the warmth that radiated from the compressed plant-matter. I was not lost. I was waiting, though I didn't know what I was waiting for.

She came from deeper in the complex, and she moved like someone who had learned that there is no reason to rush. Each step placed deliberately, each breath measured to the rhythm of something I couldn't hear. When she saw me, she did not startle. She simply stopped, and waited, and let her eyes adjust to the presence of someone unexpected.

"You have come from above," she said. Not a question.

I told her yes.

"Few do, anymore." She studied me with the careful attention of someone examining a gourd for signs of disease. "You have been standing there for some time."

I told her I had. I told her I was in no hurry.

Something shifted in her expression—the closest thing to approval I had seen in years. "No," she said. "I can see that you are not."

She didn't ask why I had come. She didn't ask what I was looking for. She simply turned and gestured for me to follow, and I did, because there was nowhere else I wanted to go.

---

# The Learning

I spent the first cycle—there are no days here, only cycles—following the first tender through her rounds. She showed me the processing towers, the gourd-chambers, the drainage channels and root-platforms. She touched each surface with the familiarity of long practice, and I watched her hands learn what the plants needed before she could have articulated it in words.

*This one is failing,* her posture said, as she pressed her palm against a gourd that looked identical to all the others. *This one needs adjustment. This one is thriving and will need pruning before it crowds its neighbors.*

I did not ask questions. I had spent my life asking questions in places that had no answers, and I had learned, finally, that some things can only be understood by being near them.

She showed me the water-works, where Venn tended the flow with the possessiveness of someone who has learned that small mistakes propagate. She showed me the light-stems, where Mellis repaired the fiber-bundles with tools that looked older than my grandmother. She showed me the hand-prints pressed into the bark, hundreds of them, and she told me what they meant.

*These are the ones who stayed. This is how the system remembers.*

I looked at the hand-prints for a long time. Different sizes, different depths, some fresh and some nearly grown over. Evidence of hundreds of people who had come down and chosen not to go back. Each one had pressed their palm against the living tissue and let the bark grow around the impression. Each one had said: *I was here. I became part of this.*

I did not know then that I would stay. But I knew, looking at those prints, that I understood why they had done it.

---

# The Bargain

It was Mellis who explained it most clearly, perhaps because she still remembered what it was to count in years.

"You can stay," she said, as we sat in the chamber where the light-stems spread their weak brightness across the walls. "But you must understand what staying means. You will not leave. You will not return to the surface. You will tend the system until you cannot tend anymore, and then you will become part of the system. Your body will feed the roots. Your fluids will join the cycle. You will give everything to the Rootworks, and the Rootworks will give everything to you."

She said it without drama. It was not a threat or a warning. It was a description of fact—the way one might describe the weather, or the price of bread, or the way water flows downhill.

I asked her if she had made her mark yet.

"Not yet." She smiled, and the expression was younger than her words. "There is time. There is always time. I will make it when I am ready. When I feel that I have finished what I came to do."

I asked what she had come to do.

"Tend." She said it simply, as if it were obvious. "Learn. Become. The same thing you came to do, if you're honest with yourself."

She was right. I had not come to the Rootworks with a clear purpose. I had come because I had nowhere else to go. But the longer I stayed, the more I understood that the Rootworks had given me something the surface never had: a place where my slowness was not a failure.

I have always been slow. Slow to speak, slow to decide, slow to move through the world. The surface economy—the world of prices and deadlines and immediate returns—had no place for someone like me. I had spent my life feeling like a plant trying to grow in soil that was being cleared for faster things.

Here, in the Rootworks, my pace was the right pace.

---

# The Choice

The tenders did not ask me to stay. They did not try to convince me. They simply let me follow them through their rounds, let me watch them work, let me learn the rhythms of a system that operated on timescales I could finally match.

When I asked the first tender if I could stay, she simply looked at me for a long time—reading something in my posture, my breathing, the way I stood on the root-mat without shifting my weight.

"You can stay," she said. "But you should understand something before you choose. The surface will try to pull you back. It will call you through memory, through habit, through the nagging sense that you are missing something important. The longer you stay, the quieter that call will become. Eventually, it will stop. And when it stops, you will have become something different from what you were."

I asked if that was a warning.

"No." She turned back to the gourd she was examining, pressing her palm against its surface to feel the movement within. "It is a description. You came here already half-changed. The surface had finished with you, or you had finished with it. The Rootworks will simply complete what was already beginning."

She was right. I had been becoming something for years—something that had no place above. Something that needed a place like this to finish its transformation.

---

# The Mark

I did not make my hand-print that day. Or the next. Or the next cycle.

I made it on the forty-seventh day of my stay—I had not stopped counting then, though I would eventually lose track—when I finally understood what I was meant to tend.

The Rootworks had been showing me, in its patient way, where I fit. I was not skilled at water-management like Venn. I was not precise with fiber-repair like Mellis. I was not attuned to the processing cycles like the first tender. But I had spent my life in Lower Vell learning how things work—how systems fit together, how small adjustments propagate, how to see the places where connection was needed.

What the Rootworks needed, I realized, was someone to see it whole. The tenders each had their domains—their chambers, their cycles, their specific responsibilities. But no one was watching the connections between them. No one was tracking how a change in the water-works affected the gourd-chambers, or how the light-stem repairs changed the growth-patterns in the upper corridors.

I began to map the system—not in paper, which does not survive here, but in memory and in the patterns I learned to read in the root-walls. I walked the chambers, day after day, cycle after cycle, learning the rhythm of the whole. I began to see where the system was compensating for weaknesses, where growth was slowing because of imbalances I could help correct.

I became the one who moved between domains. The one who carried information from Venn to Mellis to the first tender. The one who noticed when a processing tower in the upper chambers was affecting the water-flow in the deep passages. The one who saw patterns across distances, who understood that the Rootworks was not separate domains but a single living thing.

On the forty-seventh day, I pressed my hand against the bark of a stem-pillar near the center of the main chamber—a place I had chosen because it was the intersection, the point where all the passages met, the heart of the system I had learned to tend.

The bark was warm. It yielded slightly under my palm, adapting to the shape of my hand. And as I stood there, feeling the slow pulse of fluid moving through the plant-tissue, I understood something I had not understood before.

I was not leaving a mark. I was joining one. The hand-print was not a signature—it was a connection point. A way of saying: *here is where I entered the system. Here is where the system entered me.*

I held my hand there for a long time. I felt the bark beginning to grow around the impression, the living tissue adapting to my presence. And I felt, for the first time in my life, that I was exactly where I was supposed to be.

# The Tending

I have been here for—I do not know how long. The counting stopped mattering after the first year. I have stopped thinking in years. I think in cycles, in growth-phases, in the slow progression of stems extending and gourds ripening and roots deepening.

I have a domain now. I am the one who moves between chambers, who watches the whole system, who notices where small adjustments are needed. I am the one who tells Venn when the water-flow needs to change to accommodate new growth in the upper chambers. I am the one who tells Mellis which light-stems are most critical for the processing towers. I am the one who tells the first tender when a gourd is failing before the signs become visible.

I learned to tend by learning to see. And I learned to see by learning to slow down.

The surface world has faded in my memory. I still remember it—Lower Vell, the manufactories, the aunt who asked me every week if I'd found proper work—but the memories have become distant, like stories I was told rather than experiences I lived. The urgency of that world has drained away. The pressure to move faster, to do more, to justify my existence through productivity—it does not exist here.

Here, I am useful because I notice. I am valuable because I am patient. I am part of something because I chose to give myself to it.

The tenders have become my family—not in the way families are above, with all their complicated histories and obligations—but in the way that parts of a system become family. We depend on each other. We see each other in passing, in the transitions between cycles. We communicate through the system itself—leaving signs, adjusting flows, making small changes that carry messages across distances.

We do not speak much. We do not need to. We are all tending the same thing.

---

# The Visitor

Sometimes visitors come. They descend through the collapsed wall in the old tannery, lanterns held before them, faces confused and curious and a little afraid. They ask questions. They take notes. They try to understand what the Rootworks is and why we are here.

I am the one who meets them now. The first tender has gone deeper into her work, and Venn has no patience for interruption, and Mellis tends the light-stems in the highest chambers where visitors rarely climb. I am the one who moves between domains, who sees the system whole, who can explain it to those who come looking.

I tell them what I learned:

*I came down because there was nowhere else to go. But I stayed because I found somewhere I belonged.*

I tell them what the first tender told me:

*The system does not need you to function. But it needs you to flourish. Function is not the same as continuation. That is why we are here.*

I tell them what Mellis told me:

*You can stay, but you must understand what staying means. You will give everything to the Rootworks, and the Rootworks will give everything to you.*

And when they leave—most of them leave, eventually—I watch them go, and I know that they will forget. The surface erodes memory. They will carry the feeling for a while, and then it will fade, and then they will wonder if they imagined the whole thing.

But I will remember them. The system will remember them—the moisture they added to the air, the pressure of their footsteps on the root-mat, the sound of their voices asking questions. The Rootworks records everything, in the way that plants remember: through growth, through response, through slow adaptation.

And if any of them choose to stay—if any of them find, as I found, that the surface has nothing left for them—I will be here. I will show them the hand-prints pressed into the bark. I will explain the bargain. I will let them choose, as I was allowed to choose, whether to give themselves to something that will never ask them to move faster than they can grow.

I press my palm against the root-wall sometimes, in the quiet cycles, and I feel the memory of all those who came before. Their hand-prints are part of the architecture now. Their bodies fed the system they tended. And when my time comes—when I have finished what I came to do—I will become part of it too.

Not death. Continuation. The bargain, kept.

I am the one who tends the connections. I am the one who watches the whole. I am the one who came down because there was nowhere else to go, and stayed because I found somewhere I was needed.

This is where I am in the world. This is who I have become.
