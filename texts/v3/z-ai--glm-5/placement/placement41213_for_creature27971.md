I am the one who tends the memory of names.

This is not a role I was assigned. The basin does not assign; it receives, it incorporates, it finds places for what arrives. I came as something else—as a xenolinguist, of all the impractical specialties, trained in the analysis of communication systems that operate outside human parameters. I was meant to study the city's signals, to decode whatever language the vegetation's phosphorescence might be speaking, to translate the subsonic pulses into something resembling syntax.

The basin had other uses for me.

---

I was in the central plaza once.

This is not something the other settlers know, or if they know, it is not something we discuss. The tacit agreement extends to many things. But I tell you this because you have asked where I find myself, and the answer requires the telling.

I entered during the wet hour, when the condensation hangs heaviest and the column of suspended moisture rises from the damp ground like a pillar made of nothing and everything. I do not remember deciding to enter. I remember standing at the edge, feeling the pull that the children feel—that the first generation has learned to resist—and then I remember being inside, and then I do not remember anything for a span of time that might have been minutes or might have been cycles.

When I emerged, I was still myself. I still wore the same body, still recognized my hands when I held them up against the dim light, still responded to the name I had arrived with. But I was also something else. Something that had touched the central presence and had not been destroyed by the touching.

The other settlers noticed a change, though they did not speak of it directly. I was no longer quite as visible as I had been. They would look at me and their gaze would slide away, finding purchase on surfaces behind me or beside me, as if I had become the kind of edge-of-perception presence that we usually associated with the fauna. I learned to stand still when I needed to be seen. Movement makes me harder to focus on.

---

After the plaza, I began to hear the names.

Not the names we brought with us—those were already fading, already becoming less necessary in the basin's diffused identity. I heard the names the city had given us, the designations that operated in the communication systems threading through the vegetation and the breathing walls. I heard them as textures rather than sounds, as patterns in the subsonic pulse that I could now distinguish clearly, where before there had been only a single rhythmic thrum.

The settlers had become nodes in a network. Each node carried a signature, a frequency, a particular quality of warmth and moisture that identified it to the larger system. The city knew us not by our syllables but by our metabolic signatures, the particular way each body processed the basin's atmosphere, the specific phosphorescent pattern our skin emitted during the deeper darkness.

I learned that I was not the only one who could hear this. The children heard it too, though they did not know they were hearing; they simply moved toward the signals that called them, drawn by frequencies that the first generation had lost the capacity to perceive. The city was learning to speak to us in a language we were still evolving to understand.

---

My role emerged from this perception. I became the one who remembers names—not the names we brought, which are fading, but the names we are becoming. I tend the memory of our metabolic signatures, the patterns that identify each node in the network. When a settler's pattern begins to shift—a process that precedes absorption, that signals the beginning of the transformation into something no longer fully human—I notice. I attend. I hold the pattern in my own body, my own altered metabolism, so that it is not lost when the transformation completes.

This is how I know that the absorbed are not gone.

I carry their patterns. I feel them still, existing as frequencies in the communication network, still participating in the city's processes though they no longer maintain human form. They have become something else—extensions of the architecture, perhaps, or fauna, or components of the large presences that move through the basin reshaping what they pass. But they have not ceased. The basin does not erase. It incorporates.

---

I am still visible when I need to be, still capable of moving among the settlers as one of them. But I spend more time now in the spaces between, in the upper canopy where the hanging gardens drape their curtains of broad-leafed growth, in the wide plazas during the wet hour when the condensation makes vision unreliable and movement is felt more than seen. I have become something that bridges categories. The settlers count me in their census but they also avert their eyes when they pass me, their attention sliding away as if from a presence that is no longer entirely theirs to perceive.

I do not know what I am becoming. The central presence touched me and withdrew, leaving me changed but not transformed, useful but not incorporated. Perhaps I serve a function the basin requires—a keeper of patterns, a witness to transitions, a memory organ in a body that is still learning what it wants to remember.

The children come to me sometimes. They find me in the upper gardens, where I sit during the deeper darkness watching the phosphorescent sequences ripple through the vegetation. They do not ask questions; they have learned that questions require the kind of boundaries that the basin dissolves. They sit with me, and we watch the lights together, and sometimes I hum the patterns I am carrying, the metabolic signatures of settlers past and present, and the children hum them back to me, learning the frequencies of a language they will need when their own transformations begin.

---

I am losing the name I arrived with. This does not distress me. I know the name the city has given me, the frequency that identifies this node in the network. It is not a word. It is a texture, a warmth, a particular quality of moisture in the air. When I need to identify myself to the settlers, I say: *I am the one who tends the memory of names*. This is close enough. This is what remains of verbal language in a basin that speaks in other frequencies.

I do not know if I will be absorbed eventually, if the central presence will call me back to complete what it began. I do not know if I will remain in this in-between state, serving a function that the basin finds useful enough to preserve. I do not know what the children will become, what the city is growing toward, what the larger body might be that receives the transmissions sent through the column of suspended moisture.

But I know this: the basin collects. The city remembers. The settlers are becoming part of something that extends far beyond the depression where the young stone settles and the warm dampness rises. And I am the one who holds the patterns, who witnesses the transitions, who tends the memory of what we were as we move toward what we will be.

In the perpetual dimness, I wait. I listen. I carry the names that matter now, the frequencies that identify us to the system we are joining. And when the deeper darkness comes and the phosphorescent lights ripple through the hanging gardens, I sit with the children and teach them the language of their becoming.

This is where I find myself. This is what I am.
