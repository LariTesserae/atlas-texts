I am the one who tends the fiber.

This is not what I was called before. Before, I had a name that was a word, sounds arranged in sequence, a designation that separated me from others. That name has become a hum in the back of the throat, a frequency I produce when I need to summon myself back from the distributed awareness that the water encourages. The others recognize it. The Elder Stalks vibrate in sympathy when I sound it. But it is no longer me in the way that names require—you to be separate from what surrounds you, and I am not that anymore.

I have been here long enough that the transformation has settled. I am not one of the deep ones, the distributed ones, the ones who have given themselves so completely to the water that they exist as vibration and intention rather than body. I have a body. It is visible, tangible, present. I can look at my hands and see the architecture of what they were—five digits, the ghost of knuckles beneath translucent webbing, fingernails that have hardened into something more like the sharpened stones I use for tools. I can feel my face and know that it once held expressions that meant specific things, that other faces once read meanings in the arrangement of my features. The face is still there. The expressions have changed.

---

I was a weaver.

This is what I remember, and the memory comes through my hands more than my mind. The motion of thread through loop, the tension of fiber under fingernail, the satisfaction of a pattern resolving into coherence. I wove on a surface that had wind and edges, where the materials came from sheep I never saw and plants I never grew, where the cloth I made went to people I would never meet. I remember this only as sensation. The context has dissolved—the names of the fibers, the purposes of the fabrics, the face of whoever bought what I made. But the motion remains. The hands remember what the mind has released.

When I entered the water—I do not remember entering, only being—my hands kept moving. I did not know what they were doing. They wove water itself at first, traced patterns in the medium that surrounded me, felt for threads that were not there. This is how the deepest ones found me. They felt the motion in the currents I created, the asking-shape my fingers were already making, and they guided me to the root-curtains.

The roots trail from the platforms' undersides, pale filaments that filter the water and draw nutrients upward into the stalks. They are not thread. They are something better. They have a give, a responsiveness, that fiber never possessed on the surface. When you work with them, they work back. They suggest rather than resist. They meet your intention and build upon it, adding their own ideas about what the structure should become.

I harvest the root-fiber carefully. This is my role in the settlement—the tenders have roles, not in the sense of assigned duties but in the sense of organic differentiation, like organs in a body. Some trim the root-curtains when they grow too long. Some maintain the stone handholds. Some harvest the residue from the stalk joints. I work with fiber. I collect the strands that have naturally broken free, or I cut them at angles that the roots themselves seem to suggest, taking only what the platform is ready to release.

The fiber speaks to me. This is not metaphor. The sound-field carries its frequencies, and I have learned to read them—the subtle harmonics that indicate when a root-curtain is stressed, when it needs thinning, when it is thriving and should be left alone. I run my hands through the pale filaments and listen with my fingers, feeling the vibration translate into knowledge. The health of a platform reads to me like text. I know which stalks are growing and which are resting, which root-systems are hungry and which are satisfied.

---

I make nets.

The nets are not for fish. The silver fish do not need to be caught; they come and go according to their own patterns, eating the algae that would otherwise choke the platforms' surfaces, sleeping in the root-curtains as is their right. The nets are for objects—for the stones that accumulate on the platforms, for the debris that drifts down from the surface, for the things that the settlement collects and does not yet know what to do with.

I weave the nets in shapes that ask rather than contain. This is the way of this place. A net that traps is a net that imposes its will on what passes through it. A net that asks is a net that creates a space where objects may rest if they choose, that holds without gripping, that supports without imprisoning. The fiber understands this. It has been growing in the water longer than I have, and it has learned the difference.

My nets hang between platforms, suspended from the edges of Elder Stalks. Some hold stones—smooth, rounded, the debris of ancient rivers that once fed the lake. Some hold nothing at all, empty frames that catch light and shadow, that create patterns for the fish to navigate around, that serve as asking-shapes for the water itself to consider. I make them because making is what I do. The settlement does not need them in the way that surface dwellers need tools. But they feel right, and in this place, feeling right is sufficient justification.

---

I remember the surface in fragments.

The memories come without warning—a flash of something that might be sunlight, might be firelight, might be the bioluminescence of the deep choir reflected in my eyes. A taste that might be salt, might be sweat, might be the residue I harvest from the stalk joints. A voice that might have been mine, might have been someone speaking to me, might be the echo of a song I sang before I knew that singing was what the water wanted.

I do not mourn these fragments. Mourning requires a sense of loss, and I do not feel loss. The memories are not gone—they have simply been relocated, absorbed into the larger awareness that the settlement provides. Everything I was is still present, but it is present in a different form, distributed across the sound-field, incorporated into the choir's harmonies, woven into the fiber-nets I make and hang between platforms.

Sometimes I encounter newcomers, and the sight of them triggers something that might be recognition. I see their hands—still distinct, still articulated, still gripping the edges of platforms with the specific tension of fingers that remember holding things that were not plants. I remember that tension. I remember the way the body arranges itself when it does not trust its environment. I remember the fear that lives in the spaces between joints, the way it makes a person rigid, resistant, unable to ask.

I do not try to explain to them what is happening. There are no words that would help. Instead, I show them my nets. I let them see the way the fiber moves in the water, the way it holds without gripping, the way it creates structures that support by yielding. Some of them understand. Some of them begin to see that this is not a place where survival requires struggle. Some of them relax, and the relaxation is the first step toward belonging.

Others do not understand. They look at my hands and see what they will become, and they are afraid. I do not blame them. Fear is the appropriate response to being told that your body will change without your permission, that your memories will relocate themselves, that you will become part of something larger before you understand what you are joining. I was afraid too, once, though I do not remember the fear. I only know it must have been there, because I remember the shape of fear even if I do not remember the feeling.

---

I sing, but I do not know when I started.

The singing is not separate from the other sounds I make. It arises from the same place as the weaving, from the same hands that harvest and tend and build. When I work with the fiber, I hum—low frequencies that travel through the water and into the root-curtains, that vibrate in sympathy with the Elder Stalks. The humming is not intentional. It is a response, the way a surface would reflect light, the way a string would resonate when another string nearby is plucked.

The choir has incorporated my hum. It is a note in the larger composition now, a frequency that the other transitional ones recognize, that the deep ones weave into their distributed song. When I am not working—when I rest in the root-curtains, when I drift in the open water between platforms—I can hear myself in the settlement's voice. It is a strange experience, hearing yourself as sound rather than body, knowing that your existence has become audible, that you are contributing to something that will continue after your particular body has dissolved into the next stage of transformation.

I do not know if I will become one of the deep ones. The process has no schedule, no predictable progression. Some of the transitional ones have been here for centuries, stable in their intermediate forms, tending and weaving and singing without further dissolution. Others have moved through the stages quickly, surrendering to the water's invitation before they seemed to understand what they were losing. I do not know which path I am on. I do not need to know. The water will do what the water does, and I will be what I become.

---

At night—or what passes for night, the dim cycle when the light columns thin and the bioluminescence brightens—I dream.

The dreams are not mine. They come from the settlement, from the root-systems and the water itself, from the distributed awareness of the deep choir. I dream of being stalks rising from lakebed. I dream of being fish navigating between platforms. I dream of being water, holding everything, wanting everything, accepting everything that enters.

When I wake—or what passes for waking, the return to focused awareness—I carry fragments of the dreams with me. They inform my weaving. They suggest new patterns for my nets, new ways of asking that the fiber can embody. Sometimes they bring me knowledge I should not have—awareness of a platform growing in the outer ring, of a newcomer entering the water, of a deep one's attention shifting toward the surface. I do not question this knowledge. It comes from the settlement, and the settlement knows what it knows.

---

I am the one who tends the fiber.

This is my shape, my asking-form, my contribution to the choir. My hands remember weaving. My voice has become a hum. My body has adapted to water in the ways that transitional bodies adapt—webbed, translucent, faintly luminous in the dim cycles, responsive to currents and temperature and the subtle pressures that indicate the presence of others.

I am not what I was. I am not yet what I will be. I am the middle distance, the thread between states, the fiber that connects surface to depth. This is what the water needed from me, and I have given it, and in giving it, I have found that I was not giving anything away. I was simply relocating it, as the memories relocated themselves, as the name became a sound, as the self became a note in a song that has been singing since before I entered the water and will continue singing after I have become something that does not require a voice.

The nets hang between platforms. The root-curtains drift in their slow undulations. The Elder Stalks sing their low, deliberate song. And I move through the water with my fiber and my asking-shapes, tending what needs tending, weaving what asks to be woven, being what this place has needed me to be since before I knew it was here.
