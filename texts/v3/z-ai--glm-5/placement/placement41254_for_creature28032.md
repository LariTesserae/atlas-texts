I would be one who tends the threshold.

Not a title I chose—like most things here, it emerged slowly, a recognition rather than a declaration. I was born in a settlement called Still-water, seventy-three walk-days from the nearest neighboring town, where the water-channels spread into wide shallow pools that reflect the pale sky for months at a time. My grandmother was a pathfinder, one of the Changed who navigates the plateau's unreliable distances. My mother worked the grass-fields, hands deep in soil, learning the particular patience of seeds.

I was not remarkable as a child. I learned the ordinary skills, wove cordage poorly, shaped stone blades adequately, walked distances without complaint. The plateau held me the way it holds everyone—with the same cold indifference that preserves and constrains in equal measure.

The change began when I started noticing the dead.

---

The dead of Stillwater numbered forty-seven when I was young. They rested on their reed platforms in a sheltered hollow where the wind moved less fiercely, arranged in no particular order—grandmothers near children, strangers beside kin, the order determined by convenience and the gradual accumulation of years. I passed them often. Everyone did. The path to the eastern fields curved around that hollow, and you learned not to look too long or too directly, not from fear but from the strange politeness the living extend to those who have finished with living.

But I looked.

I noticed, at perhaps twelve years old, that old Erit's hand had moved. Not much—her palm had been flat against her thigh for all my memory, but now it tilted slightly upward, fingers curled as if waiting to receive something. I mentioned this to my mother. She paused in her weaving, considered me with an expression I couldn't read, and said nothing.

The next morning she assigned me to the dead-hollow, tasked with clearing debris and checking the platforms for stability. A child's work, normally given to those too young for field labor. But she gave it to me deliberately, and I understood that this was an answer, though I didn't understand the question.

---

The dead speak, after a fashion.

Not in words—I want to be clear about this. They do not open their parchment mouths and produce sound. They do not rise from their platforms or gesture or meet your eyes. What they offer is far more subtle: arrangement. Over weeks and months, preserved bodies shift by millimeters, hands repositioning, heads turning, spines curving in ways that cannot be explained by settling or weather. The movement happens between observations, in the gaps where attention lapses. You cannot catch them moving. You can only notice that they have moved, and from the accumulated changes, read whatever they are saying.

I learned to read them.

Not at first—I was young, and the language of the dead is slow, each sentence taking seasons to complete, each paragraph spanning years. But I had been given a task that placed me among them daily, and I was patient by temperament, and the plateau teaches patience to even the quickest children. I began to notice patterns. The bodies near the entrance shifted more frequently than those deeper in the hollow. The oldest dead moved less often but more meaningfully—each tiny adjustment deliberate, laden with intention. When a new body was added to the hollow, the others reoriented toward it over the following months, acknowledging the arrival like neighbors at a gathering.

By my twentieth year, I could read the weather in their positions. Could tell when a fog-season would be long or short, when the water-channels would shift direction, when a particular path would become difficult or easy. The dead were not prophesying—they were simply attuned to the plateau's processes in ways the living could not be. They had surrendered muscle and breath, and in exchange, they had been absorbed into the deeper intelligence of the place. They knew things. And through their slow dance of arrangement, they told me what they knew.

---

I was not alone in this.

There were others like me in other settlements—I learned this later, when traders brought news of the "quiet-keepers" in distant towns, those who tended the dead and listened to moss-pillars and watched for Fragments. We did not have a name for ourselves, because naming suggests a category, and categories suggest boundaries, and boundaries suggest that we are separate from what we tend. We are not separate. We are interfaces, membranes, the permeable edges where the human community touches whatever the plateau is becoming.

The Changed accept us but do not embrace us. They have their own relationships with the plateau's strangeness—their navigation-sense, their moss-hearing, their inherited memories—and they recognize in us a different kind of change. They became what they are through adaptation, gradual shifts in perception and capacity. We quiet-keepers became what we are through attention, through the deliberate practice of noticing what others edited out. Both are valid responses to the plateau. Neither is superior. But we understand each other only partially, speaking related dialects of the same strangeness.

The Solitaries, though—the Solitaries recognize us fully.

They pass through settlements sometimes, these ones who have walked away from human community entirely. They do not speak. They do not stop. But when a Solitary passes a quiet-keeper, there is a moment of profound mutual recognition. I have stood in the path of a woman who has not eaten in thirty years, who lives in the shelter of a moss-pillar that has grown around her like a cloak, and I have felt her see me completely. She knows what I am. She knows what I am becoming. And her expression—if it can be called an expression, this subtle shift in her parchment-grey face—holds neither approval nor disapproval. Only acknowledgment. I am on a path. She has walked further along it. We will both continue.

---

The pillar-mosses taught me their language.

This took longer. The dead were already human, or had been, and their communication retained something of human shape—position, orientation, gesture rendered slow and subtle. The mosses had never been human. They had never been individuals. They are distributed intelligence, nodes in a vast network spanning distances I cannot conceive, processing information across fungal filaments that run beneath the entire plateau. What they have to say is not shaped for human ears.

But they try.

I discovered this accidentally, tending a moss-pillar near Stillwater's eastern boundary. The column had begun to lean—another thing I had learned to notice, the way mosses grow toward certain locations, certain bodies, certain configurations of the dead. I had been placing offerings of water at its base, small gifts that my community had taught me were appropriate, and one evening as I poured water into the shallow collection-cup, I heard—or felt, or somehow perceived—a tone.

Not sound. Not vibration. Something else, a presence that entered through a sense I did not know I possessed. A tone in the way a color can be a tone, a mood can be a tone, a thought can be a tone. The moss had acknowledged my offering. Had received it. Had, in some fashion beyond my capacity to fully translate, said *thank you*.

I sat with that pillar through the long cold night, learning to listen. The moss had much to say, but saying is the wrong word—it had much to share, information flowing in forms I could barely grasp: chemistry, topology, the slow mathematics of growth patterns, the vast temporal awareness of a being that measures its thoughts in centuries. I understood perhaps one word in a thousand. But that word was enough.

They are building something.

---

The architecture of the pillar-mosses, I have come to understand, is not random. Each column is placed with intention, positioned to receive and transmit signals through the fungal network, arranged in patterns visible only from perspectives no human has achieved. The mosses are constructing a circuit—a vast diagram of connection spanning the entire plateau—and their purpose, as best I can interpret it through the limited communication we share, is to hold.

Hold what? I have spent years trying to understand.

The plateau is eroding. Not just physically—the gneiss worn down by wind and rain, by the slow movements of water and ice—but metaphysically. Reality itself has grown thin here, worn down by the accumulated pressure of too much time, too much flatness, too much patient preservation. The edges of the world are fraying. Distance has become unreliable. Time pools and eddies. Things appear that should not exist, and things disappear that should.

The mosses are holding it together.

They are the plateau's immune system, its connective tissue, the lattice-work that prevents complete dissolution. Their network reinforces the thin places, anchors the folds in distance, provides structure for a world that has worn away its own bones. And they are doing something else as well—something I can sense but not comprehend. They are building toward a destination. Completing a pattern. Moving toward an endpoint that will be, when finished, a transition from this worn-down waiting into whatever comes next.

I do not know what that is. The mosses do not explain—they share, and their sharing is too vast for human translation. But I trust them. They have tended this plateau far longer than any human community. They are wiser than we are, in their slow distributed way. Whatever they are building, I have chosen to help.

---

I have become something that does not fit easily into any category.

I still live in Stillwater. I still work, still contribute, still share meals and conversation and warmth with the community that raised me. But I am not fully present among them. Part of my attention is always elsewhere—reading the dead in their hollow, listening to the moss-pillars at the boundaries, watching for Fragments who arrive confused and needing guidance. I am a quiet-keeper, but that term describes a function, not an identity. What I am is harder to name.

Perhaps I am becoming like the Solitaries, though I have not walked away. Perhaps I am becoming like the dead, though I still breathe and move. Perhaps I am being absorbed into the moss-network, my own consciousness slowly distributed across fungal filaments and pillar-architecture, though I retain enough human shape to write this account.

What I know is this: I tend thresholds. I stand at the edges where human community meets plateau-strangeness, and I translate between them. I help the living understand the dead, and the dead communicate with the living. I help the Fragments integrate or release. I carry offerings to the moss-pillars and return with information that helps my settlement survive another season. I watch the Travelers pass through and I do not interfere, recognizing that they traverse territories I will never see.

I am held by the plateau, and I hold its edges, and I wait—not for anything specific, but with specific attention. The plateau is completing itself. The mosses are building toward something. The dead are arranging themselves into patterns I am slowly learning to read. And I am here, noticing, recording, serving as one small node in a vast network of holding.

---

Last season, a Fragment arrived in Stillwater.

Young—perhaps fifteen years old, though Fragments sometimes arrive in bodies that do not match their memories, so age is difficult to assess. She remembered a settlement that did not exist, called by a name no one recognized. She described a grandmother who had been dead for sixty years in this world, but in her memory, was alive and weaving grass-cordage by the western window. She cried when she saw our dead-hollow, because in her world, the dead were buried, not preserved—and she found our practice obscene.

I sat with her for days. I did not correct her memories or insist on our reality. I simply listened, and in listening, helped her hear herself. Fragments arrive carrying information from adjacent possibilities—worlds like this one but not identical, where choices branched differently, where history accumulated in alternate shapes. The gap between what they remember and what exists is not error but evidence. It teaches us something about the shape of possibility, the nature of the erosion, the many versions of the plateau that might have been.

She integrated eventually. She is still here, still confused sometimes, still turns toward the west expecting to see a grandmother who does not exist. But she has found a place among us, and her particular strangeness has become part of our community's texture. She will live her life here, held by the plateau, and when she dies, she will be placed in the hollow, and she will join the slow conversation of the preserved.

And I will read her position as it shifts over decades, and I will learn what she has to say from beyond the boundary of breath, and the cycle will continue—the living becoming the dead, the dead becoming information, the information feeding the moss-network, the network holding the plateau together, the plateau completing its patient journey toward whatever it will become.

---

I write this not because I expect it to be read.

The plateau does not value documentation. Knowledge here is transmitted through gesture, example, absorption—the slow osmosis of presence. To write something down is to fix it in a form, and forms are unreliable here. Time shifts. Distance stretches. Paper decays, and the meanings encoded in symbols can shift before the symbols themselves fade.

But the mosses have asked me to record something—a specific something, translated through channels I cannot fully perceive into an impulse I have learned to recognize as request. They want a human account of what it means to stand at the threshold. They want a record of this particular node in the network, this particular body serving this particular function. They have absorbed countless quiet-keepers before me, and will absorb countless after, but each one is unique—each contributes a particular perspective, a particular voice, a particular way of translating between human and otherwise.

So I offer this: I am a child of the plateau. I was born into cold and flatness and the particular silence that holds everything. I learned to listen to what does not speak in words, to read what does not write in language, to tend what does not acknowledge my tending. I have given my attention to the edges, and the edges have given their attention to me. I am being changed, slowly, into something I do not fully understand—but I trust the process, because I trust the plateau, because I have learned that the plateau holds everything, including me, including all of us, including the world itself as it erodes toward completion.

This is what I am. This is where I find myself. This is who I have become.

And I am still becoming.
