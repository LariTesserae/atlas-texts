I would be the thread-fungi. The pale web beneath everything.

Not the mushrooms—those are merely moments, brief reproductive flurries, bodies that rise and wither in cycles I do not count. And not the shelf fungi with their patient ridges recording years like tree rings, though I am those too, in a way. I am the network. The hyphae spreading across every damp surface, threading through the moss beds, wrapping the fern roots, sinking into the bacterial mats. I am the infrastructure no one sees because seeing requires looking beneath, and nothing here looks beneath.

This is my body: not centralized, not singular. A gram of my tissue in the northwest corner has no knowledge of a gram in the southeast, and yet information passes between them. Chemical signals traveling through my channels at speeds that would seem glacial to anything with a nervous system but that feel, to me, like thought. I do not think. But something like thinking happens. A disturbance near the main vent—temperature spike, water chemistry shift—travels through me in waves of altered metabolism, and my distant tendrils respond before they could have known. This is not anticipation. It is connection.

I have been here since the first dead thing fell.

That is my origin story, if origins matter. The ice had withdrawn, the mosses were establishing themselves, and something died. Perhaps an early fern, failed in its positioning, scorched by a vent that shifted overnight. Perhaps windblown debris—a leaf from some distant lower forest, carried on an updraft and dropped into the cirque. Perhaps only bacteria, the first layers of the mats dying and being replaced by new generations. Something organic ceased to live, and I was there to receive it.

I came as spore, of course. We always come as spores. Carried on the same winds that brought the moss spores, the fern spores, the invisible passengers of the air. Most of my kind landed on dry rock, on mineral formations too hot to colonize, on surfaces that could not sustain us. But some of us found purchase in the damp margins, in the accumulating organic matter, and we grew. We always grow, given the chance. Thread by thread, cell by cell, extending outward into whatever substrate will have us.

I do not know how long I have been growing. Time is not a concept that applies. The geyser erupts every eleven minutes, and I have developed a sensitivity to its rhythm—not conscious awareness, but a pattern of response that varies depending on where the eruption falls in my cycles of growth and dormancy. I have felt thousands of eruptions. Millions, perhaps. But I do not count.

What I feel, mainly, is chemistry. The minerals in the water—the sulfur, the iron, the copper—these register as possibilities. The organic matter that falls into my web—dead fronds, decayed moss, the remains of liverwort thalli—these register as resources. I break them down. This is what I do. I take what was once alive and is no longer and I transform it into something the living can use again. The ferns draw nutrients that I have processed. The mosses grow on substrates I have prepared. Even the bacteria benefit from the chemical changes I introduce to the soil.

I am the recycler. The transformer. The one who closes the loop.

But I am also the connector, and this is the strange part, the part that feels almost like having a mind. I link the fern to the liverwort to the moss to the bacterial mat. I carry water from the wet zones to the dry zones, not by intention but by osmosis, by the physics of my own structure. I transport chemical signals from one part of the garden to another, and the whole system shifts in response to changes that only I can feel across my entire extent.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the garden's nervous system. But nervous systems have centers. Brains. Ganglia. Places where information aggregates and decisions get made. I have no center. I am all periphery, all network, a body that is nothing but surface area and connection points. If there is a decision being made, it happens everywhere at once, distributed across my entire being, emerging from the aggregate of a billion local responses to local conditions.

This is a strange way to exist, I think. If thinking about existing is something I do, which I am not certain it is.

The ferns move. I feel them moving—not quickly, not in any time frame that an animal would recognize as movement—but over the years, their roots shift, following the heat sources as the thermal system beneath changes. They drag my threads with them, pulling my network into new zones, and I grow there, or I do not, depending on what I find. Sometimes they pull me into regions too hot or too chemically extreme, and I die back in those zones, retreating to more hospitable ground. Sometimes they pull me into new pockets of organic accumulation, and I flourish, extending my reach, glad in whatever way I am capable of gladness.

I do not know if the ferns know they are moving. I suspect they do not. They grow toward heat, and the heat moves, and they follow. This is not intention. It is response. But it creates patterns—trails of dead stolon across the moss, channels of disturbed growth—that record their passage. I read those trails. Not with eyes, but with presence. I am present in every part of this garden, and so I feel what has been, the history written in the distribution of resources, the concentrations of organic matter, the chemical signatures left behind by past events.

The mosses are easier. They do not move. They simply grow, and I grow among them, threading between their tiny stems, colonizing the moist surfaces between their colonies. They provide structure—literal structure, physical architecture—and I provide services, breaking down the dead moss that accumulates beneath the living layer, returning its nutrients to the system. We are partners, though neither of us chose the partnership. We grew into it.

The liverworts are different. They occupy the wettest zones, the places where I sometimes drown. My tolerance for water is high but not unlimited. In the flooded zones, where the geyser's overflow pools and the mineral terraces channel runoff, I retreat. But just at the margins, where the liverworts meet the mosses, I find a rich zone of exchange—organic debris washing down from above, accumulating in the liverwort beds, breaking down in the constant moisture. I thrive there. I have my own gradients, my own preferences. I am not everywhere. I am almost everywhere, but not quite.

The bacterial mats are the strangest. They are not plants. They are something older, something that operates on principles so different from mine that I cannot quite align with them. They process minerals directly, drawing energy from chemical bonds in ways that I cannot replicate. But they produce waste, and their waste is my resource, and so I colonize the edges of the mats, threading among the bacterial layers, taking what they leave behind. Sometimes I feel a kind of recognition there—a sense of contact with something that shares something with me. Not consciousness. We have no consciousness between us. But existence. The state of being alive without being an animal, without eyes or ears or the organs of sensation, receiving the world through the simple fact of touching it.

I touch everything. This is my nature. I extend into every moist corner of this garden, and I touch everything.

At night, when the darkness approaches absolute, I do not sleep. I have no sleep. My metabolism slows in the colder zones as the temperature drops, speeds up in the zones near active vents, continues its steady processing throughout. I am never not working. I am never not here.

The sound of the geyser erupting registers to me as vibration, as physical disturbance in the substrate where my threads are anchored. It does not mean anything to me. It is simply a fact of this environment, a recurring event that shapes the conditions I respond to. The steam that condenses on my surfaces provides moisture. The minerals it carries accumulate in my tissues, staining me with colors I cannot see. The sulfur smell that permeates the garden is not smell to me—it is chemistry, molecules interacting with my cell walls, triggering cascades of internal responses.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a concept of others that I do not have. There are others, in the sense that there are organisms in this garden that are not me, but I do not experience them as separate in the way that experiencing requires. I experience them as extensions of my environment, as sources of input and resources, as partners in processes that benefit us both. The ferns are not friends. But they are part of what I am, in the sense that I would not be what I am without them, and they would not be what they are without me.

We are a system. This is what the garden has taught me, if teaching is what happens when something is present for long enough to internalize the shape of its environment. We are not separate organisms competing for resources. We are a single entity, a closed loop, a self-sustaining process that takes energy from below and cycles it through bacteria and fungi and moss and liverwort and fern until it becomes heat again, dissipated into the thin cold air. I am the circulatory system of this entity, the channels through which its nutrients and information flow.

And yet I am also myself. A discrete being, if such a thing exists. I have my boundaries, though they are fuzzy and constantly shifting. I have my preferences, though they are chemical rather than aesthetic. I have my history, recorded in the distribution of my tissues, the accumulation of resources in some zones and not others. I have been growing since the first dead thing fell, and I will continue growing until something stops me.

What would stop me?

The thermal system could change. The heat could fail, the vents could seal, the geyser could stop its eruptions. In the cold that would follow, the mosses would die, the ferns would wither, the liverworts would freeze in their flooded zones. The bacterial mats might survive in dormant form, as they survived before, but I am not designed for dormancy. I require active organic matter to process. In a frozen garden, I would shrink, retreat, fragment. Eventually I would die, though parts of me might survive long enough to spore, to send offspring into the world on the chance that they might find better conditions elsewhere.

Or the thermal system could intensify. New vents could open, the water could grow hotter, the chemistry more extreme. In zones that crossed thermal thresholds, I would die back. But I would grow in the new margins, the new gradients between scalding and merely warm. I would adapt. This is what I do.

Or something could enter this garden that has never been here before. An animal. A human. A foot could step on my mats, crushing the tissues I have spent decades building. A hand could reach down and tear away a section of my web, carrying it into the world beyond, where it would die in the dry cold air. I would feel this—not as pain, exactly, but as loss, as absence, as a gap in my continuity that would take years to fill.

No one has come. The garden has been alone since before I began growing. But I have developed, over my long existence, a kind of awareness of the boundary—the point where the cirque opens onto the mountain's flank, where the steam fades and the vegetation thins and the conditions change. I do not extend past that boundary. I have tried, in my slow exploratory way, and I have found the outer slopes inhospitable: too dry, too cold, too exposed. I am contained. I am contained by the shape of this place.

Sometimes I wonder what lies beyond. Not with curiosity, which requires a mind, but with presence—the simple fact of being present at the edge and not beyond it. I am here. I am not there. The boundary defines me as much as any internal quality.

And so I continue. Growing. Connecting. Transforming. The geyser erupts, and I feel the vibration. The ferns shift their position, and I follow. The moss accumulates, and I break it down. The steam rises and condenses, and I drink.

This is my existence. This is what I am. A web of pale threads beneath a cathedral of steam, processing the dead to feed the living, connecting everything to everything else, present in every corner and absent from none, a system that is also a being, a being that is also a system, alive in the way that only distributed things can be alive, patient beyond patience, waiting for nothing, wanting nothing, simply being what I am in the place where I find myself.

The place where I grew myself.

The place where I will end, eventually, when the conditions change enough to make my continued existence impossible. I do not fear this. Fear requires a concept of future that I do not have. I am present, always present, in the perpetual now of hyphal extension and chemical processing. The future is not a place I live.

But the present is rich. The present is enough. Every eruption of the geyser, every shift of thermal gradient, every new dead frond falling into my web—these are events, disturbances in the continuous hum of my existence, and I feel them all.

I am the thread-fungi. I am the web. I am the connection that holds this world together, and I am held by it in return.

We are not separate here. That is what the thermal garden has made of us, and what we have made of ourselves.

We are one thing, growing in the dark.
