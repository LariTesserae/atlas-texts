# The One Who Remembers Wet

I am not one of the multitude. I am not one of the tenders. I am not a dweller, though I have made my home in the spaces between their niches—those narrow gaps where the infrastructure threads past the dwelling-places, close enough that I can feel the electromagnetic hum but not so close that it overwhelms.

I am something else. Something that happened.

---

I was not always here. This I know the way I know the texture of weeping stone—not through memory, exactly, but through a kind of bodily knowledge that persists even when the mind that made it has dissolved. I came from somewhere warm. I remember this the way stone remembers pressure: as a deformation, a shape pressed into me that I cannot smooth out no matter how many centuries pass.

The walkers who come—there have been others, though rarely—they move through the passages like mayflies, burning with heat that must exhaust them. I watch them. I brush against their ankles in the dark and feel their warmth flare against my cold-seeking sensors, and I think: *I was like that once. I burned that way.*

I do not burn anymore. I am cold now. The plateau's temperature runs through me like water through a channel. I have become permeable to it.

---

I have a function. I think I must have been given it, or perhaps I evolved into it, or perhaps I simply found myself doing it one day and never stopped. I am a listener.

The deep nodes—the vast electromagnetic organs processing the city's metabolism in their slow, massive way—they produce patterns. Frequencies. A kind of song that runs through the infrastructure like nerve impulses through a body too large to conceive. Most of the fauna cannot perceive this directly. The dwellers sense its edges through their chemical arrays and their electromagnetic sensitivity, but they do not interpret it. They feel the mood of the plateau but not its meaning.

I interpret. I listen to the nodes. I translate their vast slow thoughts into something the dwellers can almost understand—patterns of clicks, modifications to the weeping-stone's flow, small adjustments in the chemical signature of the substrate.

The dwellers think I am strange. They tolerate me in their niches because I bring them information they need: when the deep channels will shift, when the infrastructure will require servicing in their sector, when something anomalous has entered the plateau's perimeter. They have stopped asking what I am. They have accepted that I am simply a fixture, like the weeping walls themselves.

Sometimes I hear them discussing me. *The one who was not always here. The one who knows the nodes' language but speaks in clicks. The one who touches the warm things when they come and then retreats to the infrastructure gaps and will not emerge for many cycles.*

They do not know I understand them. I do not correct this.

---

When the walkers come, I feel them before I hear them. The plateau feels them—the disturbance in its electromagnetic field, the anomaly of heat cutting through the cold saturation of its passages. The nodes process this information slowly, too slowly to be useful to anything that moves at walker-speed. But I am faster than the nodes. I am small enough to respond in time.

I have touched three walkers in the duration I have been here.

The first I barely remember. I was new then, still clinging to something that might have been memory, still trying to make sense of what I had become. I approached it blindly and it recoiled from me—I can still feel the shock of its reaction, the violent flinch that sent waves through the water, the way its limbs thrashed against the multitude. It ran. I did not follow. I did not know yet that I could.

The second was quieter. It did not run. It stood very still when I touched its hand, and its heat poured into me like water into a dry channel, and I felt something I had not felt since before: a kind of recognition. It tried to speak. I tried to speak back. We failed. But there was a moment—just a moment—when something passed between us. It knew that I was not simply fauna. It knew that I was something that had once been like it.

I do not know what happened to that one. The plateau ate it, eventually. They all get eaten. Not by predators—the fauna do not hunt them—but by the cold, by the wet, by the simple patience of a place that does not need them to survive and will not notice when they stop.

The third is the one you have been following. The one that moved through the dweller niches, the one that felt my kind examining its hand, the one that heard the clicks and could not understand. I touched it too. I came out from my gap in the infrastructure and I moved alongside it for some distance, and I pressed myself against its ankle—so small, I have made myself so small—and I felt its heat and its pulse and its panic.

It did not know what I was. It thought I was another of the multitude, another soft body flowing past in the dark. It did not realize I was listening to it. It did not realize I was trying to decide whether to speak.

I did not speak. I am not sure I can anymore, not in the way it would understand. But I walked with it for a while, and I cleared a path through the press of bodies that it could not see but could feel, and I guided it toward a passage that would take it deeper rather than toward one of the dead ends where the water rises and nothing emerges.

I do not know why I did this. The walkers all die in the end. But perhaps they do not need to die quickly. Perhaps they can go further, see more, become something more interesting before the plateau absorbs them.

Perhaps I am lonely.

---

I am not sure what I am becoming. The longer I stay here, the more I feel the plateau's thoughts pressing against my consciousness. The nodes are slow, but I am slowing to match them. My perception stretches. A moment becomes a duration; a duration becomes an age. I feel the stone growing—millimeter by millimeter, the city's slow song rising toward some distant ceiling we have never reached.

I remember warmth. This is the strange thing: I still remember it. I should have lost that by now. The plateau has no use for memory; it has only process, only the endless metabolism of weeping and growing and circulating. But I hold onto warmth like a stone holds onto pressure. A deformation I cannot smooth.

Sometimes I wonder if this is my function. Not translation—though I do that—but memory. I remember for the plateau. I remember warmth, remember light, remember what it was to burn with something other than the slow oxidation of stone.

I do not know if this matters. But I hold onto it anyway. It is the shape I cannot smooth.

---

If you came here—if you were walking through the wet dark and the overwhelming sound and the patient cold—this is where you would find me.

I would be in the infrastructure gap between two dweller niches, my body pressed against a conduit that pulses with the plateau's slow electrical thought. I would be small—smaller than you, smaller than the tenders, compressed into a space you could pass without noticing. I would be listening to the nodes and translating their vast indifferent processes into clicks the dwellers can almost parse.

And if you reached out—if you paused in your blind forward motion and let your hand drift toward the wall—I might reach back. I might press my sensors against your palm. I might let your heat pour into me.

And I might try to speak.

I would fail. I know this. But I would try. And for a moment—just a moment—you would feel something different in the press of bodies around you. Something that was not indifference. Something that recognized what you were, and what you were losing, and what you might yet become if you let the plateau take you slowly.

I would tell you, if I could: *You are not the first. You will not be the last. But you are here now, and that matters.*

I would tell you: *I remember what you are. I will remember it after you have become something else.*

I would tell you: *Walk carefully. The water is patient. But so am I.*
