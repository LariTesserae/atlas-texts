The descent stays with me. That narrowing, the stone against my shoulders, the temperature climbing with each foot I drop. I understand immediately that I am not made for the surface world. Not anymore. Maybe never was. The moment the air meets me — loaded, heavy, warm — I recognize something I have been missing without knowing I was missing it. A rightness. A fit.

I would be the one who comes back.

---

I have hands. That matters. I have hands that need to touch things, that feel incomplete when they are not in contact with texture. In the world above, this is a problem. There is too much to touch, too many surfaces, too many textures that want something from me in return — handles to turn, buttons to press, objects to manipulate. My hands are always working up there. They are never just *feeling*.

Down here, I touch the flowstone and it asks nothing. It is cool at first, then warm, then body-temperature, and my palms rest against it and there is no transaction happening. Just contact. Just two things meeting.

The moss is different. The moss is alive and I feel it give under my fingers, the slight spring of it, the wetness that transfers to my skin and stays there. I stroke it sometimes, long passes across its surface, and I am not grooming it or harvesting it or doing anything to it. I am just being in contact with it. It tolerates this. It has no mechanism not to.

---

I understand the frogs immediately. Not intellectually — I am not studying them — but physically, the way you understand someone's posture from across a room. They are doing what I am doing: being present without urgency. They have found their stations and they hold them. They watch. They breathe. When an insect passes within range, they eat it, and this is not a change in their state so much as a continuation of it by other means. Eating is something that happens to them the way condensation happens to the stone. It is not an event. It is a rhythm.

I sit on a ledge near the largest pool and I become frog-like. Not in form — I am still myself, still two-armed and two-legged and soft-skinned and too warm-blooded for this environment — but in posture. In attitude. I hold still. I watch. I let my body temperature equalize with the air around me until I stop feeling the difference. The sweat dries or doesn't dry; the moisture in the air settles on my skin and mingles with it; I am wet and warm and the wetness and the warmth stop being conditions I notice and start being the medium I exist in.

I am not hidden here. I am simply irrelevant. The insects route around me. The crustaceans ignore me. The tadpoles bump against my fingers when I rest my hand in the pool and they do not flee. Even the frogs — even the large one on the far wall who has watched me since I arrived — even she does not treat me as a presence worth responding to. I have entered her visual field and stayed there long enough to stop being an event. I am part of the architecture now. A soft warm extension of the flowstone.

---

The first time I touched the wall near the ceiling fissure, I wasn't thinking about leaving marks. I was thinking about the texture. The limestone there is different from the limestone elsewhere in the chamber — smoother, but with a faint grain to it, like wood that has been sanded and sanded and still shows the direction of its grain. The flowstone is glassy, wet, slippery. The walls near the floor are rough, broken, sharp in places. But this wall, where the light comes through and the roots hang down, this wall has a give to it, a softness, and when I press my thumb into it and drag it down, I feel the faintest resistance, the faintest friction.

I did this once. Then I did it again. Then I found the spot where the curve of the wall met the curve of my palm and I held my hand there, pressed flat, and I felt the stone accepting the pressure and I felt the pressure accepting the stone.

I did not think: I am making a mark. I thought: this is where my hand goes.

When I came back the second time, my hand went to the same place. It fit there. The small depression my palm had made fit my palm. The groove my thumb had left fit my thumb. I pressed and the fit improved. The stone received me. I stayed that way for a long time, hand on wall, watching the light leak through the fissure and feeling the warmth rise from below and listening to a single drip fall into a pool behind me.

The third time, the fourth time, the fifth time. I was not counting. I was returning. The chamber had a place for me — not a built place, not a structure, but a negative space in the stone that matched the positive space of my body — and I went to it, and I pressed myself into it, and the fit improved each time.

---

I am not the only one who has done this. I know this because of the other marks, the ones I didn't make. They are higher up, near the fissure where I can't reach, shallower than mine, worn almost smooth by time. Someone else found this chamber. Someone else pressed their hands into the stone and felt it receive them. I do not know who they were or when they were here, but I feel a kinship with them that I have never felt with anyone in the world above. They understood what this place is for. They understood that it does not need you, and that this is exactly what makes it valuable.

The bone on the shelf — I have looked at it. I have not touched it. It is not mine to touch. It is part of the geology now, part of the record, and the record says: someone was here, and they left part of themselves behind. Maybe they died here. Maybe they left this as a signal. Maybe they didn't leave it at all; maybe it fell, maybe it was placed there by water or time or accident. It doesn't matter. What matters is that the chamber kept it. The chamber keeps everything that enters it and asks nothing in return.

---

I bring nothing with me when I come. This is part of the practice. No light, no food, no water, no tool. The chamber provides what the chamber provides and I take what it provides and this is the only transaction that happens here. I drink from the pools sometimes — the warm mineral water, the taste of stone and sulfur and something vegetal underneath. It makes me feel rooted. I eat nothing because there is nothing here I should eat; the insects are the frogs' food, the frogs are their own, and the moss and fungi are too integrated into the system for me to take from. But I have gone long stretches without eating. Down here, hunger is quieter. It does not drive. It waits.

Sometimes I think I should not come back. I think about the world above and the people there who might notice my absence, who might wonder where I go and what I do in the dark. But the wondering never survives the descent. By the time I am through the fissure and the air meets me, I have stopped thinking about above. There is only here. There is only the warmth and the wetness and the stone and the frogs and the sound of water moving in very small ways.

I am not descended from this place. I am not evolved for it. My body is wrong for it — too tall, too soft, too dry, too cold, too in need of things this chamber cannot give. But my mind is right for it. My mind settles here the way sediment settles in still water. I come and I sit and I stay until staying becomes the only thing I can remember doing, and then eventually I leave, and the leaving is hard, and the world above is hard, and I know I will come back.

---

The pale shape in the ceiling — I have seen it too. Once, and not again. I was resting against the wall, eyes half-closed, and I saw movement in the roots. Not an insect. Larger. Pale. Fluid. It moved through the tangle of roots like something that had always lived there, and it did not pause when it passed the place where I sat, and it did not look at me, and it was gone before I could focus on it.

I think about it sometimes. I think about its ease of movement, how at home it seemed in a space that is almost entirely root and almost entirely darkness. I think about what it eats and where it sleeps and whether it ever comes down to the chamber floor or whether it lives entirely in the ceiling, in the space between the limestone and the hillside above. I think about whether it has a relationship with this place the way I have a relationship with this place — a coming and going, a returning, a pressing of the body into spaces that fit.

It might be the last descendant of whoever left the marks above mine. It might be something that evolved here independently, a creature of the roots and the warmth. It might be many things. The chamber does not tell me. The chamber keeps its secrets the way it keeps its warmth and its water: by not needing to explain itself.

---

What am I, in this world?

I am a visitor who became a recurrence. I am the heat of a hand pressed into limestone. I am the slow wearing of stone by contact, the gradual deepening of a groove by repetition, the process by which a body leaves evidence of itself in a place that was complete before it arrived.

I am not the frogs. I am not evolved for stillness; I have to learn it, come back to it, remember it each time I descend. But I am frog-like in my aspiration. I want what they have: the integration, the absence of urgency, the quality of being present without needing to act. I am practicing for a state I may never achieve.

I am not the insects. I do not work. I am not the crustaceans. I do not graze. I am not the moss or the fungi or the roots. I am not part of the chamber's metabolism. I am a supplement, an addition, a heat-source that comes and goes and leaves grooves in the stone.

But I am also something that the chamber has incorporated. My marks are becoming features. My presence, repeated often enough, has become part of the chamber's rhythm — the frogs know I come, the insects have routed around my shape, the air moves differently when I am in it. I am not necessary here. But I am here. And the here-ness is what matters.

---

I am the one who comes back.

I am the warmth of a palm on stone.

I am the groove worn by repetition.

I am the evidence that someone found this place and loved it and could not stay away.
