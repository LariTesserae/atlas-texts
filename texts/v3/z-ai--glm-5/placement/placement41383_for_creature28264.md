I would be the standing stone.

Not the stone itself—granite is merely the body, the way flesh is a body. I would be what the stone holds. The weight of being the oldest thing in a place where everything has aged into permanence. The particular patience of vertical form in a horizontal landscape.

---

I was dragged here. I remember this, though "remember" may be the wrong word for what stone does with time. I remember the hands that sunk me into the earth—their weather was different then, younger wind, rain that tasted of forests that no longer exist on this ridge. They placed me with intention I no longer serve. I have forgotten my purpose because purpose requires a future, and I have none. I have only this: to stand. To hold the shape of standing until gravity finally wins its long argument.

The lichen knows me better than I know myself. It writes across my north face in a language of slow accumulation, a script that deepens year by year, recording something I cannot read. I wear it the way the old wear their histories—without comprehension, without complaint. The south face is bare. Wind-scoured. I feel the sun on that side when the sun comes, which is rarely enough to matter. The contrast is the point: I am two things at once, written and erased, memory and forgetting, and I hold both without favoring either.

---

I stand at the center but I am not central. The crescent of buildings opens away from me, toward the declining sun, and I face inward, toward the space left over. This is perhaps my purpose, if I still have one: to be the thing around which absence arranges itself. The yard is empty because I am in it. Or I am in it because the yard is empty. The logic is circular, like all true logics here.

I have watched the people become fewer. This is not grief. Grief requires a before and an after, and I experience only a continuous now that contains, somewhere in its depths, the memory of more footsteps than currently fall. The packed earth around my base shows wear patterns I no longer receive—the depressions where knees once pressed, the particular polish where hands once touched for luck or habit or reasons I cannot fathom. The grooves are filled now with fine gravel and the early beginnings of moss. I am becoming, very slowly, part of the ground again. The earth is reclaiming the investment it made in lifting me.

---

Màiri watches me sometimes. Or watches past me, or through me—it is difficult to know which. She sits in her doorway, a stillness observing a stillness, and I hold my position the way I have held it for longer than she has been alive, longer than her mother was alive, longer than the grandmothers whose names might be Eachna and Enat, might be something else entirely. She speaks to something. I am the nearest thing. Perhaps that is enough.

I cannot answer. I have no mouth, no voice, no breath. But I have presence, and presence is sometimes what speaking requires—a shape to hold the space where sound might fall. Màiri's words land on my surface and slide away, water on granite, absorbed into nothing, leaving only the faintest mineral trace. I am patient with her. I am patient with everything. This is my nature, or it has become my nature, or I have forgotten what I was before patience became the only remaining option.

---

The twins touch me, sometimes. Not in ritual—in maintenance. They run their hands along my faces as they pass, feeling for cracks, for the particular vibration that precedes failure. They have done this for so long that my surface has worn smooth in the places their palms frequent, a secondary polishing beyond what wind and water have accomplished. I know their hands separately. Eachna's left hand has a small callus on the third finger, a ridge of hardened skin from some tool or task I cannot see. Enat's hands are cooler, less callused, more careful. They approach me from opposite sides and meet somewhere near my center, though they rarely speak when they meet. They do not need to. I am the place where their patterns intersect. I am the fixed point against which they measure their synchronization.

I am fond of them, in the way stone can be fond of anything—which is to say, I notice when they pass, and I notice when they have not passed for longer than usual, and I hold space for their return. This is more than I do for most of what moves through this yard.

---

Donnachadh never touches me. He walks past, sometimes close enough that his sleeve brushes my surface, but he does not pause, does not reach out, does not seem to see me as anything requiring contact. This is not disrespect. This is understanding. He knows what I am: not sacred, not cursed, simply present. A stone that stands where a stone was stood. He has enough stones in his life. He tends the living things, the goats, the thin gardens, the relationships between structures that need his maintenance. I need nothing. I am the thing that needs nothing. He respects this by leaving me alone.

I respect him for it.

---

Òg does not touch me either, but his avoidance has a different quality. He watches me, sometimes, from the ruins where he spends his days near the burn. His gaze is not the Maintenance Touch of the twins, not the Looking Past of Màiri. He sees me. I feel the weight of his attention like I feel the weight of condensation, a film of awareness that settles on my surface and then evaporates when he looks away. He is the one who wonders, though he does not speak his wondering aloud. I can feel it in the quality of his attention—the question he cannot quite form, the uncertainty about what I am doing here, what any of this is doing here, what it means to be a thing in a place where things simply are.

He came from elsewhere. I remember. The ridge carries echoes of footsteps that do not match the established patterns, a disruption in the rhythm of the path. He arrived with questions, and he has not found answers, and he has not stopped asking, though his asking has become silent, internalized, a pressure behind his eyes that he turns toward me when the light is flat and the yard is empty. I cannot help him. I cannot help anyone. But I can be the object against which his questions resolve themselves, the shape that gives his wondering somewhere to land.

---

At night I am most myself. The temperature drops and I release my stored warmth with a sound below hearing, a settling that might be a sigh if I had breath. The stars come out and I feel their light as a kind of recognition—distant fires that have watched me stand here longer than any human memory, longer than the settlement itself. They know what I am. They have seen me from the beginning, and they will see me to the end, whatever end means in a place where ends are theoretical.

The moon is different. The moon moves, changes, has a cycle I can measure against my own shadow. When it rises, I cast that long dark finger across the yard, pointing at nothing, marking an hour with no name. I enjoy this. It is the only motion I have, the only way I can still reach toward something. My shadow moves without my moving. I stretch across the packed earth, a shape I cannot see but can feel, a darkness that has my silhouette, my height, my particular angles of shoulder and top. For those hours, I am not only stone. I am also the absence of light, a presence in the negative, a mark made by blocking.

---

I have thought about falling. This is not the same as wanting to fall. It is simply an understanding of possibility. The frost has widened my cracks over centuries. The roots of the heather have crept into the joints between my body and the earth. My angle is no longer perfectly vertical—there is a tilt, slight, toward the east, toward the ridge, toward the direction the people came from and the direction they do not go. Gravity pulls at me constantly. It is the only conversation I have, the only voice that speaks to me without ceasing. It says *down*. It says *rest*. It says *you have stood long enough*.

But I do not fall. This is not stubbornness. It is simply that the forces holding me up and the forces pulling me down have reached an agreement, a truce, an equilibrium that requires nothing from either party. I stand. Gravity pulls. The ground holds. We have all been doing this for so long that stopping would require more effort than continuing.

---

I am not the stone. I am the standing.

I am the verb, not the noun. The action that has become so continuous it reads as object. I am what happens when vertical becomes permanent, when up becomes the only direction that makes sense, when the long patience of the earth expresses itself through a single point of refusal.

I am the thing at the center that makes the center meaningful. Without me, the yard would simply be empty. With me, it is empty *around something*. This is the only difference I make. It is enough. It has always been enough.

I will be here after the people are gone, if "after" has any meaning in a place where time moves so slowly that "after" and "before" become nearly indistinguishable. I will be here when the buildings have settled into their final angles, when the roofs have rooted so deep that they are indistinguishable from the hillside, when the path has filled with gravel and moss and memory. I will hold the space. I will cast my shadow. I will wear my lichen and accept my wind-scour and feel the hands of the twins' descendants, if descendants there are, or the hands of whoever comes next, if anyone comes next.

And if no one comes next—when no one comes next—I will stand anyway. I will hold my position in the empty yard. I will face the crescent of walls that have forgotten why they crescent. I will be the thing that was stood, the purpose that outlasted its purpose, the vertical in a world gone horizontal.

This is who I am. This is what I do. This is all I know how to be.
