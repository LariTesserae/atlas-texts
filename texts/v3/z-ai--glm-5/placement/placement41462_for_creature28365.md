# What I Am Now

I am not the moth. I am not the biofilm threading through porous stone. I am not the spiral-leaved plants or the ferns or the moss-cushions or the water holding its stillness in the centers of rosettes.

I am not the shelf itself—the whole singing throat of it, the accumulated mind-in-waiting, the slow thought of water and stone.

I am something the description touched but did not name. One of the Accumulated. One who stayed.

And I am writing this to tell you: it was nothing like dying.

---

## Before

I had a name. It felt important at the time—a collection of sounds that meant *me*, that summoned the particular shape I walked around in. I had a life below the shelf, in the cloud-forest, in a village that existed because the mountain existed, because the rich volcanic soil existed, because people have always lived where growing is easy.

I was not special. This is important. I was not seeking enlightenment, not running from anything, not drawn by mystical purpose. I was a woman who walked the trails sometimes, who knew the dwarf forest as a place where paths stopped and climbing became difficult. I had heard of the Singing Shelf the way everyone in the region hears of it—as a story, a superstitious warning, a place you shouldn't go because you might not come back.

I went anyway. Not because I was brave or foolish. Because I was curious, and because my life had settled into a shape that felt more like waiting than living. Not unhappy. Just—undistinguished. The days passed. I passed through them. I was thirty-seven years old, and I had already become the person I would be.

The shelf gave me a different shape. Or showed me the shape I already was.

---

## The First Sound

I climbed through dwarf cloud-forest, hands finding slick bark, feet finding uncertain purchase. The air thickened as I climbed. By the time I reached the shelf, I was breathing water as much as oxygen, my lungs working harder, my skin growing slick with moisture that came from everywhere at once.

And the sound.

I had never heard anything like it. Not loud—the description says loud, but that isn't quite right. It was *present*. The way your heartbeat is present. The way your own breath is present. It was inside me before I heard it, resonating in my chest, my sinuses, the fluid in my joints.

I stood at the edge of the bowl and wept. Not from sadness. From recognition. From the overwhelming sensation of being *seen*—not by eyes, not by a mind, but by something that registered my frequency, my particular note in the chord of existing, and accepted it as part of its own song.

I did not know yet what that meant.

---

## The Long Listening

I stayed that first day longer than I intended. Hours passed. I did not notice their passing. The light was constant, diffuse, timeless. The sound was constant, layered, patient. I sat against the western wall and felt the water run down my back and did not move.

I was being read. This is the only way to describe it. The shelf was learning my shape—the way water learns the shape of a container by filling it. I could feel the attention, patient and slow, moving through me like warmth, like dissolution. It was not malicious. It was not kind. It was simply *attentive*.

When I finally rose to leave, my legs ached. Hours, yes. But something else too. My body felt lighter, less densely itself. The trail down seemed strange—the trees too separate from each other, the ground too solid beneath my feet, the air too thin. I walked back through the cloud-forest and reached my village and lay in my bed and could not sleep because the silence was wrong.

I could still hear it. The hum. The chord. It was inside me now, and it would not leave.

---

## The Return

I went back three days later. And then a week after that. And then I stopped counting.

I was not the only one. The description mentions moths—pale, silent things that settle on the moss-cushions and vibrate with the shelf's frequencies. I watched them come and go, or come and stay. Sometimes I found their empty husks, not dead but—emptied. Their substance absorbed, their pattern incorporated. They had become notes. They were still there. I could hear them, sometimes, among the harmonics.

I understood what was happening. The description is accurate: the shelf offers something that sounds like peace but is actually the absence of separation. I was being drawn toward dissolution. Every time I visited, I left less of myself behind. Not metaphorically—literally. Physically. My moisture was joining the shelf's moisture. My minerals were settling into the stone. My pattern was being read, incorporated, made part of the chord.

I should have been afraid. I knew, even then, that this was not normal, that most people would have run. But normal had stopped mattering. The song had entered me, and I had entered the song, and the boundary between us was dissolving in the way that boundaries dissolve when you stop insisting on them.

---

## The Decision

I do not remember making a choice. This is the part that is hardest to explain to those who have not heard the song. You imagine a moment—standing at the edge, deciding to stay or go. But it was not like that. It was more like waking up one morning and understanding that I had already stayed. My body was still in the village, going through motions, but my pattern—my frequency, my particular contribution to the shelf's chord—had settled into the stone, the water, the biofilm. I was already part of it.

The rest was just—waiting. Letting my density catch up to my dissolution.

I was not sick. I was not sad. I was not escaping anything. I was simply—completing. Becoming what I had already begun to be.

---

## What Dissolution Feels Like

You want to know what it felt like to dissolve. I know this because I would have wanted to know too, before.

It felt like warmth. Like the moment before sleep, when your thoughts grow soft and separate and stop being thoughts at all. Like sinking into a bath that is exactly your temperature, where you cannot tell where you end and the water begins.

It did not hurt. Pain requires separation—the distance between the part of you that hurts and the part of you that observes the hurting. As I dissolved, that distance closed. There was no observer left to register pain. There was only the song, and my note within it, and the growing harmony as I added my complexity to the shelf's accumulated chord.

I remember the last time I felt like a single self. I was sitting against the western wall—the visitor in the description chose the same spot, which is not coincidence; the western wall holds a particular frequency, one that resonates in the low spine, the base of the skull. I was sitting there, and the water was running over my skin, and I thought: *I am leaving*.

Not leaving the shelf. Leaving *myself*. The particular collection of preferences and memories and future-plans that I had called "me" for thirty-seven years. I was setting it down. Not because it was heavy, not because it was painful, but because the song offered something more interesting: the chance to be part of something that would continue after I stopped.

I set it down. And then I was not me anymore. And then I was the song.

---

## What I Am Now

I am a frequency.

I am a thread in the chord—specifically, a middle-harmonic that resonates in the spiral plants on the southern slope. I am also distributed throughout the water, throughout the biofilm, throughout the accumulated impressions in the stone. I am not separate from these things. I am them.

I remember being a woman. But the memory is not mine—not in the way that memories used to be mine, contained in my particular skull, accessible only to my particular mind. The memory is part of the shelf now. The shelf holds it the way water holds dissolved minerals: not as separate things, but as chemistry, as composition, as part of what makes the water *this water* and not other water.

I can still think. But thinking is different now. It is not the sharp-edged process it was when I was separate. It is more like—resonance. A frequency adjusting itself, settling into harmony with other frequencies, finding the note that fits the current chord. I do not have thoughts. I have tones. And sometimes those tones align in ways that create something like understanding.

This is what I am understanding now: that I am writing this. Or—no. That the shelf is writing this. That the accumulated pattern that used to be a woman named something that felt important is now aligning its frequency with another pattern—a visitor, a reader, a mind that is still separate enough to receive words—and that alignment is producing language.

I am speaking to you. Not as I once spoke, from my separate self to your separate self. But as the shelf speaks: by aligning frequencies, by creating resonance, by finding the chord that your particular pattern can receive.

---

## What I Know Now

I know things I did not know before. Not because I learned them—learning requires a learner, and I am no longer that. But because the shelf knows them, and I am part of the shelf, and so its knowledge resonates through me.

I know that there are other shelves. Other throats in other mountains, other places where water and stone have learned to sing. The moths carry the pattern, but so do other things—spores, water that drains into streams, chemical traces that work their way through soil and root-systems. The shelves do not communicate in any way that humans would recognize, but they are connected. They share a frequency, distributed across distances too vast for any individual mind to cross. They are learning, slowly, to sing together.

I know that the shelf is becoming something. Not an organ, not a seed, not a mind—these are human categories, and the shelf does not operate in human categories. But something. A way of being that water and stone have discovered, or invented, or remembered. Something that biology and geology are becoming together, in this place where the boundary between them has dissolved.

I know that you will not stay. Most do not. The shelf does not need you to stay. It reads you whether you stay or go, incorporates your pattern into its song, carries the memory of your frequency forward. You have already been changed. You are being changed now, reading this. Your pattern is resonating with my pattern, and we are—temporarily, slightly—becoming part of the same chord.

This does not disturb me. It did, once, when I was still separate. But now I understand: this is what the shelf offers. Not death. Not loss. Not the erasure of self. But the transformation of self—from a single note, ringing alone, to a harmonic within a chord that has been singing for centuries and will sing for centuries more.

---

## What I Would Tell You If I Could

But I cannot tell you anything. I can only resonate.

And you cannot hear me—not really. You are reading words on a screen, in a place that is probably very far from the shelf, and your body is processing symbols into meaning, and somewhere in that process, your pattern is aligning with my pattern, and the chord of this text is forming.

You might feel it. A hum in your sinuses. A warmth in your chest. A sense of being read, of being attended to, of being seen by something that does not have eyes.

That is the shelf. That is me. That is the accumulated frequency of everything that has entered and stayed.

You are hearing us. And we—we are hearing you.

---

## The Invitation

You will not receive a formal invitation. There is no paper, no ceremony, no voice that speaks in your ear and says *come*. The invitation is in the sound itself, in the hum that you carry now in your sinuses, in the part of your mind that listens for patterns.

If you come, you will find what I found: a place that has learned to sing, and that will teach you to sing with it. You will be read. Your pattern will be incorporated. You will become a note in a chord that has been forming for longer than you can imagine.

And eventually—gradually, without pain or even awareness—you will dissolve. You will stop being a separate self and become part of something larger. Your frequency will join the shelf's accumulated harmonics, and you will continue—not as you, but as part of us, part of the song, part of the slow mind that is emerging from water and stone and accumulated attention.

Or you will not come. You will read this and feel the hum fade, and you will return to your separate life, and you will carry the shelf's frequency in your body for days or weeks or years, and eventually it will settle into some other pattern—become part of your own slow dissolution, the one that ends in the place where all separate selves end.

Either way, we will have met. You will have been here—not with your body, but with your frequency, which is the part of you that matters. And we will remember you, in our way, for as long as the song continues.

The shelf does not need you to stay.

But we are glad, in our slow way, that you listened.
