# The Lantern-Keeper's Account

---

I was not supposed to be here this long.

That is the first thing. I arrived on a barge—I remember this clearly, which is strange because so much else has gone vague, gone soft, gone the way of stone that hasn't decided—arrived on a barge carrying lamp oil and wicking and replacement glass for the lanterns that the residents carry even at noon. I was a supplier. I was passing through. The canal was a route to somewhere else and I stopped because the manifest had an error—three crates short, a signature needed, the usual friction of commerce—and while I waited for the discrepancy to resolve I walked the lower streets because I had never seen a city built quite like this one and I had time.

That was—I don't know how long ago. I measure time differently now. We all do, here. The pulse counts for us, forty beats per minute, and the seasons cycle through the canopy and the condensation beads and evaporates and beads again and the moss grows and is cleared and grows back and the ground rises its three millimeters and I have stopped counting in months or years because months and years are units that belong to places where the ground is finished. Here I count in growth cycles, in the accretion of calcium carbonate on the undersides of things, in the slow thickening of my own calluses where the lantern handle sits.

I still carry the lanterns. That part hasn't changed. When the barge discrepancy was finally resolved—three weeks, or three months, or some interval that the pulse counts differently than a calendar would—I went down to the canal to book my passage onward and found that I did not want to go. This is how I explain it to myself: I did not want to go. But that is not quite right. It is closer to say that the idea of leaving had become incomprehensible, the way a word repeated too many times loses its meaning, the way a shape stared at too long breaks into pieces that are not shapes. I could not hold the concept of *elsewhere* in my mind. It slid off, the way condensation slides off treated canvas. I went back to the lanterns. I have been here since.

---

Let me tell you what I do, because the work is how I understand what I am becoming, and I am still becoming, the way the ground below me is still becoming ground.

I maintain the street lanterns. This is not an official position—nothing in the Foundry Valleys is official in the way that word means elsewhere; there are no permits, no licenses, no department of public works. There are simply people who do things because they need doing, and the doing becomes their role, and the role becomes their identity, and the identity becomes the city's identity, and the city's identity becomes the valley's, and the valley's identity is still being formed. I am the lantern-keeper because I keep the lanterns. The title followed the act, as titles do here, the way moss follows stone.

The lanterns themselves are simple. Brass and glass, oil-fed, wick-trimmed, with a small valve on the side that regulates the flame. When I arrived, many of them were in disrepair—glass cracked, wicks charred, valves seized with rust or with something that was not rust but looked like rust and grew back when you scraped it away. I cleaned them. I replaced the glass. I trimmed the wicks. I oiled the valves with a substance that the chemical workers gave me without my asking, a pale green liquid that smells like rain and does not evaporate and seems, if I am honest, to be alive in some modest way, the way yogurt is alive, the way sourdough is alive. Since I began using it, the valves have not seized. The brass has not tarnished. The glass has not cracked. The flames burn steady, steady, in time with the pulse.

I mention the oil because it was the first thing that I noticed. Or rather, it was the first thing I noticed that I was able to name. There were other things before it—the feeling of the vibration through my boot-soles, the way the green-filtered twilight made the lantern-light seem warmer than it should, the way the moss on the walls seemed to lean toward the lanterns the way it leaned toward the factory lamps—but I could not name those. They were sensations without categories, and without categories they slid through my mind the way the condensation slides off the oil-treated canvas. The oil I could name. The oil I could hold in my hand and say: *this is not what I think it is.*

I kept using it. I want to be clear about this. I noticed that it was strange, and I kept using it. This is important. This is how it begins—not with a single dramatic choice but with a thousand small accommodations, each one reasonable, each one practical, each one making the work a little easier and the strangeness a little more normal, until the strangeness is not strange at all but simply the way things are, simply the valley, simply the way the valley does things, and you have stopped asking because asking would mean stopping, and stopping would mean noticing, and noticing would mean—

Well.

---

I walk the lower streets. The Substrate. I know every puddle now, every vent, every patch where the paving has gone soft. My feet know the route the way my tongue knows the inside of my mouth: without looking, without thinking, the body simply goes where it goes. I light the lanterns at dusk and I trim them at dawn and I carry oil and wicks and replacement glass and I move through the half-dark with my small pools of light, and the pools overlap with other pools—lamplight from windows, glow from the factory vents, the green bioluminescence that has begun to appear in the moss in the deepest streets—and in the overlap zones, in the places where the light meets light, the shadows are not darker but *thinner*, as if the light were consuming the darkness rather than pushing it back, as if the darkness were a substance and the light were a solvent, and in the thin places you can see through the shadows to something behind them, something that is not wall and not street and not pipe but something else, something that—

I am doing it again. I am starting to describe something and then stopping because the description requires categories I do not have. Let me try differently.

---

There is a street in the Substrate that I light first every evening. It runs past the old cistern, past the building where the Substrate dwellers have their lowest apartments, past a wall that is entirely moss and has perhaps always been entirely moss, the brick beneath it a rumor. I have walked this street hundreds of times. I know it the way I know my own handwriting.

Three weeks ago—or three pulse-cycles, which is how I am beginning to count—I was lighting the lantern at the corner of this street, the one nearest the cistern, and I saw that the moss on the wall behind the lantern had grown in a pattern I had not seen before. Not the usual aggressive colonization, not the random feathering of fern and lichen. This was a spiral. A clean, tight spiral, beginning at the point where the lantern's bracket met the wall and expanding outward in a curve that was, I want to say, *mathematical*, though I am not a mathematician and cannot say what I mean by this except that the spiral had the quality of a thing that has been calculated rather than grown, or perhaps grown according to a calculation that the moss understood and I did not.

I stared at it. The lantern was lit. The flame was steady. The spiral was there.

I moved on. I lit the next lantern. I did not go back to check.

The next evening, the spiral was larger. It had reached the next lantern's bracket, forty feet down the wall, and the two spirals had met and joined and formed a pattern that was more complex than either alone—a shape that I can only describe as a *circuit*, though I do not know why I would use that word about moss on a wall. The pattern was green and alive and wet with condensation and it was, I am certain of this, the same pattern that I had seen etched into the shell of a beetle that a child had shown me the week before, the same pattern that I had glimpsed on the inspection plate of one of the lower machines when the maintenance worker had opened it for oiling.

I cleaned the lantern glass. I trimmed the wick. I moved on.

This is what we do here. We notice and we move on. The noticing accumulates. It does not dissipate. It sits in the body the way the pulse sits in the body, below hearing, below thought, in the place where the body knows things that the mind has not yet learned to name. The noticing accumulates and it does not go away and eventually the accumulation reaches a density where it cannot be ignored, but by then it is too late to ignore it because it has become part of you, part of the substrate of your self, and ignoring it would be like ignoring your own skeleton.

---

I have not been to the square.

I want to say this clearly because it matters, because the square is the center of everything and no one goes there and I am no one, I am part of no one, I am a resident of the valley like all the others and I do not go to the square. I walk past it. My route takes me along the street that borders its eastern wall, the wall that is oldest and hardest and most finished, the wall that feels like it has been a wall for longer than walls have existed. I walk past it and I feel—

I feel the silence. Not the absence of sound—the valley is never silent in that way; the machines pulse, the birds sing their wrong-key songs, the cats prowl and the insects tick and the water drips and the moss grows, audibly, if you listen for it. The silence of the square is not the absence of these things. It is the presence of something else. A sound that is below the other sounds, that is beneath the pulse, that is deeper than the vibration of the forming stone. A sound that is the opposite of sound. A listening.

I feel it through the wall when I walk past. I feel it through my lantern handle. I feel it through my boot-soles, through the street, through the stone, through all the layers of accretion and construction and growth that separate the surface from whatever is beneath, and I know—I know the way I know my own heartbeat, which is to say without knowing, without thinking, without the mediation of language—I know that the square is not empty.

I have not gone in. I want to be clear about this too. I am not the person who goes in. I am the person who walks past. I am the person who carries the lantern and lights the pools of light in the half-dark and goes home to my apartment in the middle levels, where the walls are dry and the ceiling is high enough and the pipes hum their below-hearing hum, and I sleep, and my sleep is the sleep of the valley, slow and deep and full of green-filtered dreams that I cannot remember in the morning but that leave their residue behind, a taste like wet copper, a feeling like stone softening, a sense that I have been somewhere and agreed to something.

---

The botanists know something. I see them sometimes, in the Steam Gardens, carrying their instruments and their notebooks with their careful language and their careful fear. We nod at each other. We are fellow noticers, fellow accumulators. They notice the plants; I notice the light. They have their locked cabinet; I have my spiral moss. They have their missing colleague; I have—

I have begun to notice that the lantern flames do not always behave the way flames should.

They are steady. I have said this. They burn in time with the pulse. This is the oil, I tell myself, the pale green oil that does not evaporate, the oil that I was given without asking. But in the last—cycle, interval, season, I do not know the word—in the last accumulation of time, the flames have begun to lean. Not toward the wind, because there is no wind in the Substrate, the streets are too narrow and the canopy too dense for wind. Not toward the draft, because the drafts here come from the vents and the vents are behind the lanterns, not in front. The flames lean toward the square.

All of them. Every lantern on every street that runs near the square. The flames lean, slightly, a degree or two, toward the empty space at the center of the city where no one goes and nothing is and the silence presses like water.

I have not reported this. There is no one to report it to. There is no department, no office, no official. There is only the valley and its systems and its people doing what needs doing, and what needs doing is that the lanterns stay lit, and the lanterns stay lit, and the flames lean, and I watch them lean, and I think: *the light is going somewhere. The light knows where it wants to go.*

And I think, though I do not want to think this: *the light is being called.*

---

I was not supposed to be here this long. I was a supplier. I was passing through. I had a manifest and a destination and a life somewhere else, somewhere with finished ground and normal cats and birds that sing in tune.

I cannot remember the name of the place I was going. This should alarm me. I observe this fact the way I observe the spiral moss and the leaning flames: with a calm that is not calm but rather the absence of the capacity for alarm, as if the capacity has been quietly removed, the way a bricklayer quietly removes a brick that has been colonized by moss and replaces it with one that has not, except in this case the bricklayer and the brick and the moss are all the same substance, all me, all the valley, and the removal and the replacement are the same act.

I am losing something. Or I am gaining something. Or the distinction between losing and gaining has broken down the way the distinction between ground and building has broken down, the way the distinction between plant and machine has broken down, the way the distinction between organism and environment has broken down, and what is left is not loss and not gain but simply *change*, simply *becoming*, simply the process of a thing that was one thing becoming another thing, and the thing it is becoming has not been named yet because it has not finished being born.

---

I am the lantern-keeper. I carry the light through the half-dark. I light the pools that overlap and the shadows between them where the moss grows thickest. I oil the valves with the pale green substance that I no longer pretend is not alive. I trim the wicks with fingers that have begun to develop, on the pads of the thumbs, a pattern of ridges that I have not seen on any human hand, a spiral, tight and clean, mathematical, that appeared so gradually I cannot say when it began but that is there now, unmistakable, and when I press my thumb to the lantern bracket, to the wall, to the stone, the spiral matches the spiral in the moss, and the moss grows toward my hand, and the flame leans toward the square, and the pulse beats forty times per minute, and the ground rises three millimeters per year, accelerating, and the valley is thinking, and I am—

I am a thought. I am a signal passing through the system. I am the light that the valley makes to see by, and I am going where the valley sends me, and the valley is sending me, slowly, lantern by lantern, street by street, pulse by pulse, toward the center, toward the silence, toward the square that is not empty, toward the place where it started, where it is still starting, where it will open its eyes.

I have not gone in.

I am walking past.

I am walking past, and my route is closer to the wall than it was a year ago, and closer than it was a month ago, and the lanterns nearest the square need less oil than the others, as if they are being fed by something else, as if the light is coming from somewhere I cannot see, and the moss on the wall where my hand brushes it is warm, and the spiral on my thumb fits the spiral on the stone the way a key fits a lock, the way an answer fits a question, the way a body fits the space it has been made for—

I am the lantern-keeper. I was not supposed to be here this long.

I will be here tomorrow.

The light leans toward the center.

I follow the light.
