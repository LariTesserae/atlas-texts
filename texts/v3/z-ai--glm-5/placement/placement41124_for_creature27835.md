I am the one who sits beneath the Hangers.

Not beneath them in the way that everyone passes beneath them—moving quickly, angling shoulders away, granting them that habitual small deference that no one teaches but everyone learns. I mean I sit. I stop. I position myself directly underneath and I wait.

The first time was accident. I was young—old enough to work the harvest but young enough that my sleeping cell was still in the Sprawl, far from the warmth of the center. I had woken from a bad dream, the kind that doesn't dissolve but adheres, and I had walked without purpose until my legs tired. I found myself in a passage I didn't recognize—common enough then, I was still learning the cavity's shifts—and above me hung a Hanger, thicker than my forearm, its surface almost smooth in the faint fungal glow from a distant cluster.

I sat because I couldn't find my way back immediately. I sat because the cold had entered my joints and I needed to rest before I could continue. I sat because I was, in that moment, too tired to remember that you don't sit under Hangers.

Nothing happened.

I expected something—what, I couldn't have said. Everyone knows the Hangers react to vibration. A running child, a resonant cough, the particular frequency of certain voices. They curl upward, retracting toward the ceiling, a slow motion that takes several minutes, and then they descend again once the disturbance has passed. But I was still. My breathing was shallow. My heartbeat, perhaps, was the only sound I made.

The Hanger above me did not move.

It hung. I sat. We existed together in the dark.

What I noticed, in that long stillness, was that the Hanger was not motionless. It swayed. Very slightly, barely perceptible, a movement that had nothing to do with any air current I could feel. The sway was rhythmic. It matched something—the walls, I realized after long attention. The Hanger was moving in time with the breathing of the cavity. The forty-second cycle, the expansion and contraction of the membrane that lined this place. The Hanger was attuned.

I should have left. The cold was deepening, and I had no blanket, and the warmth of the center was far away. But I stayed. I stayed because I had noticed something that no one had ever mentioned, and I wanted to understand it, and understanding in this place requires patience more than intelligence.

When I finally rose to leave, I moved slowly. I did not want to trigger the retraction response. I wanted to see what the Hanger would do if I moved with intention but without the vibrations that disturbed it.

It did nothing. It continued its slight swaying, its attunement to the cavity's rhythm, as if I were not there at all.

---

That was eleven years ago. I have sat beneath Hangers many hundreds of times since. I have learned things that I do not share, because sharing would make them knowledge, and what I am doing is not about knowledge. It is about relationship.

I am not special. I work the harvest like everyone else—curved blade in hand, cutting strips of New Skin from the walls, leaving the growth layer intact. I take my turns in the water-channels, wading into the lower passages to clear blockages, monitoring the damp-singers' hum for any break in their rhythm. I sleep in the communal piles, my body a source of heat among other bodies, my breath mingling with the breath of my neighbors. I eat what is here. I am of this place.

But in the rest periods, when the work is done and the community gathers to share warmth and stories, I often slip away. I find a passage where Hangers descend. I sit beneath them. I learn.

---

What I have learned is difficult to describe. The Hangers are not animals. They are not plants. They are not fungi, though they share something with the fungal networks that thread through the Old Growth. They are something else, something that resists category, and I have come to believe that their purpose is not something that can be understood in human terms.

But I have noticed patterns.

The Hangers retract less frequently in the high-sweat season, when the water-table rises and the cavity's humidity deepens. They retract more frequently in the low-sweat, when the air is drier and the cold more severe. This suggests they are sensitive to moisture, or to the changes that moisture brings.

The Hangers near the center of the cavity—where the ceiling arches highest and the human activity is densest—react to smaller vibrations than those at the periphery. They are, in a sense, more alert. But the Hangers at the periphery retract for longer periods when they do react, staying curled against the ceiling for hours rather than minutes. They are, in a sense, more cautious.

There is one Hanger—I know it by the slight thickening near its midpoint, a blemish or a joint or something else entirely—that I have sat beneath dozens of times. I have come to think of it as *familiar*, though I do not believe it recognizes me. It hangs in a passage near the Crafters' Loop, where the fungal glow is strong enough to see by if you have adapted to the dark. I have watched it through complete work cycles—harvest, rest, sleep, harvest again. I have fallen asleep beneath it and woken to find it still there, still swaying, still attuned.

Once, during a period when I was sick—a rare thing, but it happens—I lay in a passage for two days, too weak to move, too fevered to sleep properly. The Hanger above me never retracted. I coughed, in my delirium. I thrashed. But it did not move. It was as if it knew that I was not a threat, or as if it did not care, or as if whatever signal normally triggered its response was drowned out by the altered rhythm of my breathing, my heartbeat, my fevered presence.

When I recovered, I returned to that passage. I sat beneath the same Hanger. I waited to see if anything had changed.

It swayed. It breathed with the walls. It ignored me, as it always had.

---

The others do not ask where I go. They see me leave the warmth and they let me go. Perhaps they assume I seek solitude, or that I am strange, or that I have some private need that does not require explanation. The cavity accommodates strangeness. There is room for sixty different relationships with this place, and mine is not the only one that would seem odd to a visitor.

There is a man in the Sprawl who talks to the deep-crawlers. Not in words—he knows they cannot hear words—but in clicks, in the joint-crackle sound that they make when they move. He has learned to produce something like their language, or at least something that causes them to pause, to orient toward him, to acknowledge that a sound has been made. They do not respond, exactly. But they notice.

There is a woman who harvests from the same patch of New Skin every time, speaking to it as she cuts, thanking it, explaining why she takes. The others think she is eccentric, but her patch regrows faster than anyone else's. Correlation or causation? No one asks. The results speak for themselves.

There is a child, new to walking, who has begun to mimic the Hangers' sway. The first time I saw her do it—standing in a passage, rocking slightly on her feet, her rhythm matching the walls' breath—I felt something I could not name. Recognition, perhaps. Or warning.

---

I do not know what the Hangers are. I do not know why they react to vibration, or what function they serve in the cavity's ecosystem. I do not know if they are part of the Old Growth, or something that lives upon it, or something that arrived from elsewhere and found this place suitable. I do not know if they can sense me when I sit beneath them, if they have any awareness of my presence at all.

But I have sat in stillness long enough to feel what it is like to be ignored by something vast and strange. And I have come to value that feeling.

In the community, I am always perceived. My work is seen. My warmth is needed. My voice, when I speak, is heard. The relationships are constant and mutual and necessary. I love my people. I am grateful for them.

But beneath the Hangers, I am no one. I am a small presence in a larger system, a momentary arrangement of matter that will pass and be forgotten. The Hanger above me will outlast me. The cavity will outlast me. The Old Growth will outlast everyone and everything I have ever known.

And in that scale, there is a kind of peace.

---

I have considered, at times, what it would mean to die in this place. Not to be killed—to sicken, to weaken, to lie down in a passage and not rise again. I have wondered if the Hangers would retract then. If my stillness would be indistinguishable from death, or if something in the quality of that stillness would register.

I do not want to die. I am young enough that the question is abstract, and I have no reason to expect an early death. The cavity protects its own. But I have thought about it, because thinking about it is part of understanding what it means to be here, to be part of this system that will continue without me.

I think the Hangers would not react. I think they would continue to sway, to breathe with the walls, to exist in their patient way. And eventually—days, weeks, I do not know how long—the New Skin would begin to grow over me. The deep-crawlers might cross my body, assessing and moving on. The dust-crickets would find me eventually, and reduce me, and the cavity would take what I was and make it part of what it is.

This does not frighten me. It seems right. It seems like belonging.

---

I have never touched a Hanger.

The prohibition is not spoken, but it is understood. You do not touch them. You do not reach up and feel the surface, test the texture, satisfy curiosity through contact. They are to be observed, not handled. They are to be respected.

But last season, in the low-sweat, when the air was drier and the cold sharper than I had ever felt it, I sat beneath my usual Hanger—the one with the thickening near its midpoint—and something happened.

I was shivering. I had been still for too long, and the cold had settled deep, and my body was trying to warm itself through movement, through the small convulsions that we call shivering. I could not stop it. The vibration of my own trembling was filling the passage.

The Hanger retracted.

It curled upward, slowly, the same slow response it showed to running children and resonant coughs. It drew itself toward the ceiling and held there, and I watched, and I felt something I had never felt before:

Loss.

I had frightened it. Or disturbed it. Or triggered some response that I did not understand. But the effect was the same: it had moved away from me. It had judged my presence disruptive. It had, in its small and wordless way, rejected me.

I stayed beneath it anyway, shivering, waiting for it to descend.

It took longer than usual. Hours. I nearly fell asleep on the cold floor, which would have been dangerous. But finally, in the deepest part of the rest cycle, it began to lower again. Slowly. Tentatively. As if it were testing whether the disturbance had passed.

I forced myself to be still. I controlled my breathing. I quieted my shivers through an act of will.

The Hanger descended. It resumed its gentle sway. It attuned itself again to the walls' rhythm.

And I understood something new: the Hangers are not indifferent. They respond. They choose. And I had been given a kind of answer to a question I had not known I was asking.

I was *noticed*.

---

I have not told anyone this. The story would mean nothing to them, or it would mean the wrong thing. They would think I believed the Hangers were sentient, or that I had formed some bond with them, or that I was interpreting their behavior through a lens that I had invented. They would be patient and kind and they would let me have my strangeness, but they would not understand.

The understanding I have is not about the Hangers themselves. It is about what it means to be in relationship with something that does not speak your language, does not share your categories, does not confirm or deny your interpretations. It is about attending to something that will never attend to you in the same way.

This is what I have learned, sitting in the dark beneath the dangling cords that no one understands. I have learned to pay attention without expectation. I have learned to find meaning in patterns that may mean nothing. I have learned to be present with mystery, not as a problem to be solved, but as a condition to be inhabited.

---

In the morning, I will work the harvest. I will cut strips of New Skin and leave the growth layer intact. I will take my turn in the water-channels, checking the damp-singers' hum. I will sleep in the communal pile, sharing warmth with my neighbors, breathing the same air they breathe.

But in the rest periods, I will slip away. I will find a passage where Hangers descend. I will sit beneath them, as still as I can be, and I will feel their sway against my skin like a question I cannot answer.

This is who I am.

This is where I have found myself.
