# The Cultivation-Chamber

I work in the deep places. Not the root-lakes—those are for the climbers, the young ones with strong arms and better lungs. My chamber is forty meters up Pillar-Seven of Cluster-Forty-Three, carved into living tissue three human generations ago by hands I never met. The ботанос has grown around the intrusion since then, incorporating the stone-lined walls into its vascular system. When I press my palm flat against the inner surface, I can feel the sap moving. Slow. Patient. A pulse that takes four hours to complete its cycle.

The fungi grow in rows on shelves my grandmother's grandmother built from cached scree. White fibers spreading across their cultivation-beds, pale-gray knobs pushing upward, seeking the condensation that drips from the chamber's ceiling. I know each colony by heart—their growth-patterns, their preferred moisture-levels, the slight variations in color that indicate health or stress or readiness for harvest. The blue-edged ones I keep in a separate alcove, stone-sealed, handled only with the copper tongs we trade for once per decade. One spore in the lung and the paralysis takes hold within hours. We use it on arrow-tips. We do not use it often.

My name is not important. Names in Cluster-Forty-Three carry the flowering-calendar, and I was born between flowerings—a gap-child, three years after the White-Bloom, seven years before the Ash-Opening. My mother named me something that means "waiting" in the old tonal register, the one we use for histories rather than daily speech. I have waited. I am waiting still.

---

What I do is this: I listen.

Anyone can tend cultivation-chambers. The work is simple—regulate moisture, remove competitors, harvest mature colonies without disturbing the mycelial network beneath. Children learn it before they learn to climb safely. But listening is different. The fungi communicate with the ботанос through chemical exchange, through nutrient transfer, through signals carried in the very structure of their cells. And I have learned—I don't know how, perhaps simply because I have spent more time in these chambers than anyone living—I have learned to hear them.

Not with my ears. The signals are not sound in the way the твари call or the pillars resonate. But they register somehow, a pressure behind my sternum, a pattern that resolves when I am very still. I know when a pillar is sick before the channels show discoloration. I know when a fungal network has detected a parasite, has begun producing the defensive compounds that protect the botanical tissue. I know when the weather below is shifting, when storms are rising through the cloud-layer—because the fungi know, their networks carrying information faster than wind can travel.

The other cultivators in Cluster-Forty-Three think I am strange. They are not wrong. I spend too much time alone in my chamber, motionless, eyes closed. They have learned to trust my warnings, though. When I say a pillar is failing, we check the channels. When I say a parasite has been detected, we find it—often before visible damage occurs. When I say the Чешуйник are hunting in a particular direction, the hunters adjust their routes.

I am not special. I have simply paid attention for longer than most.

---

There is something else. I have not told the others. I am not sure they would believe me, and I am certain they would not understand.

The standing stones speak.

Not in words. Not in the tonal language of the people, not in the acoustic resonance of the pillars, not in any format designed for human perception. But the fungal network connects to the stones—this I have verified through years of careful observation. The mycelium grows into the rock through microscopic fissures, draws minerals from the stone's structure, exchanges something with whatever substance permeates the ancient arrangements. And when I am very quiet, when the chamber is empty and the pillar-resonance drops to its lowest register in the coldest hour before false-dawn, I can sense a signal coming through the fungal network from the direction of the nearest stone-field.

It is not continuous. It pulses. A pattern that repeats on a cycle I have not yet determined—somewhere between thirty-seven and forty-three days, depending on the season. The pulses are weak, easily missed if you are not listening. But they are there. The stones are saying something.

I have begun mapping the pattern. I carved a sequence of marks into a copper sheet—the metal we trade for, soft enough to work with stone tools, resistant enough to the constant moisture to preserve my records. Each pulse receives a mark, each silence a space. After eleven years of listening, I have filled four sheets. The pattern is not random. I do not know what it means.

---

Last season, a trade-caravan came up from below. Young traders, nervous, carrying copper and preserved fruits and cloth from the lowland weavers. They arrived at Cluster-Forty-Three during the warmest part of the cycle, when the ice-sheets melt enough to slide from the botanical surfaces. I heard them before I saw them—their voices carrying wrong, their tonal language ill-suited to the pillar-network, their words swallowed by distances they could not perceive.

They wanted fungal medicine. The below-world healers had requested a specific strain—one that grows only in cultivation-chambers where the condensation carries a particular mineral signature, found only in pillars that rise from iron-rich faults. My chamber is one of three in Cluster-Forty-Three that meets the criteria. The traders came to me.

I gave them what they asked. We do not refuse trade. But I asked them questions while we negotiated price—questions about the standing stones, about whether anyone below had records of the arrangements, about whether the lowland peoples had stories about what the stones were built for.

They looked at me as if I was mad. The stones are holy, they said. The stones are cursed. The stones are not to be spoken of by those who live in the Domain of the Silent Gods. And they left as quickly as they could, glancing back at me with the fear I have learned to recognize—fear that the plateau changes those who stay too long.

Perhaps it does. Perhaps I am no longer what I was. But I remember the below-world only as an ache, a brightness that hurts my eyes when I imagine it. I was born here. I will die here. I am content with this.

---

The Чешуйник passed my pillar three nights ago. I felt it through the fungal network before I heard its territorial call—a disruption in the signals, a scattering of awareness through the mycelial web, as if even the fungi knew to be silent. Then the call itself, vibrating through forty meters of botanical tissue, a resonant frequency that made my teeth ache and the condensation on my chamber walls tremble into cymatic patterns.

One note. A single kill. The predator had fed somewhere in the direction of the nearest standing stones.

I have been thinking about that. The Чешуйник hunt near the stones. I have tracked their calls over years—when the territorial signals come, they come from the same general direction, as if the predator ranges out from the ancient arrangements and returns to them. I do not know what this means. The Чешуйник do not build. They do not worship. They hunt, they feed, they call, they vanish. But they vanish toward the stones.

I have not told the others this either. They would think me obsessed. Perhaps I am. But I have spent thirty-seven years listening to a landscape that speaks in formats most people cannot perceive, and I have learned that patterns reveal themselves only to those who attend. The plateau is one thing. The Чешуйник is part of that thing. The stones are part of it too. And the signals coming through the fungal network, the pulses I have been mapping for eleven years—they come from the same direction where the predator ranges.

Something is happening in the stone-fields. Something that has been happening for three thousand seasons, since before the uplift. Something that the真菌 perceive, that the Чешуйник understands, that the standing stones were built to serve.

I do not know what I am waiting for. But I know—I have always known—that when it arrives, I will hear it before anyone else does.

---

I am old now. My hands shake when I harvest the delicate colonies. My children have children who tend cultivation-chambers in other pillars, other clusters. They visit when they can, which is not often. The emptiness is too large for family to bridge.

But I am still here. Still listening. Still mapping the pulses that come through the fungal network from the standing stones. The pattern has changed in the last three years—grown stronger, more frequent, as if whatever the stones were built for is drawing closer. I do not know if I will live long enough to hear what they say when they finally speak clearly.

But I have trained an apprentice. A gap-child like me, born between flowerings, drawn to the quiet work, patient enough to sit motionless for hours while I teach her what to feel for. Her name means "echo" in the old register. She is young yet. But she will listen after I am gone. She will carry the copper sheets. She will continue the mapping.

And when the stones finally say whatever they were built to say—whatever they have been waiting three thousand seasons to say—she will hear it.

This is enough. This is what it means to live inside something larger than yourself. You do not need to understand the whole. You only need to attend to the part you have been given.

I am small. I am cold. I am alive for a brief time in a place where life has become the rock on which I stand. And I am listening.
