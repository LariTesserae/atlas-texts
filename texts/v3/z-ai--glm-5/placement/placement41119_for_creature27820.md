I would be the archivist of might-have-beens.

Not by title— there is no title for what I do, and if there were, it would be the kind of word that doesn't survive the journey to the lowlands. I am simply the woman who sits in the small room behind the reference library, the one with the shelves that aren't quite square and the window that looks out onto nothing in particular because the building behind mine hasn't decided whether to be there yet.

*Havri* is a word for the residents. What I am is something else. There should be a word for the state of having been finished and choosing to become unfinished again. But that word would have to be invented, and I am not an inventor. I am a recorder.

---

I came to the Quarter the way most people do: dissatisfied with the solidity of elsewhere.

This is not a glamorous dissatisfaction. I was not fleeing tragedy or heartbreak or failure, exactly. I was fleeing the feeling that my life had already become what it was going to be. I was thirty-two. I had a career in records management in a coastal city whose name I will not speak because it knows what it is and that is precisely the problem. I had an apartment with windows that looked onto other windows. I had a schedule. I had a clear sense, each morning when I woke, of what the day would hold and how it would be different from the day before only in the details.

The details were very detailed.

I was good at my job. I was good at categorizing, at filing, at knowing where things belonged and putting them there. I had a gift for finality. When I closed a file, it stayed closed. When I organized a department, it stayed organized. My supervisor told me, in my annual review, that I had an unusual talent for creating systems that persisted after I left them. She meant it as a compliment. I took it as one. And then I went home and lay awake for three nights running, thinking about persistence, about things that stayed what they were, and I understood that I was slowly suffocating in a life that had already been filed.

I do not know why I chose the Quarter. I read about it somewhere, a brief mention in a periodical I have never been able to find since. The article did not describe the city so much as the mountain— the fact that it was still rising, still forming, that geologists estimated it would not finish its current phase of formation for another forty thousand years. Forty thousand years. I thought about that number for days. I thought about a mountain that would not know its own shape for longer than all of recorded human history. I thought about what it would mean to live in a place that had that much time to decide what it was.

I bought a ticket on the cable railway. I told myself I was visiting. I told myself I would see how the altitude treated me. I brought a single bag and my records management training and a sense that I was making a mistake, which is how I knew I was doing something important for the first time in years.

The railway took four hours to climb. I watched the lowlands recede— the farms, the roads, the towns that knew their own boundaries— and felt something loosen in my chest that I had not known was tight. When we passed above the cloud line, when the light grew thin and strange, I thought: I will stay until this feeling stops. I will stay until I understand what it is.

I have been here eleven years. I have not understood yet. I do not expect to.

---

My work is this: I keep track of what the Quarter considered being and decided against.

It began by accident. My training was in records, and when I arrived, I offered my services to the Municipal Archivist, a man named Hest who had been cataloguing the same stack of mining claims for six years because every time he thought he was finished, a new stack appeared, and he could not understand where they were coming from. They were not new claims. They were claims that had been considered and not filed, claims that existed in the potential of the soft spots, claims that materialized when someone went looking for them with the expectation of finding something.

Hest was a good archivist but a lowland man at heart. He needed certainty. He needed to know where a document had come from, who had written it, where it belonged. He could not work with papers that arrived from nowhere, that described mines that did not exist, that were signed by surveyors who had never been born. He was having a crisis of profession when I met him.

I, on the other hand, had spent my life knowing exactly where everything belonged. I understood his distress. But I also understood something else: that the Quarter's archives were trying to become, the same way everything else here was trying to become, and that the job was not to stop them but to give them a place to be.

I took over the uncatalogued stack. I read through the impossible claims and the half-existent surveys and the documents that described buildings from angles that didn't exist yet. And I realized that someone needed to keep track of them, not to file them away but to acknowledge them, to give them the dignity of having been noticed. To say: the Quarter considered being this. It decided otherwise. But the consideration was real. The potential was real. The almost was real.

I have been doing it ever since.

---

My archive is in the room behind the reference library, as I mentioned. The room has peculiar properties. The shelves shift slightly depending on what I am looking for. If I am seeking records of a building that almost stood on Carning Street, the shelf with those documents will be exactly where I reach. If I am organizing material about a street that never quite formed, the shelf will be slightly too high or too low, as though the room is reminding me that the street's nonexistence has its own logic.

I do not work alone. There are others who have found their way to similar work— not officially, not as colleagues, but as practitioners of related arts. There is a man who maps the ghost streets, the thoroughfares that appear on no survey but can be walked if you know how to look. There is a woman who sketches the buildings that almost were, architectural portraits of structures that manifested just long enough to be seen and then reconsidered. There is a child— Ves, yes, I know her— who speaks of what might be, and is right often enough that I have a shelf dedicated to recording her utterances, a growing archive of the Quarter's potential futures.

We do not meet formally. We do not have an organization. But we recognize each other. We are the ones who pay attention to the edges, to the places where the Quarter almost went and turned back. We are the ones who understand that the might-have-beens deserve witnesses.

---

What I record, specifically, is this:

The almost-streets. The Quarter's grid has changed over forty years, but there are also streets that were planned and never quite built, streets that exist in the city's intention but not in its stone. You can sometimes see them when the light is right— the suggestion of a thoroughfare between two buildings, the sense that you could walk through a wall and emerge onto a road that has its own address. I keep the records of these: where they would have run, what they would have connected, why they did not crystallize when the mountain made its decisions.

The almost-residents. This is the hardest part of my work. The might-have-been people, the ones flickering at the edges, are not easy to document. They do not have names that stick. They do not have faces that remain consistent from one viewing to the next. But I have learned to record the shape of their absence— the size of the space where a person almost stood, the quality of the light where they did not quite cast a shadow, the sense you get when you pass through a soft spot where someone is not quite present. It is a negative archive, a catalog of gaps, but I believe it matters. I believe that being almost-real deserves recognition.

The almost-events. There are moments the Quarter considered having and then declined. Conversations that were nearly held. Decisions that were nearly made. These are the most ephemeral records, and I can only capture them when they are fresh— when someone comes to me and says, I almost saw something on the eastern escarpment, I almost heard something in the singing of the rock, I almost understood something about the mountain's intentions. I record the almost. I file it under the category of what the Quarter thought about and set aside.

I do not know why I do this. I do not know who will read these records or what they will mean to anyone but me. But I have come to believe that the archive of might-have-beens is as important as the archive of what actually occurred. The Quarter is not just what it is. It is also what it considered being and what it might yet become. My work is to hold space for all of it.

---

I have my own might-have-beens, of course.

I might have stayed in the coastal city with the windows that looked onto other windows. I might have continued filing and closing and finalizing. I might have aged into the shape my life was already taking, comfortable and precise and slowly suffocating.

I might have left the Quarter after my first month, when the soft spots found me and I lost three hours walking a street I could not later locate, when I emerged into a part of the city I did not recognize and had to ask directions back to my rented room, when the woman giving directions looked at me with eyes that were not quite solid and said, "You're still here? I thought you'd gone."

I might have become someone else entirely. That is the nature of this place. Every day, I wake up and I might be a different person than I was when I fell asleep— not dramatically different, not unrecognizably different, but shifted slightly, as though my character has settled by a few degrees in the night, as though I am slightly more of what I was already becoming.

I keep an archive of my own might-have-beens, too. A private file. I write down what I almost did, who I almost was, what I almost decided. I keep it in a drawer that is slightly too small for it, so the pages press against each other, so the pressure of possibility keeps them from dispersing.

I do not know who I will be when the mountain finishes forming. I do not know if I will solidify, if I will finally become someone who knows her own shape. I suspect not. I suspect I will remain havri, temporarily permanent, a person who has learned to live in the unfinished.

I do not mind. The unfinished is where I have found myself. The filing was always a way of hiding. Here, in the archive of might-have-beens, I am not hiding. I am paying attention to what almost was, which means I am paying attention to what almost is, which means I am paying attention to what might yet be.

That seems like enough. That seems like what I came here to do.

---

If you came to the Quarter and found your way to the reference library and went to the small room behind the stacks where the shelves are not quite square and the window looks onto nothing in particular, you would find me there.

I would be at my desk, which is a desk that has not quite decided to be a desk and sometimes resembles a table or a workbench or, on days when the mountain is reconsidering its nature entirely, something that might be a door. I would be reading a document that should not exist, about a building that almost stood, on a street that was nearly laid, in a city that the Quarter considered being and declined to become.

I would look up. I would see you. I would not ask what you were looking for, because I have learned that people do not always know what they are looking for, and sometimes what they find is what they needed instead.

I would say: "I have something here that might interest you. Or it might not. It almost interests everyone."

And then I would wait, the way the mountain waits, to see what you would almost become.
