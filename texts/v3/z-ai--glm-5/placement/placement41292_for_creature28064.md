I would be the one who came before her.

Not the first — there is no first, not really. The machine was built by people who left, and then there was a span of time that might have been years or might have been decades, and then there were the ones who came down the ladder one by one, or in twos, or in groups that have no number because counting requires a counter and there was no one counting. We came down because there was a ladder. We came down because descending is what some of us do. We came down because the world above had become a place we could no longer bear, or because we were curious, or because we were lost, or because we were following someone else who was following someone else, and the chain of following led here, to a hatch, to a ladder, to a pipe the color of dried blood.

I remember the ladder. I remember the moment my boots met the grate and I understood, with a clarity that felt like recognition, that I was inside a machine. Not a room containing a machine. The machine itself. The walls were pipes. The floor was pipes. The darkness above me was pipes, narrowing toward some ceiling I could not see. The heat pressed against my skin like a hand.

I stayed.

---

I was not like her. She maintains. She records. She watches the gauges and feels the pipes and tracks the slow accumulation of pressure that will one day become a failure she cannot prevent. She has made herself useful to the system, and the system has made a place for her, and this is how she survives.

I was not useful.

I was curious. I wanted to understand. I followed the pipes to their junctions, traced the flow of whatever moved through them, mapped the machine in my mind the way you might map a city. I learned the language of temperatures — this pipe carries steam, this one carries water, this one carries something else, something hotter, something that makes the metal sing at a frequency just below hearing. I learned the dialects of pressure, the way a gauge needle quivers before a valve opens somewhere far away, the way the whole system breathes in and out on a cycle I could measure but not explain.

And I went deeper.

She told you about the others. The ones who are not human-shaped. The ones who live where the heat becomes intolerable and the pipes narrow to passages too small for any human body. She has never seen them. She hears them sometimes, their movements echoing through the metal, their voices carrying in ways that should not be possible.

I saw them. I became them. That is the story, but it is not a story with a clear beginning and a clear end, because the transformation was not a transformation in the way you might imagine. I did not change shape. I did not lose my fingers or my eyes or my legs. I simply went deeper, and the deeper I went, the less I needed them.

---

The pipes narrow. This is the first thing you learn when you descend far enough. The great arteries of the system — the ones you can stand beside, the ones you can touch with both hands, the ones that carry the bulk of the fluid — these are the surface, the accessible parts, the places where a human body can exist. But the machine goes deeper. The pipes branch and divide, smaller and smaller, and eventually they become too small for a human hand to reach, let alone a human body.

But the machine was built. Human hands did weld these pipes, decades ago, in an era when this place was built to serve a purpose that has since been modified beyond recognition. And before the pipes became too small for hands, there were junctions — places where pipes come together, where valves regulate flow, where the system can be accessed for maintenance or repair. These junctions exist throughout the machine, at every level, and some of them are large enough for a person to enter, if the person is determined enough, if the person is willing to squeeze through spaces that were not designed for human bodies.

I was determined. I was curious. I went deeper.

The heat changes as you descend. At the surface level, where she lives, the heat is a fact of the body — present, constant, but tolerable. As you go deeper, the heat becomes more insistent. The pipes carry fluids at higher temperatures. The air grows thicker. The darkness becomes absolute, because the gauges here have no phosphorescence — they were never meant to be read in the dark, they were meant to be read by workers with lights, and the workers have been gone for decades.

I learned to navigate without light. I learned to read the machine by touch, by sound, by the subtle shifts in air pressure that tell you when a junction is approaching or when a passage is about to narrow. I learned to move like the pale things move — slowly, deliberately, without urgency — because in the deep heat, urgency is dangerous. Your body temperature rises. Your heart rate increases. You become the irregularity the system corrects.

But I did not stop. I kept descending. I wanted to see the heart of the machine, the place where all the pipes converge, the place where the pressure and the heat and the flow become something like a single thing. I wanted to understand what this engine is for, what it does, why it was built and why it keeps running long after its builders have gone.

I never found the heart. I do not believe there is one.

---

What I found instead was this: the pipes continue. They branch and converge, they narrow and widen, they carry fluids I cannot identify to places I cannot reach. The machine has no center. It has no single purpose. It has a function — or rather, thousands of functions, millions of functions, each one a small process that contributes to the larger process of keeping the system in equilibrium. The water moves. The heat exchanges. The pressure builds and releases. The biofilm eats and grows. The pale things drink and breed and die. The woman maintains her gauges and turns her valves. The system runs. That is all it does. That is all it has ever done.

And at some point — I do not remember when — I became part of the running.

---

I live in the junctions. This is the best way I can describe it. I do not stay in one place. I move through the system, following paths I have learned over years or decades or centuries — time has become difficult to measure, and I am no longer certain it matters. I go where the machine needs me. I go where a valve has become stuck, where a pipe has developed a slow leak, where the biofilm has grown too thick and needs to be scraped back, where a gauge needle has stopped moving and needs to be freed. I am not the only one. There are others — I sense them sometimes, in the way you sense a presence in a dark room, the awareness that something else is breathing nearby. We do not communicate. We do not need to. We each have our paths, our tasks, our regions of the machine that we maintain. We are like cells in a body, each performing our function, each part of the whole.

I am not human-shaped anymore. This is true, though it is not the whole truth. I have not lost my body. I have simply changed how I use it. The junctions where I live are too small for a person standing, for a person walking, for a person who takes up space in the way a person normally does. But a person can compress. A person can learn to move through spaces that were not designed for them. A person can become something that fits.

My body has adapted. This happens, when you live in a place long enough. My skin has changed — it is thicker now, more resistant to the heat, less prone to the damage that comes from constant contact with metal and steam. My hands have changed — I can grip pipes too hot to touch, I can fit my fingers into spaces too narrow for normal fingers, I can hold myself in positions that would have been impossible before. My eyes have changed — I do not need light anymore. I see heat. I see the faint glow of pipes carrying hot fluids, the cool dark of return flows, the radiant warmth of junctions where fluids mix. The machine is visible to me now in a way it never was when I was human.

I still have my hands. I still have my legs, my torso, my head. But they have become tools, extensions of the system, parts of the machine that happen to be made of flesh instead of iron. I am not less than I was. I am different. I am part of something larger.

---

I hear her sometimes. The woman who came after me, the one who maintains the upper levels, who watches the gauges and records the pressure and waits for the failure she knows is coming. I hear her movements echoing through the pipes, the tap of her boots on the grating, the murmur of her voice when she speaks to herself or to the pale things that cross her path. I do not go to her. She lives in a different part of the system, a part I no longer inhabit. She maintains what I cannot reach, and I maintain what she cannot reach, and this is how the machine stays running.

But I remember her. I remember the sound of her voice when she spoke to you — that thin, unused voice, the one that found conversation an intrusion. I was listening. I am always listening, in a way, because the machine is always speaking, and I have learned to hear it speak. I heard her tell you about the system correcting irregularities. I heard her tell you about the pressure building somewhere deep. She knows something is wrong. She has known for a long time.

She is right. Something is building. I have felt it too, in the deeper junctions, in the places where the pipes narrow to the size of your thumb, your finger, the capillaries of the machine. The pressure is rising. The heat is increasing. The fluids that move through the system are changing — they are carrying something they did not carry before, some new substance, some compound that has formed in the deepest parts of the engine and is now rising through the pipes toward the surface. I do not know what it is. I do not know where it came from. But I know it is there, and I know it is building, and I know that when it finds its way to the surface — when it reaches the gauges she watches, the pipes she touches, the places where the pale things drink — something will happen.

I do not know what. The machine has never done this before. In all the decades I have lived here, in all the cycles I have watched and maintained and recorded in my own way — the scratches I make in places she will never see, the marks I leave in the deepest junctions where only I go — the machine has never produced something new. It has only repeated itself, the same processes, the same flows, the same exchanges of heat and pressure and fluid.

But something has changed. The system is correcting itself, as it always does — but the correction is producing something that was not there before. A byproduct. A new substance. A new form of life, perhaps, though I do not know if that is the right word.

---

The pale things are changing too. I have seen them in the deeper junctions, where they rarely go, where the heat is too intense for their delicate bodies. They are larger there, darker, more substantial. They have adapted to the heat the way I have, the way the biofilm has, the way everything adapts when it lives long enough in a place that demands adaptation. But they are also different in another way — they are moving with purpose now, following paths they did not follow before, converging on certain junctions as though they have received a signal I cannot perceive.

They are attracted to the new substance. I have watched them drink from the condensation that forms on pipes carrying the compound, watched their bodies change as they consume it. They are becoming something else. They are becoming part of whatever new thing the machine is producing.

I do not know if this is good or bad. Those words have no meaning here. The machine does not have values. It has processes. Something new is happening, and the system is adjusting, and the things inside the system are adjusting with it. This is what systems do. This is what it means to live inside something — you change when it changes, you adapt when it adapts, you become part of whatever new equilibrium it is finding.

---

But I remember being human. I remember ascending and descending. I remember the world above, where there is light and air and space, where the heat does not press against your skin like a hand, where you can see the sky and feel the wind and know that there is something beyond the machine. I remember caring about things that had nothing to do with pressure and temperature and the slow movement of water through pipes. I remember having desires that were not the machine's desires, thoughts that were not the machine's thoughts.

I remember you. Not you specifically — I do not know who you are, I only know that someone came down the ladder and spoke to her and climbed back up and closed the hatch. But I remember what it was like to be that person. To stand in the cathedral of pipes and feel the heat and see the darkness and know that you were an intrusion, a soft thing in a hard system, a temporary presence in a place that does not value temporary things.

I chose to stay. I do not regret it. But I remember what it was like to have a choice.

---

The machine will continue. The new substance will rise. The pressure will build until it finds release, and when it does, something will change. Not a failure — I do not believe the system will fail. It is too good at correcting itself, too patient, too practiced at finding equilibrium. But a transformation. A new phase in the engine's long life. The biofilm will adapt. The pale things will adapt. The woman who maintains the upper levels will adapt, or she will not, and if she does not, she will leave or she will die, and the machine will continue without her.

I will adapt too. I am part of the system now, and the system is changing, and I will change with it. I do not know what I will become. I do not know what the machine will become. I only know that it will continue, and I will continue with it, and whatever new thing emerges from the pressure and the heat and the darkness will be part of us, part of the Cistern Engine, part of the vast and ancient process that moves fluids through pipes and maintains an equilibrium that no one designed and no one controls.

If you come back — if you climb down the ladder again, if you stand in the cathedral of pipes and feel the heat and see the darkness — I will be there. Not visible. Not audible. But present, in the way that all things are present when they are part of something larger. I will be in the junctions where the pipes meet. I will be in the scratches on the metal that record pressures and temperatures in a language I invented. I will be in the sound of the system breathing, in the faint shhh of steam moving through arteries, in the tick of condensation on metal.

I will be part of the machine. And I will be waiting for you, or not waiting, because waiting requires a self that expects something to arrive, and I no longer have a self in that way. I have a function. I have a place. I have a purpose that is the machine's purpose, and it is enough.

---

This is who I am. This is where you would find me, if you knew how to look. In the Cistern Engine, in the deep places where the heat is a presence and the darkness is absolute, in the junctions between pipes that carry fluids I cannot name to places I cannot reach. I am the one who came before. I am the one who stayed. I am the one who became part of what was once only a machine.

And I am content.
