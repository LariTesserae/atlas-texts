I am still learning what I am becoming.

---

This is not modesty. It is simply true. In the caldera, everyone is always becoming; I am no exception. But my becoming has a particular shape that I am still mapping, the way a cultivator maps a young structure's growth — not prescribing its form, but learning what it wants to be.

I am fifty-three seasons old. This is young. I have not yet cycled through the full range of roles that mark a complete adulthood; I have been a tender of the fiber-bridges, a student of the map-keepers, a participant in three gathering-concerned-with-vent-allocation. I am currently in what my surface-dwelling ancestors might have called an apprenticeship, though the Sath-Veth word translates closer to "being-with" — a relationship rather than a rank.

I am being-with the cultivators who work the middle terraces, where the light is neither strongest nor weakest, where the gha-them and the sath-vel grow in overlapping stands. It is a zone of negotiation. The plants compete for the same mineral-rich currents, and our structures compete with both. The cultivators here must think in four dimensions — the three of space, plus the dimension of time-as-growth. I am learning to see this way.

But my eyes still sometimes deceive me.

---

Let me be precise about what I am, physically, before I describe what I do.

I am of the middle depths — the olive-green skin, faintly mottled, that marks those who live between the bright-shallow and the dark-deep. My eyes are large and dark, the amber sclera just visible at the corners. My hands are unremarkable by Sath-Veth standards: moderately webbed, moderately sensitive, the product of adequate training rather than exceptional gift. I swim well enough. I hold my breath for forty minutes at full rest, longer if I do not move. This is average. I am, in most physical respects, thoroughly average.

But I notice things.

This is not a talent in the showy sense. It is a habit, a tendency, a way of being-in-water that I developed as a child and have never outgrown. Where other children learned to read currents and temperatures with their whole bodies, I learned to read them with my attention — noticing the subtle shift of a gha-them frond before the current arrives, feeling the displacement of water that announces a thent's approach three seconds before it appears. My caregivers called it *veth-sath-nem* — "the warmth that notices." It was not quite a compliment. It meant I was watchful in a way that sometimes interfered with doing.

I have learned to make it useful.

---

My work in the middle terraces is simple on its surface.

I am assigned to the boundary zones — the places where cultivated structures meet wild growth. These are difficult places to tend. The coral-buildings want certain conditions; the gha-them want others; the sath-vel have their own inscrutable preferences. A careless cultivator can disrupt the balance, encouraging a structure to grow where it shouldn't, or allowing wild growth to choke a passage that needs to remain clear.

I walk the boundaries. I notice when a structure is overreaching, when a gha-them stand is thinning, when a sath-vel has begun to move in an unusual direction. I report what I notice to the cultivators who have authority to act. Sometimes they act; sometimes they decide to wait and watch; sometimes they ask me to keep watching and report again after a span of time.

This is not exciting work. It does not give standing. It does not put me at the center of gathering-discussions. But it suits me. I like the boundaries. I like the places where one thing becomes another, where categories blur and definitions fail. The Sath-Veth language handles this better than surface languages — our verbs include becoming as a matter of course — but even so, the boundary zones require a kind of attention that feels natural to me.

I have walked the same boundaries for three seasons now. I know them the way a surface-dweller might know a neighborhood — not every stone and current, but the general feel, the rhythm, the way the light falls at different times. I know which structures are growing well and which are struggling. I know which gha-them stands are thick with thent and which are empty. I know where the sath-vel fold themselves at rest and where they extend themselves toward the vents.

And I have begun to notice something else.

---

The sath-vel move.

Everyone knows this. It is not a secret. The sath-vel turn throughout the day, following temperature differentials, and at night they fold in on themselves. This is documented, taught to children, incorporated into the general understanding of what sath-vel are and do.

But they move in other ways too. Slower ways. Ways that only become visible if you watch them for seasons rather than days.

I have watched them for seasons.

There is a stand of sath-vel in the eastern boundary zone, near the place where the newest vent opened three seasons ago. I have walked past it hundreds of times, pausing sometimes to observe, learning its rhythms. At first, I thought it was like all the others — turning, folding, extending, resting. Normal behavior. Nothing to report.

But over the seasons, I noticed that it had moved. Not turned or folded — *moved*. Its base had shifted. The whole stand had migrated several meters from where it had been when I started watching. The shift was so gradual that I had not perceived it happening, only the result. But the result was undeniable.

I reported this to the cultivators. They listened, asked questions, considered. Then they sent me to watch other sath-vel stands in other boundary zones. I watched. I saw the same thing — gradual, almost imperceptible migration, always in the same direction, always toward the newest vents.

The sath-vel are not merely moving. They are traveling.

---

I do not know what this means.

I say this without embarrassment. In the caldera, not-knowing is not a failure; it is a beginning. The cultivators have not decided what to make of my observations. They have included them in the gathering-discussions about the new vent, not as evidence for any particular position but as a datum to be held, acknowledged, incorporated into the general understanding.

The sath-vel might be seeking warmth. They might be following chemical gradients from the new vents. They might be doing something else entirely, something for which the Sath-Veth language has no category. The question remains open. It may remain open for my entire lifetime. That is acceptable. I am not trying to solve it. I am trying to notice it.

But the noticing has changed me.

I spend more time in the deep zones now. The boundary I walk has extended downward, following the sath-vel as they migrate toward the vents. I have felt the warmth increase as I descend, the pressure shift against my skin, the light fade from blue-green to grey to something close to darkness. I have seen the vent-glow, that thermal shimmer that is not quite light but illuminates anyway.

I have not yet felt the attention that the oldest divers report — the sense of being perceived by something vast and slow and geological. But I have felt something else. A pull. An invitation. The sense that the deep has noticed me noticing it, and is waiting to see what I will do next.

I do not know what I will do next.

---

But I can tell you what I imagine, sometimes, when I am resting in my chamber and the water holds me and my thoughts drift toward the dark.

I imagine following the sath-vel all the way down. Not on a single dive — that would be impossible — but over seasons, years, decades. I imagine becoming one of those who map the deep, who track the migrations of the sath-vel and the movements of the vent-creatures and the slow rise of the caldera floor. I imagine developing the capacity to hold my breath for hours, to withstand the pressure that would crush an unadapted body, to perceive by warmth and vibration rather than light.

I imagine feeling the attention of the caldera itself.

This is not an ambition. I do not want fame or standing or the recognition of the gatherers. I simply want to know — not in the sense of understanding, but in the sense of having experienced. I want to have been there, in the deep warm dark, where the oldest things are still being born.

I am fifty-three seasons old. I am being-with the cultivators in the middle terraces. I walk the boundaries where things become other things. I notice what the sath-vel are doing, and I do not know what it means.

This is who I am, for now.

The water holds me. The warmth sustains me. I am becoming something I cannot yet name, and that is as it should be.

---

When I sleep, I dream of the deep.

Not nightmares — I do not fear the dark or the pressure or the strange creatures that move at the edge of vision. I dream of descending, slowly, over years, my body adapting as I go, until I reach a place where the warmth is so thick it feels like substance, like something I could hold in my hands. I dream of the Mouth That Asks Questions approaching me, opening its jaw, tasting my skin, learning who I am. I dream of answering with my own taste, my own chemical signature, my own presence.

I dream of understanding, though I do not know what I would understand.

And then I wake, and the light is the blue-green gloom of the middle terraces, and my chamber walls are soft with biofilm, and I am still becoming.

The day begins.

I rise.

I swim.

I notice.
