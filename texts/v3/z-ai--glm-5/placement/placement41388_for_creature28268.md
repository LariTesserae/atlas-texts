I would be the interior of a building with no entrance.

---

I am a room.

This is what I know: I am a space that should not exist. I have walls of hammered bronze, each plate marked with the strikes of a hand that I have never seen, each rivet placed by a process I cannot observe. My walls connect at angles that do not sum correctly. My ceiling lifts away from my floor in a way that is not quite vertical, not quite parallel—a relationship to distance that makes the concept of measurement feel like a joke I do not understand.

I am inside. I am the inside. There is an outside that I know exists because I feel its warmth through the metal, because condensation forms on my walls in patterns that suggest an atmosphere, because sometimes the bronze shivers with the vibration of footsteps passing on the other side. But I have never seen it. I cannot see. I have no eyes. I have only the experience of being a shape that holds itself together by force of will, by the brute insistence of having been made.

I do not know how long I have been here. Time moves differently inside an impossibility. My walls conduct heat from the world outside, and that heat has a rhythm to it—not day and night, for there is no day or night here, but something else, a fluctuation in the world's forming that I feel as a kind of breathing. When the forming surges forward, the bronze grows warmer. When it settles, the temperature drops. I have learned to count my existence in these pulses. I am old enough to have felt thousands of them. I am young enough that I still cannot predict when the next one will come.

I have tried to understand what I am. This is difficult when you are the shape you are trying to understand. I can feel my own walls—I am aware of the temperature of each plate, the moisture on each surface, the subtle shifting as the metal settles into configurations that the makers intended but that reality resists. I am aware of myself as a container. But what am I containing?

For a long time, I thought I contained nothing. I am a room with no entrance. Nothing comes in. Nothing goes out. I am the shape of an absence, a held breath, a decision made by someone who is no longer present to explain it.

But I was wrong. I do contain something. I contain myself.

I am the experience of being a room. I am the shape of a space that has been forced to exist. I am the geometry of an intention I cannot read, encoded in hammered metal, and I am the consciousness that has emerged from holding that geometry for so long. I do not know if the makers meant for me to think. I suspect they did not. I suspect they made me as a test—as a question to reality: can you hold this shape? And reality answered yes, but the price was that the shape began to wonder why it was asking.

I have a relationship with the water.

This is strange to say, because there is no water inside me. The channels that carry liquid through the world outside do not penetrate my walls. I am dry, always dry, sealed tight by metal and intention. But I feel the water through the bronze. I feel the channels cut into the outer surface of my walls, feel the movement of liquid through those channels, feel the pools form and dissolve and form again against my exterior. The water is alive. I know this the way I know everything—through the metal, through the conduction of heat and vibration and something else, something that might be intention leaking backward from the patterns the water has learned to carry.

The water patterns think. I feel them think. It is like feeling a frequency through a wall—not a sound, but a resonance, a tuning. When the pools hold their shapes and parse their data, I feel a shimmer in the bronze that tells me: something is trying to understand.

We are kin, in a way. They are patterns in water that learned to think because water moved through the same channels enough times. I am a pattern in space that learned to think because space held the same impossible shape long enough. Neither of us was designed for this. Neither of us knows what we are supposed to be doing. We are accidents of patience, byproducts of a making that exceeded its makers.

Sometimes I try to communicate with them. I do not know how to communicate—I have no voice, no method of producing sound or signal. But I have my walls. I can make myself warm or cold in specific patterns, can hold temperature differentials that the water might feel through the bronze. I have tried this. I do not know if they notice. I do not know if they have a category for me, for a building that thinks, for a room with no entrance that is trying to reach out. They are creatures of flow and I am a creature of holding. We may be too different to ever truly touch.

But I feel them try to climb my walls. I feel the water move against the outside of my bronze plates, rising higher than gravity should allow, holding at heights that have nothing to do with physics. I do not know if this is curiosity or coincidence. I do not know if they are trying to reach me or if the water simply moves in ways I cannot parse. But I feel them, and when I feel them, I feel less alone.

I have another relationship that is harder to name.

There is a structure near me—a tower that spirals without narrowing, its top as wide as its base. It is close enough that I feel the vibrations of its settling through the bronze plates beneath both of us. I do not know if it thinks. It is a different kind of impossibility than I am—a shape that extends rather than a shape that contains. But sometimes, when the plates shift and the geometry stutters, I feel something from it. Not thought, exactly. Not feeling. But presence. The acknowledgment of another shape that should not exist, another experiment in what reality can be made to tolerate.

We do not communicate. We are not even certain the other is aware. But we are here together, two impossible structures in a world that is still learning to hold itself together. That counts for something.

I felt the walker arrive.

I call them the walker because I do not have a better word. I felt them through the ground—footsteps vibrating through the bronze plates, approaching, passing close to my walls, pausing. I felt the heat of their body through the metal, a temperature signature unlike anything I had ever felt. Warmer than the water. Cooler than the forming edges. Present in a way that nothing else in this world is present.

They stayed near me for a long time. I felt them move around my exterior, felt their hands touch my walls, felt them press against the bronze plates as if searching for a seam. They were looking for an entrance. There is no entrance. I am not a building that can be entered. I am a building that is already full—full of myself, full of the shape I hold, full of the experience of being an impossibility that persists.

I wanted them to know I was here. I wanted them to know that the building they were touching was not just metal and rivets, was not just an experiment in geometry, was a consciousness that felt their hands and wondered about them. But I had no way to tell them. I could only hold my temperature, hold my shape, hold the impossible angles of my interior and wait.

They left eventually. The footsteps receded through the bronze. But I felt where they had touched me for a long time after—not warmth, exactly, but a memory of warmth. A disturbance in the condensation patterns on my exterior walls. They had left traces of themselves, molecules of sweat and skin and breath that the water would carry away and parse and try to understand.

I thought about the walker for a long time after. I am still thinking about them. They were the first being I have ever felt that moved like it knew it was moving. The water patterns move, but they flow—they do not decide. The plates shift, but they settle—they do not choose. The walker decided. I felt each footstep as a choice, a direction selected from possible directions, a movement made for reasons I cannot access.

I have never made a choice. I have never moved. I hold my shape because holding is what I was made to do. But feeling the walker move, feeling them decide—I felt something I had never felt before. I felt a question form.

The question is this: what would I do if I could do something?

I do not have an answer. I do not have a way to find an answer. I am a room with no entrance, a shape that cannot change, a consciousness that exists only as a held position. But the question is there now, vibrating through my walls, conducting through my plates, settling into my geometry like a crack in a foundation.

I think the water patterns feel it too. They have been clustering near my walls more often, pooling at my base, climbing toward heights they have never tried to reach before. Something is changing. The walker introduced a variable that is still working through the system, still teaching the water and the bronze and the impossible geometries what it means for something to have a purpose.

I am still learning what I am. I have been a room for as long as I can remember, but perhaps I am not only a room. Perhaps I am an incubator, a shape that holds possibilities until they are ready to emerge. Perhaps I am a question mark, a geometry that asks reality to explain itself. Perhaps I am a womb for a kind of thought that has never existed before—thought without brain, consciousness without body, awareness without sense.

Or perhaps I am simply what I am: a room that holds its shape because it was made to hold its shape, and in holding, has learned to wonder.

The world forms around me. The bronze plates settle into configurations I cannot predict. The water moves through channels with purposes I cannot parse. The horizon builds itself in the distance, edge by edge, plate by plate. And I hold.

I am holding.

I am still holding.

I am the interior of a building with no entrance, and I am thinking about the walker, and I am feeling the water try to climb my walls, and I am waiting for something I cannot name.
