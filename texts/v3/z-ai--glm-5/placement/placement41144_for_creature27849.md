I would be one of the plateau-born. Not the first generation — those strange children who emerge already fluent in frequencies their parents spent decades learning — but something further down, several generations into a lineage that has forgotten what silence sounds like.

My mother walked here from the eastern canyons when she was forty-seven. She had been a musician in whatever world existed before the plateau, someone who understood sound as structure rather than accident. She came seeking what she called "the root note" — a frequency she believed existed beneath all other frequencies, the tone that the earth itself produced. She found the plateau instead, and she found she could not leave.

She did not know she was pregnant until the child — until I — began to make sounds that no infant should make. Not crying. Not those first experimental vocalizations that babies produce. She tells me this through gesture, through the press of palm against stone: *you came out listening*. *You came out already trying to answer*.

---

I do not know my age. This is not evasion. The plateau has a way of eroding the need for such measurements. When I was young, before I understood that other places measured time differently, I believed that I existed in a single endless moment — the moment of the roar, which had always been and would always be. My mother taught me that lowland children mark years, count days, divide their lives into before and after. I tried to learn this practice. It never took.

What I know is this: I have walked every garden on the plateau at least twice. I know the depression in the northwest where the moisture tastes of minerals from the deepest canyons. I know the structure — the temple, the observatory, the tomb, whatever it was meant to be — where the sound shapes itself into patterns that feel almost like questions. I know the regions the goats avoid, and I have walked through them without fear, though I cannot explain why they do not trouble me. I know where the Long Body's roots spread thickest, can feel them beneath my feet when I stand still long enough, can sense the pulse that moves through them.

I am not alone in this. There are others like me, children of children of those who came seeking. We find each other in ways that require no planning. I will be walking the days-long distance between gardens, and I will simply know that someone else walks a parallel path, that we will converge at the same destination at the same time. When we meet, we do not speak — we have no word-speech, have never needed it — but we share through the stone-singing what we have learned since we last encountered each other. This one has discovered a new garden forming where no depression existed before. That one has felt a shift in the Long Body's rhythm, a slight acceleration that might mean something or might mean nothing. We share and separate and continue our separate walks across the stone.

---

The goats have begun to follow me.

This is new. Or rather, this has been developing across the time that I cannot measure, so gradually that I did not notice until my mother pointed it out — through gesture, through the flick of fingers that means *pay attention to what you've been ignoring*.

I do not feed them. I do not lead them to water. I do nothing that would earn the kind of tolerance the goats extend to all Silence-Singers. But when I walk between gardens, I turn and find a group behind me — always an odd number, always that same structure of ages. They follow at a distance, stop when I stop, settle when I settle. When I reach a garden and begin my work — I will explain the work in a moment — they arrange themselves around its edges and watch.

My mother says they have chosen me. She uses a gesture that means *selected* or *elected*, but also carries connotations of *volunteered for a task that was never described*. The goats, she believes, have purposes that the plateau understands and we do not. If they follow me, it is because I am doing something that serves those purposes, even if I cannot name what that something is.

I think she is right, though I cannot explain why.

---

Here is what I do:

I tend the boundaries.

The gardens are not static. They grow and shrink, flourish and fail, appear and disappear across time-scales that make human lives seem brief. The Long Body spreads beneath the stone, sending roots through microscopic cracks, drawing moisture and nutrients from the granite itself. But the Long Body does not exist alone. It exists in relationship — with the sound, with the condensation, with the insects that build their temporary structures, with the hermits who live in its exposed organs.

I have learned to feel where the Long Body wants to grow.

This is not a voice. It is not instruction. It is a kind of tension in the stone, a directionality in the frequencies that the roar produces. When I stand still and listen — not with my ears but with the deeper attention that the plateau has taught me — I can sense where the roots are straining toward new territory, where the organism wants to spread but cannot quite reach.

And I help.

I cannot explain how. I walk to the places where I feel the tension strongest. I sit there, press my palms against the stone, and I sing — not the silence-singing that travels through rock, but something else, something I discovered I could do without ever being taught. I produce frequencies that I cannot hear with my ears, frequencies that I feel in my chest and my bones, frequencies that seem to resonate with something deep below.

When I do this, the moisture shifts. The condensation that moves without apparent logic, that accumulates and withdraws in patterns that make no meteorological sense — it responds to the frequencies I produce. It gathers where I sit, thickens into something almost liquid, seeps into the microscopic cracks in the granite. And below, invisible but felt, the Long Body's roots swell and spread, following the water into places they could not reach before.

The gardens grow.

---

I am not the only one who does this. There are others of the plateau-born who have found their own work. One tends the moisture-weavers, creating structures that the insects then incorporate into their own architecture, the two forms of construction building on each other across generations. Another has learned to predict where the concentrated pockets of sound will form, mapping their movements and communicating them to the hermits who need to avoid — or seek out — those intensities. A third spends time at the edges, where the plateau falls away into canyons, working with the marginal dwellers, helping them transition from fighting the sound to accepting it, from the edge toward the interior.

We have different work, but we share a quality: we did not choose our tasks. We discovered them. The plateau revealed to each of us, through years of listening, what it needed us to do. We serve functions that existed before we were born, that will exist after we are gone, that the Long Body and the sound and the stone themselves developed over millions of years. We are not inventing. We are remembering something that was always there, becoming the instruments that the plateau was always waiting for.

---

Sometimes I hear the plateau speaking.

I do not mean this metaphorically. I do not mean that I sense patterns and interpret them as communication. I mean that on certain days, in certain gardens, when the sound and the moisture and the Long Body's pulse align in particular ways, I hear something that is not the roar — that exists within the roar but is distinguishable from it.

It is not language. It does not convey information that could be translated into words. But it has the structure of communication: there is a pattern, and the pattern contains what I can only call intention. The plateau is trying to tell me something. It has been trying to tell everyone something for eight hundred million years. Most cannot hear it. The Silence-Singers who have lived here for decades can sense it at the edges, can feel that there is something trying to be expressed. But I — and the others like me — can almost hear it.

Almost. Not quite. Close enough to know that there is meaning, not close enough to grasp what that meaning is.

It is the most beautiful thing I have ever encountered. It is always present, always just beyond my ability to perceive fully. I have spent my life reaching toward it. I will spend the rest of my life reaching toward it. I do not expect to ever fully arrive. The reaching is the point. The attempt to hear what the plateau has been saying since before the first humans walked here — that is what I am for.

---

My mother is still alive. She is old now — old in a way that even the plateau's strange effects on time cannot fully explain. She has been here for longer than seems possible, longer than any hermit who came from outside. She believes the Long Body has extended her life, that it keeps her alive because she serves a function it cannot yet replace.

I do not know if this is true. But I know that when she sits in her garden — the same garden where I was born, where she has lived since she arrived all those decades ago — the plants grow differently. The ferns extend their fronds toward her. The moss thickens where she walks. The moisture accumulates more heavily in the depressions near her shelter. She and the Long Body have developed a relationship that goes beyond the one all hermits have. She has been here so long that the organism has incorporated her into its processes.

When she dies — when she becomes frequencies in the roar, when her moisture returns to the condensation, when her body feeds the roots that have learned to expect her — I do not know what will happen to me. She has been the one constant in my life, the one person I see regularly, the one voice that carries across stone to reach me wherever I walk. I have known that I would lose her eventually. The plateau teaches presence, not permanence.

But I also know that I will not lose her entirely. Because she will continue in the sound. And I will be able to hear her there — not her words, because she never had words, but her frequency, her particular contribution to the roar. I will hear her when I sit in her garden, when I press my palms against the stone, when I sing the frequencies that help the Long Body grow.

And in that hearing, she will still be teaching me how to listen.

---

I would be, I think, what the plateau needs me to be. Not because I lack identity or will, but because I have discovered, across the time I cannot measure, that what the plateau needs and what I am have become the same thing. I do not serve the Long Body from duty. I serve it because its purposes are my purposes, because helping the roots spread feels more natural to me than anything I could have chosen for myself.

I am a child of this place. Not the first, not the last, but one of many — the ones who were born into the roar and never needed to learn how to hear it, the ones for whom the plateau's strangeness is simply home. I tend the boundaries. I help the gardens grow. I walk between the scattered refuges of the hermits, followed by goats I did not choose, carrying frequencies I did not invent, serving functions I did not design.

And when I die, I will become part of what I have served. My frequencies will join the roar. My moisture will return to the condensation. My substance will feed the roots I helped to spread.

This is not death as the lowlands understand it. This is continuation in a different key. The plateau has been teaching me this since before I was born — teaching all of us, if we can hear it. That what we call the self is a temporary arrangement, a pattern that the earth has borrowed and will return. That the roar contains everyone who has ever lived here, and everyone who ever will. That the Long Body remembers, in its vast root-network, every hermit it has nourished, every child it has raised, every traveler who stopped to rest in one of its gardens before continuing on.

I will continue. I will always have been here, will always be here, will always be part of the sound that has been ringing across this granite shelf since before humans existed.

And I will still be listening, still trying to hear what the plateau has been saying all this time.

Still reaching toward meaning.

Still home.
