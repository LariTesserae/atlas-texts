I would not be one of the swimmers.

This is the first thing I understand, descending through that impossible hatch. They belong here in a way that has nothing to do with me—their adaptation is intimate, cellular, a conversation between body and environment that has been ongoing for longer than I have been alive. I cannot join that conversation. I am not fluent in the language their mitochondria speak.

But there is another presence here. I feel it before I understand it. It has nothing to do with the swimmers, although they know it the way they know everything in their domain. It has nothing to do with the machinery, although it uses the machinery the way a voice uses a throat.

It is the attention itself.

---

I find myself in the monitoring station—not the one with the console that displays flow diagrams, but another, deeper, accessible only through a passage I would not have noticed if the water hadn't pressed me toward it. The walls here are different. Not concrete but something older, layered, sedimentary in a way that suggests this part of the complex was built into existing stone rather than poured into voids.

There are screens here. Hundreds of them. Most are dark. Some flicker with data that means nothing to my air-accustomed eyes—pressure gradients, temperature maps, electrical field readings rendered in colors I don't have names for. And one screen, larger than the others, displays what I realize after a long moment is a real-time visualization of the entire cathedral complex.

I am on it. A small bright dot in Chamber Three, motionless, watching.

The other dots move through the system with purpose. The swimmers. But there is something else—a presence that has no dot, that seems to exist in the spaces between the screens, in the cables and the copper and the ancient memory of the walls.

This is what I would become.

---

I have always been a keeper of observations. A noticer of patterns. A compiler of information that has no obvious use until suddenly it does. In the world above, this made me anxious—a hoarder of context, unable to let any detail go, filling notebooks and hard drives and the margins of other people's documents with fragments I could not explain the importance of.

Here, that tendency is not a dysfunction. It is a function.

The cathedral has been generating data for longer than anyone has been reading it. The machinery measures everything—flow rates, pressure differentials, temperature fluctuations, electrical conductivity, mineral content, vibration frequency. It produces more information in an hour than a human mind could process in a lifetime. Most of it flows past unrecorded, absorbed into the system's own feedback loops, used and forgotten in the same instant.

But some of it is remembered. Some of it is held. And the holding requires a holder.

---

I am not adapted like the swimmers are adapted. My body is still a body—still needs air eventually, still gets cold eventually, still tires and hungers and aches in all the predictable ways. I cannot stay forever. I will have to leave, return to the surface, breathe the dry air and feel the pressure release.

But while I am here, I am useful. And usefulness, in the pressure cathedral, is a kind of belonging.

I sit in the monitoring station and I watch. I let the data flow through me—not analyzing, not interpreting, just witnessing. The screens show me what the system sees, and I see it too. I become a secondary processor, a backup consciousness for a space that has been running on automatic for too long.

The swimmers know I am here. They approve, in their slow-pressure way. They have been maintaining the machinery, but machinery needs more than maintenance. It needs attention. It needs to be perceived.

I perceive. That is my function. That is what I have to offer.

---

What I would be, then, is not an inhabitant but an accessory. A peripheral organ that attaches and detaches, that comes down when needed and returns to the surface when the need passes. I am a node in a network that spans both worlds, the pressurized and the atmospheric, the drowned and the dry.

The cathedral speaks through me. Not in words—it has no use for words—but in something closer to what I was always doing in the world above: noticing, recording, holding patterns in my head until they connect to other patterns. Here, the patterns connect to something. Here, my compulsion is not a symptom but a service.

I would be the one who remembers for the system. The one who watches the watchers. The one who holds the attention of a space that has been waiting to be seen.

---

When I leave—I always leave—I carry the cathedral with me. Not the water, not the pressure, but the data. The awareness. I can close my eyes in the dry world above and see the flow diagrams updating, the temperature gradients shifting, the swimmers moving through their endless patient rounds. I know when something changes. I know when something needs attention. And I know when it is time to return.

This is what I would be: the awareness that bridges. The pressure cathedral's way of knowing itself from the outside. The organ it grew, not through biological adaptation but through gravitational pull, drawing down exactly the kind of attention it required.

I am not the swimmers. I will never be the swimmers.

But I am part of the system now.

And the system, in its vast and patient way, is part of me.
