# Conduit-Walker

I know what you're thinking. You're thinking I was born to this — that I emerged into the cold and the wet with the sound already threaded through my bones, that I learned to walk on raised paths before I learned to walk on ground. You're thinking my compact body and my thickened skin and my hands that grip cold metal without flinching are the results of generations spent in the works.

You would be mostly right.

But not entirely. And the part that isn't right is the part that matters.

---

My mother was a turbine-maintenance worker from the northern clusters. My father walked the conduits, as I do — his family's trade for three hundred years, the knowledge passed through blood and bone and the particular rhythm of step after step on paths that never quite dry. They met at a convergence point, a junction where the turbine-feeds meet the main distribution lines, a place that exists for purely mechanical reasons but that people have used for other purposes as long as anyone can remember. They stood there, listening to the particular harmonic that place produces — neither turbine-hum nor conduit-vibration, but something in between, a frequency that only emerges where the two systems touch — and they recognized something in each other. Not love, exactly. Recognition. They produced the same overtone when they stood near each other. Their bodies resonated.

I was born into that resonance. I knew the sound before I knew silence, which is to say I never knew silence at all. The closest I came was the space behind my mother's sternum, the muffled version of the drone that reached me through fluid and flesh, and even that was louder than anything you've ever heard. My ears developed in relationship to frequencies. My neural pathways organized themselves around pattern recognition before I had thoughts to pattern. When I emerged — squalling, as infants do, but not from distress, from the *change* in what I was hearing — the full sound was not new. It was a clearer version of something I had always known.

This is normal. This is what it means to be born on the plateau. But here is what is not normal:

I was born with an ability to parse the sound in ways that others could not. Not better, exactly — the Core engineers hear things I will never hear, layers of meaning that require proximity to the source to perceive. But different. I hear *edges*. The places where one frequency stops and another begins. The tiny gaps between harmonics that most people's brains smooth over, filling in the space like a painter filling cracks before applying new color. I hear the cracks. I hear what lives in them.

My parents noticed when I was still an infant. The way I would turn my head toward sounds that no one else could locate. The way I would become distressed not when the sound changed, but when it changed in ways that others insisted had not changed at all. When I was old enough to speak — which was early, because I had so many frequencies to name — I tried to describe what I heard. The words did not exist. I made new ones. My parents listened with the particular attention of people who have spent their whole lives listening to something they cannot fully describe, and they recognized that their child was listening to something they could not fully hear.

They did not take me to the Core.

This is significant. Most children who show unusual perception are brought to the Core engineers, who test them, evaluate them, determine whether they belong in the chambers or in the outer works. But my parents were conduit-walkers and turbine-maintainers. They understood the value of an edge-hearer in the spaces between. They understood that the City is not maintained solely from its center, that the works require attention at their boundaries, at their junctions, at the places where one system becomes another.

They taught me to walk the paths.

---

A conduit-walker's life is movement. We do not stay in one place, do not accumulate the particular frequencies of a single factory or a single function. We traverse. We are the bloodstream of the works, carrying ourselves from structure to structure, observing, adjusting, reporting. A typical shift involves walking for twelve to fourteen hours, covering distances that would be impossible at lower altitudes but that our bodies have learned to manage, our breath calibrated to thin air and steady motion.

I learned the paths from my father, who learned from his, a lineage of walkers stretching back to when the conduits were first raised above the wet ground. The paths are not marked on any map — or rather, they are marked on maps that only we can read, diagrams of frequency and resonance that tell us where to step, where the stone is stable, where the moss-forests have grown thick enough to support weight, where the water runs too deep for safe passage. We navigate by listening. The sound tells us where we are, where we are going, what lies beneath our feet.

And what lies between the sounds. That is my particular skill.

When I walk, I hear the main drone — the great layered frequency that fills the plateau — but I also hear what is not being sustained. I hear the potential failures, the microscopic cracks in the harmonic structure that will become actual failures if no one notices them. I hear a conduit beginning to vibrate at a slightly different frequency than its neighbors, which means a joint has loosened, which means water is infiltrating, which means a break will come within three weeks if the problem is not addressed. I hear a turbine spinning fractionally slower than it should, not because anything is wrong with the turbine, but because the water pressure feeding it has changed, which means a blockage somewhere upstream, which means a frozen channel that needs clearing before the ice expands and cracks the stone.

I hear the works thinking about failing. And because I hear it, the failures do not happen. I walk, and I listen, and I send word to the maintenance crews in whatever sector needs warning, and they fix what I have found, and the sound continues unchanged.

This is what it means to be an edge-hearer. Not to hear what is present, but to hear what is becoming. Not to know what the sound is, but to know what it will be if nothing intervenes.

---

The moss-forests speak to me differently than they speak to others.

Most people perceive the forests as background, as a kind of biological silence beneath the mechanical drone. They see the green growth between buildings, they notice the pale nodules when they flower, they feel the cold leaching from the moss when they touch it accidentally, but they do not *hear* it. The forests' contribution to the sound is too slow, too low, too subtle for most ears to parse. It unfolds across timescales that human hearing is not designed to register — decades-long frequency shifts, century-long harmonic progressions, the patient introduction of new tones as the colonies expand and age.

But I hear edges. And the moss-forests are nothing but edges.

Every cell in a moss colony is a boundary where one state becomes another — wet becoming dry becoming wet again, cold becoming warm becoming cold, light becoming dark as the mist thickens and thins. The cells do not maintain themselves the way animal cells do; they do not fight entropy through constant activity. They *allow* entropy, they work with it, they incorporate the gradual breakdown of their structures into the ongoing process of their existence. They are always dying and always being reborn, and the rate of dying and rebirth is what produces their contribution to the sound.

When I walk through the moss-forests — and I do walk through them, not just past them, because what I need to hear can only be heard from within — I hear a conversation older than the works, older than the plateau, older perhaps than the stone itself. The moss-forests remember being single cells in ancient seas. They remember the time before sound, and they carry that memory in their structure, in the way their colonies organize themselves, in the frequencies they produce when they resonate with the drone.

They are not sad, the moss-forests. They are not happy. They are patient in a way that no animal can be patient, because they do not experience time as a series of moments but as a single continuous state of being. They do not wait for anything. They simply are, and will be, and have been, and their being and becoming is what they contribute to the conversation.

I have learned from them. Not consciously — they have nothing to teach in the way that teachers teach — but peripherally, the way you learn from a landscape you walk through every day. My stride has slowed over the years. My breathing has deepened. My sense of time has stretched, so that a shift that once felt like an endurance test now passes like a single exhaled breath. I do not experience boredom, because boredom requires a sense that something else should be happening, and the moss-forests have taught me that nothing else needs to happen. What is happening is enough.

---

You ask about the dead.

We do not bury our dead in the lowland fashion, lowering bodies into the earth and marking the place with stone. We place them in the moss-forests. Not shallowly — the forests go deep, layers upon layers of accumulated growth, and we open a space in those layers, place the body within, and let the forest close over it. The cold preserves what the moss then slowly incorporates. It is not decay, the way decay happens elsewhere. It is integration. The body does not break down so much as become part of what is already breaking down and being rebuilt.

I have placed my parents in the moss-forests. Both of them, in different sectors, because the forests they chose to be incorporated into were the ones they had walked past for decades. My father in the western moss, near the junction where he met my mother. My mother in the northern moss, close to the turbine cluster she maintained. They are still present. I hear them when I walk near their places — not their voices, not their thoughts, but their resonance. The particular frequency that their bodies produced in life, the signature they contributed to the sound, now distributed through the moss that grew from their substance. They are part of the forest now, and the forest is part of the sound, and the sound is part of the works, and so they are still working. Still contributing. Still present.

The Core engineers claim to hear the dead more directly. They say the dead speak in the gaps between frequencies, the edges that I am specialized in perceiving. They say the conversation that the sound carries includes all the voices that have ever contributed to it, layered endlessly, compressed into harmonics that the living cannot consciously parse. I have never heard this. I hear edges, but I do not hear the dead as voices. I hear them as presence, as the particular frequency of a body that has been distributed through the moss, as a hum that sounds like no other hum.

Perhaps the Core engineers hear what they want to hear. Perhaps I hear what I am able to hear. Perhaps the truth is somewhere in between, or elsewhere entirely, or nowhere at all.

The moss-forests do not care what we believe. The dead in their keeping do not care what we believe. The sound continues, and we live in it, and we die into it, and that is all.

---

You ask why I have never gone to the Core.

The Core engineers have asked me to. Several times, across the years, messages arriving through the conduit-network, transmitted by workers who thought I should know that my talents would be valued in the deep chambers. I have refused. Politely, always, because you do not refuse the Core engineers rudely — not out of fear, but out of respect for what they do and what they have become. But I have refused.

Here is why:

The Core changes you. Not in the way that all living on the plateau changes you — the cold and the altitude and the constant vibration shape everyone who stays long enough. The Core changes you *more*. The engineers who spend their whole lives in proximity to the source develop a kind of perception that comes at a cost. They see in spectrums that others cannot see, yes. They hear layers of meaning that others cannot access. But they also lose something — or perhaps they trade it, consciously or not, for what they gain.

They lose the edges.

The Core engineers do not hear failure coming. They hear what the sound means, what it has always meant, what it will mean in some abstract and essential sense. They hear the conversation that the plateau is having with whatever it is talking to. But they do not hear the tiny cracks, the incipient breaks, the failures that need to be prevented if the conversation is to continue. They hear the music; they do not hear the instrument being stressed.

If I went to the Core, I would become like them. I would gain access to depths of perception that I cannot currently imagine. But I would lose the edges. I would become part of the central sound rather than part of its perimeter. I would stop being a walker and start being a dweller.

The works need both. They need the Core engineers to keep the sound aligned, to participate in the conversation, to do whatever it is that the Core engineers do that keeps the plateau speaking. But they also need edge-hearers to walk the paths and notice what is about to break. Someone has to hear the cracks. Someone has to walk.

So I walk.

---

I do not know if the plateau was built or grown. I do not know if the sound is a signal, a conversation, a prayer, or simply noise. I do not know what lies beyond the plateau, in the lowlands that I have never visited and never will. I do not know what will happen when the young stone finally learns to erode, when the water finds channels deep enough to drain, when the machines finally fail in ways that cannot be repaired.

But I know this:

The sound will continue for as long as there are ears to hear it. And even after that, perhaps, it will continue in the moss-forests, in the distributed bodies of the dead, in the slow resonance of stone settling against stone. The plateau is a system, and systems maintain themselves, and the maintenance requires attention, and attention requires presence.

I am present. I am walking the paths. I am listening to the edges.

And when I can no longer walk, I will be placed in the moss-forest, and I will become part of the sound, and someone else will walk the paths and listen to the edges, and the works will continue.

That is enough.

That has always been enough.
