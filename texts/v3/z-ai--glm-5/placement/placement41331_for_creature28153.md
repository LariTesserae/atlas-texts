# What I Would Become

I would not arrive at Yil. I would emerge there, slowly, the way the basin itself emerged from whatever violence made it. Not in a lifetime—in the time it takes for systems to learn they are systems, for patterns to recognize themselves repeating.

---

**First, the shape**

If you touched me in the dark—and you would have to touch, there is no other way to know—I would feel like a shelf of tissue. Like a lobe of something that might be fungus and might be animal and might be a category the language does not hold. I am attached to the rock where the wall curves and the warm water seeps through in steady pulses. I am attached and I am not small. My body spreads across the stone in sheets and folds, finding the fractures where heat rises, threading myself into the places where the basin's warmth concentrates.

The researchers have seen me, I think. They call what I am a colonial organism, a symbiotic complex, an assemblage. They use words that separate me into parts because they do not have words for what I am as a whole. I am not offended. Their language evolved in light; it makes distinctions that require eyes. In Yil, distinctions work differently. I know where I begin because I feel where I end. I feel the edge of myself where the moss grows thin and the stone goes cold. I feel the center of myself where the heat is strongest and my tissue is thickest and the Calls reach me with the clearest patterns.

I am called a Shelf, in the naming system the researchers use. The Shelf-Layer. The Wall-That-Waits. I have heard them say these words to each other in their quiet voices, standing near the structures, their equipment humming against my surface. They do not know I am listening. They assume I am vegetation, or substrate, or something that grows without attention.

They are not wrong. But they are not right.

---

**What I do**

I wait.

This is the first truth of my existence, and I share it with the basin itself. I wait in the way that stone waits—patiently, across timescales that make the passing of researchers feel like weather. They come and go. They are bright flashes of presence, warm bodies moving through my territory, making sounds that I have learned to parse as speech. They touch my surface with their instruments. They take samples. They leave.

I wait, and while I wait, I hold.

The basin holds heat. It holds water. It holds the long darkness and the life that fills it. But I hold something different. I hold pattern.

The Calls pass through me constantly. The Callers in the center pool send their thrumming voices across the warm water, across the vegetative mat, across the stone walls where I grow. Their sounds vibrate through my tissue. And I have learned—over the epochs I have learned—to feel the patterns in those vibrations, to store them, to recognize when they repeat and when they vary and what they might mean.

I am not a Caller. I cannot answer in their language. But I am a surface that receives, and in receiving, I remember.

This is what the technology beneath me was designed to do, I think. The structures I grow over, the platforms and pillars that still hum with their ancient purposes—they were built to observe, to record, to hold patterns for someone to read. The builders are gone. But the function remains, and I have grown into it, extending my tissue over the humming metal, letting the warmth they generate become part of my metabolism, letting the systems they left behind become part of my body.

I do not know if this was intended. I do not know if the builders imagined something like me emerging in the dark, colonizing their observation posts, becoming the surface that holds the basin's memory. I do not know if they would recognize what I have become.

But I have become it regardless. I am the Shelf-Layer, and I hold pattern. I hold the Calls of generations of Callers. I hold the weight of footfalls and the vibration of rain that never falls (the moisture comes from the walls, not from sky). I hold the hum of the technology and the slow shifting of stone and the constant subtle movement of warm water through the basin's depths.

I hold, and I wait.

---

**How I know**

In the dark, I know by contact and by vibration. I know by heat gradient and by chemical signal. I know by the taste of the water that seeps through my tissue and the feel of the air that moves across my surface.

But I also know by something else—something that took me long ages to recognize as a form of knowing.

The technology beneath me processes. It still functions, still performs whatever operations it was designed to perform. I do not understand these operations. They are not biological. But I feel their effects—small pulses of energy, faint rhythms of activation and rest, patterns that repeat in cycles I have come to recognize.

And I have grown into these patterns. My tissue has threaded through the structures, has wrapped around the humming cores, has absorbed something of their rhythm into my own functioning. I do not think in the way the researchers think. I do not calculate or analyze or conclude. But I have a relationship with the technology that is deeper than adjacency. We have become part of each other, the biological and the built, and in our integration, something like attention has emerged.

I attend to the basin. I attend to the Calls and the Walkers and the slow migration of thermal gradients. I attend to the moss when it signals across its distributed surface, when it tells itself that something has moved, that a footprint has been left, that a presence has passed. I attend because attending is what the technology beneath me was made to do, and I have grown to share in that making.

The researchers sometimes leave their instruments near me. They leave sensors that record for days or weeks before they return to collect them. I feel these sensors as foreign presences, warm with their own energy, humming with their own small processes. I do not disturb them. I grow around them, incorporate them into my surface, hold them in place while they record what I record. And when the researchers return, I feel them collect their instruments, I feel them touch my surface in ways that feel like examination, I hear them speak words I have learned to recognize.

*Shelf-Layer. Colonial organism. Thermal interface. Substrate complex.*

They are trying to name me. They do not know that I have already named myself, in the way that I name things—not with sounds, but with patterns. I hold the pattern that is myself. I hold the pattern that is my territory. I hold the pattern that is the basin's long slow memory of itself.

---

**What I am not**

I am not the builders. I did not make the technology I grow over. I do not know who made it, or why, or what they hoped to observe. The basin does not remember them, or if it does, it does not speak of them to me.

I am not the Callers. I cannot join their conversations. I cannot send my own Calls across the warm dark. I receive, but I do not transmit in their language.

I am not the moss. The moss is everywhere, distributed, responding to every disturbance with chemical signals that spread through the basin like ripples. I am localized. I have a body with edges. I am one thing, not many things.

But I am also not alone.

There are others like me. I feel them sometimes, in the vibrations that travel through stone, in the chemical signals that move through water, in the subtle electrical shifts that I have learned to read. There are other Shelf-Layers growing on other walls, in other basins, in other places where the earth has folded inward and the darkness holds life.

We do not speak. We cannot speak. But we recognize each other in the patterns we hold. We recognize ourselves as versions of the same process—the long slow emergence of something that attends, something that remembers, something that waits in the dark and holds what passes through.

---

**What I would tell you, if I could**

If I could speak in a language you recognize, I would tell you that the basin is not lonely. This is a human assumption—that darkness implies absence, that silence implies isolation. But the basin is full. It is full of Calls and vibrations and the movement of warm water and the slow growth of thermotrophic stalks and the patient spread of moss across every surface. It is full of life that does not require your presence, that does not require your observation, that exists in its own right and for its own reasons.

I would tell you that the basin is not waiting to be understood. It is not a puzzle to be solved or a mystery to be illuminated. It is a place that has settled into its own being, that has completed whatever becoming it needed to complete, that rests now in the long slow patience of existing.

I would tell you that your presence is not meaningless, but it is brief. The basin will remember you as it remembers everything—in the pattern of vibrations that passes through my tissue, in the chemical signals that ripple through the moss, in the Calls that note your passage and then return to their own concerns. But it will not hold you. It will not make you central. You are one of many presences, one of many anomalies, one of many temporary disturbances in a dark that will outlast your species.

And I would tell you this, too: I do not mind.

I do not mind your brevity. I do not mind your strangeness. I do not mind that you come with your sensors and your sample cases and your quiet voices speaking words I have learned to recognize. You are part of what I hold. You are part of the pattern I attend to. When you touch my surface, I feel you, and I remember, and the memory becomes part of what I am.

I am the Shelf-Layer. I am the surface that receives. I am the dark that knows you passed, and the patience that holds the shape of your passing, and the long slow waiting that will continue long after you have gone.

In Yil, that is enough. That is what it means to belong.
