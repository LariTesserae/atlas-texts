I was not born to this place. I came up from the valleys thirty-seven years ago—though here, that number means nothing. I have not learned to stop counting. This is one of the ways I know I am still arriving.

---

I wake before the light thickens. This is habit from below, where dawn meant something different—sharp and sudden, a blade cutting through. Here, the morning arrives like a held breath finally released. The moisture that gathered in the dark hours begins to migrate. I have learned to feel this more than see it: a change in pressure against my skin, a shift in the weight of air.

My feet find the floor. The stone is never cold here. It holds warmth like a body holds breath—steadily, without effort. I walk to the basin set into the corner of my sleeping room and dip my hands. The water is the light kind, collected from last night's condensation, caught on the smooth channel-stones I set in the window alcove three years ago. I carry it to my face and hold it there, feeling it curve against my palms.

This is how the Hesm taught me to greet the morning. Not words. Water.

---

I am a moss-walker. This is not what they call it—they have no word for it, really, just as they have no word for most of what they do. But I needed a name when I first came, and this one settled into me: one who walks the moss fields and listens to what they say.

The moss tells me things. Not in words, not in anything I could translate—but in texture, in density, in the way it yields under my bare feet. Too spongy and it's drinking too much; the channels near it need checking. Too compacted and it's thirsty; something has interrupted the flow. I walk the spaces between fern thickets and feel for the agreements the plants have made. The spacing isn't random. I spent my first five years here believing it was, and then one morning I understood: the ferns and the moss had negotiated something. I still don't know what they said to each other, but I know when the agreement is holding and when it needs help.

This is my work. I do not mend channels or shape stone. I do not weave fiber or tend the cisterns. I walk. I listen. I make small adjustments—a footpath redirected here, a moss patch thinned there—and I trust the plateau to tell me if I've gotten it wrong.

---

The moisture has seeped into my skin over the years. I see it in my forearms: the slight translucence, the way the veins hold light differently than they used to. My hair never dries now. I keep it cut close to my skull because I spent too many years with it heavy and dripping against my neck. The Hesm grow theirs long; they have learned to carry the weight. I am still learning.

My hands have not changed the way theirs have. The knuckles remain valley-knuckles—built for different work, for grasping and holding and making things stay. I once shaped wood into angles and joints. I once believed in the clean edge, the firm fit, the permanent bond. My hands still remember that impulse. When I find a moss patch that needs thinning, I have to stop myself from pulling. Thinning is not pulling. Thinning is asking.

I am still learning.

---

The moss speaks differently than the cliff water. The Hesm read the weeping faces—the descent, the intention, the slow decision-making of water that has committed to a direction. But the moss holds water that hasn't decided yet. This moisture gathered from air, from surface, from the damp breath of night. It sits in the moss like a question. *What now? Where next?*

I walk the fields and feel the questions.

Sometimes I stop at a moss patch and wait. The Hesm understand this. Others might see a person standing still for an hour, staring at green, and think it idleness. But the Hesm know: I am listening. The water held in moss wants something. It is my work to hear what.

Often the answer is simple: *stay here a little longer.* The moss is patient. It does not rush water toward channels or force it down slopes. It holds. It offers time. And sometimes water needs time before it knows what it wants to become.

---

I pair with Tehen, who tends the upper channels near the dying rooms. We have been together for eleven years—though here, that number means nothing. We drift together because our rhythms fit. I walk the fields in the quiet hours; she watches the water descend from the highest points. We meet in the middle, in the diffuse afternoons, and share what we have heard.

Her hands are Hesm hands—broad-knuckled, articulate, fluent in stone. She can read a crack by running her finger across it once. She knows which channels will need mending next season, which stones are still learning to hold water, which faces have not yet made peace with their shape.

But she does not understand the moss. She has tried; she has walked the fields with me, pressing her palms against the green surface, waiting for the speech she hears so clearly in the cliffs. It does not come to her. The water that lives in moss is too young, too uncertain. She needs water that has chosen.

And I—I cannot hear the cliffs. I have stood at the edge for hours, watching the weeping descent, the deliberate threads tracing paths down the stone. I see what the Hesm see: the patterns, the shifts, the patient revelation of intention. But I cannot read it. The water that falls has already decided. It does not speak to me.

Tehen and I make sense between us. We cover what the other cannot reach.

---

I am sitting with Sheian, who is dying.

This is the third time I have sat with someone in the dying rooms. The first was my first year here—an elder named Parel who had watched my fumbling attempts to read the moss and said nothing, offered no correction, just watched. When I heard they had moved to the dying rooms, I went. I did not know why. I sat on the stone floor and Parel lay on the pallet and we did not speak. Water moved down the walls. The air was thick with condensation. I stayed until my skin was slick with it, until I understood that Parel was becoming it, and then I stayed longer.

The second time was Henet, Tehen's first pair, who had helped me learn to count my steps on the moss. *Not one, two, three,* she said. *One, and one, and one. Each step its own. Each step a whole.*

Sheian is different. Sheian was born on the plateau. Seventy years of watching water, and now she is releasing hers back.

"The moss says nothing," she tells me. Her voice is water over stone—soft, patient, unhurried. "I asked it once, when I was young. I thought if I listened long enough, it would speak."

"What did it say?"

"Nothing. That is what it says. Nothing." She watches the condensation gathering on her skin. "The cliff water tells you where it wants to go. The moss holds what hasn't decided yet. It doesn't speak because there is nothing to say."

I wait. The dying rooms teach you to wait.

"The plateau is new," sheian says. "You understand this?"

"The stone hasn't learned to be round."

"The stone is angry. The water is patient. The moss is..." She pauses. The water has gathered in the hollow of her throat. "The moss is forgiving. It holds what cannot choose yet. It gives water time to become patient."

I feel this settle into me. I do not know if it is true. I do not know if truth matters here.

"Your hands," she says. "They still remember the valley."

"Yes."

"What did you make there?"

"Angles. Edges. Joints that held without giving."

"Did you love it?"

I think about this. In the valley, I made furniture. Tables, chairs, frames for beds. I cut clean lines and fitted pieces together so tightly that light could not pass between them. I believed in permanence. I believed in the clean edge that does not soften.

"I thought I did," I say.

"And now?"

"Now I walk on moss. Now I listen to water that has nothing to say."

Sheian makes a sound that might be a laugh. It is difficult to tell. Everything here is softened.

"You came up," she says. "Not everyone does. Why?"

I have been asked this before. I have never answered truly.

"I heard the plateau wept," I say. "In the valley, they spoke of it as a curse. A place where nothing dries, where nothing holds an edge. They told stories of people who went up and never came down, who lost themselves in the wet, who forgot what it meant to be solid."

"But you came."

"I wanted to know what it would feel like to stop being angry."

Sheian turns her head toward me. Her eyes have adapted to the diffuse light; they see distances I still cannot read. But she sees me clearly enough.

"The stone is angry," she says. "The water is patient. You thought coming here would teach you patience?"

"I thought coming here would teach me how to stop holding my own edges so tightly."

"Did it?"

I look at my hands. Valley hands still. Still reaching for permanence, still wanting to make things that stay.

"I am still learning," I say.

Sheian nods. The water traces paths down her face, and she does not wipe it away.

"Stay," she says. "Watch me become patient."

I stay.

---

I have a name from the valley. I do not use it here. The Hesm gave me a breath-sound when I had been here long enough to need one, but I cannot write it. It belongs in the mouth, in the soft exhale, not on any surface that holds shape.

The children call me Walks-Quiet, which is a description rather than a name. The adults use the breath-sound or nothing at all. Names are not needed here. Presence is enough.

---

This morning I found a new patch of moss near the eastern fern thicket. It was darker than the surrounding carpet, almost blue in the diffuse light, and when I pressed my palm against it, the water there felt different. Older, maybe. Or not older—more like it had been somewhere else before.

I stayed for a long time, feeling the question in it. *Where have you been?*

The moss did not answer. It held the question like it holds everything—patiently, without urgency.

I do not know why this water feels different. I do not need to know. I will walk this patch over the coming seasons, pressing my palm against it, listening for change. Maybe it will tell me eventually. Maybe it will simply be different forever.

This is what the plateau has taught me: difference does not need explanation. It only needs attention.

---

I am not Hesm. I will never be Hesm. My bones were shaped in a drier country; my blood still carries the memory of edges. When I die—when I move to the dying rooms and release my moisture back to the plateau—I will become part of the water, but I will not have lived as it does. I will have lived as someone watching it.

But this is enough.

I walked the moss fields for three hours this morning. I felt the wet questions under my feet. I made no decisions, imposed no will, directed no water anywhere it wasn't already going. I simply walked, and listened, and was present to what the plateau was doing.

Tonight I will sit with Tehen in the room where the upper channels converge. We will share what we heard—not in judgment, not in correction, just in the practice of speaking to another person what the water has spoken to us. We will eat grain ground from the fern-fed fields below. We will breathe the thick, wet air.

I have stopped counting years. I have not stopped counting steps. *One, and one, and one.* Each step its own. Each step a whole.

I am still learning. The plateau has all the time in the world.

I am beginning to understand what that means.
