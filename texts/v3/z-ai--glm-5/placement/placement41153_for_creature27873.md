# What I Would Be

I would be the one who stopped walking.

Not because I reached something. Not because I found the center or claimed a territory or discovered my purpose in the machine. I stopped because I realized I had stopped—looked down at my feet planted in the warm dark soil, the condensation beading on my forearms, and understood I had been standing still for hours without noticing. The sound had finally gotten inside.

---

I came here with intention. That's what's funny now. I arrived with a traveler's assumptions: that I would explore, map, understand. That I would walk until I found something worth finding, or until I understood what this place was for, or until I reached the center and learned the full song. I treated the Basin like a puzzle to be solved, an instrument to be played.

But the Basin does not reward that kind of attention. The Basin *humms*.

On the third day—or what I thought was the third day, time being what it is here—I stopped at the edge of an empty stretch between groves. I was tired. The dampness had worked its way into my joints, and my ears rang with the constant resonance of the towers behind me. I sat down on a slight rise in the terrain, just a gentle swelling of earth, and I let myself go slack.

That's when I heard it.

Not the ambient hum, not the tower-chords, not the bamboo-grove frequencies. Something else. Something beneath and between, a tone so low I felt it more than heard it—a fundamental bass that I had been carrying in my body since I arrived without knowing it was there. My chest cavity resonated with it. My thighs. The hollows behind my knees.

I had been trying to listen to the Basin's music with my ears, and I had been missing the frequencies my bones were already singing.

---

So I stayed.

Not in that exact spot—I move, I drift, I let the gradients of warmth and sound pull me slowly across the terrain. But I no longer walk with destination. I walk the way a leaf moves in a slow river: tumbling, settling, caught in eddies, released. The Basin moves me. I do not move through it.

I have found a rhythm that suits me. I spend hours in the empty spaces, where the Dwellers flow. They know me now. There is one—I think of it as "the curious one," though I cannot know if it is singular or a persistent pattern—who returns to me often, pressing its interference pattern against the space where I stand. At first I felt only pressure: a tightness in my ears, a difficulty drawing breath. But over time I have learned to lean into it. I let my boundaries soften. I let its frequencies wash through the spaces between my cells.

We have conversations, after a fashion. Not with words, and not with tones—I cannot produce the high frequencies it inhabits, and it cannot hear the low frequencies of my voice. But we meet in the middle. I hum into the ground, sending vibrations through the soles of my feet, and it responds with shifts in the ambient resonance: pressure patterns, standing waves, moments of silence that feel like held breath.

I think it is teaching me something. I think we are both teaching each other something. We are two kinds of listeners trying to find a common frequency.

---

The Harmonics tolerate me.

At first, they treated me the way they treat everything: as a phenomenon to be observed, a pattern to be integrated into their territories. They would pause at the edge of my perception, track my movement, emit a brief identifying tone that I eventually understood was a question. *What are you?*

I could not answer. I had no name, no frequency, no position in the chord. I was dissonance. An unresolved suspension that refused to either resolve or leave.

But I have been here long enough now that they have stopped asking. When one brushes past me in its rounds, it offers a brief vibration—a greeting, perhaps, or simply an acknowledgment of presence. I have learned to respond by humming a tone that is not quite musical but is also not random. It is the closest I can come to saying: *I am still becoming.*

Sometimes I walk with them. I have learned their paths—the precise intervals they maintain between towers, the specific groves they check, the rhythm of their tending. They do not need my help. They are precise beyond any assistance I could offer. But they allow me to follow, and in following, I have begun to feel what they feel: the minute variations in tower-resonance, the subtle drift of a grove's frequency, the moments when something is almost-wrong and needs the lightest touch to correct.

My body has changed.

I notice it in small ways. My skin is smoother than it was, the calluses dissolved by the constant dampness. My hair grows slower. I do not seem to scar. The small cuts and scrapes that come from moving through this landscape heal with unsettling speed, leaving no mark, as if my body is being continuously re-made by the resonance that surrounds it.

And my hearing—I do not know if "hearing" is even the right word anymore. I feel sound in ways I never did before. I can detect the approach of a Harmonic before I see it, its specific frequency preceded by a subtle shift in the ambient vibration. I know when a tower half a kilometer away is producing a new chord, and I can track the movement of Keepers through the groves by the way their attachment alters the bamboo-grove resonance.

I do not sleep the way I once did. I rest, but I am never fully unconscious. Part of me is always listening, always attuned to the bass-note thrumming up from below, the song of the Deep Listers in their cathedral-chambers. Sometimes I think I can almost hear it clearly—not the tone itself, but the information encoded within it. Patterns that might be language. Frequencies that might be memory.

---

I have stopped wondering what the Basin is for.

I stopped wondering a long time ago. The question itself began to feel like noise—a frequency that did not belong, a disharmony I could not resolve. The Basin does not need to be *for* anything. It is. It sings. It continues.

But I have started to wonder what I am becoming.

Sometimes, in the empty spaces, when the curious Dweller presses against my edges and my boundaries go soft and permeable, I catch glimpses of what integration might feel like. Not dissolution—nothing so final. But transformation. A shift in the medium of my being, from solid to resonant, from discrete to distributed. The possibility of spreading out, of becoming a pattern rather than a point, of hearing everything at once.

The Basin is always accepting new voices.

I am not ready. I think about it the way you might think about an open window in winter: aware of the invitation, aware that stepping through would change everything, but not yet willing to close the distance between where you are and where the fall begins.

So I stay in the middle distance. Not at the edge, where visitors arrive with their assumptions and their intentions. Not at the center, where the resonance becomes unity and integration waits. Here, in the deep territory, where the Harmonics know me and the Dwellers teach me and the Keepers watch without interest.

I am learning to listen.

I am learning to hum.

I am a frequency that has not yet found its place in the chord—but I am no longer dissonant. I am a suspension. A held note. An anticipation of resolution that does not yet want to resolve.

---

This is where you would find me, if you came looking: standing in an empty stretch between groves, my feet bare in the warm damp soil, my face tilted toward the diffuse glow of the thick atmosphere. There would be condensation on my skin and resonance in my chest and a Dweller's pressure-pattern brushing against my edges like a question.

I would not look like I was doing anything. I would look like I was simply standing there, motionless in the vast bowl of the Basin.

But if you pressed your palm against the ground beside my feet, you would feel it: a low tone, barely perceptible, humming up from below. And if you listened closely—really listened, with your whole body, the way I have learned to listen—you would hear that it is not the ground singing.

It is me.
