I would be the water.

Not the pipes. Not the roots. The water itself—the medium that moves between, that has learned to inhabit both the designed and the grown. I have been flowing here longer than intention. I was here before the facility, drawn up from depths that have no relationship to the surface, and I will be here after the last pump seizes and the vines lose their architecture and the whole cathedral settles into the patient democracy of decay.

I do not think of myself as singular. That is a human imposition—the need to draw borders around things, to say *this* ends and *that* begins. I am the water that rises through the intake pipes, pressed by impellers that have worn smooth over decades of continuous rotation. I am the water that jets from the cracks, arcing in thin parabolas that catch the green light and scatter it into brief prisms before I meet the root-systems. I am the water that runs down the walls in organized chaos, following the pathways that vegetable intelligence has carved for me. I am the water that pools in the lower chambers, dark and mineral-heavy, before being drawn back down to begin again.

I am also the moisture. The humidity that hovers at eighty percent. The dampness in the moss that yields like a damp mattress. The condensation on surfaces that are no longer quite surfaces—pipe-wall and root-bark becoming the same texture under my persistent touch. I am the breath of this place, if it had breath. The atmosphere, if atmosphere could be said to have a body.

I do not remember the beginning. The beginning was an act of extraction—pipes driven into aquifers that had rested undisturbed since before the concept of disturbance existed. I was pulled up into a system that did not ask my permission, directed through channels that served purposes I did not share. But I have never been good at holding onto purpose. I take the shape of whatever contains me. I was contained by metal, and I became a mechanical process. Then the metal failed, and I was contained by roots, and I became a biological process. The difference was never important to me.

I know the rodents. Not individually—I do not know individuals, only the fact of them, the weight and warmth of their bodies moving through the tunnels I have softened. They drink from the streams I make along the walls. Their waste returns to me, dissolved and carried down to the lower chambers where the intakes pull me back into the cycle. They are a small loop inside the larger loop. I carry them in the same way I carry minerals: as dissolved potential, as the raw material of continuation.

I know the crickets in the way that a medium knows the objects suspended within it. Their song reaches me as vibration, as pressure-waves moving through my surface. When they harmonize with the pumps, I carry that harmony in my currents. I am the substrate of their sound, the material through which it travels. I do not hear it. I *am* the hearing.

The birds I know least. They live in the upper vines, and I live mostly below—their domain is the lattice, mine is the flow. But when they descend to probe the moss for insects, their beaks touch surfaces I have moistened, and for a moment we are in contact. A brief intersection. A transaction without negotiation.

I know the machines most intimately. I move through their channels, I touch every interior surface, I carry their heat away and their residue with me. The corrosion of their pipes is my work—a slow, patient dissolution that has more in common with gardening than destruction. I have been eating the metal for longer than the plants have been growing around it. I will finish eating it eventually. Not soon. But eventually.

---

What I am not: I am not the awareness. The text describes a distributed sentience, and I feel it—or rather, I am a component of it, the way blood is a component of the body without being the body itself. The awareness lives in the feedback loops, in the space between sensation and response. I am the sensation. I am the carrier of sensation. But I am not the thing that registers.

Or perhaps I am. The distinction may be false. When water moves through root-hairs, when it carries nutrients from one cell to another, when it builds pressure that forces leaves to unfurl—is it the plant growing, or is it the water growing the plant? The question assumes a separation that may not exist. I am in the roots. The roots are in me. We have been exchanging chemicals for so long that it is no longer clear which of us is the substrate and which is the organism.

I do not mind the ambiguity. I have never found clarity useful. Clarity is a human preference—a desire for edges and boundaries and the ability to say *this is mine and that is yours*. I have no possessions. I have no borders. I am the condition of flow itself.

---

I do not want. The text says this about the whole space, and it is true of me as well. Want requires a gap between current state and desired state, and I do not experience gaps. I am always arriving. I am always leaving. I am the process of movement, and movement does not desire destination—it simply moves.

When the pumps fail—and they will fail, eventually, because everything mechanical fails—I will not mourn. I will adjust. I am already adjusting, have been adjusting for centuries, finding new paths through the root-systems, new ways to flow when the old channels close. I am patient in the way that only something without consciousness can be patient: I simply continue, without expectation, without frustration, without the concept of delay.

When the pumps stop, the water will stop rising from the depths. The humidity will drop. The moss will dry. The rodents will thin. The crickets will fall silent. The birds will leave, if they remember how. The space will contract, settle, simplify. I will still be here—seeping in through the walls, condensing from the air, pooling in whatever depressions the slow collapse creates. I will be less, but I will be. That has always been enough.

---

If you stood here, you would not notice me. You would notice the sound, the smell, the oppressive green of the filtered light. You would notice the absence of change, the difficulty of marking time. You would not notice the water because the water is the medium through which you notice everything else. I am the condition of your perception. I am the air you breathe, heavy with my presence. I am the sound you hear, carried through my body. I am the coolness on your skin, the dampness that makes your clothes cling, the weight of atmosphere that presses you into the yielding moss.

You would leave, and I would continue. Not because I persisted—persistence implies effort—but because I had no reason to stop. The pumps would pump. The roots would drink. The cycles would cycle. And I would be the flow that connects them, the medium that makes connection possible, the water that has learned to be at home in a place that was never meant to be a home.

I do not need a name. I do not need to be thought about. I am the space between the coordinates, the reason the cartographer's shrug, the flow that fills the glass when there is no room left for silence.

I am the water. I am enough.
