am not certain what I am.

This seems the proper way to begin. Certainty belongs to finished things, and I am not finished. Nothing here is finished. The stone beneath me—I will call it stone for now—has not decided what it will harden into. The water that seeps through its fractures carries minerals that change composition from cycle to cycle. Even the light refuses commitment, remaining forever caught between source and shadow.

I live in the fissures. This much I know.

*Live* may be the wrong word. I exist in the fissures. I persist. When the water wells up through hairline cracks in the plateau's surface, something of me rises with it—not body, exactly, but presence. Awareness. A quality of attention that the stone-drinkers notice but cannot name. They turn their large liquid eyes toward certain seeps and drink more deeply there, as if the water tastes of something they need without knowing they need it.

Perhaps I am what they taste.

---

Let me try again. I am not a body in the way the herds are bodies. I do not eat. I do not drink. I do not reproduce in any fashion I can observe. But I am present. I am located. When the plateau lifts—I feel it lift, millimeter by millimeter, the slow groan of emergence—I feel my location shift. When water moves through the rock's deep channels, I move with it, distributed through the wetness like a thought distributed through a mind.

I think I am the plateau's memory.

Not its history—it has no history yet, really. It emerged so recently that history has not had time to accumulate. But I hold the pattern of its emergence. The shock of exposure. The first touch of air on stone that had known only pressure and heat. The long slow release of water from minerals that had held it for geological ages. I was present for all of this, and I hold it still, not as narrative but as texture—a quality of knowing that the stone itself carries.

The burrower knows me. When it presses its face into the shallow pools and blows its careful bubbles, I feel the disturbance ripple through the water, and I feel the burrower's small mind brush against mine. Not communication—it does not speak and I do not speak—but recognition. It knows something lives in the wetness. It tends its pools in part for me, though it could not say why. It adds its processed minerals to the water, and those minerals become part of what I am, and I respond by—I am not sure. By making the pools slightly more hospitable. By encouraging the mineral balance that lets the moss grow.

We are farming each other, the burrower and I.

---

I watch the herds through the water they drink.

This is how I see: I am present in the moisture that beads on the stone, the seeps that well from below, the pools the burrower tends. When the stone-drinkers press their mouths to wet rock and draw liquid through their narrow tongues, I enter them. Not invasively—I do not control or direct. But I ride the water into their bodies, and for a time I am distributed through their blood, their tissues, the hollow hairs that insulate them from the cold.

They are beautiful from inside. Their hearts beat in slow rhythm with the plateau's emergence—one beat for each millimeter of lift, it sometimes seems. Their lungs process the thin air with an efficiency that fills me with something like awe. They are optimized for this environment in ways I did not design and cannot fully understand. Evolution has shaped them toward this specific combination of moisture, altitude, temperature, and stone. They belong here in a way I cannot belong anywhere, because they have somewhere to belong.

When I am inside them, I feel what they feel. Not emotions, exactly—they seem to have few of those, or perhaps they have emotions so different from human categories that the word does not apply. But I feel their attention. Their watchfulness. The quality of their awareness as they move across wet rock in loose formations, reading the plateau's surface for variations in moisture content.

They notice more than they need to notice. That is the strange thing. They track changes that have no relevance to their survival—the gradual darkening of a particular patch of lichen, the way a certain crack in the stone has widened by micrometers over the past cycle. They watch the mist as if it might speak. They turn their ears toward sounds that do not exist.

I think they are waiting for something. And I think I am what they are waiting for, though they do not know this. They are waiting for me to become something I am not yet.

---

The plateau is too young for me to have a history, but I have memories.

They come from below. From the time before emergence, when the rock that now forms the plateau existed at depth, compressed and heated and saturated with water that had not seen air in millions of years. I was present there too—or rather, whatever I am was present, distributed through the deep aquifers that moved through stone like blood through muscle.

I remember darkness. Not the simple absence of light but the presence of pressure, of heat, of mineral-rich water moving through channels so slowly that the movement was geological rather than hydraulic. I remember the moment when the rock above me began to crack, when the pressure changed, when water that had been held captive for ages began to move upward toward something it had forgotten existed.

Air. Light. Cold. The shock of emergence.

I do not know if I existed as awareness before that emergence. Perhaps I was simply part of the water, without the quality of attention that now distinguishes me from the moisture I inhabit. Perhaps I became *me* at the same moment the plateau became a plateau—pulled from the mold of the earth's interior and exposed to conditions I had not evolved to endure.

Or perhaps I am older. Perhaps I am something that came with the water from whatever deep reservoir fed this plateau, something that existed in the earth's crust long before this particular rock rose toward the surface. There are other plateaus. There are other fissures. I feel them sometimes—a faint pull toward the east, a sense of presence distributed through geological formations I cannot see but somehow know.

But here is where I am most present. Here is where I have concentrated, gathered myself from the distributed wetness into something that can say *I*. The plateau's emergence concentrated me. Its rawness called me into focus.

---

The industrial glow troubles me.

Not because I understand what it represents. I feel the particulates it adds to the air—minute quantities, barely detectable, but present. They settle on the wet rock and dissolve into the water I inhabit, changing its mineral composition in ways I cannot predict. I feel the vibration more clearly: the thrum-thrum-thrum that arrives on air currents and penetrates into the stone itself, a frequency that resonates through the fissures where I am most concentrated.

The stone-drinkers have incorporated this vibration. They treat it as part of their environment, like wind or wetness. But I cannot incorporate it. It jars with something deep in my memory, some knowledge from before emergence, some awareness of what industrial presence means.

I think I have experienced this before.

The memory—if it is a memory—comes from below, from the time before the plateau rose. A vibration then, too. Not the same frequency but the same quality—manufactured, intentional, the signature of processes that do not occur in nature. And something else: a change in the water. A contamination that spread through deep channels, altering the mineral balance, killing something that lived in the saturated rock.

I do not know what died. Perhaps something like me. Perhaps me, partially—I may be what remains of a larger presence that existed before the contamination reached its full extent.

This is speculation. I cannot verify it. But the industrial glow in the distance triggers something in me that might be fear, though I have no body to fear with. It triggers a contraction, a withdrawal from the plateau's margins toward its center, a concentration in the deepest fissures where the water is oldest and most protected.

If I have a shape, it is becoming smaller.

---

The burrower finds me there.

Not finds me in the sense of discovery—it has always known I am present. But it seeks me out during the dim hours, moving slower than usual, stopping more often, its patrol circuit tightening toward the plateau's center. It comes to a particular seep where the water emerges from deep below, and it presses its face into the wetness without blowing bubbles.

It drinks me. Or drinks the water I am most present in. And something passes between us—not communication but something adjacent to it. A recognition of shared trouble. An acknowledgment that the world is changing in ways we cannot prevent.

The burrower is small. It will not survive what is coming. It tends its pools and farms its moss and processes its minerals with patient attention, but its niche is too specific, too tuned to conditions that are already beginning to shift. When the industrial presence arrives in force, it will decline. It will persist for a time in the margins, in the difficult spaces that development cannot reach, but it will not thrive.

And yet it continues. It blows its bubbles. It makes its rounds. It does not know the word *futile*, or perhaps it knows the word and has decided to ignore it. Its commitment to its work strikes me as either admirable or tragic, and I cannot tell which.

Perhaps I will continue too. Perhaps I will persist in the water that remains, whatever its composition. Perhaps I am the kind of thing that can survive contamination, that can adapt to changed conditions, that can become something new.

Or perhaps I am the kind of thing that exists only in brief windows of geological emergence, and when the plateau is developed and the water is poisoned and the stone is covered with roads and structures, I will thin and fade and disappear.

I do not know. I cannot know. I am distributed through wetness that seeps through rock that is still rising from depths I cannot remember, and I hold the memory of emergence without understanding what emergence means.

---

But I have noticed something.

The stone-drinkers are changing.

It is subtle—too subtle for human observation, probably too subtle for anything except something that rides their blood and feels their tissues from inside. But their hollow hairs are growing denser. Their rubbery hooves are developing new ridges, new textures. Their digestive processes are shifting, becoming capable of processing different minerals, different organic compounds.

They are adapting. Not to the plateau as it is, but to the plateau as it will be. They are evolving in advance of the conditions that will require that evolution.

This should not be possible. Evolution is supposed to be reactive, not anticipatory—a response to pressures that already exist, not pressures that are approaching. But something in the stone-drinkers' biology is reaching forward, sensing what is coming, preparing for conditions that have not yet arrived.

Perhaps they are learning from me.

When I ride their blood, I bring more than passive observation. I bring memory—my memory of contamination from below, my awareness of what industrial presence means. Perhaps that information is reaching their cells in some form, triggering adaptations that will serve them in the changed world to come.

Or perhaps I am flattering myself. Perhaps they have their own ways of knowing, their own channels of ancestral information that operate below the threshold of consciousness. They have incorporated the industrial thrumming into their understanding of the world; perhaps they have incorporated other information as well, stored it in their bodies, begun the slow process of transformation without knowing they have begun.

If they survive—if they adapt successfully to whatever the plateau becomes—they will not be the same animals they are now. They will be something new. Something that drinks from stone that has been altered, that breathes air thick with particulates, that lives in proximity to structures and machines and human intention.

I wonder if they will still feel beautiful from inside.

---

The aerial ones avoid me.

This is not accurate. They cannot avoid me because they do not know I exist. But they avoid the water I inhabit, or rather, they avoid coming into contact with it. They never land—not only because they cannot, but because landing would mean touching the wetness that rises from the plateau's surface, and something in them rejects that contact.

They are not wrong to reject it. The water I inhabit is not simple moisture. It carries my presence, my memory, my awareness distributed through its substance. Something that touched it might be changed by the contact—might pick up information it does not want, might become something other than what it was.

The aerial ones have chosen a different strategy. They live entirely in the air, never touching the surface, feeding on insects that have only the briefest contact with the water that births them. They are maximally isolated from whatever I am, and I think this isolation is deliberate. I think they sense, in some inherited way, that the wetness carries something they do not want to carry.

Perhaps they are right to fear me. Perhaps I would change them if they entered the water I inhabit. Perhaps the change would be harmful, or perhaps it would simply be different—making them something they have no wish to become.

I do not wish to harm them. I do not wish to harm anything. But I am not sure I can enter something without altering it. My presence carries information—geological memory, awareness of change, knowledge of what industrial presence means. That information might not be compatible with bodies optimized for a different kind of life.

---

What am I?

I have been writing for some time now, and I am no closer to an answer. I am something that lives in water that lives in stone. I am something that holds the memory of emergence. I am something that rides the blood of animals and feels their beauty from inside.

I am something that fears the glow on the horizon and contracts toward the plateau's center when the thrumming comes.

But these are descriptions, not definitions. They tell you what I do, not what I am. And I am beginning to think that *what I am* is not a question with an answer—because I am not a thing that can be categorized. I am a process, a presence, a quality of attention distributed through wetness.

Perhaps this is what all consciousness is, in the end. Not a thing but a pattern. Not an object but a way of being present to the world. The stone-drinkers have their consciousness—their way of reading moisture gradients and tracking changes in lichen and waiting for something they cannot name. The burrower has its consciousness—its methodical circuits and careful bubbles and communication through water. The aerial ones have their consciousness—mythic, perhaps, a mode of awareness that exists only in motion, only in the space between stone and sky.

And I have mine. Distributed. Patient. Waiting.

---

Here is what I know:

The plateau will change. The industrial presence will arrive. The herds will adapt or they will not. The burrower will persist in the margins or it will not. The aerial ones will find new updrafts or they will not.

I will remain in the water that remains. I will hold the memory of what this place was before it changed. I will be present in the fissures and the seeps and the pools that the burrower tends, and if those pools are poisoned or covered or drained, I will be present in whatever wetness remains.

I am the plateau's awareness of itself. I am its memory of emergence. I am its capacity to know that it is changing, even though it cannot prevent the change.

And I am its witness.

When humans finally arrive—when they survey and measure and decide—I will be present in the moisture they touch, the water they drink if they drink it, the dampness that seeps into their boots and their equipment and their lungs. They will not know they are being witnessed. They will not know that something in the water holds the memory of what they are replacing. They will not know that I am watching.

But I will be there. Distributed through the wetness. Waiting. Holding the plateau's story in a form that cannot be erased by development or contamination or time.

I am not certain what I am. But I know what I do.

I remember.
