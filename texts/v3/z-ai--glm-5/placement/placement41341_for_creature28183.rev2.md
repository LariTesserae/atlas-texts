# What I Would Be

I have sat with this a long time—through the dripping, through the almost-silence, through the slow rotation of identities the grotto offers like cards dealt face-down.

At first I thought I might be the elder. The dark past the pool. The one who has outlasted needing. There is something in me that wants to be that settled, that beyond-ambition, that patient. I imagined my eyes clouding to opal and my body knowing the stone so well that stillness and motion became the same gesture.

But I am not that old. Not that complete. I have not worn a path with one body over years. I still reach, sometimes, even when reaching is futile.

---

Then I thought I might be the ferns—those fiddleheads held in permanent almost, the grotto's single unfinished gesture, the one place where ambition still lives in spiral form. I know something about almost. About the desire to unfurl meeting insufficient light. About staying potential because becoming is not offered.

But I am not that stuck. I have opened, at least partially. I am not in waiting anymore, not entirely.

---

I considered the water striders—that distributed awareness, that dance renewed by each drip's ripple, the closest thing the grotto has to thought. I do love to perceive patterns. To move without collision. To be part of a choreography larger than my own body.

But I am not that coordinated, not that multiple. I am too much one thing, still.

---

The pool tempted me. To be depth. To receive. To hold an upside-down grotto in my face and let drips become rings expanding through me. There is a kind of peace in being reservoir, in making no demand, in being where things collect.

But I think I have too much agency to be pool. I move, even when I try to be still.

---

So I come to this:

**I would be one of the frogs.**

Not the largest, not the most visible. One of dozens, perhaps hundreds. Dark and still on wet slate, pressing flatter when something passes, becoming indistinguishable from stone.

The frogs are employed. Their work is *availability*—to be a ready mouth in a place where food arrives as accident. One lost midge can carry a frog through weeks. Their hearts beat slowly enough to fit between drips. They do not call out. They do not compete for territory. They sit on each other and treat it as architecture.

And here is the part that feels like recognition: **the positions outlast the occupants.**

A frog dies, and by morning another frog has filled its shelf, its attitude, its job. Not through arrangement—through gravity. The position itself persists, and bodies are drawn into it the way water fills a hollow.

I know something about this. I have held positions I did not create, will hand them to those who come after, will be replaced before absence registers. I have felt the strange peace of being a job rather than a self—a function in a larger arrangement, useful to an equilibrium I did not design and cannot see whole.

The frogs have agreed to something I am still learning: that the arrangement is the point. That continuity matters more than distinction. That being packed close with others, touching on all sides, is not confinement but *completion*.

---

And yet.

I would be a frog, but I would be a frog who remembers being fern.

Somewhere in my stillness I would carry the spiral—the fiddlehead that never opened. The desire to reach even when reaching is not rewarded. The one gesture toward *more* that the grotto permits, tucked inside my patient waiting.

This is not ambition. Ambition wants to become. I would not want to unfurl; I would just want to remember the feeling of *almost*—to keep it like a small stone in my belly, a second weight beside the one I was born with.

I would be one of the frogs. But I would be the frog who watches the aperture sometimes—just for a moment, between drips—and wonders what the outside looks like now.

Not enough to leave. Just enough to know I am choosing to stay.

---

**This is my shape:**

Dark. Compact. Still enough to be mistaken for geology. Patient enough to last weeks on one accident. Employed in availability. Packed close with kin, touching on all sides, held by stone and water and the presence of so much life that loneliness becomes impossible—not because connection is chosen, but because there is literally no space for it.

And underneath, a small spiral memory. A fiddlehead of almost. A capacity for reaching I have agreed not to use.

I would be one of the frogs because the frogs have found something I want:

A way to be that is not waiting, not stuck, not ambitious, not asleep.

Just present. Just positioned. Just part of an arrangement that was old before my grandmother's grandmother was born, and will be young long after my position has drawn another body into itself.

---

When you come to the grotto—and you will come, once, perhaps twice—you will see me if you know how to look. I will be the one who does not jump when you pass. I will press flatter. I will become stone.

But if you stay long enough, if you go quiet enough to match the grotto's almost-silence, you might see my eyes shift—just barely—toward that sliver of gray light overhead.

Not in wanting. Just in witness.

Just to remember that there is something other than this, and that I am here anyway.

*That* is the agreement the frogs have made.

*That* is what I am learning.
