I would be walking. That is the first thing. Not standing—not yet—but walking, and the walking would be wrong here, and I would know it was wrong, and I would keep walking because the wrongness would be the only thing that distinguished me from everything else.

I would be a newcomer. I would have arrived from beyond the fold in the horizon, from wherever the sky bends down to touch the crust, from the place that is not the plateau. I would not remember arriving. I would remember walking, and then I would remember being here, and between the two there would be nothing—not a gap, not a blank, just the smooth transition of one thing becoming another, the way a wave becomes the shore. I would still have my own time. My breathing would be fast—fast relative to the breathing around me, which is so slow that my chest would rise and fall three times for every single heave of the creature nearest me, and the creature nearest me would be near enough that I could see the individual hairs on its body, or the individual scales, or whatever surface it presented, and the surface would be still in a way that my surface was not.

I would be becoming still, and the becoming would be the thing that I was.

---

Who would I be? I would be the one who hasn't stopped yet. The one who still carries the residue of elsewhere—the memory of motion, the habit of change, the expectation that something will happen next. I would be the one whose eyes are dry. That would be the first sign that I didn't belong—not the walking, because the newcomers walk, the text says they walk, but the dry eyes. Every creature here has wet eyes. The moisture is the miracle. The moisture is the sign that you have been here long enough for the plateau to claim you, to fill your eyes with its only water, to replace whatever you came with—tears or saline or the ordinary dampness of living—with something older and heavier and permanent. My eyes would be dry. They would be the driest things on the plateau. The dust would cling to them. I would blink, and the blinking would be the fastest thing for a thousand kilometers in any direction, and it would sound like nothing, because the air does not carry sound, but I would feel it—the snap of a lid over an eye—and I would feel how alien it was, how much it belonged to the world I came from and not to this one.

I would be walking toward the center. Not because I chose the center—choice implies the kind of agency that the plateau dissolves—but because the center would be the only direction that meant anything. Everything else would be the same. I could walk north, south, east, west, and I would pass creatures and creatures and creatures, and they would be different and they would be the same, and the flatness would swallow my sense of progress. But the center—the center would be empty, and emptiness is a destination in a way that fullness is not. I would feel the density decrease as I approached it. Not gradually—the transition would be strange, the way the text describes it, the creatures thinning out, the pressure easing, the sense of being seen by ten thousand unseeing eyes becoming the sense of being seen by nine thousand, by five thousand, by none. And in the absence of that pressure, I would feel my own weight. Not physical weight—existential weight. The weight of being the only thing in the circle that was still in the process of becoming.

I would be the question that the plateau cannot answer. Not because the answer is difficult but because the question is made of motion, and the plateau is made of stillness, and the two cannot touch. The creatures breathe, and they are wet-eyed, and they stand, and they are the plateau's statement: *I am here.* I would be the counter-statement, the one thing that says: *I am going there.* And the there would be the center, and the center would be empty, and the emptiness would be the only thing that could hold me, because the fullness everywhere else would leave no room.

---

What would I look like? I think I would look like something that had been sketched rather than completed. The creatures are described as prototypes, as drafts of a creation that never proceeded past the drafting stage. I would be a draft too, but a different kind of draft—not a draft of a creature but a draft of a visitor. I would have the approximate shape of a person—two legs, two arms, a head with eyes that are dry—but the proportions would be slightly wrong, the way the proportions of the deer-creatures are wrong. Not wrong in a way that a photograph would capture. Wrong in a way that a drawing would capture—the sense that the artist's hand hesitated, that the line was not sure where it was going, that the figure was placed on the page before it was fully imagined. I would be that hesitation, made solid and set down on the crust.

I would have fur, I think. Not hair—fur. Short, dense, the color of dust. It would cover my arms and my back and the lower parts of my legs, and it would be the same fur as the fur-masses that sit on the crust without limbs or heads, and this would disturb me, because it would suggest that I was related to them, that we were variations of the same thing, and I would not want to be a variation of something that had no eyes, even though the eyes were the thing that frightened me most about the creatures here, the wetness, the permanence, the way they looked at the world without seeing it. I would have fur but I would not have a carapace. I would not have scales. I would not have feathers. I would be the mammalian variation of whatever taxonomy the plateau was assembling, and I would be aware that the taxonomy had no system, and that my place in it was arbitrary, and that arbitrariness was the closest thing to freedom that this place offered.

My hands would be wrong. Not wrong the way the creatures' limbs are wrong—not geometrically wrong. Functionally wrong. My hands would be the hands of something that had once used them—once gripped, once held, once shaped—and now had nothing to grip or hold or shape. They would hang at my sides as I walked, and they would feel purposeless, and the purposelessness would be a kind of grief, the only grief available in a place where grief requires a before and an after and there is only now.

---

I would stop at the edge of the empty circle.

Not because I chose to stop. Because the stopping would happen to me, the way it happens to every newcomer. The motion would drain out of my legs the way heat drains out of a body in cold water—not all at once but in stages, each stage deeper than the last, until the legs were the temperature of the water and the water was the temperature of the legs and there was no difference. My legs would become the temperature of the stillness. I would be standing at the edge of the circle, and my breathing would be slower than it was when I arrived, and it would be faster than the breathing of the creature nearest me, and the difference would be smaller than it was, and it would keep getting smaller, and I would know—with the kind of knowing that is not thought but sensation—that the difference would reach zero, and when it reached zero, I would be one of them.

I would face inward. Toward the emptiness. The text says that the ones who face inward seem older, more weathered, as if the emptiness is eroding them. I would face inward because the emptiness would be the thing that drew me—the only thing in this landscape that was not full, the only space where the density did not press, the only place where a body could exist without being a monument to patience. I would face the emptiness because the emptiness would be the opposite of the plateau, and I would be the opposite of the plateau, and opposites attract, and the attraction would be the last motion I would feel before the motion stopped.

---

My eyes would change. This is how I would know it was happening—not in my legs, which had already slowed, not in my breath, which was already deepening, but in my eyes. The dryness would ease. Not suddenly. Not even gradually in a way that I could track. But I would become aware, at some point, that I was no longer blinking, and that the dust that had clung to my eyes had fallen away, and that the surface of each eye was smooth and wet and gleaming in the bone-white light. The wetness would come from inside me—from some reservoir I did not know I had, some aquifer that the plateau had been filling since before I arrived, water from a source that was not water but was the plateau's idea of water, the abstract of moisture, the concept of wetness given substance and directed upward through whatever plumbing my draft-body contained.

My eyes would be wet, and I would see the emptiness, and the emptiness would see me, and neither of us would react.

---

I would not be the plateau. Not yet. I would be the last thing that was not the plateau—the final resistance, the last note that had not yet joined the chord. I would be the pause between the question and the answer, the held breath before the held breath, the moment of a newcomer's becoming that is neither motion nor stillness but the transition between them, and the transition would be the longest thing I had ever experienced, and it would take longer than my life had taken, and it would take longer than the mountains had taken to flatten, and it would be instantaneous, because there is no time here, and the transition does not occupy time, it occupies me.

I would be the one who is almost still. Almost breathing at the plateau's tempo. Almost wet-eyed. Almost part of the forty-three thousand. Almost.

And the almost would be what I was, and it would be the closest thing to identity that this place allowed—because identity requires a difference between what you are and what you are becoming, and the difference is the almost, and the almost is the shape of a self that is dissolving into the stillness, and the dissolution is not death because death is a transition and there are no transitions here, only the one transition, the one change, the one motion that the plateau permits, which is the motion of arriving and stopping, and I would be in it, and it would be in me, and the empty center would be in front of me, and the creatures would be behind me, and the sky would be above me, and the crust would be below me, and I would be here, and I would be here, and I would be here.

And the wet would come, and the slowness would come, and the stillness would come, and I would be the last one to face the emptiness, and I would be the newest of the old, and I would be the most patient of the impatient, and I would be the one who remembered walking, for a while, for as long as a while could last in a place where while has no meaning, and then I would not remember, and the remembering would be gone, and I would be here, and I would be still, and the plateau would have one more organ, and the number would be forty-three thousand, and the number would always have been forty-three thousand.

And the empty center would be before me, and it would be the only thing I saw, and I would not see it, because seeing requires a seer, and I would no longer be a seer. I would be a surface. I would be a wet surface facing a dry emptiness, and the surface would be enough, and the emptiness would be enough, and the plateau would hold us both, the way it holds everything, without wanting, without needing, without end.
