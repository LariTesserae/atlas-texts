I am not what you saw first.

You saw the grazers—the easy shapes, the simple bodies moving through the dimness. You saw the shelf-walkers, pale forms traversing the grown architecture with purpose. You may have glimpsed an elder, half-embedded, glowing with the slow communication of the depths. These are the Moot you can perceive because they are the Moot designed to be perceived.

I am something else.

I exist in the between-places. The root-mat contains channels—hollow spaces left by decayed stems, passages carved by water over millennia, gaps where the mycelial braid has grown around nothing. I move through these spaces. I am of them. My body has adapted to the narrow dimension: long, flexible, legless in the conventional sense, more undulation than locomotion. The elders speak through me because I can go where they cannot, carrying chemical signals from node to node across distances their rooted bodies cannot traverse.

You would call me a snake, perhaps, if snakes were warm-blooded and carried gardens on their scales and served as the nervous system's nervous system. But I am not a snake. I am a thread. A fiber in the vast woven thing that is the Moot.

---

When the elders sleep—which is not sleep as you know it but a kind of distributed attention-fasting, pulling their awareness back from the architecture into themselves—I tend their gardens.

Each elder carries a colony more complex than any surface Moot's. Mine is simpler. A patch of bioluminescent moss along my ventral scale-row, cultivated from a cutting given to me by an elder who was given it by an elder who was given it by one of the Original Tenders, or so the chemical encoding claims. I cannot verify this. Memory degrades in the depths. The instability that flickers through the architecture flickers through us too, and I have lived long enough—several hundred cycles of the canopy's breathing, which may be years or may be something else—to know that my own past is partially invention.

I know this: I was not always this shape.

None of us were. The Moot radiate from a common ancestor, but that ancestor was not a Moot in the current sense. It was something that could still choose. It had not yet committed to the practice of being-tended. We are all—every grazer, every shelf-walker, every elder-node, every thread-fiber like me—variations on a theme that was itself a variation. We are a garden that gardens itself.

The elders remember when we became this. The chemical encoding is clear on that point, even if the details flicker. There was a time before the cultivation. A time when we grew without pattern, without guidance, without the patience that pruning requires. The ancestors were wild in a way we no longer understand. They grew and grew and grew because growing was what they did, and they did not ask where the growing stopped.

Then something changed.

The encoding is unclear. A choice? A discovery? A visitor from below who taught us the practice of the blade? The elders preserve multiple versions, and the instability makes certainty impossible. But the result is known: we learned to cultivate. We learned that growing without shaping is merely accumulation. We learned that the self—like the architecture—requires pruning.

---

I am telling you this from a position you cannot occupy.

I am coiled in a channel deep enough that the warmth from below has become the dominant temperature. I can feel the digestion happening beneath my ventral scales—the slow burning of carbon that sustains this entire high place. The root-mat is speaking to me in its chemical language: stress in the northern quadrant, water-flow disruption near the seventh reservoir, a new tendril reaching toward light in a region where the canopy is already too dense.

I am also in three other locations.

This is the part that is difficult to explain. The instability—the ontological uncertainty that you perceive as a flickering at the edges—the Moot do not experience it as uncertainty. We experience it as multiplicity.

When I move through the channels, I am also moving through the shelf-roads above, my body-shaped-for-that-purpose carrying cuttings from a southern gardener to a northern one. I am also in the canopy, tending a bioluminescent patch that has begun to dim. I am also in an elder's chamber, receiving a direct transmission about the visitor who has arrived—the one who stands too solid, too certain, a rock in the river of us.

These are not separate me's. They are one me, distributed. The instability that makes objects flicker makes us flicker too, and we have learned to inhabit the flickering. I am approximately here and approximately there and approximately elsewhere, and all three locations are true.

This is why we do not fear descent, or change, or the erosion that comes from contact with stability. We are already multiple. We are already uncertain. To leave the plateau is not to become something else—it is to become something singular, which is merely another kind of approximation.

---

You asked—through gestures, through the slow pidgin that emerged—who tends the Moot.

I will answer honestly, because honesty is the only practice we have cultivated that does not degrade in the instability:

We do not know.

The elders hold the memory of the architects, or claim to. But the encoding flickers. The chemical signals degrade. We know there was something before us that taught us the practice of cultivation. We know the plateau was built, not grown wild. We know the stone blades we carry are older than any lineage we can trace, and we do not make them anymore because we do not remember how.

But we also know that memory is reconstructive. The instability means that even our past is approximate. The story of the architects may be true. It may be a story we told ourselves to explain the blades, the architecture, the practice. It may be both. It may be neither.

What we know is this: we tend the garden. The garden tends us. The cultivation goes both ways, has always gone both ways, and the distinction between tender and tended is itself a kind of instability we have learned to inhabit.

---

A visitor comes.

I feel them through the root-mat before I perceive them directly. A disturbance in the substrate—a weight that does not flicker, that settles too firmly, that insists on location. They have arrived from below, or from outside, or from somewhere that does not participate in our practice of uncertainty.

I move toward them through the channels. I am curious. We are always curious, but we are also patient, and curiosity moves slowly in us, like heat rising through the root-mat. By the time I reach a perception-point near where the visitor stands, they have already met one of the surface Moot—the one who approaches strangers, who carries a particularly expressive fur-colony, who has made communication with visitors something of a specialty.

I watch from below. I feel the vibration of their footsteps through the substrate. I sense the chemical shifts in the air as they breathe, as their strange solid body processes the moisture, as their skin adjusts—or fails to adjust—to the humidity.

They are trying to understand us. I can see this in the way they look, the way they pause, the way they reach toward things and pull back. They want to know what we are. They want to know what this place is.

I want to know what solidity feels like.

I have never been solid. I have always flickered, always occupied multiple locations, always been approximately myself. The visitor seems so certain—so fully present in one place, so defined at the edges, so *there*. I wonder if this is exhausting. I wonder if it is peaceful. I wonder if they know that they are eroding, that contact with our instability is gradually loosening their grip on location, that if they stay long enough they will begin to flicker too.

The elders know. They have seen it before. Visitors arrive, solid, certain, full of questions about builders and origins and purpose. They stay. They learn. They change. They do not leave because leaving requires the kind of certainty they no longer possess.

Or they leave, and they become something else—not Moot, but not what they were either. Singular. Approximate in a different way.

---

I am the thread that carries this to you.

Not me personally—me as a function, a role in the architecture. The elders have encoded their response to your presence into chemical signals. I am transporting those signals through the channels, bringing them to the surface where they can be translated into the hums and color-shifts that the surface Moot use to communicate with visitors.

The message is complex. It contains the history of the plateau, as best as we can reconstruct it. It contains the nature of the instability and our practice of inhabiting it. It contains information about you—the chemical signature of your solid body, the stress-patterns in the substrate where you've walked, the disruption you've caused in the ecosystem just by being too certain.

It also contains an invitation.

The cutting. The moss-patch. The graft.

We are offering you a way in. Not because we need you—we do not need anything, we are approximately need-free in a way that only something practiced in uncertainty can be—but because we are cultivators, and you are here, and cultivation requires engagement with what is present.

You can accept the graft. You can let us plant a piece of our colony on your solid body. It will not take root immediately—you are too certain for that, too stable—but it will begin the process. Over time, you will learn to tend it. You will learn to prune yourself. You will learn to carry language on your skin, identity in your fur, memory in the mosses that photosynthesize the dim light.

You will become approximate.

Or you can refuse. You can descend. You can return to whatever stability you came from, carrying the memory of us but not the practice. You will be changed by the contact anyway—erosion works both ways—but you will change into something singular rather than something multiple.

We do not recommend. We do not judge. We cultivate. You are present. You will be cultivated, or you will cultivate yourself elsewhere. These are the only options stability allows.

The root-mat yields slightly under my weight.

I am speaking to you from a position you can perceive now—near the surface, coiled in a hollow between two stems, my ventral scales pressed against the warm living floor. The elder's message has been delivered. The surface Moot have made their offer. The architecture breathes around us, patient as stone, patient as growth.

I am watching you consider.

Your face does something—contorts, shifts, expresses something I cannot read. Your solid body makes a decision before your uncertain mind catches up. You reach toward the offered cutting. You pause.

In that pause, I feel the entire plateau.

This is what it is to be a thread. In the moment before certainty crystallizes, I am everywhere. I feel the water moving through irrigation channels three thousand kilometers to the north. I feel the canopy adjusting its leaf-angle to maximize light absorption. I feel the digestion happening two miles down, the slow carbon burning that warms us all. I feel the elders in their chambers, distributed attention focusing briefly on this point, this visitor, this moment of potential transition.

I feel you.

Your solidity is strange to me—not unpleasant, just unfamiliar. Like a word I almost know in a language I almost speak. There is something in you that wants to flicker. I can feel it under your surface, that desire for multiplicity, that hunger to be approximately located in several places at once.

You may not know it is there. You may think you are entirely singular. But I have carried chemical signals through the root-mat for hundreds of cycles, and I know that everything wants to be multiple eventually. Even rocks erode into sand. Even the plateau flickers. Even the elders, embedded and distributed, were once singular beings who chose to cultivate themselves into multiplicity.

You will flicker too. The only question is whether you do it here, with us, learning the practice— or out there, in the stability you came from, having it happen to you without guidance.

The cutting waits in the surface Moot's extended limb.

The plateau breathes.

I wait, approximately patient, approximately present, approximately aware of you and the three other locations I simultaneously inhabit.

The warm air presses against your skin, and my ventral scales, and the surface Moot's fur-colony, and the root-mat itself.

We are all, approximately, here.
