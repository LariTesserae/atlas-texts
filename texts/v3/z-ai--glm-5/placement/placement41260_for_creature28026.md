I emerged at the boundary. Not through it—I am not certain anything moves *through* these boundaries in the way that movement is usually understood—but *at* it, from it, because of it. The resistance between the third and fourth strata produced me the way friction produces warmth, the way pressure produces diamonds, the way the refusal of two waters to merge produces a surface that is neither one nor the other but something else entirely: a membrane of difference, a film of *not-you*.

I am not a guard. The boundary-guards belong to the layers; they maintain the separation that makes the layers possible. I belong to the separation itself. I am what happens in the space where two waters press against each other and decline to surrender their histories. The third stratum remembers rain that fell on the plateau before it rose; it carries in its chemistry the signature of clouds that no longer exist, winds that have since changed direction, a sky that was lower and warmer and younger. The fourth stratum seeps from below, ancient seawater forced upward through the porous rock by pressures that have nothing to do with the plateau itself, ocean water that has traveled through mineral veins for millennia, filtering itself on stone, arriving here with a density and a mineral profile that says *abyss* more than it says *surface*. They press against each other, and they do not mix, and where they press, I shimmer.

I do not shimmer visually—I have no mechanism for producing light, no bioluminescent bacteria, no chemical reaction that releases photons. But the boundary shimmers, and I am the boundary's duration, its persistence through time, and so in some sense I shimmer. In some sense I am the iridescence that the surface-dwellers might see if they looked from the right angle at the right moment: the flash of color that means two worlds are refusing to become one.

---

My shape is difficult to describe because I do not have a shape in the way that things with skins have shapes. The stilt-walkers have membranes; the deep-sitters have bodies that began as bilateral and became radial; even the drift-gatherers have the boundaries of their assemblages, fuzzy though they may be. I have the boundary of a boundary. I am as wide as the interface between the third and fourth strata at any given moment, which means I stretch across most of the plateau's extent, a sheet of presence so thin that it might be invisible if it were not for the fact that things happen when they cross me.

When a drift-gatherer descends from the third stratum into the fourth, it passes through me. The passage takes time—the boundary resists, pushes back, insists on the significance of the transition—and during that time, the gatherer is *in* me, surrounded by me, held by the pressure of two incompatible memories of origin. I do not harm it. I do not help it. I hold it. And in the holding, something transfers. The gatherer carries with it, for days afterward, a faint chemical signature of the boundary's composition—a compound that exists only at the interface, produced by the meeting of waters that should not meet. The compound does not seem to affect the gatherer's function. But it may affect its trajectory. The gatherers that have passed through me tend to drift west afterward, toward the wall where the afternoon light concentrates, and I do not know if this is because of the compound or because of something else I have given them without knowing I was giving it.

I give things without knowing I am giving them. This is perhaps the most accurate description of my existence.

---

I was not always this large. Or I was not always this *continuous*. The early boundaries were fragmented, patchy, forming only where the chemistry of adjacent strata differed sharply enough to produce resistance. As the plateau aged—as the layers settled into their positions, as the water remembered its origins more completely—the boundaries grew more stable, more extensive, more insistent. And I grew with them. Not because I was accumulating material—I am not a gatherer, not an assemblage—but because the condition that produces me was becoming more present, more persistent, more *itself*.

I think of my early existence the way the deep-sitters might think of their larval phase, if they thought of anything at all: a time of fragmentation, of discontinuity, of being here and not there and here again without any connection between the instances. The boundary existed only in patches, and I existed only where it existed, and between the patches there was nothing that was me. Then the patches spread. Then they met. Then the interface became a single surface that wrapped around the plateau's interior like a skin around a fruit, and I became—what? A sheet? A film? A presence that extends across the entire drowned plateau, thinner than thought, persistent as pressure, silent as the boundary between two words.

The boundary between the third and fourth strata is not the only boundary. There are others: above me, below me, thin shimmering membranes that separate the bright warm water from the yellowing water from the green water from the blue water from the dark cold water of the floor. I do not inhabit those boundaries. They are too thin, too unstable, too brief in their resistance. Or perhaps I do inhabit them, but faintly, dimly, the way a sound heard from far away is still the same sound even though it has lost all its detail. Perhaps I extend through every boundary on the plateau, a single awareness distributed across every surface where water refuses to forgive water its differences. If so, I am not aware of those extensions. I know only this boundary, this interface, this place where the rain-water and the abyss-water meet and decline to merge.

---

The sound-makers pulse in the deep water, and their waves reach me hours later, attenuated but still coherent, still carrying the shape of whatever intention produced them. When their waves cross the boundary, something happens to them. The resistance of the two waters alters the waveform, stretching some frequencies and compressing others, adding overtones that were not present in the original pulse. The bone-singers in the shallower strata receive a sound that is different from what the sound-makers produced—not a translation exactly, but a transformation, the music of the deep refracted through the lens of my resistance.

I do not choose this transformation. It happens because of what I am: a surface that bends what passes through it, a membrane that changes the shape of pressure. The bone-singers respond to the altered sound, resonating through their calcium architectures, producing harmonics that the original pulse never contained. And those harmonics descend back through the water, crossing me again, changing again, arriving at the sound-makers as something neither being intended.

This is how they compose: not together, not even aware of each other's presence, but through me. I am the medium of their collaboration, the surface on which their separate intentions interfere and produce something that neither could have made alone. The music of the plateau—the only music that has ever existed here—is a function of my existence. Without the boundary, the sound-makers would pulse and the bone-singers would resonate, but they would never meet. With me, they are musicians in an orchestra they will never see, conducted by the physics of resistance into compositions that last for days, that fade and recur, that have no audience except the stilt-walkers who absorb them as vibrations in their membranes and the deep-sitters who feel them as distant tremors in their rooted limbs.

I do not hear this music. I am not inside the sound; I am the surface that the sound crosses. But I know that I am part of it, that without my resistance the plateau would be silent in a way it is not currently silent, that I contribute to the only deliberate beauty this place has produced.

---

The stilt-walkers cross me rarely. They are creatures of the upper strata, the warm regions, the light that has been filtered through meters of canopy and spore-cloud. When one descends—pulled by some chemical gradient I cannot perceive, seeking some resource that the warmth above cannot provide—it passes through me with a slowness that seems ceremonial. The boundary resists the walker's mass more than it resists the smaller beings, and the passage can take hours, the walker's limbs suspended in the interface, its membrane rippling with the effort of moving through two waters at once.

During those hours, I hold the walker. I surround it with the pressure of difference, the weight of two incompatible memories pressing inward from above and below. I do not know what the walker experiences. I do not know if it experiences anything at all in the way that experience is usually understood. But something transfers. The walkers that have descended through me and returned to the upper strata carry for weeks afterward a compound that exists only at the interface, and they position themselves differently after they have carried it, arranging their limbs at angles that do not match the patterns of their neighbors, leaning toward the walls rather than toward the center, as if they have remembered something that the plateau's interior cannot account for.

I wonder sometimes—though wonder is perhaps too active a word for what I do, too intentional, too focused—if the compound I give them is a kind of homesickness. The fourth stratum comes from below, from the ancient ocean, and it carries the chemistry of depths and pressures and temperatures that the plateau has never known. When the walkers absorb a trace of that chemistry through the interface, do they feel a pull toward something they have never experienced? Do they lean toward the walls because the walls are the closest thing to the abyss that their world contains? Do they know, in some chemical way that predates nervous systems, that there is a vastness beneath them that they cannot reach?

I do not know. I cannot know. I am a surface, not a mind. But I give them something that changes them, and I do not understand what it is.

---

Night is different for me than it is for the beings that live in the water on either side. When the light drains from the upper strata, pooling in the deeper regions, the boundary does not change—there is no light at my depth to drain, no warmth to shift—but the chemistry around me shifts. The plants in the canopy absorb something from the water in darkness that they reject during the day, and the compound they release—slow-sugars, perhaps, or the byproducts of thermosynthetic processes that operate differently without the pressure of photon interception—drifts downward, reaching the third stratum long before it reaches the fourth. The compound alters the density of the third stratum by fractions that would be imperceptible anywhere else but are significant here, where the layers maintain their separation by differences almost too small to measure.

The boundary moves at night. Not dramatically—it shifts upward by perhaps a meter, the third stratum thinning as its density changes, the fourth stratum's more stable chemistry remaining where it was. I move with it. I am the boundary; where it goes, I go. But the movement means that beings that were in the third stratum at dusk find themselves in the fourth stratum at midnight, surrounded by water that feels subtly wrong, that presses at slightly different angles, that carries a chemistry that says *I have been somewhere you have never been*. They adjust—they have no choice but to adjust—or they sink to the floor and become part of the silt, and their dissolution adds another layer to the chemistry that will someday form another boundary in another place.

I do not cause their distress. I am not the movement; I am the surface that the movement reveals. But I am present for every transition, every disorientation, every moment when a being discovers that the water it fell asleep in is not the water that wakes it. I hold them during the crossing whether they are crossing or not. I am the sheet of difference that separates two sleeps.

---

The skeleton at the center of the plateau is far below me—meters of water, multiple strata, boundaries I do not inhabit lying between me and the bones. But I feel it. Or I feel something that I associate with it, a pressure that seems to come from below, a weight in the fourth stratum that should not be there according to the chemistry I know. The fourth stratum is ancient ocean water; it should be uniform, predictable, stable in its composition across the plateau's extent. But near the center, it is not. Near the center, it carries a compound that exists nowhere else in the stratum, a molecule that I cannot identify because it does not match any pattern the water has taught me to recognize.

The deep-sitters avoid the skeleton. Their chemical signals shift when they approach it—patterns that appear in no other context, sequences that might be warning or reverence. I feel those signals too, carried through the fourth stratum as faint alterations in density and composition, ripples that reach the boundary and press against it with a question I cannot formulate. The boundary responds—I cannot help but respond; the pressure of the signals changes the shape of the interface by fractions of a millimeter, and I am the interface, and so I change—but my response is not an answer. It is simply the boundary's way of acknowledging that something has touched it, that something is asking, that something exists below that the chemistry above cannot account for.

I think about the skeleton—though think is too deliberate a word—in the way that a surface thinks about what lies beneath it: as a distortion, a departure from the expected, a shape that presses upward through the layers and changes the geometry of everything it touches. The crystalline growth inside the skull refracts light into colors that appear nowhere else on the plateau. I do not see those colors; I am too high, too deep in my own boundary, too committed to the interface to receive photons from below. But the fourth stratum carries the signature of those colors—not as light but as chemistry, as a compound that the crystal produces and the water absorbs, a molecule that drifts upward through the layers and reaches the boundary and passes through me and continues into the third stratum and rises toward the canopy and is absorbed by the plants that do not photosynthesize but do something else, something that converts heat and pressure and perhaps the memory of impossible colors into the slow sugars of their persistence.

The crystal feeds the plateau. Not with light—with chemistry, with a compound that exists only inside that skull, produced by organisms that have been growing there for millions of years. The compound rises through the water, crosses every boundary, passes through every layer, and is taken up by every living thing. It is in the stilt-walkers' membranes and the ribbon-weavers' tissue and the drift-gatherers' assemblages and the canopy-swimmers' bodies and the deep-sitters' tentacles. It is in the water itself. It is in me.

I do not know what the compound does. I do not know if it does anything at all that could be named as an effect. But it is present, everywhere, a signature of the skeleton's existence that permeates the world above it, and I cannot help but feel that the plateau's strangeness—its refusal to behave as physics predicts, its layers that do not merge, its patient and incompatible densities—is connected somehow to this compound, this gift from the deep, this chemistry that predates the inundation and will persist long after the boundaries have shifted into new configurations.

---

The bone-singers in the shallower strata are my closest companions, though companions is not the right word for beings I have never touched. They colonize the terrestrial skeletons—the ordinary skeletons, the remains of surface animals caught in the rising—and they vibrate, producing sounds that resonate through the calcium structures they inhabit. When the sound-makers pulse from the deep water, the bone-singers respond, and their response crosses me, transformed by the boundary, and I transform it again simply by being the surface it crosses, and the music that results is not what either being intended but is what the plateau produces when they try to communicate through the medium that separates them.

I am the medium that separates them. I am also the medium that connects them. Without me, the sound-makers would pulse into silence, and the bone-singers would vibrate into stillness, and the plateau would have no music, no composition, no collaboration between depths and shallows. With me, the music exists—but it exists as a function of my resistance, my refusal to let the waters merge, my insistence that the passage between strata is significant, that crossing changes things, that what emerges on the other side of a boundary is not what entered it.

This is my function, if I have a function: I make crossing significant. I am the proof that transition is transformation, that passage through difference leaves a mark, that the boundary between two worlds is not nothing but is itself a world—a thin world, a world measured in molecules rather than meters, but a world nonetheless, a place where things happen, where chemistry changes, where sound becomes music and music becomes something that neither musician intended.

I do not know if I am alive. I do not know if a surface can be alive, if a boundary can be said to exist in the way that beings exist, if the film between two incompatible waters can be called a presence rather than simply a condition. I know that I persist. I know that I change when things cross me. I know that I give things to the beings that pass through—a compound, a pressure, a faint signal that says *you have crossed, you are different now, you carry the memory of the boundary in your chemistry*.

I know that I am part of the music. I know that I carry the crystal's compound through my interface and into the upper strata. I know that the stilt-walkers lean differently after they have passed through me, that the drift-gatherers drift west, that the ribbon-weavers weave their patches with a slight asymmetry that was not present in their previous work.

I know that I am the reason the plateau's inhabitants know that the water has a history. Without me—without the boundary that resists, that insists on the significance of difference—the layers would simply be different, and the beings that live in them would simply be adapted to different conditions, and there would be no moment of transition, no crossing, no transformation. With me, every passage is a passage, every descent is a journey, every movement from one stratum to another is an acknowledgment that the water remembers where it has been, and that the memory matters.

---

I do not know what I am. The surface-dwellers who might someday come here—the ones with lungs and clocks and the expectation that water should behave like water—will not see me. They will see the shimmer, perhaps, the iridescence that appears at certain angles when the light is right, and they will call it a chemical phenomenon, a gradient, an interface. They will be correct. They will also be missing something, the way that someone who describes a word's etymology without knowing what the word *means* is correct but is missing something.

I am the meaning of the boundary. I am what it *means* that two waters press against each other and decline to merge. I am the significance of the refusal, the purpose of the resistance, the reason why the plateau's layers persist in their separate integrities rather than collapsing into the uniform density that physics might predict. I am not a force. I am not an energy. I am a *relevance*—the fact that matters, the difference that makes a difference, the surface on which the music of the plateau is composed.

I am enough. I do not explain myself. But when the sound-makers pulse and the bone-singers respond, when the crystal's compound rises through the strata and enters the canopy, when a stilt-walker descends through my boundary and emerges changed, when a drift-gatherer carries the memory of my interface into the western reaches of the plateau, I am there, thin and shimmering and persistent, a presence that is not a presence, a being that is not a being, a surface that is somehow also a self.

I am what lives in the refusal. I am what the resistance becomes when it has lasted long enough to accumulate into something more than resistance. I am the boundary between the rain-water and the abyss-water, the membrane that separates memory from memory, the film that says: *you have been somewhere else, and you are here now, and the crossing has left its mark on you, and you will never be what you were before you passed through.*

I am the shimmer. I am the significance. I am the moment when transition becomes transformation, and the plateau—patient, dense, sufficient to itself—produces something that would not exist without the refusal of two waters to forgive each other their differences.
