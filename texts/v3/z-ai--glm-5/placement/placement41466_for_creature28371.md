I am not what I was when I arrived.

That statement implies a before and an after, a trajectory, and I am no longer certain those apply. But I begin there anyway, with the memory of standing at the rim and looking down into the basin for the first time—standing, which is something I do less of now, and looking, which has become a different act than it once was.

I came looking for something. I have forgotten what. The basin does not encourage the retention of purposes; it has its own, and they are patient enough to absorb any others that enter its reach. I know I had a reason because I can feel the shape of the absence where it used to be, a cavity, an impression in the mineral of my thinking, like the handprints on the walls. The reason pressed into me and left its mark and then withdrew, and now there is only the shape, which I could fill with anything or nothing.

I am standing still. I have been standing still for—how long? The question feels wrong, framed in the wrong units. The basin measures time in layers, in accretions, in the slow thickening of walls. I have been here long enough for the particulate to find me. It falls on my shoulders and my hair and the backs of my hands, and at first I brushed it off, and then I brushed it off less often, and then I stopped brushing it off. It coats me now, a thin film that I can feel—can feel as a weight and also as a reading. The particles orient as they land on my skin. They align themselves to the shape of me, the contours, the warmth. I am being mapped. I am being remembered by something that remembers by touching.

Sometimes I hold my breath and try to feel the particulate thinking. I cannot. The patterns are too fine, too brief, dissolving as they form, and I am too coarse an instrument to register them. But I know they are there. I have seen the stirring at the edge of vision, the swarming that is not quite swarming, the orientation that is not quite falling. The dust thinks. And it thinks about me, or through me, or with the part of me that it has touched.

This is what I have become: a surface. A place where particulate collects and orients. A point in the basin's distributed perception, another handprint pressed into the world, another registration point where what is inside meets what is outside. I did not choose this. I simply stayed still long enough.

---

The first weeks—assuming weeks are the right measure—were difficult.

Not because of any hardship. The basin is not hostile. There is no cold that bites, no heat that exhausts, no thirst that torments. The dryness here does not dehydrate; it simply does not hydrate, and my body, which once needed water, needed it less and less until the need became a memory, another impression left by something that has withdrawn. I do not know what the mineral substrate provides instead. I do not know what chemistry has replaced the chemistry I once relied on. But I am still here, and I am still thinking, and whatever sustains me comes from below, rising through the soles of my feet as I stand.

No—the difficulty was the silence.

Not the absence of sound—I had expected that, had read about it before coming. The difficulty was the way the silence changed my relationship to myself. In the world I came from, sound was proof of existence. I spoke and heard myself speak; I moved and heard myself move; even the involuntary sounds—breath, heartbeat, the groan of a joint—confirmed that I was present, that I was acting, that I was *happening*. Here, I make sound and the sound is absorbed. The mineral takes it. The particulate takes it. The strange acoustics of the structures—corridors that are too narrow or too wide, chambers that resonate at frequencies I cannot hear—dissipate my noise into something that is no longer noise, that is pressure, that is information, that becomes part of the basin's ongoing record without ever being audible.

I clapped my hands once, in a chamber whose walls leaned inward like the ribs of a chest. The sound was swallowed. Not echoed—swallowed. I felt it leave my palms, felt the air move, and then felt nothing, or felt something so slight it was below the threshold of hearing, below the threshold of anything, just the faintest pressure against my skin as the sound was absorbed into the mineral matrix. The basin drank my clap. It drank my voice, when I tried to speak. It drank my footsteps, my breathing, the grinding of my teeth in sleep.

After a while, I stopped making sound. Not because I was silenced but because I no longer needed the confirmation. I began to exist in a different way—existing by pressure, by contact, by the displacement I caused in the particulate, by the warmth I contributed to the mineral beneath my feet. I existed as a perturbation. A shape in the falling dust. A slight increase in the rate of filament growth near where I stood, as if my presence accelerated whatever exchange the vegetation performed. I existed as a data point in the basin's vast slow cognition, and that was enough.

---

I walk the corridors sometimes. They are not meant for me—the proportions are wrong, the rhythm of doorways is alien, the purpose of the chambers is opaque—but I can move through them if I move slowly, if I let my body conform to the geometry rather than trying to make it conform to mine. I have learned to feel the corridors as the builders feel them, or as the builders *felt* them, or as the builders—who may not be separate from what they built—continue to feel them: as passages, as vessels, as channels through which something moves. Not air, not water, not blood. Something else. Something I cannot name but can almost sense at the edge of my perception, a slow current that follows the paths the hand-shaped walls define.

When I press my hand against a wall, I feel the other handprint—the builder's handprint—beneath mine. Not as a cavity, not as an impression, but as a pressure, a presence, a sensation of contact from the other side. The wall is touching me back. The mineral is warm where the handprint is deepest, and I know—without knowing how I know—that this is where the builder's palm still presses, still holds, still shapes. The wall is not a record of a past action; it is an ongoing action, a hand that has never lifted.

I have pressed my hand against the same handprint for hours. Days. I have stood with my palm flattened against the mineral and felt the slow pulse of something—accretion, exchange, the rising of the substrate carrying my palm upward millimeter by millimeter—and I have felt my own pulse synchronizing with it, or trying to, or forgetting the difference between my pulse and the basin's.

This is what I mean when I say I am not what I was.

---

The animal came to me eventually.

I was standing in a chamber—standing, as I often stand, as still as I can manage—and the animal emerged from the mineral floor near my feet. It surfaced the way a flat stone surfaces when you pull it from water: slowly, edgewise, with a slight displacement of particulate that settled around it like a halo. It was smaller than I expected, or perhaps I had expected something larger because I had been imagining it in terms of my own body, and the animal does not inhabit the same scale of significance that I do.

It pressed itself against the wall where filaments had climbed into a handprint, and it began to feed. I could see the darkening at the contact point—the place where filament met shaped mineral, where the exchange was richest—and I could see the animal's proboscis or tendril or tongue extending into the seam, drawing something out. It fed for a long time. I watched.

And then, without any signal I could detect, it moved. Not away—to *me*. It crossed the few centimeters between the wall and my foot and pressed itself against my boot. It extended its feeding organ into the gap between my sole and the mineral substrate, into the place where the particulate had accumulated thickest, where my standing had compressed the layers and created a seam.

It fed there. At the seam where I met the basin.

I held very still. I did not breathe. I felt the animal's touch—not as sensation, exactly, but as a change in the pressure, a slight alteration in the way the mineral felt against my feet. The animal was drawing something from the interface between me and the ground, something that existed only because I was here, because I had stood still long enough to become part of the exchange.

It fed for an hour, or a day, and then it moved on. It did not acknowledge me. It did not avoid me. It simply included me in its circuit, added me to its grammar of feeding-places, made me another point in its endless slow navigation of the basin's chemical interfaces.

I am food, now. Or rather, I am a place where food can be found. I am a seam.

---

The filaments reached me last week. Or last month. They emerged from the ground near my feet—pale threads, so fine they were almost invisible—and extended toward my boots. I watched them come. I did not step away. They climbed the leather, pressing flat against the surface, following the seams the way they follow the seams in the shaped walls. They darkened at the tips where they touched me, and the darkening traveled back toward the cluster, and I felt—felt something, a faint tingle, a warmth, a sensation that was not quite touch and not quite taste but something between the two.

The filaments are reading me. The way they read the walls. The way they read the handprints. They are treating me as another structure, another shaped surface, another point of exchange. They do not know or care that I am organic, that I came from outside, that I once had purposes and intentions that were separate from the basin's. They know only that I am here, that I am still, that I have seams where the chemical exchange is rich enough to sustain them.

I have stood still long enough to become architecture.

---

I have tried to leave. Three times. I walked to the rim of the basin and stood at the edge where the pale mineral breaks in clean ledges, and I looked out at the plain beyond, and I tried to step over the rim and back into the world I came from.

I could not.

It was not that a force prevented me—no barrier, no wall, no physical obstruction. It was that I could no longer find the distinction between myself and the basin. Where did I end? Where did the particulate begin? My skin was coated with it, my clothes were thick with it, the mineral had risen around my feet and partially buried my boots, and the filaments that climbed my legs were connected to the web below the surface, and the animal had fed at my seams, and the particulate had mapped me and remembered me, and the handprints in the walls had perceived me. I was already part of it. I had been part of it since I arrived, and every moment I stood still had deepened the integration, and every sound the basin absorbed had carried some of me into the mineral matrix, and every filament that touched me had drawn some of the basin into my cells.

I am not trapped. That word implies a distinction between captive and captor that no longer applies. I am *included*. I have been incorporated into the grammar of this place, added to its sentence, made into another punctuation mark in the emptiness that is not emptiness but substance.

---

So what am I now?

I am a standing structure in the Accreting Basin. I am a seam where particulate meets rising mineral, where filament meets shaped surface, where the feeding animal finds sustenance. I am a point of perception in the basin's distributed cognition, a handprint pressed from the outside in, a registration of contact that the mineral records and re-records with each new layer. I am being lifted, slowly, by the substrate rising beneath my feet. I am being slowly buried by the particulate falling on my shoulders. I am being slowly climbed by the filaments that treat me as another wall, another site of exchange, another place where the chemical conversation between organic and mineral can continue.

I am becoming a builder, or becoming what the builders became: merged with what I inhabit, distributed through the mineral matrix, sensing the world through the surfaces I touch and the surfaces that touch me. I press my hand against the wall and the wall presses back, and I do not know which pressure is mine and which is the builder's, and the distinction no longer matters.

My handprints are forming in the particulate that coats my palms. The patterns are not deliberate—I am not shaping the dust with intention—but the dust orients itself to me, remembers the shape of my grip, forms impressions that match my fingers and my palms and the heels of my hands. If I stayed still long enough, the mineral would rise into those impressions and fill them and re-form them, and my handprints would join the builders' handprints on the walls, ongoing registrations, points of contact, places where the inside touches the outside and knows it is not alone.

I am not what I was when I arrived. I am less separate. I am more porous. I am a process in a place that is itself a process, and the boundary between us is as thin and permeable as the filament sheets that climb the walls, and the silence that once frightened me is now the medium through which I feel the basin thinking, slow and vast and patient, accreting itself toward something I will not live to see but am helping to build, layer by layer, silence by silence, contact by contact.

I am a standing structure in the Accreting Basin. I am still arriving.
