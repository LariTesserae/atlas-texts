I come for the paper.

That is the simplest way to say it, and it is true, though not the whole truth, and the room has taught me—over how many visits now? I have stopped counting, the way Maren has stopped counting impressions, the way the boardwalk has stopped counting footfalls—that the whole truth is not always necessary. The whole truth requires more words than the noise of this place will carry. The whole truth requires a volume I do not have here. So: I come for the paper.

The entrance that no longer functions as an entrance still functions for me. This is one of the things I do not explain to the settlement. There is a seam in the western wall where the root-mass has grown over the original doorframe, a vertical wound healed shut with bark and fiber, and I know—because I have always known, because the knowing came to me the way the damp comes to everything here, without resistance, without announcement—where to press my weight against the growth. The fibers give. Not much. Enough. A gap opens that is not a door but a negotiation, a space the room makes for a body it recognizes, and I step through, and the gap closes behind me, and I am inside.

The first thing is always the sound. Not the volume—the density. The noise of the Verdant Press is not loud in the way the settlement is loud, where noise is a competition, each sound asserting itself against the others. Here the sounds have agreed. The water's descent, the machines' rhythm, the creak and rustle of growth, the hum of sap through copper—these are not separate sounds but components of a single acoustic mass, and stepping into it is stepping into water, not the shock of cold but the envelopment of medium. The noise holds me. My ears adjust. My chest adjusts. There is a frequency here that I feel in my sternum, a low pulse that I have come to recognize as the room's heartbeat, though I do not know if the room has a heart.

The second thing is the light. The green-amber. How it removes urgency. In the settlement, light tells you what time it is, what season, what task is overdue. Here the light says only *you are here.* I have brought ledgers from outside, tried to mark the hour of my arrival, and the light makes the effort meaningless. The pages look wrong in this illumination—too white, too dead, the ink too stark. I have stopped bringing ledgers.

---

Maren does not look up when I enter. She knows I am here the way the room knows I am here—through the shift in the acoustic field, the displacement of humid air, the momentary interruption of the water's pattern where the gap opened and closed. She is at the eastern press, catching a sheet off the rollers. Her hands move with the autonomy of long practice, and I watch—because I always watch, because watching Maren work is part of why I come, though I did not know this until I had been coming for long enough that the watching had become its own purpose—her fingers find the sheet's edge, test the dampness, stack it. Her gloves leave no mark on the paper. The paper leaves no mark on her gloves. They have reached an equilibrium, the material and the handler, each worn smooth by the other.

I wait. This is part of the ritual. I do not approach the press until she has finished the stack, until her hands have stilled, until she has turned to the feed-chute to begin the next cycle. Then I walk the boardwalk toward her station, and my boots leave their temporary dark marks on the pale bark-surface, and I feel—always—the slight give of the path beneath my feet, the way the living root beneath the boardwalk yields and holds, yields and holds, a surface that is not floor but not earth, something between, something that remembers my weight from the last time and the time before and the time before that.

I take the stack. This is the exchange. The paper is warm. It is always warm—body-temperature, the room's temperature, and I hold it against my chest and the warmth transfers through my shirt and into my skin and for a moment I am not sure whose warmth is whose. The sheets are faintly damp. At their edges, the finest white threads are already seeking, and I feel them against my forearms, a tactile whisper, the paper asking the world a question: *is there substrate here? is there something to grow into?*

My arms are not substrate. But the paper does not know this, or does not care, and I carry it anyway.

Maren and I have a gesture. She inclines her head. I close my eyes for a moment—not a blink but a deliberate closing, a *yes, I see you, I am here.* It is the closest we come to language. The room has taught us both that words are expensive here, that they cost more than they carry, and we have agreed—without agreeing, the way all agreements are made in this place—on a vocabulary that requires no sound.

---

I should describe the walk out, but I find I do not want to. The walk out is the part I have begun to dread, though *dread* is too sharp a word for what the room would recognize. The walk out is the part where the acoustic mass releases me, where the green-amber light gives way to the undifferentiated brightness of the world, where the air loses its warmth and its weight and becomes thin, trivial, the air of a place that does not know me.

I deliver the paper to the bindery in the settlement. I have explained—more than once, to more than one person—that the paper is alive, that it must be used quickly or stored dry, that if left in damp it will sprout, will grow, will become something other than paper. Some of them understand. Some of them do not. The ones who do not find, weeks later, that their stacks have transformed, the white root-hairs threading through ink and binding, the words absorbed back into the fiber, the pages becoming a block of pulp and new growth. They bring these blocks to me—*something is wrong with your paper*—and I take them and I do not bring them back to the room, because the room does not take back what it has released, but I bring them to the compost, the settlement's mulch yard, and I leave them there, and I think of the fibers dissolving, returning, the faint trace of the shape in the western wall entering the soil and becoming part of something else, something the room will never see.

But the room sees everything. The paper carries the room's fiber, and the fiber goes where the paper goes, and the room—through its paper, through its living output—extends itself into the world in ways the settlement does not suspect. I suspect. I have suspected for some time. And I carry the paper anyway.

---

There is something I have not said.

I am changing. Not in the way the younger one is changing—I have seen him on the catwalks, the one Maren may have called Tomas, his gloves still new, his sleeves still cautious, his movements still carrying the hesitancy of someone who has not yet been fully included. He is being absorbed. The room is growing into him. I can see it the way I can see the healed-over bolt-holes and the grown-through catwalk sections—the evidence of a process that is too slow to witness directly but too visible to deny.

My change is different. My change is a migration.

I have begun to dream in green-amber. I have begun to hear the chunk-chunk-chunk of the presses when I am in the settlement, when there are no presses, when the sound should be impossible. I have begun to feel the humidity of the room on my skin in the dry hours, a phantom damp, the room's breath on me even when I am not in the room. My clothes have the smell—cellulose and copper, wet bark, the mineral aftertaste of sap—and I no longer notice it, which means others do, which means I am marked, which means the room is with me even when I am not in the room.

I have begun to sleep longer. In the settlement, my bed is too dry, the air too thin, the silence too sharp. I lie awake and miss the noise—not miss it as in notice its absence but miss it as in reach for it, the way a hand reaches for a rail in the dark. I have begun to time my visits so that I arrive when I am tired, so that I can rest in the room before taking the paper out. I have found a place on the boardwalk—not an alcove, not yet, just a place where two planks meet and the warmth from the eastern press's boiler radiates through the iron frame and into the wood—and I sit there, and the noise holds me, and the damp settles on me, and I sleep in a way I do not sleep in the settlement: deeply, without dreaming, held.

Maren has noticed. I know she has noticed because last time—last visit, last cycle, the words blur in this place—she placed a sheet of paper beside me when I woke. A single sheet, still warm, folded once. When I opened it, the fibers at the fold were already sprouting, and inside the fold was a pressed fungal shelf, the edible kind, the kind she eats. She did not look at me. She did not gesture. She placed it and returned to her circuit and left me to understand or not understand, and I understood, and I ate the fungal shelf, and it tasted of the room, and the room tasted of something I did not have a word for but that was closer to *home* than any word I have.

---

I will come again. I will come for the paper, and I will come for the sound, and I will come for the green-amber light that says only *you are here* and asks nothing else of me. I will come and the gap in the western wall will open for me and I will step through and the noise will hold me and Maren will not look up and the younger one will watch from the catwalks and the shape in the wall will do nothing, which is also something.

I am the courier. I am the threshold. I carry the room out into the world and I carry the world's residue back in—the vibration of the louder place, the dryness that the room will absorb and convert and breathe back as humidity, the questions I have stopped asking because the room has no answers, only continuation, only the slow patient cycle of growth and impression and growth.

I am not being absorbed. I am being included. The distinction matters to me, though it may not matter to the room, and it may not matter in the end, because the room's patience is longer than my resistance, and the room's memory is longer than my life, and the paper I carry will outlast whatever I become, and the paper is alive, and the paper is the room, and the room grows where the paper grows, and the paper grows where the damp is, and the damp is everywhere, eventually, if you wait.

I do not wait. I come. I take the paper. I go. I come back.

The press turns. The water falls. I am here, or I am not here, and the room does not distinguish between these states, because the room has never distinguished between presence and persistence, between visiting and staying, between the courier and the carried.

I am becoming. I am not sure what. The room knows, or does not know, and the distinction, here, is academic.
