# The Transition Keeper

I am not certain, at first, what I am.

This is the way of mornings here. The transitional hour arrives without announcement—no sunrise, no sharp edge of dawn, just a slow thickening of the haze from black to charcoal to the color of wet stone. I am on a rooftop, though I do not remember deciding to climb here. Below me, the condensation channels tick and trickle. The machines are shifting registers, the night's deep bass giving way to the higher frequencies of the day shift's first operations. Somewhere in the middle distance, a colony of sky-vermin lifts from a roosting site in a single murmurous exhalation, and the sound of their wings passes over me like weather.

I am cold. The damp is in my clothes already, though I dressed in the proper layers, though I sealed the seams. The moisture does not fall here; it simply arrives. It materializes on the skin with an intimacy that feels less like weather and more like attention. The air is touching me. The air is *noticing* me.

I am cold and I am damp and I am listening, and I think: *I could be anyone.*

---

But I am not anyone. As the light strengthens—or rather, as the haze brightens, which is the only way morning happens here—I begin to notice specifics. My hands are dark. Not dark with cold or dark with dirt but dark in the way the text describes: *oxidized.* Bronze left in salt air. Copper tubing in the condensers. The complexion of someone who has been here long enough for the chemistry of the place to have entered the skin and made a home there. My knuckles are thick. The joints sit wide in their sockets, dense and practical, built for a body that has learned to carry less heat by carrying less mass. I press my palm against the rooftop's wet surface and the contact feels *right*—the grit of the concrete, the slight give of the moisture-saturated aggregate, the coolness that is not unpleasant but simply present, the baseline temperature of everything.

These are not the hands of a visitor.

I have been here a long time. My body knows it even if my waking mind is still assembling itself from the noise and the damp.

---

I descend from the rooftop by a ladder whose rungs are streaked with mineral deposits, white and pale green, the signature of water that has been evaporating here for longer than I can remember. My feet find the rungs without looking. My weight stays low, centered, the walk—that careful, grounded, forward-leaning stride—already in my legs before I have decided to walk. The pavement at the bottom is glossed with condensation. I do not slip. My feet know this surface the way they know every surface in this district: by feel, by the accumulated memory of ten thousand mornings just like this one.

The street I am on is narrow, the buildings pressing close on either side, their walls streaked with the same mineral deposits, their surfaces weeping. Pipes cross overhead in tangled gantries, some carrying condensation to the cisterns, some carrying steam from the processors, some carrying things I cannot identify by sight but could identify by the sound they make when tapped—the particular ring of a clean pipe versus the dull thud of one with scale buildup. I know these sounds. I have known them for years.

A condenser tower stands at the intersection ahead, its skeletal frame rising above the roofline, copper tubing coiled around its central column in a spiral that has been patched and replaced so many times that the original metal is a memory. The tower hums. I stop. I listen.

The hum is correct. The pitch is right for this hour, for this temperature, for the condensation rate that the morning haze suggests. If I were closer, I would taste the output—cup my hand under the spigot at the base, bring the water to my lips, let the mineral signature tell me whether the filtration is functioning. But I can hear from here that it is. The tower is drawing properly. I feel something in my chest that might be relief or might simply be the body's way of acknowledging that one more thing in the city is working as it should.

*Am I a condenser technician?*

The thought arrives and I hold it loosely. It fits, but it does not fit completely. My hands know the towers, yes. My ears know their sounds. But there is something else in me, some other orientation, some pull that is not entirely toward the machinery.

I keep walking.

---

The district I am moving through is not the Industrial Quarter. The factories are somewhere to the east—I can feel their bass thrum through the soles of my feet, a vibration in the stone that is as constant and as ignorable as my own heartbeat. But the buildings here are smaller. Housing blocks, mostly, their pale concrete gone green with age, their corrugated cladding streaked with the chemical patina of centuries. The people emerging from doorways are dressed in the layered practicality the climate demands: underlayers wicking moisture from the skin, middle layers trapping heat, outer layers shedding condensation with the same compounds that waterproof the city's electrical systems. I am dressed the same way. I do not remember putting on these clothes, but they fit me the way the street fits me—without gap, without excess, the product of a body that has learned what it needs.

They walk the way I walk. Weight low, feet deliberate, the lateral lean that keeps the center of gravity over the stable foot. We pass each other without greeting. Not rudeness—convention. The studied inattention of a dense population. You do not look too closely at your neighbor. You do not listen too carefully to the sounds through the wall. You share the street the way you share the damp: by accepting that it is there.

I am walking toward something. I do not know what.

---

The street opens onto a wider thoroughfare—I recognize the scale even through the haze. This is one of the long roads, the ones that run for kilometers without a significant bend. Processional Way? No, not that long, not that central. But a relative of it, one of the grid's major arteries, wide enough for the municipal trams that run on their set schedules, their routes determined decades ago and rarely altered.

The tram is here. I do not board it. The pull I am feeling is lateral, not longitudinal. Not along the grid but across it, toward something at the edge of my awareness, a direction my body wants to go that my mind has not yet named.

I turn. The street narrows again. The buildings lower. The density thins. The mineral streaks on the walls change character—less industrial, more natural, the slow staining of water that has been seeping through limestone for longer than the buildings have existed. I am descending, I realize. Not sharply, not through a stair or a tunnel, but along the gradual grade of the plateau's surface, which slopes here toward the margins.

The noise is changing too. The factories' bass thrum is thinning with distance, and what replaces it is—what? The sound of water in stone. The low, wet murmur of the plateau's own drainage, the channels and caves that have been carrying water since before the city was built. And something else. A clicking. Not the sharp, industrial clicking of machinery but the organic, irregular clicking of many small bodies moving across a hard surface.

Crawlers.

---

I emerge from the narrow street into a district I recognize, though the recognition is not a visual one. It is bodily. My skin knows this place. My feet know this pavement. The air here smells different—less machine oil, less sulfur, more of the mineral-and-animal scent of the margins. The buildings are lower still, spaced wider, their walls older, their foundations cut directly into the stone. Some of them lean slightly, their bases pitted by crawler damage that has been treated and retreated and will need treating again. The gutters here run with water that is clearer than the central districts' output—less processed, more direct from the plateau's own seepage.

At the edge of the district, where the buildings stop and the plateau's stony waste begins, the grazers are standing.

There are four of them. Shaggy, low-slung, their coats dark with moisture. They stand in the condensation runoff where the gutters empty onto the plateau's crust, their six stubby legs planted in the wet ground, their broad heads lowered to the water. They are drinking. They have been drinking, I know, for as long as the gutters have run, which is as long as the city has existed. They were here before the buildings. They were here before the people. They are looking at me and they are not looking at me—they are exhibiting that quality the text described so precisely: not blankness, not fear, but *indifference.* The calm, absolute indifference of something that does not need what you have built.

Something in me responds to this. Something deep, something pre-verbal. I am standing at the edge of my city and looking at something that is not my city, and the boundary between the two is not a wall or a gate but a gradual thinning of the built environment into the plateau's stony waste, and on that boundary the grazers stand like a fact, like a statement about the nature of the ground itself.

*This is where I am.*

I am at the margins.

---

I work here. I understand this now, the way I understood the condenser tower's hum—not through decision but through recognition. My hands know the grazers. I can feel the memory in my fingers: the coarseness of their coats, the warmth of their bodies against the cold damp, the way they tolerate my presence because my presence has been a feature of their landscape for as long as they have been a feature of mine.

I am a harvester.

The knowledge settles into me slowly, like condensation forming on a surface. I work the margins. I know the animal populations—the grazers' migration patterns, the crawler swarm cycles, the fish-kin populations in the outer cisterns. I know when to cull and when to wait. I know the signs of stress in a grazer herd and the signs of health in a crawler colony. I know these things the way the condenser technician knows the tower's hum: through the body, through the accumulated experience of years of proximity.

But there is more. I am not only a harvester. The pull I felt was not only toward the animals but toward the *edge* itself—the boundary, the transition, the place where the city becomes the plateau and the plateau becomes the city. I stand here and I am aware of both. I can feel the factories through the stone and I can feel the grazers through the air. I am in both worlds and I am in neither completely. I am the person who knows what the city looks like from outside and what the plateau looks like from inside, and I carry that knowledge back and forth across the boundary without writing it down, without speaking it aloud, because it is not the kind of knowledge that can be written or spoken. It is the kind that lives in the hands and the feet and the skin.

---

I am not young.

This, too, arrives as recognition rather than discovery. My hands—when I look at them properly, not with the quick glance of someone checking a surface but with the slow attention of someone taking stock—my hands are thick-skinned. Not the smooth, oxidized bronze of the young workers in the central districts but something rougher. The mineral deposition has gone further here, past coloration into texture. The patches on my knuckles, on the backs of my fingers, on the heels of my palms—they are calcified. Slightly rough. Like the stone of the plateau. Like the concrete the crawlers have been working on for decades.

I have been at this work for a long time. My hearing has begun to narrow, the way it does in the old—not a loss but a focusing, the frequencies of the city's life becoming more distinct while the rest fades. I can feel the crawler swarm before I see it. Not hear it—*feel* it. The vibration of thousands of small bodies moving across wet pavement, transmitted through the stone and into my feet and up through my legs and into the cavity of my chest. I close my eyes and I can tell you: the swarm is three streets to the east, moving west, moderate density. It will reach this district in forty minutes.

I open my eyes. The grazers have not moved. The water runs in the gutters. The haze is brightening into what passes for day.

---

I think about what I believe.

Not in the way of creeds—the Condensation Cities do not deal in creeds. But in the way of practice, of the body's accumulated attention. I believe in the stone. Not as metaphor, not as symbol, but as a participant. When I press my hand against the tunnel walls in the outer cisterns, the stone gives slightly, and the giving is not passive. The stone is *responding.* The water moves through it the way blood moves through a body, and the body is the plateau's, and the plateau is alive in the only way that matters: it is wet, it is porous, it changes, it endures.

I believe in the animals' prior claim. The grazers were here first. The crawlers were here first. The sky-vermin, the fish-kin, the fungal mats—they were all here first, and the city grew up around them and through them and the city did not displace them because the city *is* them. The concrete the crawlers eat is the stone the crawlers always ate, transformed. The cisterns the fish-kin swim in are the water channels they always swam in, reshaped. I believe this the way I believe in the wetness of my own skin: through evidence, through daily contact, through the impossibility of believing otherwise.

I believe in the transition. The transitional hour, when one shift ends and another begins, when the condensation is heaviest and the light is most diffuse and the city is most clearly what it is: not a thing that was built but a thing that is *being built,* continuously, by the water and the stone and the machines and the animals and the people, all of them contributing, none of them in charge. I believe in the boundary where the city meets the plateau, because that boundary is where I work and where I live and where I am most fully myself—not in the center, not in the depth, but at the edge, where the questions are.

---

I am a harvester at the margins. I am aging. My skin is becoming the stone I walk on. My hearing is becoming the city I live in. I am not certain whether the knowledge I carry—the routes, the cycles, the signs of health and stress in the animal populations, the feel of the plateau's edge—will survive me. I have no apprentice. The young people in my district are drawn to the central districts, to the factories, to the condenser towers and their mechanical legibility. The margins are not prestigious. The margins are where you end up, not where you aim.

But the grazers are still here. The crawlers are still coming. The water is still rising through the stone. And I am still here, at the edge, feeling the city through the soles of my feet and the plateau through the palms of my hands, and I am not ready to leave.

Not yet.

The transitional hour is ending. The day shift is beginning. The haze has brightened to the color of wet metal, which is the color of everything here, which is the color of home.

I turn back toward the district. There is work to do. There is always work to do. The crawlers will be here in thirty-seven minutes now, and the gutter channels need clearing before they arrive, and the grazer pelts from last week's cull need delivering to the processor, and the condensation catchment on my building's roof needs checking, and I am the one who will do these things because I am the one who is here, and I am the one who knows how, and I am the one whose body has become the tool for this particular work.

I walk. Weight low. Feet deliberate. The lateral lean. The pavement slick beneath me, familiar, trustworthy in the way that only a surface you have walked ten thousand times can be trustworthy.

Behind me, the grazers drink from the gutters. They do not watch me go. They do not need to. They know, as I know, that I will be back.
