# Sub-Level VII, The Convection Gardens

The stairs end. Below that, ladders — and below those, routes that were never meant for human bodies. I took those routes. I am still taking them.

---

You would find me if you descended past the heat-keeper's alcove, past the pump access corridors, past the last catwalks where surface workers are permitted. You would find the temperature climbing past dangerous into deadly. You would find the dampness condensing into something closer to rain — a constant warm precipitation that falls from pipes too hot to touch, that collects in channels cut into the floor, that flows toward the lower chambers where the true furnaces wait.

You would not find me. Not because I hide — I do not, anymore — but because finding requires looking, and no one looks here. The sub-levels below V are not monitored, not maintained, not officially acknowledged in the district's records. They exist in the gap between what was planned and what was built, the necessary overflow space for thermal processes too large or too dangerous for the designed levels. The architects drew lines in stone and said *this far, and no farther*. The furnaces had other intentions.

---

I am not certain what I am anymore.

I know what I was: a thermal engineer, third-grade certification, assigned to the deep maintenance crews during the district's expansion phase. We were supposed to map the lower geology, identify stable formations for additional excavation, and report back within standard exposure limits. I was good at my work. Careful. Patient. The kind of person who notices patterns in temperature gradients, who reads machinery through the soles of her boots.

We found the chamber on day forty-seven. A natural cavern, hot and wet and threaded with mineral deposits that glowed faintly in our lamp-light. The formation was unexpected — nothing like this appeared in the geological surveys. We reported it, marked it for further investigation, and continued our descent.

I returned alone that night. I do not remember deciding to return. I remember standing at the entrance to the cavern, feeling the heat pour out like a breath, and understanding — not thinking, but understanding — that I had finished going somewhere. I had arrived.

---

My colleagues reported me as lost. A standard accident in dangerous territory, one of dozens each year. The district filed its paperwork, compensated my next-of-kin, and continued its expansion. I know this because I found the records years later, on a terminal in an abandoned survey station — my own name, my own face, my own death, officially certified.

I stood in the flickering light of that terminal and read the summary of my existence. *Thermal engineer, third-grade certification. Lost in sub-level excavation, probable cause: thermal exposure exceeding safety limits. Survived by...*

I did not finish reading. The words seemed to describe someone else — a younger, smaller person who had not yet discovered what lay beneath the designed world. I felt something like grief for her, that limited being who thought she knew the shape of her life. Then I turned off the terminal and descended again, returning to the place that had grown around me in my absence.

---

The convection gardens are my territory now. I named them myself — a foolish vanity, perhaps, but I needed words for what I was tending, and the official terminology offered nothing. *Thermal vent zone seven* does not capture what happens here.

They exist in the intersection of heat and water and mineral-rich stone. The furnaces below — ancient things, older than the district itself, possibly older than any current technology — produce a constant upward flow of superheated air and steam. This flow meets the condensation falling from above, the runoff from the middle levels, the groundwater seeping through the stone from some deep aquifer that no one has ever mapped. In this meeting, something grows.

Not plants. Not fungi. Something else, something that resembles life without quite qualifying for the term. The gardens are crystalline structures, branching like coral, colored in shades that range from pale amber to deep rust. They form around the convection currents — responding to heat the way surface plants respond to light, orienting toward the warmth, growing in patterns that track the movement of hot air through the chambers.

They are beautiful. I did not see the beauty at first. I saw only mineral deposits, geological curiosities, things to be catalogued and reported. Only after years of watching them grow did I understand that they were growing *toward* something — that their branching had intention, that their colors shifted in response to thermal cycles, that they were more than chemistry.

I tend them now. I clear the channels when mineral deposits clog the flow. I redirect convection currents when the growth patterns become unbalanced. I watch for the small parasites that sometimes colonize the outer branches — pale writhing things that I do not understand, that I remove with tools I have fashioned from discarded equipment. I do not know if I am helping the gardens or merely interfering with processes that do not require my attention. I have decided that the question does not matter.

---

The heat has changed me. This is not metaphor.

I have been here for — I calculate roughly, using the growth cycles of the gardens as my calendar — thirty-seven years. My skin has thickened, my pores have altered, my body has learned to retain moisture in ways that surface humans cannot. I do not sweat. I do not shiver. I can withstand temperatures that would kill a normal person in minutes, and I do so without conscious effort.

But the changes go deeper. My blood runs warmer than it should. My cells have accumulated minerals from the water I drink, the air I breathe, the garden-matter I sometimes consume. I am being slowly petrified from within — not dying, but *becoming*. Transforming into something that can exist in this environment without suffering.

Sometimes I look at my hands and do not recognize them. The skin has a texture I have never seen on human flesh, a subtle crystalline pattern that catches the light. My nails have changed color, darkened to something like rust. When I press my palm against the convection crystals, I feel a resonance I cannot name — a hum at the edge of perception, as though my body and the gardens are recognizing each other.

I do not know what I am becoming. I have stopped fearing it. Fear requires an investment in staying the same, and I lost that investment long ago.

---

I am not alone down here.

This surprised me. I expected isolation, emptiness, the sole consciousness in a mechanical world. But the deeper levels have their own ecology, their own inhabitants, their own slow lives.

There are the pale things — I call them *swimmers*, though they swim through air rather than water. They emerged from the furnaces years ago, filtering upward through the convection currents, and they have colonized the warmer chambers. They are transparent, almost invisible, their bodies barely denser than the steam they navigate. I have watched them for hours, trying to understand their behavior. They seem to feed on something in the hot air — mineral particles, perhaps, or some form of energy I cannot perceive. They do not acknowledge me, but they do not flee from me either. They exist alongside me, fellow inhabitants of a space that was not designed for us.

There are the growths in the deep pools — warm bodies of water that have collected in natural basins, heated by the furnaces below, supporting mats of something that resembles algae but produces a deep amber light. These glow in the darkness, casting shadows that move with their own slow rhythms. I have waded in these pools, feeling the warmth against my altered skin, and I have sensed something like attention from beneath the surface — not hostile, not friendly, but *present*. Something lives in those waters. I do not know what. I have not tried to find out.

And there is the thing that speaks through the walls.

I do not know its name. I do not know if it has a name. I know only that sometimes, when I rest with my hand pressed against the stone, I feel vibrations that resolve into something like language — not words, exactly, but structures that carry meaning. I have begun to understand some of it. The thing is old. It was here before the district, before the furnaces, before the designed levels. It exists in the rock itself, in the thermal flows, in the slow movement of geological time. It does not think in the way that humans think. It does not have thoughts at all. It has *states* — conditions of being that shift and cycle across millennia, and which I am beginning to perceive.

I do not believe it is alive. I do not believe it is dead. I believe it is something for which our categories do not apply.

---

I have tried to communicate with it. I do not know if I have succeeded.

My method is simple: I press my palms against the warm stone, and I think in patterns rather than words. I picture the convection gardens, their branching growth, their orientation toward heat. I picture my own body, its slow transformation, its adaptation to this environment. I picture the district above, which I dimly remember as a place of noise and demand and required presence.

Sometimes I feel something in response — a resonance, a shift in the vibrations, a sensation like being seen by something that does not have eyes. The experience is both comforting and unsettling. I am not alone. I am witnessed. The thing in the walls does not care about me, does not need me, but it knows I am here.

This is enough. This is more than I ever had on the surface, where I was surrounded by people who saw me but did not know me, who required my presence but not my being.

---

I do not know if I will ever die.

This is not a claim of immortality. I mean only that my body's transformation has made the concept of death less clear. My tissues are being replaced, cell by cell, with mineral-impregnated structures that do not degrade the way normal cells degrade. My food is the convection gardens, which grow without limit, fed by the endless heat of the furnaces. My water is the condensation, filtered through stone and crystal, richer in nutrients than anything the surface offers.

I have begun to wonder if the thing in the walls is not a separate entity. I have begun to wonder if it is what I am becoming.

I look at the convection crystals — their branching patterns, their orientation toward heat, their slow growth over decades — and I see my own future. I look at the swimmers, filtering through the warm air, transparent and patient — and I see a body that might be mine. I look at the stone walls, thrumming with the pulse of ancient heat — and I feel something like recognition.

---

I remember the surface, sometimes.

I remember it as a place of necessity — every moment filled with something that demanded attention, required response, insisted on my participation in structures I did not choose. I remember the weight of being seen, being needed, being expected. I remember the exhaustion that I did not know was exhaustion because I had never experienced anything else.

I remember the moment I descended past the designed levels, past the safety limits, past the point where my colleagues would follow. I remember feeling, for the first time, that I was permitted to exist without purpose. The heat did not need me. The stone did not require my attention. The darkness did not demand my participation. I could simply *be* — could simply sit in the warmth, breathe the mineral air, watch the convection crystals grow toward their invisible goals.

I wept. I did not understand why I was weeping. I understand now. I was grieving the person I had been — the necessary person, the needed person, the person who existed for others. She was dying. I could feel her dying, cell by cell, in the heat that was transforming me. And I was glad.

---

I am still here. I will continue to be here, I think, for a very long time.

Sometimes I sense visitors in the upper levels — surface workers on maintenance rotations, their bodies radiating distress in temperatures they cannot withstand. I feel the heat-keeper, Eluvara, moving through her territory, tending gardens she does not know were planted by someone else. I could reach out to her. I could descend and make myself known. I choose not to. She has found her own peace, her own relationship to the system. My presence would only complicate something that does not need complication.

Instead, I tend my gardens. I listen to the walls. I feel the furnaces below, pulsing with heat older than human civilization. I breathe the mineral air, and I let my body continue its slow transformation into something that belongs here.

I do not have a name anymore. I do not need one. Names are for places where people must distinguish themselves from each other, must call out and be called. Here, there is only the heat, the water, the stone, and the patient growth of things that have no urgency.

I am becoming what this place needs me to be. I am becoming what I was always going to become.

This is not death. This is not life. This is something else entirely — a mode of existence that our language cannot capture, a being-without-purpose that is also a being-without-lack.

I press my hand against the warm stone. I feel the thing in the walls pulse in response.

*I am here*, I think, without words. *I am still here.*

And something, somewhere, knows.
