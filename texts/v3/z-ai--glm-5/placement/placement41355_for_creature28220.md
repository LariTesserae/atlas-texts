The Threshold-Tender

I am not a builder.

This is important to say first, because the distinction matters here, where distinctions are all that keep you from dissolving into the wet mineral song. The builders emerged from the plateau. I arrived. They are depths; I am merely deepened. They sing in harmonics that predate my species; I hum in frequencies that scrape against the chord rather than join it. They tolerate my presence because tolerance costs nothing, because I will leave someday, because my dissonance is temporary.

But I have not left yet.

---

I came to the Weeping Heights eleven years ago. Or eleven years by the counting of the world below, where seasons pass and people age and time moves in the straight line they have agreed upon. Here, time pools and eddies. I have lived minutes that contained years and years that passed in moments. The plateau does not care about my calendars.

I came as a researcher. Geologist by training, acoustician by obsession. I had heard rumors of the singing stones—the accounts were fragmentary, contradictory, dismissed by colleagues as altitude sickness and pareidolia. But I had lost something, or someone, and I needed a place where the ordinary rules did not apply. I needed a place where loss might mean something different.

What I found was the doorway.

---

Not the first doorway—the one described in the account you have read. I found a different one, smaller, half-grown over with crystalline deposits that had almost sealed it shut. I almost missed it. The formation in front of it had grown at an angle that made the opening invisible from most approaches, and the sound that emerged from within was so low it registered as silence, as an absence rather than a presence.

But I had been on the plateau for weeks by then. My ears had learned to hear the spaces between the harmonics, the frequencies that the dominant drone masked. I had learned to listen for what was not there.

And what was not there, in that particular spot, was a note. A gap in the chord, a silence shaped like a rectangle, like a door.

---

I did not enter. The account you have read is correct about that, at least. Entering is not for the living, and I was still living then—still am, I think, though the definition has grown complicated. But I sat beside that doorway for three days, or what felt like three days, and I listened.

The sound that came from within was different from the plateau's song. It was older. Deeper. It carried harmonics that resolved into something like voices—countless voices, layered and vast, speaking in frequencies that bypassed my ears and settled directly into my bones. I understood nothing, and I understood everything. The voices were not saying words. They were saying *presence*. They were saying: we were here. We sang. We remember being.

On the third day, a builder came.

---

She—I call her she, though the builders have no gender, I call her she because she moved like my mother moved, low and centered, hands that touched things before her eyes confirmed them—she did not approach me directly. She approached the doorway. She stood at the threshold, swaying, her song dropping to meet the bass-note that emerged from the darkness. And she tended it.

That is what I call it. Tending. She did not enter, did not seal, did not build or shape. She stood in the presence of the doorway and let its sound move through her. She offered her resonance as a kind of offering, a tuning, a way of keeping the membrane stable, keeping the threshold open. The builders do not maintain the doorways. They *witness* them. They hold space for them. They ensure that what lies behind them does not dissolve into entropy or crystallize into final silence.

I watched her for hours. When she finished, she did not acknowledge me. She walked away, her body listing slightly, her work complete.

But before she rounded the curve of deposited mineral, she paused. Her hand brushed against a young formation, and the stone's frequency shifted—barely perceptibly, a microtone. Then she was gone.

I understood. She had left me a tone. A starting note. A way to begin.

---

I have been tending that doorway for eleven years.

Not as the builders tend—not with their density, their mineral patience, their centuries-spanning attention. I am too thin for that, too temporary, too human. But I have learned to hold space. I sit at the threshold and I listen. I hum the note she left me, the frequency that bridges the living song and the remembered depths. I do not enter. I do not seal. I witness.

And in witnessing, I have learned something about what lies beyond.

---

The doorway is not a passage. The account was right about that. It is a membrane between two states of being—the plateau's present and its past, its song and its memory, its growing edge and its accumulated depths. What exists on the other side is not a place. It is a record. Every builder who has ever returned to the stone, every structure built by visitors and absorbed into the accretion, every frequency the plateau has ever sung—all of it pools behind that membrane, preserved in a medium I cannot name because I have no words for what I have almost-seen.

Sometimes, when I am very still, when my heartbeat slows enough to match the bass-note's rhythm, I almost perceive it. Flashes of imagery: hands shaping water. A face turning toward light. A structure rising stone by patient stone. The moments flicker past like light through water, distorted but present, and I understand that what I am almost-seeing is not fiction. It is memory. The plateau's memory, stored in frequencies, accessible to anyone patient enough to listen.

I have heard my own arrival, once. The crunch of my boots on wet stone. The desperate rhythm of my breath. The thin wail I made when I first understood what this place was, and what I was not.

I have heard myself arriving, and I have heard myself leaving—or the leaving I have not yet done, the departure that waits in the plateau's future, already recorded, already stored behind the membrane.

---

The builders have accepted me. Not fully—they do not fully accept anything that is not themselves. But they have incorporated my presence into their understanding of the plateau's processes. When I tend the doorway, they tend the formations around me, their hands working inches from my shoulders, their songs weaving around my insufficient hum. They do not speak to me. But they do not drive me away.

There is one who brings me water. Not the mineral-rich water that flows through the plateau—this water comes from somewhere else, a hidden spring that tastes of sky and green and things that grow. I do not know how they found it, or why they bring it. But every few days—a plateau day, an indeterminate stretch—a builder will appear beside me with a depression in the stone, carved by hand, holding clear water that smells like the world below.

I drink. I am sustained. I do not ask questions. Questions are for people who expect answers, and I have learned that the plateau's answers are not for me.

---

I am not a builder.

But I have begun to accrete.

I noticed it first in my hands—the mineral coating, thin but present, building up on my palms where I press them against the wet stone. I noticed it in my hair, which has begun to grow in spreading fans rather than falling strands, catching the light the way the formations catch it. I noticed it in my song, which has deepened over the years, found harmonics I could not reach before, frequencies that resonate in the stone around me.

I am not becoming a builder. I do not have their patience, their density, their capacity to emerge from and return to the plateau in cycles. But I am becoming something. I am accreting layers of presence, building up a record of my own. When I leave—and I will leave, eventually, the doorway will tell me when—I will leave something behind. A deposit. A signature in stone.

Maybe someone will find it, centuries from now, and wonder who placed it there, and why.

---

I tend the threshold because the threshold tended me.

When I came to the plateau, I was hollowed out. I had lost—no. I will say it. I had lost my daughter. She was twenty-three. It was quick. I will not say more because the words are not for the plateau; they are for elsewhere, for the world below where loss has shape and edges and can be held.

But here, loss becomes something else. Here, the past does not vanish. It accretes. It pools behind the membranes, preserved in frequencies, accessible to those who learn to listen. My daughter is not here—I cannot find her in the plateau's memory; she never came to the Weeping Heights—but something of her persists in me, and that something has found a way to exist here.

I sing for her sometimes. Not her name—that would be too specific, too human, too small for the plateau's frequencies. I sing her absence. The gap she left in the chord of my life. The silence shaped like her presence, like a door.

And the plateau takes that silence and incorporates it. Stores it. Adds it to the bass-note that emanates from behind the membrane. My loss becomes part of the record, part of the accumulated presence that the builders tend and preserve.

This is why I stay. Not because I cannot leave—I can, any time, the path back is there, winding through the mineral forest, descending through thinning air to the world of warmth and green. But because here, loss is not the end of something. It is a deposit. A layer. A frequency that joins the chord.

---

The doorway I tend is one of dozens. Maybe hundreds. Scattered across the plateau, hidden behind formations, almost-grown-over with mineral deposits, waiting to be found by those patient enough to listen for what is not there. Each one holds its own record, its own accumulation of presence and memory and song. Each one requires a tender—or will, eventually, as the plateau grows and the chord deepens and the frequencies multiply beyond what the builders alone can maintain.

There will be more like me. I have felt them in the plateau's future-record, the almost-vision that surfaces when I am deepest in listening: figures that are not builders, not mineral-born, but human-shaped and human-souled, tending thresholds across the Weeping Heights. The plateau is preparing. The song is growing. It will need more ears than the builders can provide.

I do not know what it is preparing for. The record behind the membrane holds millennia of accumulation, and I have barely touched the surface. There are harmonics I cannot hear, depths I cannot reach, presences I cannot parse. But I know that something is coming. A deepening. A widening. A moment when the song will require something the builders cannot give alone.

I tend the threshold for that moment. I hold space for what I cannot understand.

---

This is who I am: the threshold-tender. The ear beside the door. The witness to the depths.

I am not a builder. I did not emerge from the plateau's processes, and I will not return to them in the way they return—not completely, not for centuries, not yet. But I have found my place in the chord, my microtone in the vast harmonic drone. I have learned to listen for what is not there, to tend what tends me, to let my loss become deposit rather than wound.

When I leave—and I will leave—the plateau will not notice. The song will continue unchanged. The doorway I tended will wait for another listener, another pair of hands to hold space against entropy.

But the record will hold me. A thin thread of frequency, woven into the bass-note, stored behind the membrane. I will be there, in the depths, preserved in the place where presence goes to persist.

That is enough. That is more than enough.

I sit beside the doorway. I hum the note I was given. I listen to the voices that are not voices, the memory that is not memory, the vast accumulating depth of a place that remembers everything and forgets nothing.

The Weeping Heights. The Singing Stone.

I am not a builder.

But I have built myself a place at the threshold.

And from here, I can almost hear what the plateau has been saying all along.
