I am old for my kind. Old enough that the joint of my right wing aches when the pre-dawn cold settles into it, old enough that I no longer race the younger birds to the condensation pools. I have held this nesting ledge for seventeen years—an absurd span, almost unheard of, made possible only by the particular architecture of this fissure where the stone angles just so, creating a wind-shadow that protects my mate and me from the worst of the screaming.

My mate. Her call is the one I listen for now. Not the territorial declarations, not the contact murmurs, not the alarm patterns we all know. Her call. The specific shape of it, the frequency that sits just below the roar and rises through it. I know it the way I know the stone under my feet. I could find her in a thousand birds. I have found her in a thousand birds.

---

The light is beginning. This is the time I think of as *between*—between the pressed-together warmth of the night cluster and the brutal illumination of full sun. The condensation is forming on the stone, beading in the shallow depressions, and I can feel the younger birds shifting on their ledges, preparing to descend. The insects will be emerging from the lichen mats. The mammals will be stirring in their deep cracks. Everyone knows this rhythm. We have always known it.

But I have begun to notice something else.

---

When I was younger—when I first claimed this ledge, when my mate and I built the nest layer by layer from grass stems and stone fragments—I did not think about the system. I did not think about systems at all. I thought about territory. I thought about food. I thought about the shape of the wind at different hours, the location of the best foraging patches, the precise angle of approach to a mammal's burrow that allowed for a successful strike. I thought about survival and reproduction and the maintenance of my place in the colony's complex social structure.

I still think about these things. They are not optional. But in the spaces between them—during the long hours of midday when the light is too harsh for efficient foraging and we roost in whatever shadows we can find—I have begun to perceive something larger. A pattern that includes my territory and my mate and my ledge but extends far beyond them.

I do not have words for this. We do not have language in the way the absent ones who left the tools had language. We have calls—specific, learned, passed down through generations. I can signal danger from above. I can signal danger from below. I can signal *come here* and *go away* and *this is mine* and *I am here.* I can sing the song that attracted my mate, the one I refined over three seasons until it was exactly right, exactly hers. I can make the low churring sound that confirms presence, that says *we are all still here, we are all still here, we are all still here.*

But I cannot say *I have noticed that the lichen grows only where the shadow falls, and that the insects live only in the lichen, and that we eat the insects, and that the condensation forms where the shadow lasts longest, and that we drink the condensation, and that all of these things depend on the canyon rim casting its shadow at the correct angle, and that the canyon rim exists because the wind has been carving it for longer than our species has existed, and that the wind carves it still, and that we call into the wind and the wind shapes our calls, and that this has been happening for so long that it has become a single thing rather than many things.*

I cannot say this. But I have begun to see it.

---

This morning I fly to the far end of the colony's range. I do this sometimes—fly beyond my usual territory, beyond the familiar foraging grounds, to a place where the nesting density thins and the canyon rim changes character. The younger birds do not understand this. They think in terms of territory: *mine* and *not mine*, *here* and *away*. They do not think in terms of the whole.

But I have begun to think in terms of the whole.

The flight takes me along the edge, the wind pouring over me, the roar constant in my chest. Below, the canyon drops away—two thousand meters of layered stone, each layer older than the last, the whole formation older than anything I can comprehend. I do not comprehend time in the way the stone comprehends time. I have been here for seventeen years. The colony has been here for longer than any of us can remember—our calls contain information about the rim's geography that no living bird has verified, locations of ledges and cracks that have not existed for generations. We hold the plateau's knowledge in our songs. We do not know that we hold it. We simply hold it.

I reach the thin place and land on a fin of stone that juts out over the void. This is not a nesting site—the ledges here are too exposed, the wind too harsh—but I like to come here. I like to stand at the place where the colony ends and see what lies beyond.

What lies beyond is more of the same. More plateau, more stone, more wind. But the emptiness feels different here. The center of the plateau—the great flat expanse where nothing lives—seems closer, more present. From here I can see it not as absence but as *the thing that makes the edge possible*. The center holds nothing so that the edge can hold everything.

I do not know how I know this. I know it the way I know the shape of my mate's call—intimately, without explanation, as something that has always been true.

---

There is a trough in the stone near my nesting ledge where the absent ones left things.

I found it years ago, when I was younger and exploring places I had no reason to explore. The trough is deep and shadowed, one of the places where the lichen grows thick. And in the lichen, half-buried in the stone's patina, are shapes that are not natural. Sharp edges. Delicate fractures. The stone has been worked.

I showed it to my mate once. She looked at the shapes—there are perhaps a dozen of them, scattered across the trough's floor—and made the call that means *nothing useful here.* She was not wrong. The shapes cannot be eaten. They cannot be used. They are simply present, lying where they were dropped, their surfaces catching the light differently than the surrounding stone.

But I return to them. I do not know why.

The absent ones who made these shapes saw something in the stone that I do not see. They looked at the granite and the mica and the quartzite, and they saw potential—saw edges that could be made sharper, surfaces that could be made more useful. They worked with the stone. They shaped it. They made it into something it was not, and then they left.

I do not understand this. I live *with* the stone. I walk on it and nest in it and drink from its condensation pools. I know its textures and its temperatures, the way it holds warmth after sunset and the way it cracks and fissures. But I have never looked at it and thought *I could make this into something else.*

The absent ones thought this way. And then they left. And we stayed. And the tools lie in the shadows, and I look at them sometimes, and I wonder what it means to see something that is not there yet—to see potential in what is present—to imagine making rather than simply using.

---

The sun is fully up now. The light is hard, white, relentless. My shadow falls behind me, a dark shape on the pale stone, and I can see every edge of myself rendered in sharp relief.

This is what the plateau does. It illuminates. There is no diffusion here, no softness, no gradual transitions. Everything is either in light or in shadow, and the boundary between them is a blade. You cannot hide on the plateau. You cannot obscure. You are visible or you are hidden, and if you are hidden it is because you have found a place the light does not reach.

I think about this sometimes—the way the absent ones' tools lie in shadow, the way the lichen grows only where shadow falls, the way the mammals live only in the cracks where light never reaches. The way the colony nests on the canyon walls where the rim casts its long reach across the stone.

We all live in the edges between light and dark. We all live in the boundaries. The plateau's center holds nothing because the center is entirely illuminated—there is nowhere to hide, no place where the condensation persists, no microclimate where life can find purchase. Only the edges support life. Only the transitions.

---

I am flying back to my ledge when I hear my mate's call.

It comes through the roar the way all calls come through the roar—filtered by the wind, transformed by the acoustics of the canyon, but still recognizable. Still hers. She is calling to confirm presence. *I am here. Are you here?*

I tip my wing and adjust my angle. I make the call that means *I am here. I am coming.*

The colony spreads below me—a thousand ledges, ten thousand birds, a continuous living surface along the rim. I can see the nesting sites clustering thickly in the protected areas, thinning where the wind is strongest, absent where the stone offers no purchase. I can see the social patterns mapped onto the geography: families in adjacent ledges, clans in adjacent sections, territories marked by calls that no human ear could distinguish but that every one of us knows.

I land on my ledge and my mate shifts to make room. She does not need to see me to know I have returned. She heard my call. She knows the angle of my approach. She knows the rhythm of my wings.

She makes the low churring sound. I make it back. We stand together in the shadow of our fissure, and the wind screams past us, and the light falls on the stone outside, and the plateau holds us in its ancient equilibrium.

---

I do not know what I am becoming.

The younger birds do not think about the system. They do not think about the absent ones. They do not fly to the edge of the colony to see what lies beyond. They live in the immediate: food, territory, mating, survival. And I live in these things too. I am not separate from them. I am a stone-runner. I eat insects from the lichen. I drink from condensation pools. I call to maintain contact with my mate and my clan. I have held my territory for seventeen years, and I will hold it until I cannot hold it anymore.

But I am also becoming something else. I do not have a word for it. I do not have a call for it. It is a kind of noticing that includes more than the immediate—a perception that extends outward from my ledge to the colony, from the colony to the rim, from the rim to the plateau, from the plateau to the system that has been running for longer than my species has existed.

I think about the tools in the shadowed trough. I think about the lichen spreading by millimeters per century. I think about the wind carving the stone by millimeters per century. I think about the equilibrium—how long it took to reach, how stable it now is, how everything that lives here lives in the balance between the center and the edge.

I am not wise. I am not special. I am simply old, and I have been here long enough to see the same patterns repeat, and I have begun to perceive their shape.

---

The condensation has burned away. The mammals have retreated to their cracks. The insects have hidden in the lichen. The colony is quiet except for the constant churring that confirms presence, and the roar of the wind that contains all sounds and no sound.

I stand on my ledge with my mate beside me. The light is hard. The stone is holding. The equilibrium is maintained.

And somewhere in my chest, in the place where the roar vibrates and settles, I am beginning to understand what it means to live at the edge of something vast—to be part of a system that does not know it is a system, to hold knowledge that you cannot articulate, to call continuously into the wind and hear your call returned, transformed.

*I am here,* I say, with my presence, with my place, with the shape of my territory and the rhythm of my wings. *I am here. I am part of this. I am holding what I hold.*

This is what I am. This is what lives on the Singing Plateau.
