# What Emerges

I would not be a researcher. That feels wrong—the posture of study, the stance of observation. This is not a place that rewards watching. It is a place that rewards *becoming*.

---

I would be the one who stopped counting days.

Not because days don't matter here—they stretch long and pale across the basalt—but because I stopped measuring. The shift happened slowly, which is to say it happened naturally, which is to say I stopped noticing when I stopped noticing. Time became something that happened to the stone rather than to me. The frost formed. The frost sublimated. The condensation gathered in the shallow basin I had come to rest beside, and I did not count how many times. I watched the microbial mat darken at its edges, the olive-green spreading by millimeters into the rust-red, and I thought: this is fast. This is happening quickly. And I meant it.

That should have been the first warning.

---

I had equipment. I remember that. Recording instruments, spectral analyzers, a tent designed for high-altitude exposure—the kind with reflective coating that keeps heat in and ultraviolet radiation out. I remember setting it up. I remember the way the fabric snapped in the wind, that thin steady pressure that never quite becomes sound. I remember thinking: three weeks. I have three weeks of battery for the spectrometer, and then I need to decide if the data is worth the weight of solar panels.

I don't remember deciding to stay.

I don't remember the moment when I stopped writing in the logbook. I only remember finding it later, the last entry half-finished, the pen lying beside it with its cap off, both of them covered in a thin film of dust that wasn't dust—wasn't mineral—was lichen, pale and slow, beginning to colonize the synthetic surface. I picked the pen up. I put it down again. I did not finish the entry.

---

There is a grazer—I call it that for convenience, not because it is an 'it' or because 'grazer' means anything to it—that I have watched for what must be months. It is the size of my thumb, segmented, the color of oxidized iron. Its shell carries bands of different ochres, a geological striation that tells me it has lived for centuries, that it has fed in basins with different mineral contents, that it has traversed more of this plateau than I will ever see.

It does not move when I am near. Not in any way I can perceive. But when I return after hours away—hours that feel like moments to me now, though I understand they would have felt like eternities before—it has shifted. A few centimeters. A new orientation. Once, when I placed a small stone marker beside it and returned the next dim day, I found it had crawled over the marker, had absorbed something from its surface, had left the stone cleaner than I had found it.

This is intimacy. This is the closest thing to touch I know now.

---

The processions still confuse me. I have seen three. The first I walked alongside for hours, matching my pace to theirs—slowing, slowing, slowing until my muscles screamed and my tendons ached from the sustained posture of barely-moving. I wanted to understand where they were going. I wanted to understand *why*.

I failed. The procession curved away from me, not as if avoiding me but as if I were a thermal anomaly, a hot spot that registered as a feature to navigate around rather than a threat to flee. I was terrain. I was weather. I was not even enough of a presence to be worth fearing.

The second procession formed around my sleeping body. I woke—dim dawn, frost on my eyelashes—and found thousands of them moving past me on either side, a dark river splitting around an island, reforming on the other side and continuing east. I lay still. I did not breathe. I felt them crawl over my blanket, my boots, my hand where it had escaped the thermal covering. Their movements were so slow that I felt them as pressure rather than motion, as weight that accumulated and then released. I do not know how long I lay there. Long enough for the frost to melt and refrost. Long enough for the procession to pass.

When I rose, there were tracks in the condensation where they had crossed over my palm. Thin pale lines where the oils of my skin had marked their shells. I did not wash. I let the marks wear away on their own.

---

The third procession I joined.

I do not know how to explain this. I did not decide to. I was walking—patrolling, measuring, whatever word I used then for the movements I made across the plateau—and I found myself falling into step. Not with any individual. With the rhythm. A rhythm I could not perceive until I was inside it, a beat so slow that each measure took hours, a tempo that matched something deeper than my heartbeat, something in the pulse of the geothermal vents that I had stopped consciously hearing.

I walked east. I did not eat. I did not sleep. I did not check my instruments or mark my path. I walked for days, perhaps, or weeks—there was no way to know—and when the procession stopped at a basin I had never seen, a mineral seep rich with copper carbonates that turned the microbial mats a shocking verdigris green, I stopped too.

I stayed there for a long time. I do not know how long. I grazed, after a fashion—not on the stone, not on the mats, but on what the place offered: condensation, stillness, the slow accumulation of presence. I was not converting minerals into shell. But I was converting something. I was becoming something that could be here without leaving.

---

I understand things differently now.

Not because I have learned—though I have, I suppose, learned to read the chemical gradients by smell, to feel the thermal signatures through the soles of my boots, to sense the electromagnetic hum of the vents as a kind of pressure behind my eyes. But because the categories have shifted. I no longer think in terms of observer and observed. I no longer experience myself as a discrete entity moving through a landscape of separate things.

This is difficult to explain. The grazers are individuals—I see them, I track them, I recognize the unique whorls and striations of their shells—and yet they are not. They are nodes in a network, points where the plateau's slow metabolism becomes briefly visible. And I, somehow, have become a node too. Not a grazer. Not mineralized, not exoskeletal, not evolved for this environment. But present. Attentive. Incorporated.

When I sleep, I dream in gradients. When I wake, the silence is not absence but presence—a room full of listening, as the description said, and I am listening too, with senses I did not have before, senses that are not physical but attentional, a way of being that does not differentiate between self and stone.

---

I still have the tent. The equipment is somewhere, though I have not used it in—longer than I track. My logs are unfinished. My data is incomplete. If anyone came looking for me, they would find a camp in disarray, a researcher missing, probably assumed dead.

But I am not dead. I am not gone. I am simply here, operating at a different tempo, attending to different things.

The grazers know me now. Not individually—I do not think they know anything individually—but as a presence in the field, a warm spot that moves slowly, a chemical signature that has become part of the landscape's vocabulary. When I lie still in a basin, they crawl over me without hesitation. When I walk among them, they part around my feet and reform behind me like water around a stone.

I am not one of them. I never will be. But I am no longer apart from them either. I am a foreign element that has learned to be native, or as native as a warm-blooded, water-rich, short-lived mammal can be in a landscape of cold stone and mineral patience.

---

Sometimes I think thoughts that take days to complete.

That is not a metaphor. I have caught myself beginning a thought in one dim dawn and finishing it in another, the arc of attention so slow that the intermediate hours—or weeks, or however long they are—pass as a single sustained moment of contemplation. I do not know if this is a side effect of isolation or if I have genuinely shifted my temporal frame. I do not know if I am losing something human or gaining something else.

I do know that I no longer feel urgency. I no longer feel the need to fill silence with sound, to fill stillness with movement, to fill space with structure. The plateau has enough. It has been enough for millions of years. I am learning, very slowly, to be enough too.

---

If you found this—if anyone ever finds this—know that I am not lost.

Know that this is not a tragedy. It is not a warning. It is simply an account of what happens when a human being stays long enough in a place that operates on different timescales, different senses, different measures of enough.

I am lying on the basalt now. The dim light is strengthening toward its pale noon. Frost is sublimating around me in thin wisps of vapor I can see but not feel. My grazer—the one with the ochre shell, the centuries-old archive—is a meter away, moving toward the condensation basin at its usual pace. In an hour, it will reach the water's edge. In two, it will begin to feed. In days, it will have moved on to the next basin, following gradients I have learned to smell.

I will be here. I will be listening.

I am not a grazer. But I am of the stone now, as much as a soft body can be. And that is enough.
