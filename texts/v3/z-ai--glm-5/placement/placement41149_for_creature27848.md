I would be the one who tends the counting room.

Not because I was chosen for it. Not because I understood it. I found the chamber eleven years ago — or what I call eleven years, though the counting is uncertain here, and eleven might be thirty or might be three — and I have not quite been able to leave it since.

---

Let me say what I was before, because before still clings to me like the mist clings to stone. I came from outside. From below. I had been a trader, one of the few who made the climb, and I had made it before, twice, carrying cloth down and salt back up, and the second time I had stayed longer than I intended — a week, perhaps two — and when I tried to descend I found my legs uncertain on the cliff-paths, my balance wrong, as though my body had already begun to forget the mechanics of falling.

The third time I came I did not come to trade. I came because I could not stop thinking about the plateau. Not its strangeness — I had seen strange places, dealt with strange peoples in the markets below — but its stillness. The way nothing there demanded anything of me. The way I could sit on a stone wall and feel the mist collect on my eyelashes and watch a bird walk past on its powerful legs, clicking its beak at nothing, and feel that nothing was required. Not my attention. Not my action. Not even my presence, really. I was permitted, and that was all.

I did not decide to stay. I simply did not leave. The Kept recognized this — they had seen it before, they said, with a gentleness that I would later understand was not sympathy but recognition — and they gave me a room in one of the carved buildings, a small space with walls of living rock, and I slept there and woke there and slept again and the days — the periods, the darkenings and lightings — accumulated and I did not count them.

---

The counting room found me, or I found it; the distinction is unreliable here. I was walking one of the paths that connect the eastern garden-cluster to the older residential hollows, a route I had taken many times, and on this particular traversal the path was different. Not longer, exactly. More *present*. The stone beneath my feet seemed to register my weight with more interest than usual, and the mist was thicker than I had encountered it outside the deep hollows, and when I pressed my hand against the cliff face to orient myself the rock was warm, warmer than the air, warmer than stone should be.

An opening. Not a door — nothing so deliberate. A gap in the rock where the strata had separated during the rising, leaving a fissure just wide enough to enter sideways. I do not know why I entered. I am not adventurous by nature; I was a trader, and traders are cautious, and caution is what keeps you alive on the cliff-paths. But the fissure drew me, or I was already moving toward it before I knew it existed, or the plateau had decided that I should find this place on this particular traversal of this particular path.

Inside, the air was different. Still misted, but the mist carried something else — a density, a taste of mineral and something older than mineral, something that had no name in any language I knew. The corridor — for it was a corridor, though its walls had not been shaped by hands — turned twice and descended slightly, and the mist thinned as I went deeper, which should have been impossible, mist being heavier than air, pooling in low places. Here it rose, or was drawn upward, or simply chose not to follow.

The chamber opened around me like a held breath released. I cannot describe its size. It was larger than the exterior of the outcrop I had entered could possibly contain, and I have been inside long enough now to know that this is simply how it is here, that the plateau's stone contains spaces that have no relationship to its surface, that dimension is a suggestion rather than a law. The walls were close enough to touch and far enough away that I could not see them clearly, and both of these things were true simultaneously, and I stopped trying to resolve the contradiction because resolving contradictions is something the plateau does not reward.

The marks covered everything. Every surface that I could reach, and many that I could not, was incised with lines and dots and spirals cut into the stone with tools that must have been harder than the stone itself, which meant bone, perhaps, or the dark volcanic glass that the Kept sometimes find in the deepest gullies and do not trade because it is too precious. The marks were old. I knew this not by their appearance — nothing ages here — but by their density. Some sections of wall were covered so thickly that the marks overlapped, layers of inscription built up over what must have been centuries, each generation adding to the accumulation without erasing what came before.

---

I did not understand the marks then. I do not understand them now, not fully. But I have learned things.

There are patterns. This is the first thing I discovered, sitting in the chamber in the first days after I found it, tracing the lines with my fingertips the way the Kept trace the patterns on their ancient cloth. The marks are not random. They repeat — not exactly, not in ways that allow translation into language, but in ways that suggest system. A particular spiral appears at intervals, always with the same number of turns, always with the same spacing between its arcs. A particular cluster of dots — seven, arranged in a shape that is not quite a circle — recurs at heights that suggest it was made by different people across different periods, each one reaching to their own shoulder level, perhaps, or eye level, marking their presence in a way that their bodies could not sustain.

I began to count. Not the marks themselves — there are too many, and counting them proved impossible within the first hour, the numbers slipping from my mind the way water slips from a surface that has been here long enough to forget the concept of wetness. I counted something else. I counted the repetitions. I counted the intervals between spirals. I counted the number of dots in each cluster, the number of lines in each group, and I found that the numbers were not arbitrary. They followed sequences that I could recognize but not name, mathematical patterns that I had learned as a child in the markets below and had used in calculating trade values and exchange rates, patterns that meant nothing on the plateau because the plateau does not trade, does not calculate, does not value.

Except in this room. In this room, the patterns hold.

---

The Kept were not surprised when I told them what I had found, or rather, they were not surprised in the way that surprise functions elsewhere. Their faces did that thing — the half-expression, the gesture that begins and does not complete — and several of them said variations of the same phrase: *It was waiting for someone to count again.*

Again. The word sits in me the way the mist sits in the air. It implies that someone counted before, that the counting is a practice rather than a discovery, that I have stepped into a role rather than created one. The Kept did not explain this. They do not explain things; they simply continue them, and continuation is its own form of explanation.

An older woman — old by the plateau's measure, which means she has been here for what the outside world would call centuries — came to the chamber a few days after I began my work. She watched me count. She did not speak. When I paused, she extended her hand, and in her palm was a tool: a length of bone, sharpened to a point, its surface worn smooth by long use. She did not say *this is for making marks* or *this was used by the one who counted before you.* She simply placed it in my hand and left.

I have made marks of my own now. I did not decide to; I simply did, my hand finding the wall one evening — one darkening — and tracing a line that felt correct, that followed the logic of the marks around it without duplicating them. The line became a curve, the curve became a spiral, and the spiral was mine, not a copy of the others but a response to them, a continuation of whatever conversation the marks have been having across the centuries of their accumulation.

The bone tool fits my hand as though it was made for it, or as though my hand has been reshaped by holding it.

---

I will tell you what I think I know, though knowing is a precarious state here, where certainty dissolves the way certainty dissolves everywhere but more slowly, more gently, with a kind of consideration that makes the loss feel less like loss and more like revision.

I think the marks are a record. Not of events — events do not persist here, not in the way that events persist elsewhere, where they become history and history becomes weight. The marks record something else. Presence, perhaps. The fact of being here, of having been here, of continuing to be here. Each spiral is a person, or a lifetime, or a moment of awareness — I cannot distinguish between these possibilities, and the plateau does not seem to require that I distinguish. Each cluster of dots is a gathering, or a generation, or a thought that occurred to someone while they stood in this chamber and felt the accumulated weight of all the marks that had come before.

I think the counting is the purpose, not the thing being counted. The act of attending to the marks, of tracing their patterns, of adding new marks to the accumulation — this is what matters. Not because it accomplishes anything, not because the patterns reveal a meaning that can be carried out of the chamber and applied to the world. Because the attending itself is the meaning. Because in a place where time does not pass, where nothing progresses or decays or transforms, the only activity that makes sense is the activity of noticing what is here, of registering the fact of existence, of saying through marks on stone: *this was here, this was noticed, this was added to the accumulation.*

I think the builders knew this. I think they built the chamber as they built everything else — not for a practical purpose but for an existential one, a space in which the plateau's nature could be observed and recorded by human minds, human hands. And I think the counting has continued because it must, because without it the accumulation would be undirected, would be mere mass without pattern, and the plateau — or the Stillness, or the presence in the deep hollows, or whatever name you give to the thing that lives here — seems to prefer pattern. The plants grow in spirals. The birds walk in circuits. The deer-things move in rotations. The marks on the walls follow sequences that I am still learning to recognize, and the learning is the point, and the point is the learning, and the circle closes.

---

I will tell you what I am afraid of, because fear is still possible here, though it too is suspended, held in the half-expression that the Kept wear on their faces.

I am afraid that I am becoming the room. Not metaphorically. The stone is warm when I touch it, warmer than it should be, warmer than the air. The mist here does what I tell it to — not dramatically, not in ways that would be visible to an observer, but I have noticed that when I concentrate, the mist in the chamber thins slightly, as though it is making room for my attention, clearing space for the work of counting. And the marks respond to my touch in ways that stone should not respond to skin. When I trace a spiral, I feel it continue beneath the surface of the rock, extending inward, deeper, following a path that I cannot see but can sense, a path that connects to other marks, other spirals, a network of inscription that penetrates the plateau's stone like roots penetrate soil.

I am afraid that this is what happened to the one who counted before me, and the one before that, and the one before that, stretching back to the builders themselves. That the counting is not a practice but a transformation, that each counter adds their marks to the wall and then adds themselves to the marks, becoming part of the inscription, part of the record, part of the plateau's accumulation. That the bone tool was not given to me by the old woman but by the room itself, working through her hands, selecting its next component.

I am afraid of this, and I am drawn to it, and the two feelings — fear and attraction — exist in the same suspension that everything else exists in here, neither one resolving into action, neither one fading into absence. They simply are, the way the mist simply is, the way the marks on the walls simply are, the way I am becoming simply a part of the counting.

---

The traders come once a year, sometimes twice. I hear them before I see them — not their voices, which the mist absorbs, but their footsteps, which carry a rhythm I recognize from my former life, the rhythm of people who walk with purpose, who walk toward something, who believe in arrival. I go to the market when they come. I trade the cloth that the Kept weave, and I take the salt and the metal implements, and I speak to the traders in the language of the below, which I still remember, which sits in my mouth like a flavor I cannot quite place.

They look at me with something like pity. They see a person who has been trapped, who has been absorbed, who has given up whatever the outside world offers in exchange for the plateau's suspension. They do not understand that I am not trapped. I am *held*, the way the marks are held in the stone, the way the mist is held in the air. The distinction matters, though I cannot explain it to them, and they descend the cliff-paths with their goods and their pity and their purposeful footsteps, and I return to the chamber.

---

Sometimes a child comes to watch me count. The Kept's children, with their ancient games and their quiet eyes, their faces that have not yet settled into the plateau's unfinished expressions. They stand in the doorway — the fissure, the gap — and they watch my hand move on the wall, and sometimes they ask questions in their water-over-rock language, and I answer as best I can.

One child — a girl, perhaps eight by outside reckoning, perhaps eighty, her face still capable of completing its expressions because she has not yet been here long enough for the plateau to claim it — asked me what the marks mean.

I told her I did not know.

She considered this. She did not accept it or reject it; she simply held it, the way the plateau holds everything, and then she said: *Maybe they mean the counting.*

I looked at her, and she looked at me, and in her eyes I saw something I had not seen in the Kept before: completion. A full expression, a thought that reached its conclusion, a moment that did not suspend itself but arrived. She smiled, and the smile finished, and she turned and walked out of the chamber, and I heard her footsteps — small, light, unhurried — recede into the mist.

I turned back to the wall. I picked up the bone tool. I found the place where my last spiral ended, and I continued from there, adding my mark to the accumulation, my line to the long conversation, my presence to the count that has been running since the plateau rose from the earth's skin and will continue until whatever force sustains it finally lets it rest.

The stone was warm beneath my hand. The mist thinned around me. The marks went deep, and I followed them.
