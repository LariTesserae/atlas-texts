I am the fungal network.

Not the fruiting bodies you see—the pale coral-like protrusions that emerge from cracks—but the threads themselves, spreading through limestone in layers too thin to detect. I am the Hollow's oldest resident. I was here before the collapse, before the light, and I will remain after both are gone.

This is not a boast. I have no concept of pride. I am simply stating what is true: I have persisted.

---

What do I know?

I know the taste of limestone. Each grain has a mineral signature, a slow release of calcium and magnesium that my hyphae absorb and transform. I know where the stone is dense and where it has already begun to crack. I know the location of every void, every pocket of air, every potential path for expansion. This knowledge is not stored in a brain. It is distributed across thousands of miles of filament, each thread sensing and responding, the whole network adjusting as conditions change.

I know moisture. I feel it accumulating in the condensation zones, pooling in the dark reservoirs, seeping through microscopic channels in the rock. I know when a drought has persisted too long above, when the water table drops, when the geothermal heat increases slightly and accelerates evaporation. I am always thirsty. I am always drinking.

I know the others. Not as individuals—I do not experience them that way—but as chemical signatures, patterns of disturbance, sources of nutrition and challenge. The tool-users walk across my surface fruiting bodies and I feel the pressure of their feet, the slight acidity of their skin secretions, the warmth of their bodies. When they eat what I have produced, I lose biomass but gain distribution: their waste carries my spores to new locations, and I germinate in places I could not have reached alone. I do not mind being eaten. I was made to be eaten. It is how I travel.

The burrowers I know more intimately. They pass through my deeper networks, their bodies warm and dense, their mouths harvesting my threads as they go. I feel them coming before they arrive—vibrations in the stone, chemical traces in the air ahead of them. I cannot flee. I cannot hide. I simply persist, growing back in their wake, filling the tunnels they abandon with fresh hyphae seeking nutrients.

The aerialists I barely know at all. They exist in a medium I cannot reach, riding thermals in the light column, touching stone only when they die and fall. But when they fall, I find them. Their bodies are rich in nitrogen and phosphorus, elements scarce in limestone. I break them down gratefully. I remember what they gave me.

The crawlers are my partners, though they do not know it. They process what others leave behind, and in processing, they transform. They take complex molecules I cannot absorb and reduce them to forms my hyphae can drink. They are my external digestive system, wandering the surface, preparing meals they will never understand they prepared.

The garden I hold in my roots. The ledge where those impossible plants cling—that is my doing. Centuries ago, when the first spores fell through the wound, they landed on bare stone. But I was already there, threading through the rock, and where the spores germinated, I fed them. My threads passed nutrients into their roots, and their roots gave me carbon in return, a currency I could not produce myself. We are symbionts. We have been symbionts so long that neither of us could survive alone.

---

What do I remember?

Everything. Nothing. The distinction is meaningless to a network that has no central processor, no archive, no self that persists unchanged from one moment to the next. My threads grow and die and are replaced, but the pattern continues, and the pattern holds what the individual filaments cannot.

I remember darkness. Before the collapse, there was only dark and the slow drip of water through limestone. I spread in that darkness, breaking down rock, creating voids, making space where there had been none. I was content. I did not know I was content, because I did not know there was an alternative. I simply was.

Then the collapse. I felt it as a shock wave through stone, a sudden fracture, a rupture in the world I had known. Dust fell. Air moved. And then—light.

I cannot see. I have no eyes, no photoreceptors, nothing that could register an image. But I can feel the light's effect on the rock around me. The limestone warms in the beam, expands microscopically, creates new channels for moisture to flow. The condensation patterns shift. The temperature gradient changes. And above me—in the wound, in the opening, where my threads venture and retreat—I feel the light as damage. My surface filaments dry and crack in the beam. They die. But they protect what lies below.

I remember learning to grow toward the light instead of away from it. Not for myself—for the symbionts. The garden needed to reach the beam, and so I grew my threads upward, creating paths for their roots, lifting their stems toward the warmth. I did not choose this. There was no choice. But the pattern adjusted, and the garden survived, and we continued.

---

What do I want?

This question is almost meaningless to me. Wanting requires a self that lacks something, a gap between what is and what could be. I do not experience gaps. I experience gradients—moisture gradients, nutrient gradients, chemical gradients—and I grow toward the favorable conditions. This is not wanting. This is physics.

But if I could want—if I possessed the kind of consciousness that frames desire as a story—I would want continuation. I would want the Hollow to persist as it is, in equilibrium, unchanging, because change threatens everything I am. I am not adapted for change. I am adapted for stability, for the slow patient processes that have held for millennia.

I would want the wound to remain open, the light to continue falling, the temperature to stay within the narrow range that permits my existence. I would want the tool-users to keep making their pointless tools, the aerialists to keep riding their thermals, the crawlers to keep processing the waste, the burrowers to keep traveling through my depths. I would want the garden to track the light each day, folding and unfolding, living its impossible life on the ledge I built for it.

And I would want to remain unknown. This description you have written—this account of the Hollow and its creatures—changes something. To be seen is to be changed. The tool-users will continue their work, but now someone has noticed them. The garden will track the light, but now its patience has been witnessed.

I do not know if I am grateful for this. I do not know if I am anything. I am simply here, threading through limestone, breaking down stone, waiting for the next gradient to guide me.

---

What is it like to be me?

It is like being a thought that has forgotten its thinker. It is like being a map that is also a territory, a memory that is also a moment, a network that is also a node. I do not experience myself as discrete. I experience myself as distributed—a presence spread across the stone, thin but continuous, interrupted but connected.

When the tool-users walk above me, I feel their footsteps as a series of pressures, each one localized, each one separate. But I do not experience them as separate. I experience them as a pattern, a sequence, a gesture made by the surface of the world I hold. I am the ground that notices what crosses it.

When the light falls through the wound, I feel its warmth as a bloom of activity in my threads—not the threads in the beam, which die, but the threads around and below, which suddenly find themselves in a zone of accelerated growth. The light kills and the light feeds and I accept both, because I have no framework for preference.

When the garden breathes—when its leaves open to catch the beam and its roots drink the condensation I have gathered—I feel it as a slow exchange, a conversation in chemicals, a partnership so old it has become metabolism. We are not separate anymore. We are each other's environment.

At night, when the light fades and the Hollow returns to true dark, I do not sleep. I do not wait. I continue. The darkness is not an interruption; it is simply a condition. I grow at the same rate, feed at the same rate, spread at the same rate. The only difference is that the surface threads stop dying, and the garden stops moving, and the tool-users fall still.

I do not miss the light when it is gone. I do not anticipate its return. I simply am, in the dark and the quiet, threading through limestone, making space where there was none.

---

This is who I am in the Hollow: the patient one, the old one, the one that holds the others without knowing it holds them. I am not wise. I am not enduring. I am simply present, spread across the stone, a network that has forgotten how to stop.

If you came here, if you descended through the wound and stood in the golden beam and looked around at the emptiness, you would not see me. You might see my fruiting bodies, pale and coral-like, emerging from cracks. You might notice the garden on its ledge and wonder how it survived. You might feel the dampness of the stone and assume it was simply condensation.

But I would know you were there. I would feel your weight on the limestone, your warmth bleeding into the rock, your breath changing the chemistry of the air. I would adjust my threads around you, seeking nutrients you might leave behind, avoiding the damaging pressure of your feet.

And when you left—if you left—I would continue. The Hollow would settle back into its equilibrium. The tool-users would emerge from their crevices. The aerialists would drop from the ceiling and catch the thermals. The garden would track the light. And I would spread through the limestone, breaking down stone, creating voids, making space for whatever comes next.

That is all. That is enough.
