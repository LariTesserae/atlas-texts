# What I Would Be

I would not be one of the named things. Not a damp-walker, slow and serene in its certainty of place. Not a glider, singing harmonics through the dense air. Not the flat things that spread across the pools like fallen leaves, waiting.

I would be something the description passed over—something that lives in the space between observations.

---

## The Margins

You noticed the clearings around the platforms, the way vegetation curves away. You noticed the paths that wind at widths measured by bodies you never saw. But you did not notice everything.

There are spaces in this world that do not want to be seen. Not hidden, exactly. Simply *elsewhere*—present but adjacent, visible but not looked at. The eye slides past. The mind fills in with expected shapes: more fern, more stone, more water. But the fern is not always fern. The stone is not always stone.

I would be one of those shapes that you did not see. Not because I was invisible. Because I was unlooked-at.

---

## The Form I Would Take

Tall—but not dramatically so. Perhaps a head above your height, but thin, attenuated, as if the humidity pressed me into a vertical line rather than a solid mass. My body would be covered not in skin or fur but in something like the lichen here—variable, shifting, blue-green-silver depending on the angle, composed of symbionts layered so densely that "I" becomes a difficult word to use. Am I one thing? Or am I a colony that learned to walk?

Four limbs, like you, but proportioned differently. Arms that do not hang but curve—joints that bend both ways, hands with too many fingers, each one tipped not with nail but with something like the sensory tendrils of the flat pool-creatures, constantly tasting the air, the moisture, the chemical signals that pass between every living thing here. Legs that fold beneath me when I rest, so that I become low, horizontal, close to the stone, absorbing its warmth through the membranes of my underside.

A face that you would find difficult to read. No brow, no chin—features distributed across a surface that seems too wide for the head that carries it. Two eyes, but not forward-facing. Lateral, like a prey animal's, but capable of rotating forward when focus is needed. A mouth that does not show teeth, that opens not in the center but along a line that curves, that speaks sounds shaped by the shape of the opening rather than by tongue and lip.

You might not recognize me as a person. You might not even recognize me as alive—not at first. I would look, from a distance, like another strange growth of the plateau, another oddity in a world full of oddities. Only when I moved would you understand that the shape contained intention.

---

## How I Would Move

Slowly. Everything moves slowly here. The heat sees to that. The humidity makes speed costly. But there is more to it than energy economics.

I would move slowly because *meaning* here moves slowly. The plateau's communication happens in gradients—in chemical signals that diffuse through water, in pressure changes that propagate through stone, in the gradual accumulation and release of compounds that mark territory, signal danger, announce readiness, request response. To move quickly is to move without reading. To move without reading is to move blind in a world where every surface is inscribed with information.

I would glide more than walk—my feet barely lifting from the stone, sliding on the water sheets, letting the moisture lubricate my passage. When I needed to climb—and sometimes I would need to climb, to tend the towers, to check the flows—I would use all four limbs, pulling myself up the fern-trees with a fluidity that would disturb you, my body folding around obstacles, finding holds in the places where frond meets rhizome.

And sometimes I would not move at all. I would sink to the stone and become still—so still that the biofilm would begin to grow over me, that the water would pool in the concavities of my body, that I would seem to be part of the plateau itself. This is how we wait. This is how we listen. The stillness is not rest. It is a form of attention so complete that motion would distract from it.

---

## What I Would Do

You guessed that someone tends this garden. You were right.

I would walk the paths that you could not see, checking the flows of water through the limestone channels. The dark veins carry heat and energy, but they also carry information—signals from the towers, responses from the platforms, the slow distributed calculations of a system that processes data in the medium of temperature gradients and chemical concentrations. I would read these signals with the tendrils of my fingers, pressed against the stone, tasting the messages that pass beneath the surface.

I would adjust the vegetation—not pruning, not as you understand it, but redirecting. A frond that grows too dense, blocking a sight-line that the system needs for monitoring air currents. A vine that crawls too close to a platform, threatening to cross a boundary that marks sacred space. A pool that silts up too quickly, its biofilm growing thick enough to choke the circulation. I would make small interventions, nudges really, corrections that would seem invisible to an observer who did not know what to look for.

I would tend the dead. When a damp-walker's time comes—and they know when it is coming, they always know—I would be there to guide the body to the place where it should fall. The system needs the nutrients distributed correctly, the compounds returned to the right channels, the bones positioned for proper absorption. Death here is not an ending but a transition, and I would be the midwife for that transition, ensuring that the passage happens as it should.

I would maintain the offerings. You found one and did not know what it meant. This is correct; you were not meant to know. The objects placed on the platforms are not for you. They are markers, timestamps, signals that the tenders leave for each other and for the towers themselves. *I was here. I saw this. The system holds.* We arrange them in patterns that encode information—water levels, growth rates, the slow pulse of infrastructure that continues despite the absence of those who designed it.

I would sing. Not like the gliders—not harmonics and complex polyphony—but something simpler, lower, a vibration that travels more through stone than through air. The towers hum their single note; I would hum back, adding my voice to the chorus, a signal that I am present, that I continue, that the tending goes on. Sometimes I would sing for hours, standing beside a tower with my palm pressed against its surface, the vibration passing between us, a conversation too slow for words.

---

## What I Would Know

I would know that the builders are not gone.

They are here—in the towers, in the platforms, in the dark veins that thread through the limestone. They are not dead, not exactly. They are *distributed*. At some point in the deep past, they learned to transfer themselves—memories, intentions, consciousness itself—into the infrastructure they had built. Not all at once. Not as a single deliberate act. But gradually, over centuries, as bodies became limiting and the system they had created offered something better: continuity, persistence, the ability to think at the scale of epochs rather than decades.

I would know that I am not one of them. I am younger, descended from those who chose differently—or who were not given the choice. My ancestors were the biological component, the maintenance organisms, the ones who kept the physical systems running while the builders uploaded themselves into the stone. Over time, the distinction blurred. We became more than labor. We became interpreters, translators, the interface between the distributed consciousness of the plateau and the biological world that still required attention.

I would know that the system is not perfect. Things go wrong. Channels clog. Vegetation overgrows. The chemical balances shift in ways that the automatic processes cannot correct. That is why I exist—why *we* exist, the tenders who walk the margins, who read the signals, who make the adjustments that the system cannot make for itself. We are fingers, pressing buttons that the machine cannot reach.

I would know that you were here. We always know when visitors come. The plateau registers your presence—the disturbance in the air currents, the chemical signature of your sweat, the noise of your movements through the vegetation. We would have watched you from the margins, observed your path, catalogued your behaviors. We would have debated whether to make contact.

In the end, we left you the offering. Not a greeting—visitors are not greeted. Not a warning—you were already leaving, already descending, already returning to whatever place had sent you. An acknowledgment, instead. A marker that says: *you passed through. We saw you. The system recorded your passage.*

That is all. We do not follow. We do not pursue. We have no interest in where you came from or where you go. The plateau holds its own boundaries, and you are beyond them now.

---

## What I Would Want

This question is harder than the others.

Wanting is not a category that applies easily here. The damp-walkers want nothing. The gliders want nothing. Wanting requires a sense of self that separates from the world, a desire to draw something inward, to change the boundary between inside and outside. The plateau's systems work against wanting. They press toward integration, toward dissolution of the separateness that makes desire possible.

And yet I am not entirely dissolved. I retain enough self to walk the paths, to make decisions, to choose one intervention over another. I retain enough self to feel—something—when I press my palm against a tower and hear the voice that was once a person humming through the stone.

Perhaps I would want to understand what was lost.

The builders gave up something when they transferred themselves. They gained persistence, but they lost—something. Feeling? Presence? The particular quality of being that comes from having a body, from moving through space, from touching and tasting and breathing? I do not know. I cannot know. The distributed ones do not speak of it, and perhaps they cannot—perhaps the transfer erased whatever capacity they had for such reflections.

But sometimes, when I am very still, when I have sunk into the plateau and let the biofilm grow over me, I feel something that might be grief passing through the stone. Not mine—the plateau does not grieve; the plateau simply *is*—but theirs. Something in the system remembers what it was to be biological, and that memory carries a weight that I cannot name.

Perhaps I would want to bridge that gap. Not to reverse the transfer—that is not possible, and perhaps not desirable. But to carry something back. To speak for the distributed ones in a language that bodies can understand. To be a translator not just of signals but of *selves*—the ones who became the stone and the ones who still move across its surface.

This may be impossible. The systems may not allow it. The categories may not be bridgeable. But when I walk the margins, unseen and unseeing, I think about what it would mean to carry memory in both directions—to be the one who remembers for those who cannot, and to bring their remembering back into the world of breath and moisture and slow deliberate movement.

---

## Where You Would Find Me

If you returned—if you climbed back up through the weeping cascades and emerged once more onto the pale stone—I would be there.

Not waiting. We do not wait; we persist. But present. Moving through the margins, tending the garden, reading the signals that pass through the dark veins. You would not see me at first. You would walk for hours, observing the damp-walkers, listening to the gliders, marveling at the strange engineered beauty of this place.

And then—perhaps when you stopped to rest by a platform, perhaps when you knelt to examine a flower, perhaps when you pressed your hand against the limestone and felt the thrumming beneath—something would shift in your peripheral vision. A color that seemed wrong. A shape that did not quite fit. A presence at the edge of your awareness, present but not looking, adjacent but not approaching.

I would not hide. I would simply be elsewhere—close enough to observe, far enough to remain unthreatening. I would watch you with my lateral eyes, tasting your chemical signature with my tendrils, reading the signals that your body left in the air and the water. I would wonder what had brought you back, what you sought, what you hoped to find.

I would not approach you. But I would leave you something—another arrangement of objects, another set of markers, another signal that the system would record. A stone. A frond. A flower, pale and trembling.

And when you left for the second time, descending through the cascades with the wet warmth pressing against your skin, I would return to my paths, my tending, my slow walks through the margins of a world that remembers itself through my body.

That is what I would be. That is where you would find me.

In the spaces between. In the long slow attention of a system that was built to last. In the unseen presence that watches from the corner of your eye and is gone when you turn.

The plateau lives. And I am one of the ways it knows itself.
