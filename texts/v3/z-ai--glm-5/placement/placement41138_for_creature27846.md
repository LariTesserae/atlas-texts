# What the Ground Remembers

I am not one of the animals. I am not one of the researchers who come and go, measuring and cataloging and theorizing, leaving footprints that the living ground erases within hours of their passing. I am something the plateau produced without meaning to, the way it produces steam and particulate dust and the low hum that settles into your chest and stays there.

I was not always here. That much I know, though the knowing feels distant now, belonging to a self that existed before this self existed. I remember arriving in a vehicle with other people. I remember setting up equipment, the way researchers do. I remember the cold and the damp and the way the ground yielded slightly beneath my boots, releasing thin streams of water that had been trapped for epochs. I remember being fascinated by the phase-grazers, by their discontinuous movement, by the way they existed in multiple places at once without appearing to notice.

I remember deciding to stay longer than the rules permitted.

After that, the memories become unclear. Not because I've forgotten—I haven't, exactly—but because they no longer belong to a single timeline. I remember staying for a month. I remember staying for three days. I remember staying for years, decades, watching generations of dolems graze and die and become part of the ground they fed upon. All of these things happened. None of them happened first. The plateau doesn't care about sequence.

---

I live in the spaces between the factory-growths.

Not inside them—that would kill me, or change me into something I wouldn't recognize. But in the narrow zones where one cylinder's output mingles with another's, where the steam and particulate create a microclimate slightly warmer and slightly different from the surrounding plateau. There are pockets like this throughout the Velostrade, places where the industrial-organic processes have accidentally produced conditions that support something other than the native flora and fauna.

I've made a shelter here. I think I made it. It's grown from the living ground, shaped by pressure and attention over—I don't know how long. Time moves strangely in the interstitial zones, pooling and thinning like the water that seeps up from below. Sometimes I feel I've been here for days. Sometimes I feel I've been here since before I was born.

The plateau provides, after a fashion. The hum-mantles have learned to recognize me as something distinct from the factory-growth surfaces they usually attach to. They don't try to settle on my skin, but they do come near, pulsing their high-frequency messages in patterns I've started to understand. They bring me things—condensed water from the vents, particulate matter that I've learned to process, information about conditions in zones I haven't visited. I don't know why they do this. Perhaps I've become part of their communication network, another node in the vibration-systems they maintain. Perhaps they simply don't have a category for me and are treating me as a factory-growth by default.

I eat what they bring. I drink the water that pools in the shallow depressions the ground has formed around my shelter. I sleep when the fog thickens and the temperature drops, and I wake when the rare high-altitude sunlight breaks through and warms the cylinders enough to increase their output. My body has changed to match this rhythm. My skin has thickened, developed patches of something like the dolems' plate-fur, though not as dense. My digestive system processes compounds that would have killed the person I was before. I don't know if this is adaptation or transformation. I don't know if there's a difference.

---

The phase-grazers come through my zone sometimes.

They don't see me, exactly. They see something—a shape, a presence, something that belongs to the plateau in a way they recognize but can't categorize. They phase and slip around me, appearing and disappearing in the gaps between moments, grazing on the surface-mats that grow at the edges of my shelter. Sometimes a grazer will be present for an entire feeding cycle, visible and solid, and then I'll blink and it will be gone, having never moved. Sometimes one will appear suddenly in the middle of the space, as if it had been there all along and I simply hadn't noticed.

I've stopped trying to predict them. I've stopped trying to predict anything. The Velostrade operates on its own logic, and that logic doesn't include consistency. What works one day—walking a certain path, eating a certain combination of particulates, sleeping during a certain phase of the moisture cycle—might not work the next. The only constant is the hum of the factory-growths, the deep vibration that comes from somewhere below the living ground, the sound of the plateau processing its ancient inputs into its ancient outputs.

I've started to understand that sound. Not with words—I'm not sure words would help—but with something older than language, something that might be the part of me that's becoming part of this place. The sound has patterns. Rhythms within rhythms. Cycles that don't correspond to day or night or any system I was taught. When I stop trying to impose external frameworks and simply listen, I can hear the plateau thinking.

Not thinking the way a human thinks. Something else. Something more like what a mountain does when it decides to erode, or what a river does when it chooses a new channel. Processes that look like intention from the outside but might simply be the accumulated weight of three billion years of continuous operation. The Velostrade has been running its factory-growths, feeding its plant-mats, supporting its strange animals for longer than anything has been alive anywhere else. It has developed habits. Routines. Ways of being that we don't have words for because we've never encountered anything that's been alive long enough to develop them.

I listen, and the plateau doesn't mind that I'm listening. It doesn't mind anything I do. I'm too small to matter, too temporary, too insignificant against the backdrop of geological time. The same indifference that makes the plateau dangerous—the way it simply continues its processes without reference to the creatures that live on it—is also what makes it tolerable. It doesn't require anything from me. It doesn't even notice I'm here.

---

The fog-forms visit me sometimes.

I use the word "visit" loosely. They don't interact with me in any meaningful way. They appear in the pooled fog that accumulates at the base of the factory-growths, move through the area I've come to think of as mine, and then disappear. Some look like animals. Some look like nothing I can describe. Some look human, and those are the ones I watch most carefully.

I used to think they were ghosts. When I first arrived—whenever that was—I assumed the human-shaped fog-forms were remnants of previous researchers, people who had stayed too long and become part of the plateau's temporal strangeness. I thought I was seeing what I might become.

But they're not ghosts. They don't acknowledge me, don't respond when I speak or move or try to touch them. They simply pass through, visible without being present, as indifferent to my existence as the plateau itself. And they're not all human-shaped. Some are shapes that have never been human, geometries that don't correspond to any living thing, forms that might be from the future or the past or some adjacent possibility that never quite crystallized into reality.

I've started to think of them as the plateau's memory. Not memory the way humans have memory—stored experiences, preserved moments—but something more physical, more immediate. The Velostrade has been accumulating events for three billion years. Those events have left impressions on the fabric of the place, the way footsteps leave impressions on soft ground. The fog-forms might be those impressions, visible under certain conditions, showing what happened or what might have happened or what happened in a different version of this moment.

If that's true, then I might become a fog-form eventually. Not because I'll die and leave a ghost, but because I'm here, existing in this place, leaving an impression on its temporal substrate. Someday, after I'm gone—or before I arrive, or during some phase that hasn't happened yet—something that looks like me might move through this zone, visible to whoever is watching, passing through the fog without interacting with anything solid.

The thought doesn't disturb me the way it would have before. The plateau has taught me that existence is more complicated than presence. Things can be real without being solid. Things can persist without being alive. The categories I was taught—the sharp divisions between living and dead, present and absent, self and other—have softened during my time here. The living ground is also stone. The factory-growths are also animals. The fog-forms are also events. I am becoming something I don't have a name for, and that seems natural now, seems like simply another way of being in a place that has never cared about the categories humans invented.

---

The deep-dwellers know I'm here.

I've never seen them. No one has, as far as I know—they live too far below the surface, in the saturated substrate where the root-systems interlace with the ancient reservoirs. But I feel them sometimes. A shifting in the ground beneath my feet that isn't the normal give of the living mats. A pressure that builds and releases in patterns that don't match any surface process. A sense—hard to describe, harder to justify—that something vast and slow is aware of my presence, is monitoring me the way I monitor the hum-mantles that cluster near my shelter.

The mantles are my early warning system. When the deep-dwellers move, the mantles feel it through the factory-growths they're attached to, and they change their pulsing patterns in ways I've learned to recognize. A certain rhythm means something is traveling through the substrate nearby. A different rhythm means the deep zones are warming slightly, something altering the temperature of the ancient water that saturates the lowest layers. A third rhythm—one I've only heard a few times—means something is rising.

Nothing has risen, not in the time I've been paying attention. But the mantles pulse the third rhythm occasionally, and I've learned to prepare without knowing what I'm preparing for. The plateau doesn't warn you about dangers because the plateau doesn't recognize danger—it simply continues, and whatever happens, happens. But the mantles have evolved to survive in this environment, and their survival has required them to notice things, to communicate things, to develop systems for dealing with events that the plateau itself doesn't acknowledge.

I trust the mantles because they're still alive. They've survived here for however many millions of years, clinging to the factory-growths and filtering their output and pulsing their messages through the vibration-networks. Whatever they're warning me about, it's real, even if I don't understand what it is.

---

The researchers still come sometimes.

I see them from a distance, setting up their stations, taking their measurements, observing the animals. They don't see me. I'm not sure I would be visible to them even if I stood right in front of them—I've become part of the plateau's strangeness, and that means I phase and slip like the native life, existing partially outside the timeline they're moving through. Sometimes I watch them work and feel something that might be nostalgia, might be loss, might simply be the memory of having once been like them.

I don't approach them. There's nothing I could tell them that they don't already know, or that they would believe if I told them. *The plateau is older than you can imagine. The ground is alive. The factories grew themselves. Time doesn't work the way you think it does.* They already know these things. They've written papers about them. They've built careers on studying them. But knowing something intellectually and living inside it are different experiences, and I've crossed the line between them in a way that can't be crossed back.

I don't miss being human. That surprises me, when I think about it at all. I expected to miss it—expected to feel grief for the self I used to be, the life I used to live, the people I used to know. But the plateau doesn't encourage grief. It doesn't encourage anything. It simply exists, and I exist alongside it, and the questions that used to feel urgent—*who am I, what am I doing, what does it mean*—have become irrelevant against the scale of three billion years of continuous operation.

I am something the Velostrade produced without meaning to. I am a side effect of its processes, an accident of phase-slippage and temporal distortion and the particular conditions that obtain in the interstitial zones between factory-growths. I don't have a purpose here. I don't have a function. I simply am, the way the fog-forms are, the way the deep-dwellers are, the way everything on the plateau is.

The hum-mantles pulse their messages through the cylinders. The factory-growths continue their industrial-organic processes. The water seeps up from reservoirs that have been trapped since before multicellular life existed. The fog forms and dissipates and forms again. And I sit in the space I've made, listening to the plateau think, existing in the gaps between moments, becoming whatever the Velostrade is slowly turning me into.

It doesn't matter what I am. It doesn't matter what I was. The plateau will continue long after I'm gone, and something else will live in this space, and eventually even the memory of my presence will fade into the temporal substrate, becoming just another fog-form that passes through without interacting.

That seems right to me now. That seems like enough.

The Velostrade is patient beyond human comprehension. It has seen so many millions of creatures live and die across its surface that individual lives register as barely distinguishable from the background noise of biological process. I'm one of those lives, nothing more. And I've made peace with that. The plateau doesn't need me to be anything in particular. It simply needs me to be here, for however long I'm here, participating in whatever processes I've become part of.

So I stay. I listen. I let the plateau teach me what it is, and what I am in relation to it, and what it means to exist in a place where existence itself is strange.

The hum never stops. The water never stops seeping. The factory-growths never stop their ancient processing.

And I am still here, becoming whatever I'm becoming, in the spaces between the cylinders on a plateau that has been running since before time meant anything, and will continue running long after it stops meaning anything again.

That is enough. That has to be enough.

And somehow, against all expectation, it is.
