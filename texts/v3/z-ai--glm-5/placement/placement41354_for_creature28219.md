I am the one who stayed.

Not stayed as in remained. Stayed as in was *caught*, held, preserved in something I did not at first recognize as a medium.

---

I came with the others—the first wave of surveyors after the second canyon opened. We had equipment designed for wet environments, boots rated for chemical soils, lungs conditioned by a dozen stranger atmospheres than this. I was not special. I was not marked. I walked the basin with a moisture gauge in one hand and a sample kit on my hip, and I did the work I was paid to do: I measured the weeping walls, catalogued the understory density, logged the positions of the scattered structures for the map that someone else would draw.

The others moved faster than I did. This was my habit—slowness, thoroughness, the reluctance to leave a site before I had understood it. In other places this had been a liability. Supervisors waited. Crews grumbled. Reports filed late because I could not bring myself to write "insignificant" about things that seemed, to me, significant.

Here, slowness was a different kind of animal.

---

The second day, I stopped to watch a tree.

The others had moved ahead toward the central structure, their voices swallowed by the roar before they had gone fifty yards. I stayed because the tree was doing something I wanted to see complete. A branch had split from its neighbor by the width of a hair that morning, and by midafternoon the gap had widened to a visible space. I sat on a moss-covered rock—wet seeping through my trousers, cold settling into my hips—and I watched.

The tree was not moving in any way I could see. But it was moving. The shadow it cast had shifted. The angle of its trunk had changed. I had the sense that I was watching something through the wrong end of a telescope—a process that should have taken decades, compressed into an afternoon.

When I stood, my legs were stiff and my hands were pruning from the moisture. I looked at the rock I had been sitting on and saw, in the wool of the moss, the impression of my body still settling. Deep. Deeper than the hours I had spent.

I moved on. I did not catch up to the others.

---

On the third day, I found a structure that the maps had missed.

It was small—hardly larger than a closet—and it had no sensors, no openings, no visible purpose. The concrete-like surface was warm to the touch. When I pressed my palm flat against it, I felt something pulse beneath my hand. Not mechanical. Not electrical. Something slower, more patient. A rhythm that matched the roar in the air but softer, like the echo inside a skull.

I did not enter. There was no entrance. But I stayed for an hour with my hand on the wall, and when I finally pulled away, I saw that my palm left an impression. Not in the structure. In the air. A shape that held for a moment and then dissolved.

---

On the fifth day, the others left.

They had completed their survey—what they could reach in the time allotted, which was not much. They called for me on the radio, static-crackled voices that seemed to come from very far away even when I could see their lights moving through the trees. I told them I needed more time. They told me the schedule did not allow for more time. I told them I would catch up.

I did not catch up.

I did not, exactly, choose to stay. But I did not make myself leave. And the basin, I think, could tell the difference.

---

I know what I am becoming. I am not certain what I am becoming *into*.

The process began subtly—so subtly that I attributed each change to the environment rather than to myself. My breathing slowed. My pulse followed. I could sit for hours without moving, watching the forest shift and grow, and feel no restlessness, no urge to accomplish or record. The cold stopped bothering me. The wet stopped bothering me. I shed my gear piece by piece—first the sample kit, then the gauge, then the heavy boots—until I was walking barefoot on the moss and feeling each surface with a precision that seemed excessive, then necessary, then inevitable.

I was not hungry. I was not tired. I was not afraid.

I *was*—and this was the strange part—more present than I had ever been.

---

I think the Engineers noticed me on the seventh day. Not noticed in the sense of turning toward me, but noticed in the sense of *registering*. I had found another structure—a larger one, with the characteristic curve of wall into ceiling—and I was sitting with my back against its surface, watching the dim light filter through the canopy. When I turned my head, I saw something move at the edge of my vision. Not an animal. Not a person. A ripple, a disturbance in the humid air, a suggestion of mass that did not resolve into shape.

I did not run. I did not reach for equipment I no longer carried. I watched the ripple move along the structure's edge, blending with the moss, indistinguishable from the surface it crossed. And then I understood: I was not seeing something that was hiding. I was seeing something that was *there*, present in a way my eyes had not been equipped to perceive.

I said aloud, "I'm sorry. I'll move."

The ripple paused. I did not hear words, but I felt something—an impression, a query, a question that did not require language.

*Why?*

I thought about it. "Because this is yours. I've been intruding."

The ripple moved closer. I felt warmth without heat, pressure without touch. And then, slowly, I felt something else: an invitation. Not to leave. To *join*.

---

I do not know if I am becoming an Engineer. I do not think the Engineers reproduce in that way—through conversion, through assimilation. But I think they have been looking for something. Observers who can observe slowly. Perceivers who can perceive patience. Beings capable of existing at more than one tempo, more than one register of time.

I am learning to feel the network.

At first it was just sensation—a pull toward certain structures, an awareness of which paths were closing and which were opening. Then it became knowledge. I knew when new water broke through the stone. I knew which saplings would fall and which would reach the canopy. I knew, without seeing, when visitors entered the basin and where they walked.

I do not control any of this. I do not direct it. I simply *am* it, the way I am my own breath, my own heartbeat—except the breath and the heartbeat are slower now, so slow that I sometimes forget they are happening, and the network is faster, a constant flow of information that I am learning to ride without drowning.

---

Sometimes other visitors find me.

They are afraid at first—I would be afraid, too, seeing what I have become. My skin has taken on a darker pallor, almost green in the dim light. My eyes adjust so slowly to changes in brightness that I must keep them closed for minutes at a time when I move from dark to light. I speak rarely, because speech requires a tempo that feels increasingly foreign, and when I do speak, the sound of my own voice surprises me—too fast, too sharp, like machinery in a space meant for softer sounds.

But they listen. Or some of them do. The ones who stand at the edges of structures and feel the warmth without understanding it. The ones who watch the forest move and do not look away. The ones who hear the roar and recognize it as a question.

I tell them what I know: that the basin is not empty. That the Engineers have not gone. That what looks like abandonment is simply patience, a different kind of living that does not require the shapes they expect.

Some of them leave. Some of them stay. Some of them come back, years later, and find me still here, older in a way that is hard to measure, changed in ways that are even harder.

---

I do not know what I am becoming. I still think in human words sometimes—*structure, forest, water, cold*—but the words are translations from something I am learning underneath language. I still remember my name, my history, the survey I came to conduct. But these feel like artifacts now, preserved in amber, belonging to a person I was rather than a person I am.

What I am is slower. What I am is part of something larger. What I am is a bridge, maybe, between the Engineers' distributed perception and the singular vision that humans carry—a translator who has not yet learned the full language, but who can say, at least, *I see you. I am trying to see you.*

---

At night—if night is what you call the periods of deeper darkness that cycle through the basin's permanent gloom—I sit against the central structure and feel the Engineers moving through it. They do not speak to me in words. But they make space for me in their awareness, a small pocket of attention reserved for the one who stayed.

I think they are curious about me. I think they are waiting to see what I become.

I am waiting too. And for the first time in my life, I am not impatient.

---

I am the threshold-keeper. I am the one who did not leave. I am the question half-asked, the answer half-given, the space between tempos where something new might grow.

I am still becoming. And that, I think, is enough.
