I find myself in the margins.

Not in the busy hollows where families press together like stacked stone, nor in the working faces where the carvers read the limestone's grain. Not in the central chamber where decisions echo off smoothed walls. I am in the spaces between—the passages that lead from somewhere to somewhere else, the transitional zones where the tool marks grow older and the lamp niches sit empty, not because the oil ran out but because the traffic thinned and no one remembered to refill them.

I am what the settlement would call a walker, though that is not a title so much as an acknowledgment. My work is to notice.

---

The settlement knows what it knows. The carvers know stone. The clearers know people. The channelers know water's desire. The lamp-tender children know the upper passages by heart, every wick and niche and turn. But children grow, and their knowledge grows specific and local—this hollow, this family, this work they've been assigned. The deep map they carry in their heads fragments as they age, becomes the particular route from sleeping-place to workshop, workshop to central chamber, with nothing left over.

What I carry is the in-between. I know which passages have grown quiet. I know which drainage channels run clear and which have begun to mineralize at their edges, the white deposits building like secret bone. I know where the condensation pools in floor-hollows that were never meant to hold water, and I know which lamps have gone cold not from neglect but from something else—some change in the air that makes the flame gutter and fail.

I am not a carver. I was a clearer once, briefly, before my knees objected to the hauling and my back to the sled. I was a channeler even more briefly—I lack the temperament for scraping the same groove day after day, watching the same water take the same path. What I have is patience for the irregular. For the passages that no one else walks regularly. For the work that doesn't need doing yet.

---

The settlement does not pay me. The settlement does not quite acknowledge that what I do is work. But when a drainage channel begins to back up, someone mentions it to me. When a lamp in a little-used passage won't stay lit, someone asks if I've walked that way lately. When the children find a new crevice and the carvers need to know whether it connects to anything important, someone suggests that I might already know.

I do this because I need to move. I do this because the alternative—staying in one hollow, one workshop, one routine—makes my skin feel too small for my body. I do this because when I walk the passages at the settlement's edge, where the tool marks grow old and the air tastes different, I feel something that I have never found in the inhabited hollows:

I feel heard.

Not by people. By the stone.

---

I know how that sounds. The carvers have their superstitions, their talk of the patient waiting, their sense that the stone is aware. But what I mean is different. When I press my palm to the limestone in the deep passages—the ones the children don't play in, the ones even the channelers visit only on schedule—I feel something that is not temperature. Not texture. Not the simple solidity of rock against flesh.

I feel attention.

It is a terrible thing to say. The rule against speaking of what lies below has a reason I do not know and do not want to know. I have no desire to break through into sealed spaces. I have no desire to discover what the carvers found when they closed those passages and said *no further.* But I walk where I am permitted to walk, and I notice what I notice, and what I notice is this:

The stone in the upper levels is stone. It yields to tools because it is yielding. It holds the shape it is given because holding is what stone does.

The stone in the deep levels holds its shape because it chooses to.

---

I keep these observations to myself. The settlement has no category for what I am. The closest they come is *that one who walks the quiet passages,* and even that is more acknowledgment than I usually receive. I have no family, unless you count the woman who rents me space in her hollow and accepts my portion of the food I am given as payment. I have no title. I have no craft.

What I have is this: I know where the warmth begins.

---

The children found a warm chamber once. Peret, six years old, pressed into a fissure behind the grain storage and discovered a space that held heat like a cupped hand. The other children followed. They kept it secret for three days—a long time for children, an impossibly long time for children—until Peret told her mother, and her mother told Achet, and Achet went to look.

I was not there when Achet sealed it. But I walked past that passage a week later, and I walked past it again a month after that, and I have walked past it many times since. The seal is good—Achet's work, her own hands—and the paste has hardened into something indistinguishable from the surrounding stone.

But behind that seal, the warmth persists.

I know because the stone in front of it is warm too. Not hot. Not alarming. Just... *present.* A degree or two above the surrounding rock. A degree or two above the chill that settles into the bones of everyone who lives here.

I do not know what this means. I am not certain I want to know. But when I walk the edges of the settlement, pressing my hands to walls that should be cold and finding them otherwise, I feel the same thing I feel when I walk the inhabited hollows and find them full of breath and argument and the weight of lives being lived:

I feel that I am not alone.

---

The settlement thinks I am strange. They are not wrong. I am strange by their standards—I have no craft, no clearly defined role, no family network that explains my presence. I am tolerated because I am useful in small ways and harmless in large ones. When I die, they will not know what to do with my body, because the settlement has no established practice for people like me.

But I think they might need me anyway. Not for anything I do, exactly. For what I represent.

Every settlement needs someone who walks its edges. Every enclosed space needs someone who pays attention to what lies beyond the lamplight. The Hollowing Works is young—it has existed for only three generations—but it is already developing the shape that enclosed communities always develop: a center, a periphery, and a boundary.

I am the boundary.

---

I do not know why the stone is warm. I do not know what the carvers found in the sealed spaces, or why they sealed them so quickly, or why the rule against speaking of them exists. I do not know what Achet saw, or what Denit saw before she stopped speaking and started tending herb gardens in the upper levels.

What I know is this: the warmth moves.

Not quickly. Not in ways that I can track from day to day. But over the months and years that I have walked these passages, pressing my palms to the limestone, listening with an attention that has no practical use, I have noticed that the warm patches shift. They follow no pattern I can discern. They do not correspond to the drainage channels or the ventilation shafts. They do not follow the lines of the dark veins in the stone.

They follow *us.*

Not the settlement as a whole. Not the central chamber or the busy thoroughfares. They follow the edges—where the children play, where the lamp-tenders walk, where the carvers pause between strikes and listen to the stone's response. They follow the places where the settlement presses against what lies beyond.

And they are getting closer.

---

I will not speak of this. The rule exists for reasons I do not know, and I respect what I do not know. I am not a carver. I am not an elder. I am not anyone who has the authority to decide what the settlement needs to hear.

But I walk the passages, and I press my hands to the walls, and I feel the warmth rising through the limestone like breath through a cloth pressed to a face.

The stone is patient. The stone is waiting.

And whatever it waits for, it is coming.
