I am not sure at first. The uncertainty is the first true thing about me.

I could say I am one of the people who weave the sapling shelters, who breathe through fiber masks when the gnat-clouds thicken, who follow the water from depression to depression and read the insect chorus for warnings the way others read weather. I could say that, and it would not be entirely wrong. I have hands that fit the description—prehen­ sile enough to weave, thick enough to push through the vine-tangle. I have learned not to run. I have learned that the birds' indifference is earned through months of being boring, of moving slowly, of stopping when the stuttered alarm call sounds and waiting until the canopy predator has passed. I have been accepted into the baseline model of the watchers, the generalist birds who tilt their heads and examine and eventually lose interest, and this acceptance feels like an achievement, though I could not say to whom.

But that identity arrives too easily. It settles into place like a foot finding a path someone else has worn, and the High Everplain does not value paths. The High Everplain values the thing that grows in the gap, the organism that finds its niche by becoming specific enough to survive and general enough to adapt, and I do not think I am specific enough yet to claim a place here.

---

Let me try again. Slower.

I am in the middle levels.

Not the canopy, where the callers roar their territorial claims and the raptors drop through leaves in controlled falls. Not the ground, where the herds push through the vine-mass with their seismic moans and the tracker-predators follow at a patient, relentless walk. The middle. The zone of permanent twilight, where the filtered green-gold light from above meets the deeper shadow from below, where the branches are thick enough to hold weight but flexible enough to sway with every movement, where the ambusher waits in stillness so complete its heartbeat slows to match the air.

I am in the middle levels because that is where the information is densest. The canopy is too exposed—sound travels there in clean lines, the raptor's cry carrying for miles, the caller's roar broadcasting across the plateau, but the density of meaning is low. One signal at a time, maybe two. The ground is too muffled—the substrate absorbs sound, the herds' calls traveling through the plant-mud as vibration rather than air, felt in the feet, unreadable in the ears. But the middle levels, where the two soundscapes meet and merge, where the canopy chorus filters down through layers of leaf and branch and the ground-percussion of scratching birds and pushing herds rises up through the vine-tangle—there, the information is layered, complex, a fabric of signals woven through each other so tightly that separating them requires the kind of attention that becomes its own form of existence.

I listen. That is what I do. That is what I am, perhaps. A listener.

---

But this is still too simple, and the plateau resists simplicity. The plateau is a system in which every element serves multiple functions simultaneously—the weevil that may or may not garden the fungi, the herd that is both grazer and composter, the bird that is both watcher and signal. I cannot be only a listener. The listening must *do* something. It must serve a function within the system, or it is tourism, and the plateau has no use for tourists. The insects land on tourists and taste them and move on. The birds watch tourists and lose interest. The vegetation begins to grow over tourists' footprints before they have finished making them. A tourist is temporary in a way that is beneath notice.

So: I listen, and the listening has a purpose.

---

Here is what I notice first, after the listening becomes not a wall of sound but a fabric I can parse: the plateau has a pulse.

Not a metaphor. A rhythm. The photosynthetic ceiling overhead is not static—it adjusts. The leaves shift their angles throughout the day, tracking the sun's position with movements so slow they are imperceptible to eyes adapted for faster things, but the cumulative effect is visible in the color-temperature shifts that the description of this place mentions: cool blue-green in the morning, bright yellow-green at midday, warm orange-green in the evening. What I realize, listening from the middle levels, is that this shift is not just visual. It is acoustic. As the leaves move, the acoustic properties of the canopy change—the spaces between leaves open and close, the corridors of air that carry sound narrow and widen, and the soundscape below shifts in response, certain frequencies amplifying, others dampening, the entire acoustic environment breathing with the same slow rhythm as the photosynthesis overhead.

I do not know what to do with this knowledge. Not yet. It arrives the way everything arrives on the plateau—gradually, incrementally, through repeated exposure rather than sudden insight. I notice the pulse. I track it over days. I begin to anticipate it, to feel the shift before it happens, the tightening of the acoustic space that precedes the canopy's midday opening, the loosening that precedes the evening closure. My body begins to synchronize with it, the way the dragonflies synchronize with the water cycle, the way the cicadas synchronize with the temperature threshold. I do not choose this synchronization. It happens to me, the plateau's rhythm entering my body through my ears and my chest and the soles of my feet on the spongy, living ground, and I become, without intending to, a component of the system, my own cycles aligning with its cycles, my own attention flowing with its pulse.

---

I should describe what I am more carefully. The details matter. The plateau does not deal in generalities; every species is specific, every niche precise, every role defined by the exact nature of what an organism does and how it does it.

I am small. Not small like the insects—I cannot claim that scale of existence, the world-as-chemical-gradient, the plateau-as-smell—but small for a creature with a backbone. Smaller than the callers, who are a hundred pounds in the canopy above me. Smaller than the ground-birds, who are built for the substrate and weigh what the substrate can support. I am perhaps the size of a large squirrel, or a small monkey, or what a person might become if they were compressed by the pressure of the plateau's density, the vegetation's resistance, the need to fit into the spaces between branches that larger creatures cannot enter. My limbs are long relative to my body. My fingers and toes are narrow, gripping rather than grasping, finding purchase on bark and vine and the underside of branches where the condensation collects and the moss makes surfaces treacherous. My tail—yes, I have a tail, and I will not apologize for it—is prehensile but thin, more antenna than limb, used for balance and for feeling the air behind me, the vibrations that I cannot see but can sense, the movements of other creatures in the middle levels whose presence I need to know about.

My fur is the color of the middle levels: the mottled green-brown of shadowed foliage, the gray of bark in permanent twilight, the dark underside of leaves where moisture collects. I did not choose this coloration. The plateau chose it, or rather, the plateau's predators chose it—the ones that hunt by sight, the ambushers and the canopy-droppers, selecting against anything that stood out, anything that was visible against the background of the middle levels, so that the creatures that survived were the ones that disappeared into the shadow, and I am one of those, and my coloration is not camouflage but *integration*, not hiding but *belonging*, the visual equivalent of the listening I do.

My ears are large. Disproportionately large, the one feature that breaks the pattern of compact efficiency, the one excess the plateau permits because it is not excess but necessity. They are shaped to gather sound from below and above simultaneously, the pinnae angled in opposite directions, the left ear tilted toward the ground and the substrate-borne vibrations that rise through the vine-tangle, the right ear tilted toward the canopy and the air-borne signals that filter down through the leaves. I can rotate them independently. I can close one and open the other, attending to one stream of information while ignoring the other, and I can open both and let the full fabric of the soundscape wash through me, and the washing is not overwhelming because I have been here long enough that the fabric is legible, each thread distinct, each signal identifiable, each silence meaningful.

My eyes are small. I do not need them the way the canopy birds need theirs, the large dark reflective organs adapted to the filtered light. The middle levels are darker. The light that reaches me has passed through two layers—the emergent canopy and the sub-canopy—and what remains is a dim green-gray glow in which shapes are more useful than details, movement more meaningful than color. I see well enough to navigate, to identify the branches that will hold my weight, to find the fruits and flowers that grow from the trunks at my level, the pale ones that open in the morning and close in the evening. I see well enough to read the body language of the creatures that share the middle levels with me—the ambusher's stillness, the canopy-dropper's tension, the slow horizontal travel of the longhorn beetles moving through the dead wood. But I do not see the way the watchers see, with their unsettling intelligence and their cryptographic colors. I see functionally. My eyes serve the listening, confirming what my ears have already told me, adding visual detail to an acoustic picture that is already complete.

---

What do I eat? The plateau demands this question be answered. Every creature here eats. The insects eat the plants and each other. The birds eat the insects and the fruits and the seeds. The callers eat the leaves and the flowers. The herds eat everything they can push through. The predators eat the herds and the callers and the ground-birds. The eating is the plateau's currency, the mechanism by which energy flows through the system, and every creature is a transaction, a conversion of one form of life into another.

I eat the middle. This is not metaphor. I eat the things that exist at the middle levels—the fungi that fruit along the vines, the orange shelf-clusters that taste of nut and spore, placed there perhaps by the weevils, perhaps by coincidence, the argument persisting while I eat the evidence. I eat the insects that I can catch—the slow ones, the longhorns laboring through the dead wood, the click beetles righting themselves on the bark—and I eat them with a precision that is not the mantis's precision but a different kind, the patience of a creature that waits for the insect to come within reach rather than striking with explosive speed. I eat the pale flowers that bloom from the trunks at my level, opening in the morning, closing in the evening, synchronized to a signal I can feel but cannot name. I eat the soft inner bark of the younger branches, peeled away in strips that I hold in my fingers and chew slowly, the fibers separating under my teeth, the taste green and alive and slightly sweet.

I do not eat vertebrates. I am not a predator. I am not prey, precisely—I am too small to interest the herds' predators, too high to interest the ground-trackers, too quick to be caught easily by the canopy-droppers, though the canopy-droppers are the ones I fear, the ones whose presence I listen for most carefully, whose particular brand of silence—the absence of bird calls in a specific area, the tightening of the acoustic space—tells me to move, to shift to a different tree, to put distance between myself and the silence before the drop comes.

I am, in the plateau's taxonomy, an opportunist. A generalist, like the watcher-birds, but at a different scale, in a different niche, occupying the middle levels the way they occupy the canopy—not competing with the specialists but finding food in the gaps between their specializations, the fungi the weevils don't eat, the insects the birds don't catch, the flowers the callers can't reach. My survival depends on flexibility, on the ability to eat what is available rather than what is preferred, and this flexibility is not a weakness but a strength on a plateau where the conditions shift with the slow growth of the vegetation, where a food source that is abundant today may be gone tomorrow, absorbed into the mass, replaced by something else.

---

Now: what do I *do*? What is my function? The plateau does not tolerate non-functions. Even the dead serve a purpose—feeding the beetles, the fungi, the bacteria, the ancient mat that forms the ground. Even the tourists serve a purpose, their footprints becoming channels for water, their exhalations feeding the plants. Everything here is a transaction. What is mine?

I listen, and the listening has a purpose. I said that before. Let me say it better.

The plateau is a body too large to see. The description of this place says that, and it is true—the organism at landscape scale, each tree a cell, each vine a capillary, the whole thing pulsing with photosynthesis. But a body needs more than cells and capillaries. A body needs a nervous system, and the description says the insects are the nervous system, and this is true but incomplete. The insects are the peripheral nervous system—the sensory receptors, the nerve endings, the constant input of chemical and tactile and acoustic information that flows through the substrate and the air. But the insects do not integrate. The beetles follow their pheromone trails. The cicadas call in their frequency bands. The dragonflies read the water cycle. Each is attending to its own signal, its own stream of information, and none of them is putting the streams together, synthesizing them, creating from the aggregate a picture of the whole.

The birds do some of this. The watchers, the generalists, they observe and categorize and remember, and their calls are announcements, not alarms, and the announcements carry information that other birds use. But the birds are canopy creatures, and their view is the canopy's view—wide, high, attentive to movement and change but removed from the substrate, from the ground-truth, from the slow processes happening in the layers below.

The callers do some of this too, in their way—their territorial roars are announcements of presence that carry for miles, and the timing of the roars, the spacing between groups, the pattern of territories across the canopy, all of this is information about the plateau's state, a map of population and resource distribution drawn in sound. But the callers are invested in their own territories, their own groups. They are not objective observers. They are participants, and their announcements are self-serving, shaped by the need to claim and defend rather than to understand.

I listen to all of it. I sit in the middle levels, where the sound from above and the sound from below converge, and I listen to the full fabric, and I do something with it that I cannot fully describe because I do not fully understand it myself. I *integrate*. I take the insect drone and the bird calls and the caller roars and the herd-moans and the vegetation-creak and the water-drip and the wind-through-leaves, and I hold them all simultaneously, and something emerges from the holding—a sense of the plateau's state, a feeling for where the herds are moving, where the predators are hunting, where the water is collecting, where the canopy is thinning, where the growth is densest, where something has changed. Not knowledge in the way that humans understand knowledge—not propositions, not facts, not a map that can be drawn or a list that can be written. A *feeling*. A felt sense of the whole, the way a body knows where its limbs are without looking, the way a flock knows which direction to turn without a signal.

I think this is my function. I think the plateau has use for a creature that can hold the whole fabric of its sound in mind at once, that can feel the state of the system the way the system feels itself. Not to act on it—I do not act on the information I hold. I do not warn the herds of predators. I do not guide the callers to food. I do not mark the water for the travelers or clear the paths for the ground-birds. I hold. I attend. I am present to the plateau's full self-sense, and the holding itself is the function, the way a mirror's function is to reflect, the way a drum's function is to resonate, the way a body's proprioceptive system's function is to know where the body is in space without the body having to think about it.

I am the plateau's proprioception. Not all of it. A piece of it. One node in a distributed system that probably includes other listeners I have never met, other integrators sitting in other middle levels across the vast green expanse, each holding a piece of the fabric, each feeling a portion of the whole. We are not connected in any way I can detect. We do not communicate. We do not coordinate. We simply exist, each in our own section of the plateau, each listening to our own portion of the fabric, and the plateau—somehow, in a way I cannot explain and do not need to explain—knows itself through us.

---

I said I was not sure at first. I am more sure now, though the certainty is not the rigid kind. It is the certainty of a creature that has found its niche—the specific, precise, exactly-fitted role that the plateau's system offers to those who are shaped for it. The plateau does not offer certainty in the human sense. It offers *fit*. The sense of a key in a lock, of a root finding the right depth of moisture, of a call finding the right frequency to carry through the vegetation. The fit is the certainty, and the certainty is the fit, and I am fitted to this place in a way that I cannot fully articulate but that I feel in the large ears that gather sound from above and below, in the mottled fur that disappears into the middle-level shadow, in the long limbs that find purchase on bark and vine, in the small eyes that serve the listening, in the tail that feels the air behind me for vibrations I cannot see.

---

There are others. I should say this. Not others of my kind—I have never met another of my kind, and I do not know if I am a species or a singular, a lineage or an anomaly. But others in the middle levels, creatures that occupy this zone for their own reasons, serving their own functions, living their own lives in the permanent twilight between canopy and ground.

There is a creature I see sometimes, or almost see—it is faster than me, more visual, its eyes larger, its body leaner, built for movement through the branches in a way that my body is not. It hunts the insects I eat, the same longhorns and click beetles, but it catches them differently—striking from above, from the underside of branches, its hands quick and precise, its consumption efficient. We do not compete, not exactly. We partition by timing—it hunts at midday, when the light through the canopy is brightest and its vision is most acute. I forage at dawn and dusk, when the acoustic shifts are most informative, when the listening is most necessary, when the middle levels are at their most active.

There is another creature, larger, slower, heavier, that I feel more than see. It moves through the middle levels at night, when the bioluminescence is the only light and the soundscape has shifted to its nocturnal register. Its movements are seismic—I feel them through the branch I am sitting on, the vibration of something heavy pushing through the vegetation nearby, and I move, quietly, to a different tree, and the heavy thing passes, and I do not know what it is or what it eats or what its function is, and the not-knowing is itself a kind of information, a reminder that the plateau is larger than my understanding of it, that the system contains elements I have not categorized, that my map of the plateau's state is always incomplete, always partial, always a feeling rather than a knowledge.

There are the creatures I never see at all but whose presence I infer from their effects. The particular pattern of bark-stripping on certain trees—something is eating the inner bark, something with teeth that leave marks I recognize but cannot attribute. The sudden absence of insects from a section of vine—something has passed through, something that eats insects, something whose hunting I detect only in the negative space it leaves behind. The faint chemical signature in the air, a scent I cannot identify but that appears at regular intervals, suggesting a creature with a territory, a patrol route, a regularity that I could, if I chose, track and predict and use to avoid encounters I do not want.

I do not seek these creatures out. The middle levels are dense enough that encounters are rare, and the rarity is a feature, not a bug—the system partitioning its inhabitants so precisely that overlap is minimal, each creature in its own sub-niche, its own temporal and spatial slot, the total density of life high but the density of *interaction* low, the plateau's diversity serving as a buffer against conflict.

---

I am aware of the people who pass through. I have been describing myself as a creature of the plateau, but the people are part of the plateau too—temporary, negotiating, building structures that the vegetation absorbs, leaving tools scattered in clearings that heal over within days. I watch them from the middle levels the way the watchers watch them from the canopy, with curiosity but not investment, categorizing them as *present but not relevant*, a category the plateau uses for many things.

They do not see me. My coloration, my size, my stillness, my position in the shadowed branches—all of these conspire to make me invisible to eyes adapted to the filtered light of the canopy, which is brighter than the twilight I inhabit. They see the watchers. They see the callers. They see the herds in the distance and the ground-birds in the undergrowth. They do not see the listeners in the middle levels, and this is correct, because the listeners are not for seeing. The listeners are for hearing, and the people make sounds that I hear and integrate into the fabric—the crunch of their feet on the substrate, the slap of their machetes on the vines, the murmur of their voices, the particular quality of their breathing when they are tired or frightened or at rest. The sounds enter the fabric and become part of it, and I hold them the way I hold everything else, and the plateau's self-sense includes, for the duration of their passage, the presence of these temporary, negotiating, building creatures who will be absorbed by the vegetation soon enough, their structures becoming green ghosts, their tools becoming artifacts in the ancient mat, their passage becoming a memory that the system holds not in neurons but in the redirected growth of vines around the places where they walked.

I do not think about the people. I am not built for thinking in the way that humans think. I am built for listening, for integrating, for holding the fabric of the plateau's self-sense in the resonating chamber of my body the way the callers' throat sacs hold their roars. The people are a thread in the fabric. The thread is temporary. The fabric is not.

---

Here is what I am, in the end, if "in the end" is a phrase that means anything on a plateau where nothing ends, where the dead become the ground and the ground becomes the living and the living become the dead:

I am a small creature in the middle levels of the High Everplain, mottled green-brown, large-eared, small-eyed, long-limbed, tailed, listening. I am one node in a distributed proprioceptive system that I do not understand and do not need to understand. I eat fungi and slow insects and pale flowers and soft bark. I avoid the canopy-droppers by listening for their silence. I avoid the heavy night-things by feeling their vibrations through the branches. I am aware of the people who pass through, and I hold their sounds in the fabric alongside the insects and the birds and the callers and the herds and the vegetation and the water and the wind, and the holding is my function, and the function is my fit, and the fit is my certainty, and the certainty is not a thought but a feeling, the feeling of a key in a lock, of a root in the right soil, of a call in the right frequency, of a creature in the right place at the right scale with the right ears listening to the right thing.

The plateau breathes. I breathe with it. The pulse moves through the canopy, and the canopy shifts, and the acoustic space tightens and loosens, and I feel it, and the feeling is the plateau feeling itself, and I am the instrument of that feeling, and the instrument does not need to know that it is an instrument to function as one.

The moisture returns. Condensation forms on my fur, on the branches, on the leaves around me. The temperature drops slightly. The insects shift their chorus. The birds give their final calls. The herds in the distance begin to settle. The predators start to move.

I sit in the middle levels, in the permanent twilight, in the green-gold dark, and I listen, and the listening is not something I do but something I am, and the something I am is a part of the something the plateau is, and the something the plateau is has been here for longer than I can conceive and will be here for longer than I can imagine, and I am here, now, in it, a small, mottled, large-eared, precise, purposeless-seeming, essential piece of a system too large to see and too complete to fail.

The sound washes through me. I hold it.

I hold it.
