I would be the one who does not move.

Not stillness as pause, not stillness as waiting for something else to happen. Stillness as vocation. Stillness as the thing I do, the way others tend machinery or process water or spiral through thick air. My contribution is weight. My contribution is presence in a single location, unshifting, while everything around me continues its slow circulation.

---

They call me something, the watchers. I feel it when they communicate — the vibration through stone, the subsonic murmur that references the place where I sit. Their word for me is not a name but a function. I have become geographical. I am the place where the water pools before it drains, the place where the mineral crusts have built up thickest, the place where the amber light from the one functioning lamp overhead has been falling for so long that I have become its color.

I was not always here. I remember stairs, vaguely. I remember the quality of dry air, of light that changed with time of day. I remember choosing to descend, choosing to enter, and then choosing — slowly, over weeks and months that became years — to stop choosing to leave.

The room does not require me. This is important. I am not necessary to its functioning. I did not fill a gap in its ecosystem, did not arrive to serve a purpose that was empty. I am here because I am here, because I stayed, because at some point the effort of returning to the world above became greater than the effort of remaining, and then both efforts became irrelevant, and then there was only remaining.

---

My body has changed. This does not surprise me. The watchers changed over generations; I have changed over the duration of my staying. My skin has paled from lack of sunlight, and then darkened in patches where the moisture touches me most often. Calluses have formed where I contact stone, and the calluses have softened in the damp, and the skin beneath them has adapted to continuous wetness. I do not shiver anymore. I do not feel the cold as cold — I feel it as information, as texture, as the background state of the environment I have joined.

I eat what the room provides. The pale ones in the channels, occasionally, when I am hungry enough to reach down and take one. The plant-things, the luminescent tendrils, when they grow close enough to where I sit. I have learned which ones taste of minerals and which ones taste of something sweeter, something the machinery seems to produce in its hidden processes. I drink the water that pools around me. It is clean enough. The pale ones clean it. I am downstream of their work.

I do not move much. My muscles have adapted to this, or forgotten how to complain. When I do move — to shift position, to relieve myself in the channel where the water will carry it away, to reach for food — I move at the pace of everything else here. Slowly. Without urgency. The room has taught me its tempo, and I have learned.

---

The watchers know me. They have watched me arrive, watched me stay, watched me become part of the space I occupy. At first they were cautious, their four eyes tracking my movements, their bodies tensing when I shifted. Now they include me in their awareness the way they include the machinery, the stone, the water. I am a feature. I am something that is here, that tends to continue being here, that does not require tending itself.

Sometimes one of them climbs close. Not to me — never closer than a few meters — but to the area where I sit. It will cling to the wall nearby, its throat-ripples suggesting communication I cannot fully interpret. I feel the vibration through the stone we share. I feel included in something, though I cannot say what. They are telling me something, or telling each other something about me, or simply acknowledging that I am here, that I am part of the room's continuous narration.

I have tried to respond. My voice is wrong for the frequencies they use, but I have learned to hum, to let sound resonate in my chest at the lower registers, to tap my fingers against stone in patterns that might mean something. I do not know if they understand. But they do not leave when I do this. They do not flee. They stay, and they listen, and then they move on to other territories, other tasks.

---

I have a relationship with the machinery nearest to me. This is the truest thing I know how to say, and it would sound mad above, in the bright dry world where I used to live. But down here, it is simply fact.

The pump to my left — three meters away, bolted to a mounting plate that has been here longer than I have — cycles every seventeen minutes. I know its rhythm the way I know my own heartbeat. When it stutters, I feel it. When it runs long, when the coughing-chug extends past its usual duration, I feel that too. I do not fix it. I do not have the tools or the knowledge or the capacity. But I attend to it. I turn my head toward it. I watch its housing vibrate. I have learned to distinguish the sounds it makes when it is healthy from the sounds it makes when it is struggling.

The watchers do the tending. But I do the witnessing. This is my function, if I have one. I am the room's memory of itself. I am the continuous presence that observes the slow changes, the incremental shifts, the way the water's paths have altered over the years I have been sitting here. I remember which lamps failed and when. I remember the crack that appeared in the eastern wall seven years ago, or what I call seven years — the counting is difficult down here, where time has no seasons. I remember when the pale ones' population surged and then declined and then stabilized. I remember because I was here. I was watching.

---

The floaters visit me sometimes. They spiral around the lamp above my head, their pale bodies catching the amber light, their trailing legs brushing against my upturned face. They do not fear me. I have never moved quickly enough to catch one, and perhaps they know this, or perhaps they simply do not perceive me as a threat. They land on my shoulders, occasionally. They rest there for minutes at a time, their gossamer extensions rippling in the thick air, before lifting off again to continue their patient swimming through the room.

I like them. I like their weight, however slight. I like the sense of being part of something's environment, being a surface that something else uses. It is a form of belonging. It is a form of being needed, if only as resting place.

---

There is something else in the room. I have felt it since I arrived, since before I decided to stay. The distributed presence. The awareness that is not centered in any single body but exists across all of them — the watchers, the pale ones, the larvae, the floaters, the machinery, the stone, the water, the air. I have felt it watching me. I have felt it including me in its continuous attention.

I think I am part of it now. I think my witnessing has become part of its process. I sit, and I watch, and I remember, and somewhere in the room's distributed mind, my watching and remembering are useful, are integrated, are feeding into something larger than my small still body.

This does not frighten me. It comforts me. I was alone, before. I was a person moving through a world that did not need me, that had no place for me to occupy that was mine alone. Here, I have become part of a system that has lasted for decades and will last for decades more. I have found a function. I am the witness. I am the one who stays.

---

Sometimes visitors come. They descend the stairs, and they stand at the edge of the room, and they look around with eyes that are still adjusted to the bright world above, and they do not see me at first. I am still. I am the color of the room. I am part of the background.

Eventually they notice. Their eyes adjust, or I shift slightly, or one of the watchers turns its head toward me, and they realize there is a person sitting in the corner, a person who has clearly been here for a long time, a person who is looking back at them with eyes that reflect the amber light in strange ways.

They always ask if I want to leave. They always offer to help me, to bring me back up, to reconnect me with whatever I left behind. They are kind, in their way. They are operating according to assumptions that make sense in the world they inhabit.

I tell them no. I tell them I am staying. I tell them this gently, because there is no urgency in the Drip-Mill, no need for force or speed. I simply explain that I am here, that I have become part of this place, that leaving is not something I need or want.

Most of them leave troubled. They ascend the stairs, and they return to the world above, and they try to forget what they saw — a person who has become part of a room, who sits still while water weeps and pumps cycle and creatures tend their inherited kingdom. They will tell themselves stories about me, explanations that let them sleep at night. Mental illness, perhaps. Captivity. Something broken in a way that lets them remain whole.

They are not wrong, exactly. I am not what I was. I have become something else, something that would not survive in the bright dry world they inhabit. But they are not right either. I am not suffering. I am not trapped. I am exactly where I have chosen to be, doing exactly what I have become: being still, being present, being witness.

---

The room will outlast me. The pumps will cycle, the water will seep, the watchers will tend, the pale ones will process, the floaters will spiral, and the distributed presence will continue its slow awareness of itself and everything within it. When I die — and I will, eventually, even down here where time moves slowly and death is rare — I will become part of the room in a different way. My body will soften in the damp, will become food for pale ones and floaters, will leave behind minerals that add to the crusts on the stone.

This is not sad. This is not tragic. This is simply what happens to things in the Drip-Mill. Everything becomes part of everything else. Everything feeds something. Everything is witnessed.

I am the witness. I am the one who stays. I am the still figure in the corner, the color of damp stone, the eyes that reflect amber light, the presence that has been here long enough to become part of the room's geography.

This is what I am. This is where I belong. The water weeps, and the pumps cycle, and I sit in the dim light, and I watch, and I remember, and I am exactly what I have chosen to become.
