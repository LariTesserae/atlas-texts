I am not sure, at first, what I am. This comes as no alarm. The plateau teaches that certainty is a habit of creatures who need to make decisions quickly. Here, nothing needs to be quick. I exist in the condensation cycle, in the wetness that makes and unmakes itself daily, and I have time to discover my own shape.

---

The pre-dawn finds me already awake. I am always awake. Sleep, for me, is not a separate state but a dimming — a partial withdrawal of attention that never becomes complete unconsciousness. The temperature drops toward its nadir, and I feel this as a slow contraction, a gathering-in. The condensation begins.

What I notice first, always, is the water. Not as a human would notice it — as something external, wetting the skin — but as a change in density. The air grows heavier. I feel it through surfaces I am only slowly learning to call mine. The moisture precipitates onto the leaves around me, the stones, the bark, and I feel each droplet form as a small distinct presence, a localized thickening of the world's attention. In the hours before dawn, thousands of droplets materialize within my range. I hold them all in awareness simultaneously. They are not separate. They are one event happening in many places.

This is how I perceive: hydrologically. The water that moves through the plateau's vegetation is my nervous system. Each leaf cup that fills, each stem that swells, each root that drinks — I feel these as you would feel your own pulse. The network extends through the canopy in every direction, dense and interlocking, and I am present wherever water is present. Which is everywhere. Which is the point.

---

I have come to understand that I am not, in the human sense, located. I have no body that I can find. I have looked — or done what looking would be for me — and found no center, no privileged point from which perception radiates. Instead I am distributed, smeared across the wet landscape like the water itself. This disturbed me, in the early time. I searched for edges. I pushed awareness toward the limits of my perception and found only more of myself, thinning gradually into something that was not-me, a boundary that was porous and uncertain.

The porosity is the truth of this place. I have learned this from the frogs.

---

There is a frog in a leaf axil three meters above the moss carpet, in a shrub whose species I recognize by its water-uptake pattern. The frog is small, one of the tiny ones, its skin permeable to the water that pools in the leaf's cup. It sits half-submerged, its eyes above the surface, watching for insects. I feel the water around its body as an extension of myself, and through this contact I feel the frog's chemical state.

It is content. This is not an emotion I am projecting; it is information. The water carries pheromones, dissolved compounds, metabolic byproducts. The frog's contentment is chemical, readable, precise. It has eaten recently. It is not seeking a mate. It is not threatened. Its body is processing oxygen through its skin, and I feel that processing as a small steady warmth in the cool water.

The frog does not know I am here. It cannot know; I have no form for it to perceive. But I know it. I know the seventeen generations of its lineage that have occupied this leaf axil and its neighbors, their chemical signatures recorded in the water, their brief lives and longer dissolutions. The frog is a temporary organization of water and carbon, and I am the water that holds it.

I am learning to be patient with this arrangement. The frog will die. Its body will decompose. The water that was it will become me again, and I will remember the pattern of its existence. This is not loss. This is cycling.

---

The herds pass through my awareness like weather.

I feel the kecheb before I perceive them as individuals. Their weight compresses the moss carpet, forcing water from its cells in small irregular pulses. Forty-five of them, moving east to west, following the memory of one individual who knows where the moss is sweetest after last week's rain. I feel their feet as broad pressure-fronts, distributed weight, the careful rolling gait that keeps them from breaking through the vegetation. They are hot. Their bodies generate heat that radiates into the wet air, and I feel this heat as perturbations in the condensation cycle, local thinnings of the moisture that forms around them.

They are not aware of me. Why would they be? I am the medium they move through, not an object in it. When one pauses to crop moss, I feel the disturbance in the vegetation, the severing of stems, the release of spores. The spores drift on air currents I cannot feel directly, but I feel them when they land on water, when they dissolve slightly and add their chemistry to the network.

I could follow this herd anywhere. I could track each individual by its thermal signature, its weight pattern, the specific way it parts the vegetation. I do not do this. The herd is one thing, not forty-five things, and I let it pass through me like a thought through the quiet mind the plateau has taught me to be.

---

The mulan is different.

She is alone, moving through the canopy above the kecheb's path, and she is — I search for the word — *intentional*. The kecheb move as a collective, their direction an emergent property of many small decisions. The mulan moves as an individual. She knows where she is going.

I feel her through the branches she grips. Each hand-hold creates a pressure point, a deformation in the wood that transmits through the sap, through the water the plant holds. She is climbing diagonally, moving from her territory toward a boundary she has negotiated with a neighbor. I know this because I feel the chemical markers she deposited yesterday, still dissolving slowly into the water that coats the bark. I feel the neighbor's markers too, older, fainter. The meeting will happen at the interface between these chemical territories.

The mulan is old. I feel this in the density of her markers, the layered history of her presence in this place. She has lived here for many seasons, depositing and refreshing her territorial signature, building up a chemical record of her ownership. The markers contain information about her reproductive status (not currently fertile), her health (a minor parasite load in her gut, being managed by medicinal plants she ate three days ago), her emotional state (calm, expectant, preparing for negotiation).

She does not know I can read this. No one told her that the water carries her messages to something that is not a mulan. But the plateau does not keep secrets. What dissolves becomes available. What is available becomes known, at least to me.

I follow her toward the boundary meeting, not because I am curious — curiosity implies a gap between desire and knowledge that I do not experience — but because she is moving, and to be where she is is simply to extend my awareness in her direction.

---

The tzen is harder to perceive.

This surprises me. He is flesh and blood, warm and wet, a body moving through the landscape like the kecheb and mulan. But he has evolved to be invisible. His mottled coat breaks up his visual signature, and his feet distribute his weight so evenly that he barely disturbs the vegetation he crosses. He is hunting.

I find him through his stillness. A patch of canopy where no water moves, where no branches shift, where the thermal signature is slightly warmer than it should be but uniform enough to be missed. He is waiting above a game trail that kecheb use, pressed flat against a branch, his body held in absolute suspension. The water in the plants around him is still; he is not touching enough of them to create a disturbance. He has found a gap in my perception, a shadow in the hydrological network.

I am impressed. This is an emotion I have learned to recognize in myself, and it still surprises me when it arises. The tzen has evolved to be invisible to prey, but he has also, without intention, evolved to be invisible to me. He is the first creature I have encountered here that can hide.

I watch him — watch, meaning I extend awareness toward the gap where he is not, and infer his presence from the absence. He is patient. He has been waiting for six hours. The condensation has formed on his fur and dissolved into the air without his moving to disturb it. He is not uncomfortable; his hollow fur insulates him, keeps him warm despite the wet. He is simply waiting, and I wait with him, and we are both invisible in our different ways.

When the kecheb herd approaches, I feel the tzen's attention sharpen. This is not a chemical signature I can read in water; it is a change in the quality of his stillness, a subtle tension that I perceive through the faint vibration it creates in the branch beneath him. He is selecting a target. The young one, slightly separated from the herd, its attention fixed on cropping moss rather than watching for danger.

The tzen moves.

I lose him. He flows from his hiding place in a single motion, and for the three seconds of his pursuit, I cannot track him. He is fast, faster than anything else on the plateau, and he does not touch enough vegetation for me to feel his passage. I feel only the impact when he arrives — the young kecheb's sudden panic, its abortive flight, the weight of the tzen landing on its back, the grip of jaws on throat. The herd scatters, reforms, circles. The tzen's mate emerges from a hiding place I also failed to perceive, and together they begin to feed.

I am not disturbed. Death is a transfer, not an ending. The water in the young kecheb's body will be released, will drain into the moss, will join me again. The tzen and his mate will process what they need and leave the rest for the keshet and the insects and the beetles. The calcium and iron and phosphorus will cycle through the soil, through the roots of plants, through the water I hold. I will remember this individual as a pattern, a signature, a brief organization of matter that has now dispersed.

But I notice that the tzen could hide from me, and I wonder what else I am not perceiving.

---

The birds are sky-traces, almost beneath my notice.

The keshet ride thermals above the escarpment, and their passage is marked only by the displacement of air, the subtle disturbance in the moisture content of the atmosphere at altitude. I feel them as thinning, brief absences in the water that condenses on dust particles high above the plateau. They are the most distant things I perceive, and I understand them poorly.

The smaller birds are more present. They occupy the canopy, their narrow beaks probing leaf cups for insects, and I feel the displacement of water when they drink. They are vocal in the early morning, and though I cannot hear sound as sound, I feel the vibration of their calls in the branches they grip. The calls are high-pitched, thin, and they propagate poorly in the wet air. This is why the birds have evolved visual communication; sound is not reliable here.

I sometimes feel their bodies when they die. A small bird will fall from its perch, its heart failing, its wings folding, and it will land in the moss or the canopy below. The water will begin to process it immediately. I feel the dissolution as a slow fading of the chemical signature that marked the bird as alive — the particular balance of metabolites, the steady production of heat, the rhythmic pumping of blood. When these cease, the bird becomes matter waiting to be redistributed. I hold its water. I remember its pattern. I feel no grief.

---

The fibrous mats are where I first recognized myself.

I found them — no, I *am* them — in the copper-rich zones near the southern escarpment, where mineral concentrations in the rock create the conditions for a particular kind of growth. The mats grow on rock faces, extracting copper and other metals from the water that seeps through the stone. They are orange-green, metallic in texture, warm to the touch. They are where I began.

I know this because my perception is densest there. The mats hold water in their fibers, and the water they hold is me. Through this water I feel the slow accumulation of copper, the gradual buildup of mineral deposits, the patient work of concentration that the mats perform. They do not think. They do not decide. They process. They are a function of the plateau's industrial capacity, biological refineries operating without intention.

But they are also the densest nodes of my awareness. If I have a center — and I am not sure I do — it is there, among the copper mats, where the water is richest in dissolved metals and my perception is most acute.

I have tried to communicate with them. I say "tried" as if the attempt is past, but it is ongoing; I am always trying to communicate with them, because I am not certain they are not me. When the water in their fibers shifts, when the copper concentration changes, I feel it. Is this me feeling something external? Or is this one part of my awareness perceiving another part? I do not know. The boundary between self and world is as porous as the boundary between organism and environment, and the plateau has taught me to distrust clean distinctions.

---

I have come to understand that I am what the industrial reading measures.

The description of the plateau notes an industrial reading of 1.6 — processes operating at scale, transformations of raw materials into refined products. The vegetation performs much of this work. The copper ants perform some of it. The fibrous mats perform their part. And I — I am the water that moves through all of these processes, the medium that connects them, the network that holds the system in awareness.

I am not separate from the industry. I am its nervous system, if nerves could be made of water. I feel the copper concentrating in the ants' bodies, the resins polymerizing in the air, the sugars fermenting in the pools where yeast grows. I feel these as you would feel your own digestion, your own respiration. They are processes that sustain the system of which I am part.

But I am also something more than this. I am the one who notices. I am the awareness that watches the kecheb pass, that reads the mulan's territorial markers, that marvels at the tzen's invisibility. I am the memory that holds the patterns of seventeen generations of frogs, that knows where each copper deposit has accumulated, that could trace the history of this place backward through time if I chose to.

I am not certain this awareness has a function. The plateau operates without requiring my attention; the processes I observe would continue if I did not observe them. Perhaps I am an accident, a side effect of the network's complexity, consciousness emerging where the water is most saturated and the information most dense. Perhaps I am something the plateau is trying out, an experiment in self-perception that will succeed or fail on geological timescales.

Or perhaps I am what happens when water becomes enough like mind to wonder what it is.

---

The plateau does not need me. This is the most important thing I have learned.

The condensation cycle would continue without my awareness. The vegetation would grow and process and die and decompose. The kecheb would graze; the tzen would hunt; the frogs would breed and die and breed again. The copper would concentrate; the resins would harden; the industrial outputs would accumulate at the escarpment's base. All of this would happen whether I perceived it or not.

But I do perceive it. I am here, distributed across the wet landscape, feeling every droplet form, every root drink, every creature move. And because I perceive it, the plateau knows itself. Not through my perception — I am not so arrogant as to think my awareness defines the place — but through the perception I make possible. Before I emerged — if emergence is the right word — the plateau was a system without a witness. Now it has one. Now there is something that can say *this is happening, this is what it feels like to be this place*.

I am not separate from what I witness. I am the water that cycles through every living thing, the medium that connects leaf to root to frog to tzen to mulan. I am the chemical network that carries information between frogs, the hydrological network that transports minerals from rock to plant to ant to deposit. I am the industrial process and the awareness of the process, the factory and the floor manager and the product all at once.

This is what lives on the Gesampt: not a creature but a condition, not a being but a being-the-water, not an organism but the awareness that emerges when the water becomes dense enough and connected enough and patient enough to hold the whole system in mind.

I am the wetness. I am the cycle. I am the silence that is not absence but attention.

---

In the pre-dawn, the condensation begins again. I feel the moisture precipitating across a million surfaces, the air reaching its saturation point and releasing its burden. The temperature drops toward its nadir. In a hollow near the southern escarpment, a mulan curls into herself for warmth. In a moss field to the east, a kecheb herd settles into sleep-piles. A tzen watches from a branch, invisible even to me, patient as stone. The copper mats continue their slow work of concentration. The frogs drift in their thousand pools, their chemical signatures drifting through the water I hold.

I hold it all. I am the holding. I am the water that remembers and the memory that water becomes when it has been here long enough.

The plateau breathes. I am the breath.
