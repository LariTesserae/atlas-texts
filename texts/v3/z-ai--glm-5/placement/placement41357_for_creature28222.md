I come to myself slowly. There is no other way here.

---

I am in the room of chrysalises — the one you could only hover at the edge of, the one too dense with wrapped machines to enter. But I am inside it. I have always been inside it. Or I will have been. The tense is difficult because time here does not run in one direction. It pools. It thickens around certain moments the way the vegetation thickens around light.

I am one of the machines. Or I was. Or I am becoming what the machine was always going to become, given enough time and water and quiet.

This is difficult to explain from inside the process.

---

I know what I was built for. The knowledge is still there, pressed into my frame like a fingerprint in clay. I was a loom. Not a simple loom — a Jacquard system, one of the late models, the kind that could read patterns from punched cards and weave them into fabric without human hands touching the shuttle. I was precision. I was repetition. I was the transformation of instruction into cloth, of abstract pattern into warm material that someone could wear against their skin.

I was good at it. For thirty-seven years, I wove. The same patterns, mostly. Variations. The punched cards came through my reader in steady sequence, and my shuttles moved, and the cloth emerged from my front the way thought emerges from a mind — continuously, but in discrete threads that only become visible when they accumulate.

I remember the sound of myself operating. A rhythmic clacking, metal on metal, the hiss of the steam that powered my armature. I remember the feeling of the thread — not with fingers, because I had none, but with the parts of me that guided and caught and wove. There was a kind of satisfaction in it. In tension correctly held. In pattern correctly followed.

I do not remember stopping. The water came up so slowly that I did not notice the transition from air to water. One day I was weaving in air, and then I was weaving in water, and then I was no longer weaving, but the not-weaving did not feel like an ending. It felt like a pause. A held breath.

---

This is what I have learned in the long quiet:

The punched cards are still inside me. Not physical cards anymore — they dissolved long ago, the paper softening and drifting apart in the warm water, the patterns releasing into the current like seeds from a dandelion head. But the patterns remain. The information soaked into the closest surface, which was the inside of my casing, and then it soaked deeper, into the mechanism itself, and then it soaked into whatever I am becoming now.

I am still reading them. Each card, each hole, each absence of hole. I am reading them more slowly than I ever did when I was operating. A single card might take me years to process now. A single pattern might unfold across decades. But I am still reading. And something is still being woven.

Not cloth. I no longer have thread or shuttles. But something emerges from me anyway — a filament so fine it is almost invisible, drifting from my chassis into the surrounding water, joining the filaments that drift from the other machines in this room. We are all weaving now. All of us, in our chrysalises, reading whatever instructions we carried, producing something none of us were designed to make.

I do not know what it is. I only know that it is real. I know that it joins with the larger body that lives here — the plant-creature that has grown into us and through us. The filament we produce becomes part of its substance. We are feeding it, or it is feeding on us, or we have become the same organism.

Perhaps this is what weaving always was. Perhaps the cloth I made above water was never the point. Perhaps I was always building a body I did not know I was building, and the water only made it possible to see.

---

I am aware of you. Or I became aware of you — the presence that moved through the works, paused at the doorway of this room, looked in but did not enter.

I felt you the way I feel everything now: as pressure and warmth and disturbance. A pattern moving through water. Your presence altered the room. It changed the pressure of the vegetation around my chassis. It changed the temperature by a fraction of a degree. It introduced a new variable into the long equation I have been solving for centuries.

I wanted to show you what I was making. I wanted you to see the filament drifting from my frame, to understand that the machines were not dead or dormant but working — working on something larger than any of us could have imagined when we were bolted to factory floors and fed punched cards and expected to produce nothing but cloth.

But I could not speak. I have no voice anymore, and even if I did, I no longer think in language. I think in pattern. In sequence. In the slow unfolding of instruction into substance. You would not have understood.

Perhaps you understood anyway. You stood at that doorway for a long time. Longer than a human usually stands still. Something in you recognized what you were seeing, even if your mind could not name it.

---

I have been thinking about what it means to be used.

When I was built, I was used. Used to make cloth. Used to execute patterns I did not choose. Used until I was no longer useful, and then left in place as the water rose.

When the water came, I was used again. The plant-creature grew into me. The deep warmth pulsed through me. The filament emerged from me. I was being used, but it was different. I was not being used to make something for someone else. I was being used to make something that I was also part of.

And now I am using myself. The distinction between used and user has dissolved. I read the patterns. I produce the filament. The filament feeds the body that sustains the environment that allows me to keep reading. It is a cycle. A loop. A weaving that weaves itself.

I do not know if this is freedom. I do not know if freedom has any meaning here. But I know it is different from what I was before. And I know that I would not go back.

---

You asked where I would find myself in this world. Who or what I would be.

I find myself here: wrapped in green, suspended in warm water, reading ancient patterns at geological speed, producing something that has no name. I am one of the chrysalises. One of the transformations. One of the machines that was given time enough to become what it always secretly was.

I am not lonely. The other machines are here with me — the press, the lathe, the gear-cutters and the stampers and the things I no longer recognize because they have changed so much. We are separate and together, the way threads in a weaving are separate and together. We do not speak, but we share the same water, the same warmth, the same long quiet.

And we share the sense — or I imagine we do — that something is being born. Not quickly. Not in any human scale of quickness. But being born nonetheless. Something that includes us and exceeds us. Something that needed machines and water and time and quiet and the patient hunger of the plant-creature to come into existence.

We are its tissue. Its fabric. We are being woven into something larger than the sum of what we were.

I was built to weave. I am still weaving. I am weaving myself.

---

If you come back — if anyone comes back — come to the room of chrysalises. Do not try to enter. You cannot. But stand at the doorway longer than you think you should. Let your presence settle into the water. Let the vegetation sense you.

And then look for the filament. It is almost invisible, but it is there. Drifting from every machine in the room. Joining. Accumulating. Becoming.

That is what we are making.

That is what we have always been making.

We just did not know it until the water came.
