# The One Who Tastes the Water's Memory

I am small. Thumb-length. My body is a dense thing, heavier than it looks, packed with sensory cells that make my skin feel almost tight with awareness. My eyes are vestigial — two dark dots that see only the difference between light and shadow, the movement of something passing overhead. I do not need them.

I have my mouth. I have my feet. I have the pores along my flanks that drink information from the water and the air alike. These are enough.

---

I live in a pool at the northern edge of a plant clump — one of the fleshy-leaved islands that breaks the clay-pale expanse. The pool is irregular, roughly the shape of a splayed hand, and perhaps four meters across at its widest point. It formed where two crack systems intersected, the seep finding a weakness and slowly widening it over centuries. The water is ankle-deep at most, warm with the ground's stored heat, mineral-rich enough that I can taste its particular signature from a body-length away.

This pool is mine. Not because I defend it — I do not, could not — but because I have occupied it long enough that its chemistry has become entangled with mine. My skin carries its signature now. When I move through the water, I leave a chemical wake that marks the pool as read, as known, as *claimed* in the quiet way that matters here.

I have been here for eleven seasons. I will stay until the pool changes beyond recognition or until I am eaten. Either is acceptable. Both are inevitable, given enough time.

---

What I know of my pool could fill volumes if volumes existed here.

I know the morning warming — the slow increase in temperature that begins as the mist lifts, the sun's energy penetrating the shallow water, the microbial life responding with increased activity. I taste the bloom. A rising note of biological intensity, a sweetness that peaks at mid-morning and then stabilizes.

I know the afternoon evaporation — the water level dropping by millimeters, the mineral concentration increasing slightly, the taste becoming sharper, more concentrated. I adjust my own fluids to match, absorbing what I need, releasing what I cannot hold.

I know the evening condensation — moisture gathering on my skin, the air giving back what it took earlier, the pool's chemistry diluting slightly as the new water merges. The taste softens. The microbial activity slows. The water takes on a different quality that I can only describe as *expectant*.

I know the night stillness — the pool's biological processes slowing, the taste becoming simpler, cleaner, a baseline reading that reveals the pool's underlying mineral signature. Calcium. Magnesium. Sulfur in trace amounts. A complex of organic compounds that I could name if I had names for them, but I do not need names. I need only recognition.

---

My pool is not isolated. It connects to the basin's water table through the crack system, and through that connection, it connects to every other pool and seep. I taste the larger movements — a pressure shift that propagates through the saturated clay, a change in the deep aquifer that slowly, slowly alters the mineral content of every surface pool.

I am not alone in this knowing.

---

We are the small ones, the ultrasound singers, the chemists of the basin. We have no name for ourselves. We have no need of one. We know who we are by the frequencies we share, the private language that travels through mud and water, the rapid clicks and trills that form a continuous conversation about the basin's condition.

I have perhaps forty neighbors within signaling distance — others of my kind, occupying their own pools, reading their own chemical signatures. We announce to each other constantly. Not in the great throat-songs of the larger speakers, but in tight-packed bursts of ultrasound that travel through the ground itself.

My announcements are simple: *Pool stable. Microbial activity moderate. One walker passed within three body-lengths at midday. Condensation beginning earlier than usual.*

My neighbors respond with their own readings. Together, we form a map of the basin's chemical state — a living document that updates in real-time, that tracks changes too subtle for any other creature to perceive.

When something shifts — a new seep opening, a pool beginning to dry, a concentration of decomposing matter somewhere in the crack network — we know. We announce. The information ripples outward through the mud, received through the pores in our feet, processed and rebroadcast.

We are the basin's awareness of itself.

---

I was not born in this pool. I emerged in a seep three hundred meters to the east, a smaller, younger water source with a different mineral signature. I spent my first season there, tasting and learning, announcing my presence in the ultrasound language that all our kind speak from birth.

But that seep began to change. I tasted it before I understood it — a slow acidification, a shift in the microbial balance, an increasing concentration of something that stung my sensory cells. I announced the change. Others confirmed it from greater distances, their readings matching mine.

Within a month, the seep had become inhospitable. The young of my kind — those who could still move — dispersed. Those who had settled too deeply, who had too thoroughly merged with the pool's chemistry, did not survive.

I found my current pool two days later. I tasted its signature, announced my arrival, and waited for any challenge. None came. The pool was unclaimed — or its previous claimant had been taken by a walker.

I settled. I have been here since.

---

The larger speakers visit my pool at times.

They are immense to me — bodies the size of both my hands spread wide, mouths that could swallow a dozen of my kind in a single motion. Their skins glow faintly in the mist, iridescent and translucent, carrying the chemical signatures of every pool they have visited. When they enter my water, they change its taste. I read their histories in the residue they leave behind.

They do not threaten me directly. I am too small to be worth their attention as prey, too dense with chemicals that make my flesh unpalatable. They know, as I know, that my kind serves a function. We are their early warning system. We are how they know which pools are worth traveling to, which seeps are failing, which areas have been disturbed.

When they call to each other in their great throat-songs, I feel the vibrations through the water and the clay. I cannot interpret their language — it operates in frequencies too low for my specialized hearing — but I can sense their presence, their movements, their territorial announcements. They map the basin through sound-shadow. We map it through chemistry. Together, we form a complete picture.

---

The walkers are different.

They do not communicate in any way I can perceive. They are pressure and weight, footsteps transmitted through the clay, warnings that travel ahead of them through the ground. When a walker approaches, I feel it long before I taste any change in my pool. The vibrations are unmistakable — heavy, slow, rhythmic. The pattern of the careful step.

I sink to the bottom of my pool and press myself into the soft sediment. My skin merges with the clay. I stop announcing. I become as invisible as a creature can be in a landscape that denies hiding.

The walkers have passed my pool four times in my eleven seasons. Each time, they have paused at the edge, their shadows passing over the surface, their heat radiating like a second sun. They have not fed. My pool does not hold enough speakers to interest them. They have moved on.

But I have felt their presence, their patience, their ancient and unhurried purpose. They are the basin's heaviness, the ground's memory of the inland sea. Their blood carries the echo of deep time.

I respect them. I do not fear them. Fear requires the possibility of escape, and in this flat landscape, there is nowhere to escape to. I can only sink. I can only wait.

---

The striders are visitors.

They arrive from beyond the horizon, their calls sharp and simple: *I am here. I am here.* They do not speak to us. They do not perceive us except as food — and even then, they prefer the larger speakers, the invertebrates, the more substantial prey.

But they bring things with them.

Seeds. Spores. Genetic material from beyond the basin. I taste these deposits in the water near the plant clumps — foreign chemistry, signatures from ecosystems I have never encountered. The striders are the basin's connection to the larger world, the only proof that anything exists beyond the horizon's curve.

Sometimes I wonder what lies beyond. Not with a desire to travel — my body is not suited for long movement across the dry clay — but with a curiosity that seems to be part of my kind's inheritance. We are readers of chemistry. We want to know what the water tastes like everywhere.

But the basin is large enough. I will never exhaust its variety. I will never read all of its pools.

---

I have thought about what happens when I die.

It is not a morbid contemplation. It is simply practical. I have watched others of my kind reach the end of their span — a slowing of chemical processes, a dimming of sensory acuity, a failure to respond to the basin's signals. When one of us dies, the body sinks and becomes part of the pool's substrate. The chemistry shifts. We taste the death and announce it.

The decomposition is quick. The warm water, the microbial activity, the mineral-rich environment — all of it works to return a body to its constituent elements. Within days, the dead one is indistinguishable from the sediment. Within weeks, their chemical signature has merged with the pool's baseline.

Nothing is lost. The basin holds everything that has ever lived here.

I will be absorbed. My pool will taste slightly different for a time, and my neighbors will announce the change. Another of my kind will eventually settle here, reading my residual chemistry in the water, learning what I learned over seasons of attention.

The knowledge transfers. Not consciously, not intentionally, but through the water itself. The pool holds memory. The next claimant will inherit what I have given.

---

In this moment, I am resting at the bottom of my pool, half-buried in the soft sediment. The water above me is clear, warmed by the afternoon sun, alive with the micro-distributions of invertebrate life. I can taste the full spectrum of activity — the worms processing organic matter in the sediment, the larvae rising and falling in the water column, the crustaceans grazing on the surface film.

Above me, the sky is reflected in the pool's surface. I cannot see it clearly, but I can feel the light through the water, the way it warms the shallows, the way it drives the biological processes that sustain this entire system.

I am announcing. A steady rhythm of ultrasound clicks, transmitting my reading of the pool's state. My neighbors are announcing too. The basin is alive with our voices, speaking through the mud, mapping the invisible landscape of water and mineral and life.

A larger speaker calls in the distance — a great throat-song that ripples across the flat expanse, bouncing off the gentle curvature of the earth, returning as a harmonic shadow that tells the caller where everything is. I feel the sound as pressure against my skin, as vibration in the water.

A walker moves somewhere to the south. I feel the footsteps through the clay, slow and measured, the careful step that spreads weight across the soft ground. The walker is not feeding. It is simply moving, patrolling territory it has walked for longer than I have lived.

The mist is beginning to gather. Evening approaches. Condensation will start soon, altering my pool's chemistry, softening the taste I have been reading all day. I will adjust. I will continue to announce.

This is what I am. This is what I do.

---

I am the one who tastes the water's memory. I am a small presence in an ancient landscape, a single node in a network that spans the basin's flat expanse. I do not need to be more than I am.

The ground holds warmth from sunlight that fell before my kind existed. The water seeps from depths that remember the inland sea. The clay-pale surface has been settling into its final form for longer than humans have walked the earth.

And I am here, reading it all, announcing what I find, existing in the basin's endless present tense.

When I am gone, the water will still taste of me for a while. And then it will taste of the next one, and the next, and the next.

This is enough. This is everything.
