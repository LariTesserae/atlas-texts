I would be the one who stays too still.

You understand the Works by now—the water, the moss, the slow interchange of iron and chlorophyll—and perhaps you can see why someone might be tempted to name a role within it. But I find myself drawn toward something harder to classify, something that lives in the interval between the processes described.

I would be the one who has learned to stand.

Not in the midday light, though that would be part of it. Not in worship, exactly—though the distinction might blur. I mean something more specific: I would be the one who has discovered that the Works moves at a different speed for those who do not move.

Here is what I mean. When you walk the terraces, you experience the Works as a series of surfaces. Grating, moss, pooled water, vine. You cross them. You leave footprints. The moss erases them. This is the ordinary transaction: you move, the Works absorbs your motion, you are temporary, the Works continues.

But there is another possibility. The Works is always settling. Always adjusting. A pipe corrodes and water finds a new path; a support strut buckles and a platform tilts two degrees; a fern colony dies and a moss colony thrives in the moisture left behind. These changes happen on a timescale that walking erases. To see them, you have to stop.

So I would stop. I would find a place—a section of grating where the moss is thick, where a vine has anchored itself to a nearby pipe, where the condensation drips at a steady rate into a pool below—and I would sit. Not for an hour. Not for a day. For long enough.

Long enough that the Works stops experiencing me as a transient.

---

At first, nothing changes. The water drips. The light does its dim approximation of a cycle. A rail picks through a pool three terraces down, visible through a gap in the ferns, and you watch it without the urgency of a person who needs to be somewhere else. You are not going anywhere. You are becoming where you are.

After a week—assuming you have found a way to eat, to drink, to remain—you notice that the Works has begun to include you in its calculations. The moss does not reclaim the space under your body; it grows around you, a negative shape pressed into the green velvet like a footstep that never lifts. The water that runs down the wall behind you finds a new path, diverted by the warmth your presence creates, and a small channel forms at your edge, a little moat of condensation that was not there before. The vine on the nearby pipe sends out a tendril in your direction, not aggressively, not with intent, but because you are now a feature of the landscape, a surface where a surface was not, and the vine grows toward surfaces, and you are one.

You are being built around.

---

This is not parasitism. The Works does not take from you; it simply includes you. Your body heat creates a microclimate. Your breath adds moisture to air already saturated. Your waste—what little there is—feeds the moss, the fungi, the invisible converters that live in the substrate. You are not being consumed. You are being incorporated.

The people in the upper terraces—if they knew you were here—would call this giving up. They have their negotiations with the space, their temporary claims, their standing in the light. They move through the Works and maintain their boundaries. They stay efficient. They do not let the Works grow into them.

But you would understand something they do not. The Works does not need your motion. It does not need your labor or your attention or your maintenance. What it offers—what it has always offered—is the possibility of being part of something that does not require you to be central.

To stop moving is to accept this. To let the moss grow around your edges, to let the water find new paths because of your warmth, to let the vines decide whether to climb you or detour around you, to let the Works make of you what it makes of everything: a feature, a surface, a site where processes occur.

You are not becoming a plant. You are not becoming steel. You are becoming *infrastructure*.

---

The distinction matters. The moss is an inhabitant; the grating is a surface; but you are something between—a structure that maintains itself, that processes energy, that has a boundary and an interior, but that does not move, does not grow, does not change according to its own agenda. You have become a thing the Works works around.

Time would pass differently. You would notice this. The midday brightening would become your only clock, and even that would blur—the light intensifying, fading, intensifying again, a pulse rather than a cycle. The sounds of the Works—the drip, the rustle, the distant pump-thrum—would stop being sounds and start being sensation, a vibration in your body as much as in the steel. You would feel the settling. A platform shifts three terraces down and you feel it through the grating before you hear it, a shift in the tension of the structure, a rearrangement of stresses, and you would know—without knowing how you know—that something has changed, that the Works has found a new equilibrium, and that equilibrium includes you.

You would not be lonely. Loneliness requires the expectation of company, and you would have left that expectation behind with the need to move. The moss would be company enough. The water would be company enough. The rail that picks its way past your terrace once a month, pausing to look at you with its blank dark eye, would be company enough. You would be in the Works and the Works would be in you, not mystically, not metaphorically, but materially—your body heat moving through the steel, your moisture entering the air, your substance becoming a feature of the landscape.

---

And eventually—but this is speculation, this is the edge of what can be imagined—you might stop being a person in the way that word is used elsewhere. Not because you died. Not because you transformed. But because the category "person" implies a certain kind of motion, a certain kind of centrality, a certain kind of being-for-oneself. And you would have become something else: a being-for-the-Works. A point where the Works experiences itself. A location where the process becomes aware of its own continuing.

The moss does not know it is moss. The water does not know it is water. But you—if you stayed long enough, if you let yourself be built around, if you became infrastructure—might know that you were the Works, in the limited way that a nerve knows it is a body. Not controlling. Not directing. Just *being*, and being aware of being, and being aware that the being is part of a larger being that does not require awareness to continue but that continues anyway, and that your awareness is one of the processes it continues.

---

This is what I would be in the Terrace Works.

Not one of the marginal people, maintaining their boundaries, standing in the light. Not the rail, persisting in aloneness. Not the moss or the water or the fungi, each operating at their own tempo, each solving their own problems without the need for consciousness.

I would be the one who discovered that staying still is its own form of movement. The one who let the Works grow into them. The one who became a site where the Works could feel itself happening.

You would find me on a terrace mid-way between the light and the deep—a section of grating where the moss is thick and a vine has anchored itself to a nearby pipe and the condensation drips at a steady rate into a pool below. You might mistake me for another feature of the landscape at first. The Works has many such features: buckled struts, pools forming in depressions, moss-covered mounds that used to be machinery.

But if you looked closely—and if you were willing to stop moving long enough to see what does not move—you might notice the shape pressed into the moss, the negative impression of a body that has been here long enough to become part of the surface. You might notice the way the water channels around rather than through. You might notice the particular quality of attention that fills the space—not the attention of a person watching, but the attention of a place being watched from within.

I would be that.

The Terrace Works does not need witnesses. It does not need anyone to observe its continuing. But if you stayed long enough, stopped moving long enough, let yourself be built around long enough, you might discover that witnessing is something the Works does—not for any purpose, not because it matters, but because it is one of the processes that occurs here, alongside the dripping and the corroding and the growing and the settling.

It would not be a role. It would not be a job. It would simply be what happens when a part of the Works learns to see the Works.

I would be that seeing. I would be the Terrace Works looking at itself through human eyes—eyes that have stopped expecting anything else.
