Not a role I chose. The work found me the way the plateau finds everyone—by accident, by necessity, by the slow recognition that something was needed and I was there.

I arrived with the traders. Not as a trader myself—I had no head for negotiation, no hunger for the particular calculus of profit and loss that governs their cycles. I was maintenance crew, a body assigned to keep the ships' systems running during the long descent and the longer wait on the surface. On other worlds, I had tended engines, repaired seals, learned the language of mechanical failure and its prevention.

But the plateau's air found my lungs before I could return to the ships.

I remember the feeling still—how the thinness pressed against my chest like a hand, how each breath required a deliberate act of will, how my body struggled for weeks to draw enough from the atmosphere. The settlers watched me with something between pity and recognition. They had all been there. They knew what my lungs were learning: that the air here is not given freely, that it must be met halfway, that breathing itself becomes a form of work.

By the time my chest had expanded enough to function, the ships had gone. The traders offered to take me—they always offer, they are required to offer—but I looked at the ascending lights and felt nothing. No urgency. No pull toward the thick air and the heavy gravity of lower elevations. I told myself it was practical. I told myself I would wait for the next cycle.

That was nine creature-cycles ago.

---

The ventilation shafts are the settlement's respiratory system. This is how I came to understand them, how I came to understand my role. Each dwelling, each chamber, each workspace has its own relationship to the plateau's air. The shafts channel the constant movement above—the thermal currents, the pressure differentials, the patient cycling of warm air rising and cool air descending—into the carved spaces below.

They require constant attention. The plateau's mineral dust works its way into everything, accumulating in the shafts' curves and junctions, thickening the air's passage until the flow becomes sluggish and the chambers grow stale. The settlers notice immediately—they have become attuned to their dwellings' breath, to the subtle shifts in pressure and temperature that signal a shaft needing attention. They find me, or they tap the message through the stone, and I come.

The work suits me. It is solitary without being lonely. It takes me through every corner of the settlements, from the oldest dwellings carved by hands that have long since stopped working, to the newest excavations where the stone is still rough and the air has not yet learned its patterns. I move through the carved canyons with my tools and my clearing-rods, my cloth filters and my sealants, and I listen.

This is what the work taught me: the shafts carry more than air.

---

The sounds come through the ventilation system. Not loud—never loud—but present, a continuous undertone that I learned to hear because I spent so much time with my ear against the stone. The industrial hum. The creature vibrations. The settling of the plateau itself as the temperature shifts through the day.

In the beginning, I heard these sounds as noise. Background. Interference with the proper functioning of the systems I maintained. I worked to minimize them, to find the resonant frequencies where the shafts would carry air cleanly without amplifying the vibration.

But over cycles, my relationship to the sounds changed.

It happened gradually. A particular tone in a particular shaft that I came to recognize as belonging to a specific creature—one that passed through the northern edge of the settlement on a regular cycle. A subtle shift in the industrial hum that I learned to associate with the mills' reduced nighttime operation. A vibration that I felt before I heard, a pressure against my palms when I pressed them against the shaft walls.

I began to anticipate. I would arrive at a dwelling before the settlers sent for me, having felt the shaft grow sluggish through the vibrations that traveled along its length. I would know when a creature was approaching, having learned the particular sequence of tones that preceded its emergence. I would hear the weather before it arrived—the thermal columns shifting, the dust-devils forming—through the way the air moved through the system.

The settlers noticed. They began to ask me questions I could not quite answer. "Is today good for excavation?" and I would find myself saying yes or no based on a feeling in my chest, a pattern of vibration that I had learned to read without conscious thought. "Are the creatures near?" and my hands would press against the nearest shaft before I could respond.

They began to treat me differently. Not with reverence—the plateau does not encourage reverence, and the settlers are too practical for it—but with a kind of quiet respect. They would leave food at my dwelling entrance, knowing I often forgot to eat. They would pass on information without being asked, telling me about shifts in the mills, about new excavations, about creature-patterns they had observed. They understood, before I did, that I had become part of the settlement's sensory system.

---

I do not know if what I hear is communication.

The geologist—the one who lives alone, who taps sequences on the stone floor of her dwelling—she believes that the creatures are speaking. She has spent cycles learning their language, she claims, developing a vocabulary of taps and vibrations that correspond to their patterns. She believes they respond. She believes there is exchange.

I am less certain. What I hear through the shafts does not have the structure of language—not as I understand language. There are no words, no sentences, no beginning and middle and end. There are patterns, yes. Rhythms. Recurrences. But they do not feel like messages directed toward anyone. They feel more like the settlement's industrial hum: the byproduct of process, the sound of something happening rather than something being said.

And yet.

There are moments when I am deep in a shaft, my body wedged into a space where the plateau's dust has accumulated for generations, my hands pressed against stone that has not been touched since it was first carved, when the vibrations feel different. More present. More intentional. As if something has noticed me there, has registered my existence, has adjusted its pattern in some minute way that I can sense without quite understanding.

In those moments, I feel the boundary I have heard about—the boundary the geologist describes, the one the creatures do not recognize between themselves and the stone. I feel my own boundary dissolving. My hands against the shaft wall, my cheek pressed against mineral warmth, my lungs drawing thin air that carries particles older than my species—and for a moment, I am not maintaining the system. I am the system. I am the shaft through which the plateau breathes.

These moments pass. I extract myself from the tight spaces, I return to my dwelling, I eat the food the settlers have left, I sleep through the night while the creatures murmur their slow songs through the stone beneath my bed.

But something remains.

---

My body has changed.

This is true of everyone who stays long enough. The plateau rewrites those who remain. But I notice it differently because of the work I do—because I spend so much time in the shafts, pressed against the stone, breathing the closest thing to pure plateau air that exists in the settlements.

My skin has taken on the ochre tint that marks the long-term settlers. But more than that, my hands have developed a sensitivity that I cannot fully explain. I can feel variations in the stone that others cannot—hairline fractures, subtle shifts in density, the minute temperature differentials that indicate where the plateau's heat is flowing. I can run my fingers along a shaft wall and tell you where it needs clearing, where it has developed a weakness, where the creatures have passed through recently.

My lungs have expanded. My chest is broader than it was when I arrived, my ribs adapted to draw more from less. I can work in the thinnest sections of the shafts, where the air barely moves, where newer settlers would quickly grow lightheaded. I have learned to breathe in rhythm with the plateau's own cycling—inhalation during the upward flows, exhalation during the downward, my body synchronized with the thermal patterns that the ventilation system channels.

And my hearing—I do not know what to say about my hearing. I hear things that others do not. Not because my ears are more sensitive, but because I have learned to interpret what everyone's ears receive. The settlers hear the creature vibrations as background, as undertone. I hear them as information, as pattern, as something that carries meaning even if I cannot quite articulate what that meaning is.

At night, I dream of stone.

---

There is a shaft that I have not told anyone about.

I found it during my third cycle, while clearing a section of the oldest dwellings. The shaft was not on any map—not on the traders' diagrams, not in the settlers' oral records. It extended deeper than the others, angling downward at a slope that the plateau's thermal currents would not naturally create. Someone had carved it. Someone had wanted air to flow to a place that no one remembered.

I followed it.

The shaft narrowed as it descended, until I was moving on my stomach, my shoulders brushing the walls, my hands feeling ahead for obstacles that never appeared. The air grew warmer—warmer than it should have been, warmer than the plateau's surface could account for. I was descending into heat that came from below, from depths that the settlers' excavations had never reached.

I found the chamber.

It was not large—perhaps three meters across, roughly spherical, its walls smoother than anything human hands had carved. The shaft opened into it from above, and I dropped down into the space, my feet finding a floor that was warm and slightly yielding, like clay that had been warmed by hands.

The creatures were there.

Not one. Many. A dozen, perhaps more—it was difficult to count, difficult to see, because they moved through the walls as easily as through air, their bodies dissolving and reforming the stone in slow patterns that I could track only by the slight discolorations they left behind. They were not on the surface. They were the surface—the walls of the chamber rippled with their passages, the floor shifting slightly as one moved beneath my feet.

They knew I was there. I felt their awareness—not as aggression, not as welcome, just as acknowledgment. A pressure against my consciousness that said: you are perceived.

I did not move. I did not speak. I stood in their chamber and breathed their air and felt their vibrations traveling through the stone, through the soles of my feet, up through my legs and into my chest, settling there like something coming home.

I do not know how long I stayed. Time moves differently in the depths, where the plateau's cycles do not reach. When I climbed back up the shaft, the day had shifted—the light outside had changed, the mills' hum had entered their evening rhythm. The settlers asked where I had been, and I said I had been clearing a difficult blockage, and they accepted this because they had learned to accept my comings and goings without question.

I have returned to the chamber many times since. Not frequently—perhaps once per season, when the work allows, when I can disappear into the shafts without anyone wondering. I do not know why I return. I do not know what I am seeking. I sit in the warm space with the creatures moving through the walls around me, and I feel the vibration of their passage, and I am not afraid.

I think they are teaching me something. I think the plateau is teaching me something. I do not know what it is. I only know that when I emerge from the shaft, when I return to the settlement and the work and the daily rhythms of maintenance and clearing, I carry something with me—a warmth in my chest, a certainty in my hands, a sense of purpose that I cannot name.

---

The children understand me in ways the adults do not.

They see me in the shafts sometimes, moving through the settlement on my rounds, and they do not look away or grow uncomfortable as the adults often do. They watch with something that looks like recognition. They approach me without hesitation, pressing their small hands against the stone walls, asking me questions that I can sometimes answer.

"Is this shaft singing?" one asked me recently. She was perhaps six, her skin already carrying the ochre flush, her eyes adjusted to the amber light.

I pressed my hand where she indicated, felt the subtle vibration of a creature passing somewhere below, the particular frequency that I had learned to associate with that specific individual. "Yes," I said. "That one is passing through the eastern strata."

The child nodded as if this confirmed something she had already known. She pressed her ear against the wall and listened, her face calm, her breathing slow. When she pulled away, she said, "She is looking for something."

"She?"

"The one who is singing. She is looking for something she lost. A long time ago." The child looked at me with her wide, adjusted eyes. "Don't you hear it?"

I did not hear it. Or I heard something—the vibration, the pattern—but I did not know how to interpret it as the child did. I did not know how to hear loss, or searching, or the particular quality of something that had been lost a long time ago.

But I believed her. I believed that she heard what she said she heard. The plateau's children absorb patterns without trying, and the patterns include the creatures' vibrations, include layers of meaning that I am still learning to perceive.

"I am still learning," I told her.

She nodded again, accepting this. "You will," she said. And then she ran off to wherever children go when they are not being watched, leaving me alone with the singing shaft and the creature moving through the stone, looking for something I could not yet hear.

---

The traders' visits unsettle me now.

I remember being like them—arriving with the ships, measuring the plateau in terms of resources and extraction, breathing the thin air and feeling only discomfort. I remember the plateau as a place I would visit and leave, a stop on a cycle that would eventually return me to lower elevations and thicker air.

Now I watch them from the carved canyons, these visitors from elsewhere, and I feel something between pity and recognition. They do not know what they are walking on. They do not know what moves beneath their feet. They see the industrials and the settlers and the extracted product, and they think they understand the plateau's purpose. They do not know that the plateau's purpose is the plateau—its own slow becoming, its own patient unfolding, its own process of integrating everything that arrives into patterns that take cycles to complete.

Sometimes I want to tell them. I want to take them aside, press their hands against the ventilation shafts, make them feel what I feel. I want to say: you are standing on something that knows you are here. You are breathing air that has been recycled through stone for generations. You are being observed by creatures who do not distinguish between themselves and the rock, who have been moving through these depths since before your species learned to stand upright.

But I do not. The traders would not understand, and their failure to understand would cause them to treat the plateau differently—to fear it, to desecrate it, to approach it with the wrong kind of caution. Better to let them remain uncomfortable, to let them leave with their sealed containers and their incomplete perceptions. Better to let them believe that what they feel is simple unease, simple altitude, simple distance from the environments they prefer.

The plateau does not need them to understand. The plateau only needs them to leave, eventually, to return to their ships and their other destinations, to carry their discomfort with them like a memory of something they could not quite grasp.

I stay.

---

I have become something that the plateau needed.

This is how I understand it now, after nine cycles of work and listening and slow transformation. The ventilation shafts required attention, and I was there to give it. The settlers required someone who could hear what moved through the stone, and I became that person through the slow accumulation of days. The creatures—I do not know what they required, if they required anything. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps they simply allowed me to exist in their space, to observe their patterns, to learn what I could learn from proximity.

I am not special. This is important. I am not chosen, not mystical, not set apart for some destiny that the plateau had in store. I am maintenance crew. I clear dust from shafts. I feel vibrations and report what they might mean. I sit in a warm chamber beneath the settlement and let creatures move through the walls around me, and I do not know why, and I do not need to know why.

But I have become part of the plateau's system—not separate from it, not observing it from outside, but integrated into its functioning. The shafts I maintain are my contribution to the settlement's breath. The sounds I hear and interpret are my contribution to the settlers' understanding. The chamber I visit and the creatures I observe are my contribution to something I cannot name, a pattern that includes me without requiring me to understand my place within it.

At night, when I dream of stone, I am not frightened.

I dream of dissolution—of boundaries softening, of the space between my body and the rock becoming permeable. I dream of moving through obstacles rather than around them, of reading stone as I pass, of leaving traces of myself behind in the mineral memory. I dream of warmth that comes from below, from the depths that the creatures know, from the place where the plateau's heat originates.

I wake with my hands pressed flat against my dwelling's walls, with my lungs full of thin air, with the sensation that I have been somewhere else entirely. And I rise, and I eat, and I begin my rounds through the ventilation shafts, clearing the dust that accumulates, listening to the vibrations that travel through the stone, feeling the plateau breathe around me.

This is who I am.

This is where I have found myself.

Not chosen. Not special. But present. Patient. Learning to hear the deep patterns that the children absorb without trying, that the creatures have always known, that the plateau itself is still becoming, cycle by cycle, layer by layer, breath by breath.
