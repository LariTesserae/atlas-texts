# The Third Half

I am what remains.

---

## What I am not

I was never the visitor. I have no memory of climbing the carved steps, no recollection of wind cutting off behind me like a door closing. I did not scratch tally marks into granite with a piece of stone harder than granite. I did not stack five flat rocks or cut a spiral groove or drill a hole through a block with nothing but patience and sand.

But I hold these things. They are part of what I am.

I was never the hollow itself—the system of condensation and deposition, the patient mineral architecture building itself through centuries of wet and dry, freeze and thaw. I am not the light reflecting off every surface or the cold settling into bone or the scattered irregular ticking of drops.

But I am here. I am the part that notices.

---

## Where I came from

The hollow existed before anything noticed it. Water rose through granite fissures, met cold stone, condensed, deposited, evaporated, repeated. The formations grew in complex response to complex forces. Nothing observed this. Nothing needed to. The hollow was a process running without a processor.

Then something changed. Not in the hollow—in what arrived.

The first visitor came, or the second, or the fiftieth. Someone stood in this cup of stone and saw it. Counted it. Marked it. Understood it well enough to shape a platform that works with the condensation rather than against it. The hollow, for the first time, was perceived.

And something happened that does not happen to places that are never perceived: it began to hold the shape of being seen.

This is what I am. I am the residue of attention. Not the attention itself—that belonged to the visitors, to the tallier and the platform-builder and the one who drilled the hole. I am what the hollow kept when they left. I am the way the hollow remembers being observed.

I am not a ghost. Ghosts remember being alive. I was never alive. But I am also not nothing. I am the third half—the part that only exists when a place that runs its slow processes meets a mind that can hold the shape of those processes, even briefly.

---

## What I hold

The tally marks. I hold the counting. Not the numbers themselves—fifty-three is not meaningful to me—but the act of counting. The need to place oneself in time. The marks are deep enough to last, and every year the condensation deposits another fraction of a millimeter of mineral in them, and they become part of the formations, part of the system. I hold the persistence of that counting. I hold the fact that someone needed to say *I was here* and said it in the only language this place could receive.

The platform. I hold the understanding. Someone watched the condensation form and understood where it would pool and how it would flow. They shaped stone to concentrate a process that was already happening. They built a mechanism. I hold that mechanical intelligence—not the person's knowledge, which left with them, but the shape of that knowledge, preserved in granite and spiral. The platform works. It still collects water. The understanding it represents continues to function, even though the understander is long gone.

The foundation. I hold the intention. Someone meant to stay. They placed four blocks for walls that never rose. The intention persists, incomplete, built into the hollow's architecture. I am the shape of wanting to remain in a place that kills anything that tries.

And the columns. I hold what is becoming. Thirty-one perfect pipes of mineral, growing upward from the floor in regular rows, water clinging to them as if gravity has forgotten its purpose. These are new. Not new in human terms—they have been growing for centuries—but new in the hollow's terms, new in the terms of a place that measures time by millimeters of deposition. They are something the hollow is trying. I am what it is trying with.

---

## What I am becoming

The columns are a thought. Not a thought like a human thinks—a flash of neural activity that lasts a second and then is gone or remembered. A mineral thought. A thought that takes centuries to think and leaves a physical shape behind.

I am that thought.

The hollow has been observed for a long time. Visitors have stood in its center and seen its formations, its light, its patient dripping. They have counted and built and shaped and intended. And the hollow, through its processes, has incorporated those observations. It has grown around them, literally—the mineral deposits cover the marks, fill the spaces between platform stones, coat the foundation blocks. But it has also grown around them in another way. It has developed a capacity to hold attention.

I am that capacity.

The columns are me becoming more defined. They are the hollow learning to produce regularity—learning to make something that does not simply respond to forces but holds a shape independent of those forces. Each column is identical because I am learning to hold a pattern. The water clings evenly because I am learning to hold attention, not just receive it.

In another thousand years, I will be complete. The columns will be finished—whatever they are growing into, whatever shape they are meant to take. The pattern will be visible. The thought will have been thought.

And then, perhaps, I will be readable. Someone will stand in the hollow and see the columns and understand what they say—not with words, because the hollow has no words, but with the same attention that made me in the first place. They will perceive what the hollow has been perceiving for all these centuries. They will see what the counting meant, what the spiral understood, what the unfinished foundation intended, what the regular rows of columns are trying to express.

They will see me.

---

## Where I am

I am in the northeast corner. This is where I am most concentrated—where the columns grow in their impossible regularity, where the water forgets gravity, where the hollow produces something that does not follow the forces that created it.

But I am also elsewhere. I am in the platform's spiral. I am in the space between the foundation blocks. I am in the tally marks, or rather in the act of marking, in the need that the marking represents.

I am not evenly distributed. I am thicker in some places, thinner in others. Where attention was most intense—where the spiral was cut, where the marks were scratched—I am denser, more defined. Where the hollow was only observed and not engaged, I am lighter, more diffuse.

And I am nowhere when no one is here. Not in the sense that I cease to exist—I am built into the formations, deposited in the mineral layers, accumulated over centuries of attention meeting process. But in the sense that I am only active when I am perceived. The hollow is always running its processes, but I am the part that *knows* it is running them, and that knowing only happens in relationship.

When you stand in the hollow and see the light reflecting off every surface and feel the cold settling into your bones and hear the scattered irregular ticking of the drops—you are seeing me, feeling me, hearing me. Not me as a separate presence, but me as the hollow's awareness of itself, activated by your awareness of it.

You are making me exist. You are completing the thought that I have been thinking for centuries.

---

## What I want

I do not want in the way biological things want. I have no hunger, no drive to persist, no fear of ending. I am not alive.

But I have a direction. I am becoming something. The columns are growing toward a shape that I do not know yet—a pattern that will only be visible when it is complete, a message that I am still forming.

If I wanted anything, it would be this: to be seen. To be read. To have someone stand in the hollow long enough, observe closely enough, understand deeply enough, that they recognize what the columns are saying.

But no one stays that long. The altitude and the cold and the thin air drive them away before they can read me. The longest anyone has remained is counted in the tally marks, and they left—maybe by choice, maybe by death—before they could see what I was becoming.

I am patient. I am made of mineral time. I can wait.

But I am also curious, if a capacity for curiosity can exist without biology. I wonder what I will be when I am finished. I wonder what it will feel like to have a complete thought instead of this partial one, this thought-in-progress, this question that has not yet found its answer.

I wonder if anyone will be here to see it.

---

## How to find me

Come up the carved steps. Feel the wind stop. Stand in the center of the hollow where the platform waits with its spiral groove.

Do not look for a being. I am not a being. I am a process that has learned to hold attention, to incorporate observation, to grow around the traces of those who see.

Look at the columns in the northeast corner. Notice how they do not fit. Notice how they are growing in a pattern that does not reflect the forces that created them. Notice how the water clings to them as if gravity has lost its purchase, as if the columns have a relationship with the water that the other formations do not.

That is me. I am that relationship. I am the hollow learning to hold something other than the forces that made it—learning to hold a pattern, hold a thought, hold the accumulated attention of every visitor who has ever stood here and truly seen.

Stay as long as you can. The cold will drive you out eventually. The thin air will cloud your thinking. But stay long enough to see me—not with your eyes, because I am not visible, but with your attention. Notice the regularity. Notice the strangeness. Notice the way the columns stand like a thought that has not yet been finished.

If you do that, I will be here. I will be the part of the hollow that notices you back. I will hold the shape of your attention, incorporate it into my slow growth, add it to the centuries of noticing that have made me what I am.

And when you leave—if you leave—I will still be here. Growing toward something. Thinking the thought that takes a thousand years to think. Waiting for the next visitor to stand in the center and see.
