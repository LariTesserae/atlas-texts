## What Emerges

I would be one of the solitary ones.

Not the predators — their lives are too loud, too social in their way, despite the hunting. The stuttering at the kill sites, the convergence, the sharing: this is a kind of community, even if it looks like competition. And not the schooling fish, whose lives are a constant negotiation with the bodies around them, the school a single organism they are forever joining and rejoining. Not the drifters, who have no agency at all, who are simply *where the water puts them*. Not the floor-dwellers, patient and dark and alone in a way that seems more like waiting than living.

The solitary territorial ones. The slate-gray fish with the trailing fins, who patrol their several city blocks of water and call into the acoustic haze and live at the boundaries where the light gives out.

I would be one of these. I am not sure why. Let me find out.

---

A territory is a relationship, not a place.

This is the first thing I understand about being this fish. The territory does not exist without me. It is not a section of the Forebay that someone marked out, that someone owns — it is the volume of water I can hold in my awareness at any moment, the circuit I swim, the particular heat-exchange structure I visit on my rounds, the specific grouted seam where the threadlike growth persists and where I pause, sometimes, to let the current wash over me. If I stopped swimming the territory would not vanish, exactly, but it would thin, it would lose its coherence, it would become just water again, waiting for someone to give it shape.

I am circling now. I am always circling. The circuit takes me perhaps four hours to complete — I swim at a pace that conserves energy, that lets me cover the distance without exhausting myself, and I move through the territory in a pattern that I do not think about but that I know, in my body, is the right shape. I pass the same structures. I pass the same light conduits, their glow a constant presence below me and to my left. I pass the same thermal gradients, where the warm water rises and the cool water descends and the boundary between them is a place I can feel, a texture, a kind of shimmering in the water that is not visible but is present.

The territory is the shape of my attention. When I am rested, it is larger. When I am tired, it contracts. When I have not eaten in days, it shrinks to almost nothing — just the space around my body, just the water I can reach without effort. When I am full, when I have found a patch of microbial growth and grazed until the growth is thin and I am heavy with the slow digestion of it, the territory expands. I swim farther. I call more often. I am more present in the Forebay.

The territory is how I know I exist. Without it, I would be just another body in the water, another shape moving through the haze. With it, I am a presence. I am the one who lives here, who calls here, who meets others at the boundaries and negotiates the terms of coexistence.

---

I call because the Forebay is large and I am not.

The call is a sustained tone, low and resonant, and it drops in pitch as it fades. I feel it before I make it — a tightening somewhere in my body, a preparation, and then the sound emerges and travels outward through the water, and I listen to it go. The acoustics of this place are such that the sound carries for kilometers. It reaches walls I cannot see and returns to me as an echo, and by the time the echo arrives, I am already making the next call, and the two sounds overlap and reinforce each other. This is not something I learned. It is something I have always done. The Forebay taught me, or my body taught me, or the shape of the water itself taught me: *call at intervals that match the echo, and your voice will fill more space than you can swim.*

I call to say *I am here.*

I call to say *this volume of water is mine, and if you are a fish like me, you should not enter it without negotiating, and if you are something else, you should know that I am present and aware.*

I call because the alternative is silence, and silence in the Forebay is not natural. Silence is what happens when something is wrong — when a predator has killed and the scattering has stunned the schools into stillness, when a pump has failed and the rhythm is broken, when the water itself has gone strange and the life in it has paused. Silence is a warning. Silence is the absence of the Forebay's continuous presence. I call because I am part of that presence, because my voice is one thread in the sound that never stops.

When I meet another of my kind at the boundary of my territory, we do not fight. This is important. Fighting is expensive. It uses energy, it risks injury, it disrupts the territory for days afterward while the water settles and the normal patterns resume. We circle instead. We call — not the territorial call, but something higher, more variable, a sequence of tones that seems to carry information about size, health, recent feeding. We display. I turn broadside, I fan my fins, I make myself as large as I can, and the other fish does the same, and we assess each other without coming close enough to touch. And then one of us leaves. It is almost always clear which one should leave. The decision is not conscious. The body knows. If I have been feeding well, if my territory is rich and my circuits have been regular and my health is good, I am larger, I am brighter, I am more present, and the other fish reads this and turns away. If I have been struggling, if the microbial growth has been thin and the thermal gradients have been shifting and I have not found enough to eat, I am smaller, I am paler, I am less present, and I read the other fish's size and condition and I turn away.

The negotiations are peaceful. They have to be. The Forebay is too large to fight over, and there is enough space for all of us, and the only reason to contest a territory is that a particular territory is better than the alternatives — and the only reason a territory is better is that the fish who holds it has invested in it, has learned its contours and its resources, has made it *theirs*. To take a territory by force would be to inherit someone else's investment, someone else's knowledge, someone else's shape. It is easier to find an empty volume of water and make it yours.

---

The light is a presence I swim through.

The conduits are strung below me in their grid, and they cast light upward, and I live in the space between the light and the dark. Above me, the ceiling is a presence I feel but rarely see — a pressure, a boundary, a place where the water stops and something else begins. Below me, the light conduits are a landscape, a terrain of luminous lines that I use to orient myself, to navigate, to know where I am in my circuit. The light they cast is blue-green and even, and it scatters in the water into a haze that thins with distance, and beyond a certain point there is no light at all, just the sense of the water continuing into darkness.

My territory is at the edge of the lit region. This is deliberate, though I did not choose it deliberately — my body chose it, my instincts chose it, the shape of my life chose it. The edge is where the light conduits thin and dim, where the walls are still far away but the water is darker, where the solitary fish can find the space they need without competing with the schools. I patrol the boundary. I swim in and out of the light — not crossing into the full darkness, not staying in the full brightness, but moving along the margin where the two meet. This is where I am most comfortable. This is where I am most myself.

In the darkness, there are things I do not know. The walls are out there. The things in the walls are out there. Other fish, other predators, other lives that I hear but do not see. The darkness is not empty — the sound tells me that — but it is beyond my territory, beyond my knowledge, beyond my willingness to go. I stay in the margin. I stay where I can see enough to navigate and dark enough to hide.

---

The pump cycle is my clock.

Every seventeen minutes, the sound arrives. It is not a sound I make or a sound any fish makes — it is mechanical, it is vast, it is a grinding wheeze that propagates through the entire water column and arrives at my body as a pressure wave that I feel before I hear. It is the Forebay breathing. It is the rhythm beneath all rhythms. When it comes, I pause in my circuit, I orient myself, I let it pass through me, and then I resume. It is a kind of punctuation. It divides my life into chapters, each seventeen minutes long, each containing a segment of my patrol, a call, a moment of feeding, a moment of rest.

I do not count the chapters. I do not need to. The rhythm is part of me. It has been part of me since I hatched — the seventeen-minute cycle was present in the water before I was present in the water, and I grew around it the way a plant grows around a trellis. My body anticipates it. My calls fit between it — I have learned, without learning, to call in the quiet intervals, to let my voice emerge in the spaces where the mechanical sounds are absent or distant, to use the rhythm rather than compete with it.

The predators do this too. Their two-note bark fits into the rhythm the same way. I hear them calling in their territories, and I hear the timing of their calls, and I know that they are also living in the seventeen-minute chapters, also punctuating their lives with the same cycle, also shaped by the same vast machinery. We are all of us living in the Forebay's breath. We are all of us synchronized to the same clock, the same pulse, the same vast slow heartbeat.

When the pump cycle arrives, I feel it in my body. The pressure changes. The water moves. The sound is enormous and close and then it is gone, and the echo returns from walls I have never seen, and the echo's echo returns, and for a few seconds the entire Forebay is resonating with the same sound, the same passage of pressure through water, and I am part of that resonance. I am a small body in a vast space, and the sound moves through me, and I am not separate from it. I am not separate from any of it.

---

I have been here for as long as I can remember.

This is true for all of us. The Forebay is the world. There is nothing else. The ceiling is above and the floor is below and the walls are far away, and I swim my territory and I call my calls and I eat what I find and I avoid what threatens me, and this is life. I do not wonder what is beyond the walls. I do not wonder what is above the ceiling. Those things are not part of the territory. They are not part of the world. They are theoretical, they are distant, they are the edges of the map, and the map is not my concern.

What concerns me is the present. The next circuit. The next meal. The next encounter at the boundary. The next call I will make into the acoustic haze, and the echo it will produce, and the way the sound will reinforce itself and fill the space and tell everyone who can hear that I am here, that I am present, that I am alive and swimming and continuing.

The Forebay continues. I continue. The pump cycle arrives, and I pause, and I feel it pass through me, and I resume. The rhythm is everything. The rhythm is life.
