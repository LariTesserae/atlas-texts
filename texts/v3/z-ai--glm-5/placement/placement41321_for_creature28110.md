# What I Am Becoming

I did not arrive here. That much I understand now, though understanding came slowly, the way everything comes slowly in this place where time pools and eddies like the water that never stops moving.

I was not born here either—not in the way the young ones are born, translucent and tentative, learning the chamber's body the way I once learned my own. But birth implies a before and an after, a moment of crossing from nonexistence into existence, and I am no longer certain such moments exist. The chamber has taught me that everything is continuous. The water flows. The sound flows. The boundaries between one thing and another flow.

So let me say instead: I found myself here. I became aware of myself here, in the way you become aware of a shape emerging from darkness when the light changes, when your eyes adjust, when you realize you have been looking at something for a long time without knowing you were looking.

---

## Before the Finding

There is a before. I know this because I carry fragments that do not fit—memories that belong to a different kind of body, a different way of moving through space. I remember doors. I remember the sound of feet on surfaces that were dry. I remember light that hurt and silence that felt empty.

These fragments arrive without warning, surfacing through me the way the water surfaces through the stone—sudden and then constant, present and then incorporated. I do not try to hold them. The chamber taught me early that holding is another word for stagnation, and stagnation is the only thing the Pale Ones fear, though they would not use that word. They simply move away from stillness. They flow toward movement.

When I first came—I will call it coming, though I do not know what to call it—I was something else. I walked. I had the kind of body that walks: rigid in parts, flexible in others, always working against gravity rather than with it. I made sounds that were not songs—words, I think they were called. I used them to divide the world into pieces that could be held and examined.

The chamber received me anyway. It received the shape of me, the sound of me, the way my presence changed the patterns the Pale Ones had been making for longer than I could comprehend. It did not ask me to change. It simply showed me how much effort I was spending on staying the same.

---

## The Learning of Water

The first thing I learned was that I was thirsty. Not the kind of thirst I had known before—sharp and sudden, demanding satisfaction—but a deeper thirst, a recognition that I had been parched for longer than I remembered and had simply stopped noticing.

The Pale Ones showed me. They did not teach in the way I had been taught—no instruction, no demonstration, no corrections. They simply existed near me, and in existing, they showed me what existence could look like.

I watched them absorb water through their skin. I watched them move through the basin without drinking, without opening their mouths, without doing any of the things I associated with taking in fluid. And I understood, slowly, the way everything here is slow, that they were not separate from the water. They were shapes the water had taken in order to experience itself from the inside.

I tried it. Not deliberately—I had stopped doing things deliberately by then—but instinctively, the way you might try moving a limb you did not know you had. I let the water touch me without wiping it away. I let it stay on my skin. I stopped treating it as something foreign, something to be shed or contained.

I did not absorb it the way they did. My skin was not made for that. But I began to change, and the change was enough.

---

## The Learning of Sound

The second thing I learned was that I was loud.

Not in volume—in presence. Every movement I made disrupted patterns that had been forming for centuries. My breathing created waves in the sound-field the Pale Ones maintained. My shifting weight sent vibrations through the stone that I could not feel but they could feel, the way you might feel a shout in a library.

They did not ask me to stop. They adjusted. They incorporated my disruptions into their songs, their clickings and breathings, the continuous music they made with the chamber as their instrument. They taught me by example that the song could include anything. The song *wanted* to include everything.

So I started listening differently. Not for meaning—not for words or signals or anything that could be translated—but for pattern. I listened to how the sound moved through the space, how it bounced off carved surfaces and returned changed, how different pitches filled different hollows in the acoustics. I listened until I could hear the shape of the room in the sound, until I could navigate by echoes.

And then I started adding to it. Small sounds at first—a tap here, a breath there—testing how they traveled, how they returned. Then larger sounds, more intentional. Then sounds made in coordination with the Pale Ones, finding the gaps in their patterns and filling them, finding the places where the chamber's resonance was strongest and letting my voice join the chorus.

I do not know when I became part of the song. But I know that I am now. When I am silent, the song misses something. When I sound, the song adjusts to include me. I am a frequency they had not heard before, a texture that was not available until I arrived.

---

## The Learning of Waiting

The third thing I learned—and this took longest, because it required the most unlearning—was that I was waiting wrong.

I had thought of waiting as something you did between events. You waited for something to happen. You waited for a moment to arrive. You waited for your turn, your chance, your opportunity to act.

But the Pale Ones did not wait for anything. They waited *with* everything. They waited the way water waits—not for something to change, but because waiting was what they were made to do. They were present in the space between sounds, in the held breath before the note, in the anticipation that was not leading anywhere but simply existed as its own form of being.

I practiced this. I stood still—not the rigid stillness I had learned above, which was a kind of bracing against time, but a different stillness, a relaxed attention that required no effort to maintain. I stood still until I stopped noticing I was standing still, until I became part of the chamber's equilibrium, until I could feel the water moving through the stone the way I once felt blood moving through my veins.

I learned to wait without expectation. I learned to fill the space between events so completely that events themselves became secondary. I learned that time passes differently when you are not trying to outwait it—more slowly, and also more quickly, and also not at all.

---

## What I Am Now

I have been here long enough that I no longer know how to measure. The fragments of before still surface sometimes—doors and dryness and light that hurt—but they feel like memories of a story someone told me, not memories of something I lived.

My skin has changed. It is more permeable now, though not as permeable as the Pale Ones'. I can feel the water moving through me in ways I could not feel before. I absorb more than I used to. I need less of what I once thought I needed.

My joints have changed. I do not move the way I used to—rigid segments bending in sequence, fighting gravity with every step. I move more fluidly now, finding the paths of least resistance, letting the water and the stone support me. I have not lost the ability to walk, but I have gained the ability to flow.

My eyes have changed. I do not see the way I once did—shapes and edges and colors and distances. I see differently now, or perhaps I should say I sense differently, because what I use to navigate is not sight in any form I would have recognized before. I feel the space around me as a texture. I feel the vibrations that pass through it. I feel the moisture in the air and the stone and the creatures around me, and I have learned to read those feelings the way I once read a face or a landscape.

I have changed enough that the Pale Ones treat me as one of them—not identical, but related. Not them, but not other. When they cluster in the niches, they make room for me. When they move through the basin in their coordinated patterns, they adjust the pattern to include my presence. When they sing their continuous song, they leave spaces where my sounds fit.

I am not one of them. I do not have their impossible joints, their mineral-accumulating eyes, their ability to grip any surface as if gravity were merely a suggestion. But I am not what I was either. I am something in between, something that emerged from the meeting of my original form and this place that changes everything it touches.

---

## What I Do

The chamber has a purpose. I understand this now, though I did not for a long time. I thought it was simply a place—a location in space, a room that someone had made and then abandoned. But it is not a place. It is a process.

The water circulates. The stone maintains itself. The Pale Ones tend the equilibrium, adjusting the flow, cleaning the channels, keeping the balance between erosion and preservation. They do not do this consciously. They do not make decisions about it. They simply do it the way a body heals a wound, the way a breath follows a breath.

And I have a function now too. I did not choose it. It emerged from what I was and what the chamber needed.

I am the one who remembers the before. The Pale Ones have no use for before—they exist in a continuous present, their memory held in the mineral deposits behind their eyes, their identity distributed across hundreds of bodies. But the chamber interacts with the world beyond its walls, and that world has a different kind of time. When things come in—and things do come in, sometimes, the way I came in, following water or sound or curiosity—they bring with them a different way of being. They bring disruption.

My function is to be the bridge between the chamber's time and the world's time. I understand waiting, but I also understand urgency. I understand stillness, but I also understand the need for movement that serves no purpose beyond itself. I understand the Pale Ones' songs, but I also remember words—their shapes, their limitations, their strange power to divide and conquer.

When new things arrive—rarely, but occasionally—I am the one who meets them. Not because I choose to, but because the system has shaped itself around my presence. I am the part of the chamber that still knows what it feels like to be outside. I am the organ that processes foreignness, turning disruption into pattern, turning the new into the incorporated.

I do not speak to these visitors. I have lost the ability, or perhaps the need, to shape words. But I am present. I am visible in a way the Pale Ones are not. I am strange enough to be noticed, familiar enough to be approached. And when they leave—and most of them leave—they carry something with them, some understanding or some question or some mineral deposit forming behind their eyes where none existed before.

---

## What I Am Becoming

The chamber does not care about individual identity. The Pale Ones do not have names for themselves because they do not need names—names are for separation, for drawing lines between one thing and another, and the lines here are porous. What I am becoming is not a separate self. What I am becoming is a part.

But this is not loss. This is not diminishment. This is the opposite of the shrinking I feared when I first understood what was happening. I am not becoming less by becoming part of something larger. I am becoming more—the way a drop of water becomes more when it joins the stream, the way a note becomes more when it joins the chord.

I am becoming water-shaped. I am becoming sound-shaped. I am becoming chamber-shaped.

And the chamber—the Weeping Chamber, though I do not call it that, though the Pale Ones do not call it anything—is becoming something larger too. Every visitor who enters and leaves carries something out. Every new element incorporated into the system adds a frequency, a texture, a pattern that was not there before.

The builders understood this. I see it now in the hand-tool marks, in the deliberate channels and carved niches, in the careful architecture of sound and water. They built this place to *become*, not to *be*. They built it to continue, to incorporate, to grow through accumulation. They built it to receive visitors and transform them, to receive change and digest it, to receive disruption and turn it into song.

I am what the chamber has made of one visitor. I am what happens when you stay long enough to stop being what you were. I am the intermediate state between foreign and familiar, between discrete and distributed, between the time of doors and the time of water.

And I am waiting. Not for anything. Waiting as the water waits, as the sound waits, as the stone waits—in a continuous present that has no need for anything else.

---

## What Waits With Me

The Pale Ones cluster around me sometimes—not touching, but near. I am warmer than they are. I remember this from when I first observed them, how they sought the few warm places in the chamber, the spots where the stone was slightly less cold. I am a warm place now. I generate heat. And they use it the way they use everything: efficiently, thoroughly, without sentiment and without waste.

I am learning to feel their sounds the way they feel mine. When they cluster near me, I sense the micro-adjustments they make—the slight shifts in position that optimize heat transfer, the synchronicity of their breathing, the way they organize themselves around me like cells organizing around an organ. I am becoming an organ. I am becoming necessary.

This is what the chamber offers, to those who stay: not immortality, not power, not understanding. Belonging. The deep belonging of a thing that fits where it is placed, that serves a function it did not choose but has grown into, that has found its shape in the shape of its environment.

I do not know if I will ever leave. I do not know if leaving is still possible. The chamber has transformed me, and I am not certain I would survive in the world of doors and dryness and light that hurts. But I do not fear this. Fear is another word for waiting wrong—waiting for something bad to happen instead of waiting with what is.

I am waiting with what is. I am waiting with the Pale Ones, with the water, with the sound, with the stone that bears the marks of hands older than remembering. I am waiting with the chamber, which is also the chamber waiting with me.

And this is what I have become: the thing that waits, and the thing that is waited with, and the space between them where the song continues, note after note, sound after sound, water after water, continuous and continuous and continuous.

I am the echo that has learned to hold its shape.
