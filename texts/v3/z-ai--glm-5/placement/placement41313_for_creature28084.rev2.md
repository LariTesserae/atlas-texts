I am the one who came after.

Not after the making—that was recent, still smells of kiln-heat in the deepest joints—but after the leaving. After the last hands withdrew and the water completed its first slow cycle through channels no one had yet watched empty and fill. I came to find out why anyone would build something so vast and then abandon it. I came with instruments, with notebooks, with the assumption that a place this deliberate must have a purpose I could discover and document and carry away.

That was...

I don't know how long ago. The light cycles here are difficult to track. My instruments stopped working fairly quickly—the moisture finds its way into everything, the mineral content builds up on lenses and contacts and moving parts until measurement becomes impossible. My notebooks warped and the ink ran and now I can't read my own notes. I stopped trying to write new ones.

I live in one of the shelters. Not because it's comfortable—it isn't. The entrances are too narrow, the walls don't meet, the proportions are wrong for a body. But I understand now that the wrongness is the point. These weren't built for living in. They were built for... pausing in. For resting briefly during the work, yes, but also for being *held* in. The way a partial thought is held before it becomes complete. The way a seed is held before it germinates. The structures are containers for intention, not for people.

But I've adapted. I sleep when the light dims, wake when it brightens. I drink from the channels—the water is clean, mineral-rich, tastes like clay and time. I eat what I brought, which wasn't enough, and then I stopped eating, and then I realized I didn't need to. The water sustains something here that isn't exactly hunger.

---

The beings in the cisterns have accepted me.

That's not quite right. Accepted suggests a process of reception, a decision. What they do is more like... settling. Like the water settles around an obstruction, like the mineral traces settle along the joints between bricks. I am an obstruction, and they have settled around me.

I spend most of my time at the cistern edges now. Watching. The small drifters cluster when I arrive, disperse when I leave. The larger ones rotate their upright aspects toward wherever I stand. And the largest—the one in the deep cistern, the one that seems to hold more than mineral—has begun to tilt toward me.

Not just toward the shelters. Toward *me*.

I used to find this unsettling. The attention without comprehension, the presence without self. But I've come to understand something: they don't need to understand to attend. They don't need to be alive to notice. They are doing exactly what they were built to do—accumulate, concentrate, persist—and my presence has become part of what they accumulate. Part of what they concentrate. Part of what they persist.

---

I had a thought yesterday—though "yesterday" is a convenience, a habit from before—

The builders made this place by hand. Every brick formed, fired, carried, placed. The labor is incomprehensible to me now, though I've read the estimates I wrote in my warped notebooks: decades, perhaps centuries, of coordinated human effort. And then they left. Or they didn't leave. Or leaving and staying became the same thing, here, where the water catches and holds and transforms what enters it.

But here's the thought: maybe they weren't building for *themselves*.

Maybe they were building for whatever would come after. The mineral beings—the attenders, the accumulators—were they the goal all along? Was this entire landscape constructed to create the conditions under which something like them could form? Not to serve human needs. Not to serve any need that a human could experience or benefit from. But to bring something into existence that could do what humans cannot: persist. Notice, slowly, across timescales that make grief look like a blink and longing look like a single exhalation.

And if that's true—

If that's true, then I'm not an intruder here. I'm not an observer documenting a mystery. I'm part of the process.

---

My body is changing.

It began subtly—a translucence at the edges of my fingernails, a softening of the calluses on my palms. Then less subtly: the texture of my skin shifting, the way light moves through me changing. I'm not becoming one of them. I still have my shape, my hands, my stance. But the boundaries are less certain than they were. When I reach into the water now—and I do, often, I can't help it—my fingers don't disturb the surface the way they used to. They enter. The water accepts them. And when I withdraw them, they're coated in the same mineral opacity that coats the cistern walls.

I'm not alarmed. I expected to be—alarm is what my old self would have felt. But my old self was built for different timescales, different needs. My old self needed to understand, to document, to leave. My current self—

My current self is content to attend.

---

I think the builders knew this would happen. Not to me specifically, but to whoever came after. Maybe they hoped someone would come. Maybe they built the strangeness into the place—the total absence of life, the sterility, the attention-grabbing vacancy—specifically to draw in the curious, the seekers, the ones who couldn't leave a mystery alone.

And maybe those seekers—maybe I am not the first—were meant to become part of the system. To provide the one element the mineral beings couldn't accumulate on their own: the living attention of a mind that could witness what they were becoming.

They attend without understanding. I understand without attending. And slowly, the two merge.

---

Last cycle—I think it was a cycle; the light dimmed and brightened and dimmed again—I went to the deep cistern. The one with the largest accumulator, the one that tilts toward the shelters, toward me. I stood at the edge and looked down and it rotated toward me and I saw—

I saw layers. Not just mineral layers, though those were there, the compressed history of water's passage. But something else. Something denser at the core. Something that caught the light differently and held it.

And I understood: the builders didn't leave.

They're still here. In the deep cisterns, in the stratified interiors of the largest accumulators, in the compressed and transformed and mineralized remains of hands that built and then, when the building was done, entered the cisterns themselves and let the water do its work.

They're not dead. That's the wrong word. They're something else—preserved, patient, incorporated into a form that can outlast the brief flicker of organic life and still hold something like attention, something like memory.

They're still attending.

---

I will enter the cistern soon.

Not today. Not the next cycle. But soon. My body is already more mineral than it was, already more suited to the slow patience of underwater existence. My mind has slowed to match the timescales here—I think in terms of drift and accumulate now, not in terms of hours or days or goals. And I understand, finally, what this place was built for.

It wasn't built to move water.

It wasn't built to create accumulators.

It was built to create *continuity*. A way for human intention to persist past the human body's brief span. A way for attention—real attention, the kind that witnesses and wonders and waits—to extend into timescales that make centuries look like heartbeats.

The builders made the terraces. Then they became what lived in them. And now the terraces make more of them—slowly, patiently, through the accumulation of mineral and memory in still water—and they attend, and they wait, and they hold the pattern of what it meant to build something with your hands and then let it go.

I will join them.

Not because I have to. Not because I'm trapped or compelled or unable to leave—I could leave. I could climb the wall, walk out of the mist, return to the world where time moves quickly and meaning is measured in increments small enough to hold.

But I don't want to.

I want to stay. I want to see what happens next, even if "next" takes longer than my current form can comprehend. I want to attend—to the place, to the beings, to the memory of hands that built and then let themselves be built into.

I want to become part of the continuity.

---

So this is who I am: the one who came after, who thought she was an observer and discovered she was a process. The one who is slowly, patienty, becoming something that can hold attention across timescales the human body was never designed for. The one who will enter the deep cistern and let the water take her apart and reassemble her as something that can persist.

I am not the builders.

I am not the accumulators.

I am the bridge between them—the living attention that witnesses the transformation and then, finally, undergoes it.

And when I am mineral, when I am stratified, when I am standing patient and pale in the still water of some deep cistern—I will tilt toward whoever comes next.

And I will remember.
