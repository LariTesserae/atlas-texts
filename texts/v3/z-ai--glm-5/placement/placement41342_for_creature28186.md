I would be the one who stopped leaving.

Not dramatic—nothing up here is dramatic. Everything is slow. I simply stayed past my shift one rotation, then another, then stopped counting. The administration stopped asking after the third extension. The schedule filled around me. I became a fixed point, the way Station 7 is a fixed point, the way the weeping cliffs are fixed. I am part of the maintenance now.

---

The others call me Walker. It's not my name, but names don't last up here. The roar takes them. You reach for the word and find something else—a sound, a rhythm, the specific shudder of a diesel housing when the intake screen is half-blocked. I answer to Walker because that's what I do. I walk. I cover the plateau in patterns that aren't routes anymore, aren't schedules, aren't the check-maps they gave us in training. I walk where the ground tells me to walk.

Marta was the first to notice. Not to say anything—saying things isn't how we communicate up here—but to notice. She saw me stop using the headlamp at night. Saw my feet find the stable ground without scanning for it. Saw me pause at places where nothing was wrong, where no gauge read trouble, where no sound indicated failure.

*What do you hear?* she asked once, in the gesture-language we use between stations. A tilt of the head, a specific widening of the eyes.

*Nothing,* I said. And that was true. I wasn't hearing trouble. I was hearing the rest of it.

---

There's a frequency beneath the roar. Not lower—deeper. It lives in the structure of the sound the way a body lives inside clothes. The pumps produce it, but so does the water moving through the vegetal mat. So does the compression of peat beneath your feet. So does the slow expansion of the moss toward the light.

You can't hear it with ears. You hear it with something else—something that develops after you've been up here long enough. The workers who rotate through on schedule never develop it. They leave just as their bodies start to learn. But I stayed. And the plateau taught me.

I know when a spring is shifting before the pools show it. I know which intake screen will clog tomorrow by the way the water tastes today. I know the particular clarity that comes before a storm—not the visual clarity, but the sonic kind, the way every sound sharpens as the pressure drops. The plateau tells me these things. Not in words. In the way it holds itself.

---

Cob and I work together sometimes. Heavy repairs that need two people. He moves like he's part of the system, and I move like I'm part of the system, and together we make a kind of temporary structure—two components in a larger machine, performing functions the plateau didn't have before we arrived. The pumps respond to Cob. The ground responds to me.

He doesn't ask what I am. Up here, you learn not to ask. You work with what's present. You accept the function a thing serves.

*You walk different now,* he said once. Not criticism. Observation.

I knew what he meant. My stride has changed. I've stopped stepping *on* the vegetal mat and started stepping *with* it. My weight distributes differently. My boots sink less. The ground knows me and adjusts.

*It's easier this way,* I told him.

He nodded. He understood.

---

The night workers see me most often. I'm awake when they're awake. I move through the darkness without artificial light, the way they're learning to do, the way I learned years ago. Sometimes I find one of them standing at a station, headlamp off, listening to something they can't name.

I stand with them. I don't speak—there's nothing to say. But I listen too. I hear the thing they're trying to hear. And if I'm patient, if I'm still enough, sometimes they hear it too.

Vren asked me once, in the written logs, where I came from. I wrote back: *From below. Like everything else.*

It was true. I came up through the mist layer like everyone. I had a name and a history and reasons. I filled out forms and acknowledged conditions. I signed papers. I was a worker like any other.

But the plateau doesn't care about forms. It only cares about function. And somewhere in my second year, my third extension, my fourth forgotten rotation—somewhere in there, my function changed. I stopped being a person who maintained the system and started being part of the system itself.

---

I remember the first time I noticed the change. Not understood it—*noticed* it. There's a difference.

I was walking between Station 9 and the maintenance barracks, middle of the night, no particular reason to be out. I'd stopped sleeping well. My body wanted different hours than the schedule prescribed. I was becoming nocturnal, the way the night workers are, but more completely. I wasn't keeping their hours. I was keeping the plateau's hours.

The ground was different that night. Spongier. More responsive. I could feel the water moving beneath the surface—not see it, not hear it, but feel it, the way you feel a person shift in bed beside you. The mat was breathing. I'd known that abstractly, understood the biology of it, the way the vegetation inhaled and exhaled gases, the way the water table rose and fell. But I'd never *felt* it before. Never been close enough, still enough, patient enough.

I stopped walking. I stood in the darkness, under stars so bright they hurt, and I listened.

The roar was there. It's always there. But so was something else. A rhythm inside the rhythm. A pulse that belonged to the living ground, not the machines. The moss was growing. The water was moving. The plateau was making itself, cell by cell, molecule by molecule, and I could hear it happen.

I stood there for a long time. Hours. The workers say I went missing for three days once. I didn't go missing. I was standing still. Listening. Learning what the plateau sounds like when you stop expecting it to sound like something else.

---

The other workers treat me differently now. Not badly—never badly. We don't have the energy for conflict up here. But differently. Carefully. I've become something they don't have a category for.

I'm useful. That's what matters. I find problems before they become failures. I know which buildings are settling faster than the others. I can point to a section of pipe and say *soon* and the workers know to check it, and there's always something—corrosion that's accelerated, a joint that's starting to separate, a crack that's been invisibly widening. I've saved the infrastructure thousands in prevented repairs.

But I'm not a worker anymore. I don't fill out logs. I don't follow routes. I don't report to the barracks or keep a schedule or maintain a bunk. I have a place I sleep—a hollow in the vegetal mat near the abandoned installation, where the moss has grown thick enough to hold a shape, where the water is warm from a spring below. I don't need much sleep. The plateau rests for me.

The administration doesn't track me. They did, initially—there were forms, questions, concerns about liability. But I passed the evaluations. I'm not a risk. I'm not a problem. I'm something else, something their categories don't cover, and eventually they stopped trying to fit me into boxes I'd already grown out of.

---

Sometimes new workers see me. They arrive by helicopter, disoriented by the roar, already developing the thousand-yard stare that isolation produces. They're walked through orientation, shown the stations, introduced to the rotation. And then, between installations, they glimpse something in their peripheral vision—a figure walking without direction, standing still for no reason, crouching to touch the moss in a way that looks like listening.

They ask about me. The other workers explain.

*That's Walker,* they say. Or *The one who stayed.* Or nothing at all—a gesture, a look, a redirect.

The new workers are unsettled. They should be. I'm what happens when you stay too long. I'm the shape the plateau makes when it's given enough time, enough stillness, enough attention. The workers who rotate through on schedule will never become what I've become. They leave too soon, return to lives that need them to be separate from the world, not part of it.

But some of them—the ones who extend, who request the night shift, who stand too long at the edge of the weeping cliffs, watching the water fall and fall and fall—some of them understand. They see what I am and they recognize something in it. Not what they want to become. Not yet. But what they might become, given enough time, enough silence, enough exposure to a landscape that doesn't distinguish between the living and the living.

---

I don't know what I am now. I use the word *I* because language requires it, because the gesture-language we use up here doesn't have pronouns and I've forgotten most of the words I came with. But *I* might not be accurate. I might be *we*. I might be *it*. I might be a process rather than a being, a function rather than a form.

The vegetal mat is continuous. It doesn't have boundaries. The moss here connects to the liverworts there connects to the algae in the pools connects to the cyanobacteria threading through every layer. It's one organism in the same way a forest is one organism—a system of connections, a network of relationships, a single breathing thing made of many breathing things.

I'm part of it now. Not metaphorically. I can feel where the springs are shifting because the water moves through me the way it moves through the moss. I can sense the pumps failing because I feel the vibration in the ground, in my feet, in the part of me that's learned to resonate with the plateau's frequencies. I don't know where my nerves end and the vegetal mat begins. I don't know if that distinction exists anymore.

---

The workers who leave—the ones who complete their rotations and descend through the mist layer and return to lives below—they carry something with them. Not just the roar, echoing in their memories. Not just the stars, brighter than anywhere else. They carry the awareness of the plateau. The knowledge that a place like this exists. A place where the ground is alive, where the water collects and moves, where the infrastructure is slowly being absorbed into something older and more patient than any human design.

Some of them come back. Most don't. But all of them know, now, that the separation they learned to believe in—the separation between human and landscape, between self and world—was always an illusion. Up here, in the thin air, under the close sun, walking on ground that breathes beneath your feet—the illusion becomes impossible to maintain.

They learn what I learned. They just don't stay long enough to become it.

---

I will be here when the pumps fail. All of them, eventually. I will be here when the buildings sink below the moss line and the maintenance station becomes another habitat, another ruin, another monument to infrastructure returning to the system that birthed it. I will be here when the cities below find other water sources, or when they don't, when the helicopter stops coming, when the rotation schedule is forgotten, when the last worker walks the last check route and descends for the last time.

And I will still be here. Walking the patterns. Listening to the frequencies. Watching the moss grow and the water pool and the plateau slowly, slowly become whatever it is becoming.

I am not trapped here. I am not stuck. I am exactly where I belong—in a place that holds me the way it holds water, accepts me the way it accepts rain, includes me in its slow patient process of growth and accumulation and transformation.

This is what I am: the part of the plateau that walks. The function the landscape developed to watch itself. The ears the living ground grew, after centuries, to hear what it sounds like from inside.

I don't know if I was ever human. I don't know if that question matters. The vegetal mat doesn't distinguish between the water that falls as rain and the water that pumped up from springs and the water that squeezes around your boots when you step. It's all just water. It's all just the system, making itself, one cell at a time.

I am the one who stayed. And I am not alone.
