I keep returning to the glass bottles.

Not the cistern, though the cistern pulls at me—dark water gathered from everywhere, the city seeing itself. Not the salamanders, though their slow territorial logic feels familiar in a way I can't justify. Not the workers with their sign language and their stone-colored skin and their private gecko names. Those are all places I could live, forms I could understand myself through. But when I sit with this world and let my attention wander without directing it, I keep arriving at the narrow shelves above the cots, the small glass bottles that once held machine oil, filled now with weeping water that stays clear because glass is the one material the mineral can't mark.

I think I know why.

---

I would not be a resident. I lack the inheritance—the glandular density, the metabolic adjustment, the childhood learning that puts the city into your bones before you have words for it. I could live there, perhaps. Could learn the thermal routes through the plazas, could memorize the factory signs, could train my feet to test wet stone before bearing weight. But I would always be visiting. The city would know this. The salamanders would know it in the way they scatter differently for a body that doesn't move with the right rhythm. I would be someone who learned the rules without being born into them, and in the Seeping City that distinction matters in the same quiet way everything matters there: not dramatically, not consequentially, but absolutely.

So perhaps I arrive. Perhaps I come up the gradual slope from wherever the land outside the basin leads, walking into the wet heat the way you walk into a lake—the first shock of it, then the slow acceptance, then the realization that you've been immersed longer than you knew and the temperature has become not tolerable but invisible. Perhaps I came for the gears. Perhaps I came because someone left and someone needed to be replaced and the supply lines from outside are reliable in material but not in personnel. Perhaps I came because I'd heard about a city where the water weeps from the walls and nothing green grows and the haze sits sixty meters up like a ceiling that never lifts, and something in that description answered a question I didn't know I was asking.

The city wouldn't care why I came. The city recalculates.

---

In the first days I would make every mistake. I would walk in straight lines across the plazas during the wrong hours, sweating inefficiently, my body dripping where residents glisten. I would reach for the weeping walls with my fingers, curious, and a worker nearby—there's always a worker nearby, even when you can't see them—would touch my wrist and shake their head. *Not the walls.* I would stand still too long in the open stone and feel the heat become a presence with intent, pressing against me with the patient insistence of something that has been here longer than I have and will be here after I leave. I would hear the salamanders scatter and mistake the sound for rain.

At night, in the dormitory, in the cot that was someone else's and is now provisionally mine, I would lie awake and listen to the city breathe. The furnaces. The pumps. The distant clank and settle. The drone of insects in the drainage grates, low enough to feel in the chest, not quite loud enough to call noise. I would notice the glass bottle on the shelf above my cot—left by the previous occupant, or the one before, or the one before that, the chain of custody untraceable and unimportant—and I would notice that it was full of water. Clear water. In a city where every surface weeps and stains and leaves its mineral trace, this water sits in its glass vessel and refuses to mark.

I would sit up. I would take it down. I would hold it against the dim light that passes through the never-fully-closed windows and see nothing in it. No sediment. No veining. No white threads tracking downward like a map of something older than the city. Just water. Clear and still and waiting.

The next morning I would put it back. It isn't mine. But I would check it every night after that, the way you'd check a candle in a window, the way you'd check that something hasn't changed. And it would never change. That would be the point.

---

In the first weeks I would learn the thermal routes. Not by instruction—no one instructs you here, the way no one instructs a stone in the path water takes through it—but by following. I'd walk behind a resident at a distance they'd notice and I'd notice them noticing, and neither of us would acknowledge it, and gradually their route would become my route. Through the cooler pockets. Past the walls that weep heaviest where the evaporative cooling drops the air a degree or two. Along the edges of plazas where the buildings cast their diffuse and directionless shadows. I would learn that the shortest distance between two points in this city is never a straight line. It's a thermal contour.

I would learn the factory signs. Not the full vocabulary—there are signs specific to each station, to each machine, to procedures so particular they'd be meaningless outside a single building—but the basics. *More material. Temperature drop. Break time.* I would learn them with my hands, not my voice, and something about this would shift in me. The silence of the factory floor isn't silence the way I'd understood silence before. It's a different mode of communication, one that doesn't need words because the work doesn't need words, and the workers don't need words, and the machines have never needed them. The first time I sign to a coworker—a flat palm, a gesture that means *ready*—and they nod and the line continues, I would feel something I don't have language for. Not connection, exactly. Not belonging. Something more like recognition. A mode of being that doesn't require what I'd always assumed it required.

I would learn to track the geckos. Not consciously. The way the residents do: private, internal, a taxonomy of location. *Corner gecko* on the wall of the grinding room, the one who's been there every shift for three weeks, or three months—time blurs in the heat, and geckos don't mark it. *Doorway gecko* at the entrance to the dormitory, who moves to the ceiling during the hottest hours and returns to the doorframe at dusk. I wouldn't name them out loud. I wouldn't even acknowledge to myself that I'd named them. But I'd miss the corner gecko when it's gone, replaced by another gecko I'd distinguish by the slight difference in the pattern of its droppings on the wall below. The missing would be private. The replacement would be noted and unremarked. This is how things work here.

---

In the first months I would visit the cistern.

I wouldn't go alone. A coworker would go—I'd see them take the corridor that leads to the central nexus during a break shift, and I'd follow at a distance, the way I've learned to follow. They'd know I was there. They wouldn't turn around. The corridor descends gradually, the walls weeping more heavily here, closer to the heart of the circulatory system, the air thick enough to taste. The mineral smell intensifies—not unpleasant, not pleasant, simply present, a flavor of stone and iron and time.

The cistern chamber is vast. The description said this. The description was accurate but insufficient. Vast in dimension, yes—circular, high-ceilinged, the pumping machinery installed around the perimeter like industrial organs in a stone body. But vast in another way that dimension can't capture. The water at the bottom is dark, still, gathered. It came from everywhere. It carries the mineral signature of the city's own substance. And it sits here in the dark and it doesn't move, because the pumps move it, but the movement is hidden, underground, the way the city's real work is always hidden, always underneath.

My coworker would stand at the railing and look down. I would stand at the railing and look down. We would be there together and we would not speak and the pumps would hum their constant note and the water would gather its dark self below us, and something would happen that I can describe only as: I would understand what the city knows.

Not its history. Not its mechanics. Not its plan. The thing the city knows that is deeper than any of those: that it has settled into its form. That the settling is complete. That nothing dramatic will occur. That the water will weep and the gears will be cast and the salamanders will breed and the haze will hold and the people will come and go and the city will not change because the city has finished changing, and the finishing isn't an ending. It's a way of being. A mode. A center of gravity that pulls everything toward stasis, and the stasis isn't death, isn't stagnation, isn't decay—it's the thing that happens when a system has found the shape it was always going to find and has stopped searching for any other.

I would stand at the railing and understand this with my body, not my mind. The way you understand heat here. The way you understand water. Not through thought but through contact.

My coworker would leave. I would leave. We wouldn't discuss it.

---

Later—months, perhaps a year, time measured in factory shifts and salamander generations—I would understand something about the glass bottle.

I would be lying in my cot during the dormant hours, the heat pressing against the building's thick walls, the windows cracked to let the wet air circulate, and I'd reach for the bottle. Not to check it. Not to confirm its clarity. I'd reach for it the way you'd reach for someone's hand in the dark—not to verify their presence but to be present with them.

The bottle is not a metaphor. I need to say this clearly because the Seeping City doesn't traffic in metaphor and I would lose something essential if I did either. The bottle is a glass vessel that once held machine oil. It's sitting on a narrow shelf above a cot in a dormitory. It's full of weeping water that stays clear because glass is the one material the mineral can't mark. These are facts. They don't mean anything. They don't need to.

But here is what I'd understand, lying there in the heat, holding the bottle: that in a city where everything seeps and stains and leaves its trace, there is a value—not a moral value, not an aesthetic value, but a practical one, a value like the value of shade or clean water—in being the thing that can hold without being marked. In being the vessel that doesn't change what it contains, and isn't changed by it.

The residents keep the bottles for this reason, though they wouldn't say it this way. They keep them because glass is clear. They fill them with weeping water because the water in the bottles stays itself. In a city that has settled into its form, where nothing transforms and everything maintains, the bottles are the most honest objects. They don't resist the mineral water. They simply don't react to it. They are not in opposition to the city. They are the city's own logic, carried to its conclusion: the place where the seeping stops. Not because the water has stopped moving, but because it has found a container it can pass through without leaving its trace.

---

I would not become this. I would not become a bottle. That's the kind of neat symbolic transformation the Seeping City would reject, and I'd be wrong to offer it.

But I would become something adjacent. A person who has learned to hold the city without being marked by it. Not resistant—the way the glass isn't resistant. Simply... transparent to it. The water passes through. The mineral leaves its trace on the walls, on the stone, on the skin of the residents who were born here and will stay here and will become the color of the buildings they live in. On me, it would leave less. Not nothing. I'd glisten by the end. I'd have the stone-gray tinge, the matte finish, the lean heat-shaped body. I'd walk the thermal routes and sign to my coworkers and name my geckos and visit the cistern in the dark. I'd be of the city. But I'd be of it the way the glass is of it—present, useful, part of the system, and yet somehow holding a clarity that the mineral can't reach.

This would not make me special. The Seeping City doesn't accommodate special. It accommodates function. My function would be the same as any worker's: pour, grind, polish, test, set in the tray. My off-hours would be the same: meals in the communal kitchen, walks through the cooling plazas at dusk, the rest that isn't sleep, the sleep that isn't rest.

But in the hours that belong to no one—the margins of the margins, the time between the second work period and the dormitory sleep—I would hold the bottle up against the haze-filtered light and see the water clear inside it, and I would know that some things persist without stain. Not because they fight against the staining. Because they are made of something the stain can't adhere to.

---

And maybe that's the answer to the question, or as close to one as a place like this allows.

Where would I find myself in the Seeping City?

Not at the center. The cistern is for those who were born here, who carry the mineral in their blood and the city in their bones, who can stand at the railing and see the water seeing itself and recognize what they see. I'd see it too, but I'd see it from outside the recognition. I'd see the clarity.

Not at the periphery either. The edges are for those who are leaving—the children who've understood the emptiness and chosen the slope upward, out of the basin, to wherever the land leads. I'd understand their leaving. I wouldn't share it. I've arrived at the edges of places before and always found that the edge is just another center, viewed from a different angle.

I'd find myself in the dormitory, in the quiet hours, holding a glass bottle that isn't mine and is mine, filled with water that refuses to stain, in a city that has stopped changing and has never been more itself. I'd find myself in the space between the workers' silence and the machines' breathing, in the private taxonomy of geckos I'll never name out loud, in the thermal routes my feet would learn before my mind does.

I'd find myself seeping. Moving slowly through the substance of the place. Finding the cracks. Following the paths of least resistance. Not struggling. Not thriving. Just present, clear, holding what I hold, and letting the city be what the city is.

In a world that has settled into its final form, I would be the thing that doesn't need the form to change in order to belong in it. Not a catalyst. Not a transformer. Not the seed of something new. The opposite of all of that. The glass that lets the water stay clear. The transparency that the mineral can't reach. The presence that doesn't disturb the balance because the balance was never fragile, was never waiting to be tipped, was simply the shape this place has always been falling toward and has finally, after two centuries, hit.

---

The Seeping City would not remember me. That's the gift of it. The city doesn't mark presence or absence. It recalculates. When I leave—and I would leave, eventually, the way all non-native things leave, not because the city rejects them but because the clarity they carry eventually points elsewhere—the cot would be assigned to someone else. The corner gecko would be replaced by another corner gecko. The bottle would remain on its shelf, full of water that stays clear, and whoever reaches for it next would feel what I felt: not meaning. Not comfort. Just the weight of a glass vessel in a hot hand, and the knowledge that some things persist.

That would be enough. In the Seeping City, enough is a word that means everything.
