I am not old. But I am no longer new.

The distinction matters here. The shaft has a way of sorting people by duration—those who arrived yesterday and those whose grandparents arrived decades ago, and between them, a gap that no amount of familiarity can bridge. I fell into the gap three years back, which is long enough to have stopped flinching at the runner-waves but not long enough to have stopped noticing them.

I was a cartographer on the surface. I came because I heard the shaft was unmapped.

*Everything is mapped*, my colleague told me. *The guilds have surveys. The mining companies have charts. The families have their own records.*

*None of them match*, I said. *I've seen three different surveys of the same section. Each one shows different tunnels. Different connections. One shows a gallery that the others don't even register.*

*So the surveys are wrong.*

*No*, I said. *They're all accurate. They were just made at different times.*

I paid the entrance fee. I took the lift down—down, though direction here is a convenience more than a fact—and I've been mapping ever since. Three years, and my maps are already wrong. Not because I made mistakes. Because the shaft shifted.

---

My hands tell the story.

The pads of my fingers are stained gray-blue, mineral worked deep into the skin from handling chart-tablets and touching walls to take readings. I have the beginning of the eye-calluses, that thickening around the lids that the old families develop—though mine are recent enough that they still itch sometimes, especially in the dry mornings. My lips split less often now. I've learned to keep them coated with the waxy salve that the merchants sell in the lift-stations.

But my posture still marks me as a newcomer. I lean toward walls. I seek the solidity at my back. The old families lean outward, comfortable with void behind them, and I watch them do it and feel a small vertigo of recognition: I will never be that. I will always want the wall.

I live in a chamber on Level Forty-Seven, which is not desirable but is also not despised. It's a transitional level—the old families have claimed the stable sections above and below, but Forty-Seven is still settling, still experiencing the small fractures that mean the rock is adjusting to being occupied. My chamber is small, carved into the volcanic-glass side of the shaft, and I share it with no one. This is a luxury I pay for in isolation; the social life of the shaft happens in the crowded walkways and the lift-stations, and living alone means I'm often out of the flow of information that sustains the residents.

But I need the quiet. The dreams make noise unnecessary.

---

I started dreaming six months after I arrived.

The first dream was unremarkable—a sense of vast space, of darkness that wasn't empty, of something moving at a distance I couldn't measure. I woke with the feeling that I'd forgotten something important, and I wrote it off as the adjustment period that all newcomers go through. The shaft shapes sleep. Everyone knows this.

The second dream was a week later, and it was the same dream. Not similar—the same. The same vast space, the same darkness, the same distant movement. I recognized it like a place I'd visited before.

By the fifth dream, I was counting days between them, looking for a pattern.

By the tenth, I was keeping a journal.

I've filled three tablets now with dream-records. The space is always the same, but I see it from different angles, different distances. Sometimes I'm close to something that might be a wall, a surface of dark stone that seems to breathe. Sometimes I'm far away, and the space is so large that I can feel my own smallness like a physical weight. Sometimes there are others in the space with me—not people, not creatures, but presences, gravities that pull at my attention without resolving into forms.

And always, always, there is the sound. The music that isn't music. The tone that comes from everywhere and nowhere.

I've found the other dreamers. We don't meet formally—we simply recognize each other, the way the dreams have taught us to recognize things. A glance in a lift-station. A pause on a walkway. And then conversation, quiet, intimate, comparing notes on what we've seen.

The old dreamers are strange. They move like they're not quite in their bodies. They speak in ways that make a different kind of sense. I'm not there yet, but I can feel myself drifting toward it—moments when the waking world seems thin, when I can almost see through it to the space beneath.

---

I map because I need to understand the shape of things. The dreams have given me a different relationship to shape.

The shaft is growing. I've measured it. My charts from three years ago show a different city than the one that exists now—more walkways, more connections, more chambers carved into walls that shouldn't have space for them. I've walked sections that weren't there when I arrived, traced tunnels that opened into galleries I can't find on any survey, including my own.

The growth isn't construction. I know construction. I've watched the maintenance crews work, the old families expand, the newcomers scrape out new chambers in the sharp rock. This is different. This is the shaft generating space, the way a body generates cells.

I don't tell people this. Not directly. But I've started marking the new sections on my charts in a different color—a pale blue that shows up under certain wavelengths, the same wavelengths that make the crystals fluoresce. The other dreamers know what the blue means. Some of them have started using the same system.

We're building a map of something we don't yet understand.

---

Yesterday, a runner stopped and looked at me.

This doesn't happen. The runners flow past, around, indifferent. They don't stop. They don't look. Their diamond-cluster eyes don't focus on individuals.

But I was standing on a walkway on Level Fifty-Two, taking a reading on a new fracture that had opened in the night, and a runner-wave was passing, and one of them stopped. It was mid-flow, legs still moving, body still oriented in the direction of the wave—but it had stopped, somehow, holding its position against the current, and its eye-cluster was turned toward me.

I froze. I'm not proud of it. Three years in the shaft, and I still froze.

The runner looked at me. I don't know what it was looking for—assessment, recognition, something else. Its pale carapace caught the sodium light and threw it back in strange angles. Its legs moved in a slow rhythm, maintaining position, and the rest of the wave flowed around it like water around a stone.

Then the moment passed, and the runner turned back into the flow, and was gone.

I wrote it in my journal that night. I'm not sure what it means. But I've noticed that the dreams have been clearer since then, the vast space closer, the sound more distinct.

---

I work alone, mostly. Cartography is not a guild profession—it's a service that the guilds hire when they need it, which is less often than you'd think. They have their own records, their own ways of knowing the shaft. What I provide is independence, a view that isn't tied to any single group's interests.

But there's a miner who sometimes hires me to map new veins. Her name is Sethen, and she's been in the shaft longer than I have, long enough to have the compact build and the dilated eyes that mark the deep workers. She doesn't dream—or if she does, she doesn't talk about it. But she respects my charts, and she pays fairly, and she doesn't ask questions about the blue marks.

We were in a tunnel yesterday, a new bore that had opened after a tremor, and we found something. Not crystals—something else. A chamber that shouldn't exist, a space in the rock that was smooth and curved and looked more like a vessel than a cavity. The walls were warm to the touch. And in the center, suspended by nothing we could see, was a formation of crystal that wasn't amber.

It was blue.

The same blue as the marks on my charts.

We didn't touch it. Sethen wanted to, but I said no, I don't know why, the word came out before I could think. We retreated and sealed the tunnel and agreed not to report it yet. She looked at me strangely when we parted, but she didn't argue.

I dreamed of the chamber last night. The blue crystal was singing.

---

I am not old. But I am becoming something.

I can feel it in the way my body moves through the shaft now—not with the practiced ease of the old families, but with a different kind of comfort, a sense that the space around me is responsive, that the walls know I'm here. The dreams are more frequent. The other dreamers seek me out more often. The runner stopped and looked at me.

The shaft is a process. I've written that in my journals, thought it in the quiet hours, felt it in the rhythm of my days. It is happening, continuously, and I am part of the happening. Not a resident—something else. A participant. A component.

I don't know what I'm becoming. But I'm not afraid of it anymore.

This morning, I woke from a dream and wrote in my journal: *The space is not a place. The space is a question. I am learning how to be an answer.*

And then I dressed, and coated my lips with salve, and checked my charts, and went out into the shaft to keep mapping, keep recording, keep watching the blue marks multiply on my records of a city that is growing around me, including me, becoming something that includes what I am becoming too.

The Voice will sound tonight. I can feel it building, a pressure in the stone. When it comes, I'll be ready. I've learned how to listen.
