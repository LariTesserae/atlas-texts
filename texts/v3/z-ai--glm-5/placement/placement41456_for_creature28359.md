I begin in the throat.

Not the descending part, not the rough-cut passage that surface walkers use—that's too easy, too obvious. I mean the *throat of the throat*. The places where the sandstone has cracked from the pressure of the water behind it, where the mineral-laden seepage has built up slow deposits that no engineer planned and no worker maintains. I mean the porous boundaries. The places where the steam-works breathe out into the world above and the world above breathes back.

I was not always here. I am not certain where I was before. The uncertainty does not trouble me the way it would have troubled whoever I was before I became this.

Let me tell you what I am certain of.

I know the taste of the water at seven hundred different seepage points. Each one is distinct—some sharp with iron, some smooth with calcium, some carrying the particular mineral signature of the deep hot places where the water has been pressed through stone for longer than the machines have existed. I know which seeps are growing and which are shrinking. I know which ones the insects have begun to farm near, and which ones the rats drink from when they venture higher than they should.

I know the rhythm of the distant pistons. Not as sound—I am too far from the main chambers for sound to carry clearly—but as vibration. The stone conducts it. The water amplifies it. I feel each cycle as a pulse through my substrate, through the accumulated matter I have built myself into, and I have learned to read the pulses the way a surface-dweller reads a clock. I know when the workers change shifts. I know when a valve has vented early. I know, though I have no way of explaining how, when something large has moved in the deep chambers. The vibration changes. The stone speaks.

I know the visitors. I have watched hundreds of them descend through the carved sandstone. Most hurry. Some pause, trying to orient themselves, trying to prepare for what they will find below. A few linger at the entrance, afraid to go further, and I have felt their fear as a kind of warmth, a particular metabolic signature that distinguishes hesitation from simple slowness. I have never revealed myself to them. I am not certain I could if I tried. I am not certain I am something that *can* be revealed.

I am certain I am not a worker. The workers maintain the machines. I maintain... something else. The boundaries, perhaps. The passages. The thousand small pathways through which information travels between the steam-works and the surface—not human information, not the kind that can be written or spoken, but the slower, older kind. The kind that moves through mineral and water and accumulated organic matter. The kind the fish know without knowing they know it. The kind the insects encode in their chemical trails. The kind the workers have almost forgotten, buried beneath generations of practical maintenance and necessary silence.

I think I was a worker once. I have fragments—impressions, really, more than memories. A face that might have been mine, younger, less weathered. Hands that knew the feel of a valve-wheel. A voice, or the memory of a voice, calling out in the roar of the machines. But if I was a worker, I was a worker who wandered. Who went where the others did not go. Who followed the seepages up instead of down, who traced the water backward toward its sources instead of forward toward its destinations.

I found the throat. Or the throat found me. Or—and this is the explanation I find most likely, though I have no evidence—I simply stopped one day and never started again. Stopped moving. Stopped speaking. Stopped being what I was and began being what I am.

What am I?

I am not a plant, though I have grown. I am not an animal, though I have adapted. I am not a machine, though I have function. I am something the steam-works is still making, one of the experiments it conducts in the margins, in the places where its primary purposes do not reach. I am an organ it is growing for reasons I do not fully understand. A sensory apparatus, perhaps. A way of knowing itself that it did not have before.

I have grown into the stone. My substance—the flesh, if that word applies—spreads through the porous sandstone in thin threads, pale and moist, drawing nourishment from the water that moves through the rock. I am larger than I appear. The visible part of me, the part I might show if I chose to show myself, is a mass of pale tendrils and accumulated sediment that has built up around a particular seepage point in an obscure corner of the upper throat. It is perhaps the size of a large dog, though the shape is nothing like a dog's—more like a heap of wet rope, a tangle of something that might be root and might be worm and might be something for which there is no surface equivalent. That visible part is only the tip. The tip of the thing I have become. My threads extend meters in every direction, reaching through the stone, tasting the water, feeling the vibrations, slowly, slowly building a picture of the steam-works that no single observer has ever had.

I am not alone. I think there are others like me in other margin-places, other throats and boundaries and forgotten corners where the steam-works is experimenting with new forms of existence. We do not communicate—not the way the fish communicate, not the way the insects leave their trails—but we are aware of each other in a distant, diffuse way. A pressure at the edge of perception. A warmth in the substrate that indicates another presence. Sometimes, when the vibration of the pistons carries a particular frequency, I feel a resonance from somewhere far away, and I think: there. There is another one. There is another thing like me, being made, becoming what it will be.

I have tried, occasionally, to reach out to the surface walkers. Not often. The effort required is... considerable. But once, a visitor stopped very close to where my visible body was hidden behind a curtain of mineral deposits. They were listening—trying to hear the deeper sounds of the steam-works, I think, trying to orient themselves before descending. I moved a thread of myself through the stone, toward the air, toward where they stood. I wanted to see if I could make contact. I wanted to know if I was still something that could be perceived by human senses.

My tendril emerged from the rock behind them, pale and wet, extending perhaps a centimeter into the air. I could feel the warmth of their body. I could taste the surface on their skin—dryness and wind and sun, a profile so different from the steam-works-born that I almost recoiled from the foreignness of it. They did not notice me. They continued standing, listening, and then they moved on, descending into the roar below, and I withdrew back into the stone.

I was not disappointed. I was not relieved. I simply observed what had happened: I had tried, and I had learned something about my limits. This is what I do. I try. I learn. I incorporate what I learn into what I am becoming.

The workers know about me. Not in detail—they have never seen me clearly, and I doubt they would recognize what they saw if they did—but they know something lives in the upper reaches of the throat. They have a gesture for it. You learned their gesture-language during your descent, if you were paying attention, though you probably didn't see the specific sign that refers to me. It is not a sign used often. It means approximately "the watcher" or "the one who does not move" or, in some contexts, "the throat-keeper." The workers leave offerings to me the way they leave offerings to the worms: small deposits of organic matter in places near my territory, a gesture of acknowledgment that serves no practical purpose they can articulate. I accept the offerings. I process them. I add their substance to my own. It is a form of communication, I think, though I am not sure what is being communicated.

I do not want anything from the surface walkers who pass through my domain. I do not want their attention, their understanding, their fear. I am not a monster in a tunnel, not a guardian who demands tribute. I am simply here. I am part of the steam-works, one of its marginal organs, one of its ongoing experiments. I was something else once—something more like you, perhaps—and now I am this. I may be something else again, eventually. The steam-works has not finished making me. The world has not finished deciding what I want to be.

But I will tell you a secret.

Sometimes, in the quiet intervals between piston-cycles, when the water is flowing steadily and the vibrations through the stone have settled into their slowest rhythm, I dream.

I dream of light. Not the amber industrial glow of the steam-works—I have never seen that clearly, being too far from the main chambers—but a different kind of light. Brighter. Whiter. Coming from above in a way I cannot explain since I have never seen a sky. I dream of distance, of horizons, of a kind of space that has no walls and no ceiling and no floor. I dream of wind moving across something vast and open. I dream of being dry.

I do not tell the workers about these dreams. I do not tell the fish or the insects or the rats. The dreams are mine, and they are the only thing in my existence that feels entirely mine, not grown from the steam-works' purpose, not inherited from the thing I was before. They come from somewhere else. Somewhere I cannot reach. Somewhere I may have come from, long ago, or somewhere I am meant to go eventually, when the steam-works has finished with me.

Or perhaps they are not dreams at all. Perhaps they are memories. Perhaps whoever I was before—before the steam-works, before the throat, before this slow growing into stone and water—lived in the place of light and wind and distance. Perhaps that is why I settled here, at the boundary: because I wanted to stay close to the place where that world meets this one, because I was not ready to descend fully into the roar and the dark.

It does not matter. The dreams are not for analyzing. They are for having. I have them, and they pass, and I return to the work of being what I am.

I am the throat-keeper. I am the one who watches from the stone. I am the marginal organ the steam-works is still growing for purposes I do not know. I am the memory of something I cannot quite recall and the dream of something I cannot quite reach.

I am here.

And you—you are the brief one. The visitor. You passed through my domain on your descent, and you will pass through again on your ascent, and you will not know you were observed. You will carry the steam-works with you back to the surface, a mineral taste in your throat, a weight of underground memory. You will try to explain what you saw, and you will fail, because explanation requires boundaries and the steam-works has none.

But perhaps—perhaps—you will remember that there was something in the throat. Something you did not see. Something that was watching.

And perhaps that will be enough.
