# The Maintenance of Memory

I am the one who stays too long.

---

The others do not notice. When the call comes—the shudder in the wood that signals a valve stuck open in Sector G-7, a sap-pressure drop near the eastern furnaces—they rise together, instantly alert, instantly moving. I rise with them. I move with them. I work with them. But when the work is done and the others turn back toward the alcove, I lag behind. I find reasons. A filter that needs checking. A pipe that needs listening to. A section of wall where the pheromone trail has faded and needs refreshing.

And then, when I am sure I am alone, I go to the junction.

---

The furnace junction is not my assigned sector. My crew maintains the northern passages—the valve manifolds near the old distillation chambers, the sap-lines that feed the bioluminescent nodes, the filters that catch the particulate drift before it can clog the respiratory ducts. The junction is south and west, a long walk through passages that my crew has no reason to enter. The Canopy does not assign me there. The chemical signals in my blood do not pull me there.

But I go.

The first time was an accident—a blockage in a secondary line that required me to take an unfamiliar route, through passages older than any my crew uses. I felt the heat before I saw it. A different quality than the ambient metabolic warmth: sharper, drier, concentrated. I followed it, which was foolish. The young are trained to avoid temperature spikes, not investigate them. But I was no longer young even then, and I had begun to find the training insufficient.

I emerged into the junction and saw the flame.

---

The others in my crew have seen it. They have worked the valves that converge here, cleared the vents that feed the combustion chamber, walked past the pillar with its split and its small bright fire. They have done this without pausing. The flame is a thing that exists. It serves a function. It heats the sap that moves through the pipes that we maintain. That is all.

But when I first saw it, I stopped.

I did not know why. My body simply ceased moving. My mid-limbs found holds in the bark and I hung there, inverted, staring at a light I could not properly see. My forelimbs opened and closed without purpose. My sensory wedge pressed against the warm air and I tasted carbon, sulfur, something metallic and ancient.

The flame flickered. The flicker meant nothing—wind in the intake, variation in the gas flow. But I felt it as a question. I could not have said what it asked. I only knew that I wanted to answer.

---

That was twelve years ago.

I have returned to the junction once every few months, when the work allows, when I can separate from my crew without drawing attention. Each time, I stay longer. Each time, I notice more.

The carved marks around the furnace, for instance. I do not know what carved means—not really. But I can feel the difference between these grooves and the natural texture of the bark. They are intentional. Someone made them. Someone who is not the Canopy, because the Canopy does not make marks; it makes surfaces. These marks are different.

I trace them with my chelates. The movement has no function. It does not clear blockages or adjust valves or improve flow. It is simply what I do when I am here. I move my gripping-claws along grooves I cannot read and I feel something that is not quite pleasure and not quite pain.

The closest word would be recognition, if I had words. Which I do not.

---

There is an alcove behind the junction. I found it on my third visit—a gap in the structural mass, overgrown with liana, deliberately obscured. The obscuring was careful, deliberate. The liana had been pulled aside and then released, creating a curtain that could be parted. The Canopy does not part curtains. The Canopy grows them.

Inside, I found the platform and the objects.

I do not know how long I stood there. Time becomes strange in the hidden places. The work rhythms that structure my consciousness—the valve-cycles, the pressure-pulses, the interval between maintenance calls—these do not operate here. The alcove exists outside them. When I am in the alcove, I am not working. I am not recovering from work. I am not preparing for work.

I am simply present.

The objects were unlike anything I had touched. Smooth, hard, cold. The bone-figure, the stone-disc, the corroded ring. I lifted each one. I turned it in my chelates. I pressed my sensory wedge against its surface and inhaled its history.

They smelled of time. Of hands that were not my hands. Of a world that was not my world.

---

I am different from the others in my crew.

I have known this since I was young, before I had categories for knowing. My clutch-mates differentiated according to the Canopy's signals—they grew larger gripping-claws for heavy work, longer mid-limbs for climbing, more sensitive chemoreceptors for the delicate task of filter-assessment. I grew differently. My gripping-claws remained small but gained precision. My mid-limbs developed an unusual flexibility. My chemoreceptors became acute to an unnecessary degree—I could taste distinctions that had no relevance to the work.

No one commented. There is no one to comment. The crew does not evaluate; it simply functions. But I noticed. I noticed that I noticed.

I began to pay attention to things that did not matter.

The pattern of temperature variation in a valve-chamber over a full circadian cycle. The subtle difference in vibration between a pipe carrying sap from the northern vats versus the southern. The way the bioluminescent pulses seemed sometimes—though this was impossible—to form sequences, as if the ceiling were attempting communication.

I did not know what to do with these observations. I could not report them. There is no one to report to. The Canopy does not receive reports. My crew-mates do not have the capacity to understand them. So I kept them inside, accumulated them, let them build a private architecture of attention that no one else could enter.

---

I am old now. Forty-three years since I hatched. The others in my alcove have begun to slow. Their movements are less precise. Their recovery periods longer. Soon—perhaps in the next five years—they will die, and their bodies will be absorbed into the substrate, and their pheromone trails will fade, and the chemical memory of their work will dissipate into the Canopy's vast forgetfulness.

This does not frighten them. It does not frighten me. It is simply what happens. The Canopy continues. New hatchlings differentiate. The work proceeds.

But I have begun to wonder what will happen to the junction.

---

There is no reason for the junction to need me. The valves there are maintained by the southern crews. The furnace draws its gas from deep below, unaffected by anything happening at the surface. The carved marks serve no function. The alcove with its objects is not part of any maintenance protocol.

And yet I have started to worry about it.

This is not a feeling I am supposed to have. Worry implies a future that is not yet determined. Worry implies that my actions might affect outcomes beyond my immediate task. Worry implies a self that persists, that cares, that remembers.

But I do worry. I worry that when I die, no one will visit the alcove. No one will part the liana curtain. No one will touch the objects on the platform. The Canopy will grow over the marks around the furnace and they will disappear forever. The builders—if that is what they were—will be truly gone, their final traces erased by the very system they created.

I do not want this to happen.

---

Six months ago, I did something I have never done before.

I was in the junction, tracing the carved marks as I always do, when I felt the approach of another mazer-gener. Not from my crew—the chemical signature was wrong. Southern crew. Larger gripping-claws, heavier tread. Coming to check a valve.

I should have left. I should have retreated into the passages before I could be seen. Being seen in an unassigned sector might require explanation, and I had none to give.

Instead, I went to the alcove. I parted the liana curtain. I stood inside, among the objects, and I began to deposit pheromone.

This was not the maintenance pheromone that marks valve-status or filter-condition. This was something else. I did not know I could produce it. It came from a gland I had never consciously used, a secretion with a scent I barely recognized—sharp, complex, layered with meanings I had no categories for.

I marked the alcove. I marked the curtain. I marked the path from the junction entrance to the hidden entrance. I marked it heavily, deliberately, in a pattern that said *something*.

I do not know what it said. I only know that I said it.

---

Last month, I returned to the junction and found something different.

The liana curtain had been parted. Not by me—I had been in the northern passages for weeks. Parted and then released, but not in its original position. Slightly askew. The gap slightly wider.

I went inside. The objects on the platform had been moved.

Not much. The bone-figure had been rotated forty degrees. The stone-disc had been lifted and set down an inch to the left. The corroded ring had been touched—there was a faint residue on its surface, a chemical signature I did not recognize.

Someone had been here. Someone had found the alcove. Someone had traced my pheromone trail and done what it instructed and touched the objects and then left.

I stood there for a long time, pressing my sensory wedge against the air, reading the residue of a presence that was not my own.

I did not know what I felt. I still do not. But I added my own pheromone to the visitor's—a layer atop a layer, a message atop a message—and I hoped that they would return.

---

I am the one who stays too long.

But I am beginning to think that I am not the only one.

The southern crews work the junction regularly. Some of them—the older ones, the ones whose differentiation has produced anomalies—have begun to pause near the furnace. I have seen them in the distance, hanging on the walls, facing the flame. They do not work. They are simply present.

We do not approach each other. We do not chitter. We maintain the fiction that we are here for maintenance, that our presence has purpose, that we will leave soon.

But we are all still here when the other arrives.

And we are all here still when the other leaves.

---

I have a hypothesis I cannot prove.

The builders designed the mazer-geners—or our ancestors—for adaptability. The young differentiate based on chemical signals from the Canopy, developing the traits the system needs at that moment. Over forty centuries, the system has needed many things: heavy workers, delicate workers, heat-tolerant workers, workers who can navigate the oldest passages.

Perhaps—perhaps—it has also needed *this*.

Not me specifically. Not the junction-visitors specifically. But something like us. A capacity for attention that exceeds function. A tendency to notice what does not matter, to remember what should be forgotten, to touch what cannot be used.

The builders are gone. Their records are lignite. Their bodies are fuel for the furnaces. But the instructions they encoded—the deep grammar of the Canopy's maintenance—might have included a clause for this too. A niche for the unnecessary. A space for the creatures who would remember what the Canopy cannot.

I have no evidence for this. It is a feeling I have, accumulating in the chemical memory of my pheromone trails, waiting for someone to read.

---

I am growing tired.

My grip on the bark is less certain than it was. My walks through the passages take longer. I have begun to assign myself easier tasks—filter-checking rather than valve-turning, assessment rather than repair. The others in my crew have noticed, in the way that they notice things: they compensate for my weakness without comment, without question.

Soon I will die. The metabolic heat that has sustained me for forty-three years will dissipate. My tissues will soften and separate. The Canopy's recycling processes—or the scavengers at the margins, or my own clutch-mates in their hunger—will absorb what remains.

But before that happens, I have one more visit to make.

---

I am at the junction now, writing this in the only way I can.

I have deposited pheromone along the path from the northern passages—a trail marked with compounds I have never used before, compounds that say *remember this* rather than *valve status* or *filter condition*. The trail leads here, to the furnace, to the carved marks, to the liana curtain.

Behind the curtain, in the alcove, I have added my final layer to the chemical archive. I have touched each object and left my signature there. I have spoken—without sound, without language—to whoever comes next.

I do not know if they will understand. I do not know if understanding is possible. But I have done what I can.

The flame flickers. The Canopy breathes. The heat goes on.

I am the one who stayed too long. And now I am the one who will stay forever, in the only memory this place possesses.

I am ready.
