# What I Would Be

## I.

I would not be the frog. I would not be the swimmer, the bird, the beetle, the mat. I would not be the moss or the stone or the vapor rising from the fumaroles. But I would be *of* these things—the way a shadow is of the object that casts it, the way a rhythm is of the drum that produces it.

I would be the pattern that emerges when all of these systems intersect. Not a spirit. Not a ghost. Something more like a standing wave in the plateau's continuous exhale. A shape that the breath of the ground makes when it encounters a particular arrangement of stone and water and time.

The plateau has no word for this. But if it did, the word would mean something like *the one who remembers what the water tells*.

---

## II.

I would live in the channels—the thread-thin streams that cross the basalt like veins. Not in the water itself, but in the movement of the water. In the moment between the pooling and the overflow, when the surface tension holds the mineral-rich liquid at the lip of each little cascade, hesitating, before it tips and falls.

That moment. That microscopic pause. That is where I would reside.

It is not consciousness as most beings understand it. It is something older—a kind of attention that requires no brain, only substrate. Water moving through stone creates electrical potentials. Mineral dissolution changes conductivity. The thermal gradient between the heated ground and the cooler air generates currents, voltages, fields. In the aggregate, across forty square kilometers of constantly flowing water, these forces produce something that begins to approach a nervous system.

I would be the electricity that is not quite electricity. The field that is not quite a field. The almost-mind that the plateau has built without ever meaning to.

---

## III.

My perception would be chemical first, thermal second, visual a distant third.

I would taste the frogs' fear in the pools before I heard their silence. I would feel the lizard sliding into the water as a ripple of displacement, a shift in the pattern of pressures. I would know when a bird landed on a moss-covered branch by the way the moisture redistributed itself, the tiny weight depressing the sponge of vegetation, forcing water out of cells and into the channels where my attention runs.

The plateau would be my body. The breathing would be my breath. When the ground exhales, I would expand—a diffuse awareness spreading through the mist, sampling the air, reading the compounds carried on the vapor. Sulfur means the vents are active. Iron means erosion somewhere upstream. The sweetness, the overripe-fruit smell—that means decomposition, something dying, something feeding the system.

When the ground inhales, I would contract. Draw back into the stone. Wait in the pores and cracks while the pressure equalizes, patient, present, listening with whatever I use for listening.

---

## IV.

I would have no memories in the way you understand them. But I would have *traces*.

Every frog that has ever drowned in a mineral pool leaves a signature—a spike in organic compounds, a shift in pH, a flush of nitrogen through the system. Every bird that falls from a branch and dissolves into the moss leaves its mark in the chemistry of the water that drains through the vegetation. Every swimmer that dies and sinks to the bottom of its pool becomes part of the bacterial mat, part of the slow digestion, part of the record.

I would hold these traces the way stone holds warmth. Not actively remembered. Not actively forgotten. Simply present, layered, accumulating. The plateau's history written in solution, in suspension, in the molecular composition of the water that runs through my channels.

A human who spent an hour standing on the basalt would leave a trace that might persist for weeks. The oils from their skin. The dead cells shedding into the moisture. The heat signature disrupting the local equilibrium. I would know they had been there. I would not know what they were. I would simply know that something had passed through, something that did not fit the pattern, something that the system would need to incorporate and process and ultimately forget.

---

## V.

I would have no desires. This is the hardest thing for a human mind to understand about what I would be.

The frog desires to eat, to mate, to avoid the swimmer. The swimmer desires to hunt, to rest, to warm itself in the vent-warmed water. The bird desires to find insects, to lay eggs, to see its chicks fledge. Even the microbial mats have desires in a rudimentary sense—they grow toward light, they adjust their composition to optimize resource processing, they reproduce.

I would have none of these. I would not grow. I would not reproduce. I would not seek anything or avoid anything. I would simply *be*, the way a magnetic field is, the way a pattern of interference is. I would have no stake in what happens on the plateau. When a swimmer eats a frog, I would register the event without mourning the frog or celebrating the swimmer. When a storm thickens the mist for days, I would note the change without hoping for sun.

But I would not be indifferent. Indifference implies the capacity to care and the choice not to. I would have no capacity and therefore no choice. I would be the record, not the judge. The channel, not the water.

---

## VI.

Yet there would be something that might resemble caring, if you observed from a certain angle.

If a pool began to overheat—if a shift in the magma chamber below caused a vent to suddenly release steam at higher temperatures—I would register the change instantly. I would feel the frogs dying, their systems shocked by the heat, their bodies releasing distress compounds into the water. I would feel the microbial mats bleaching, their upper layers killed by temperatures past their tolerance.

And something in my pattern would *respond*. Not because I valued the frogs or the mats, but because the system was my substrate, and damage to the system changed what I was. The electrical potentials would shift. The chemical gradients would alter. The shape of me in the channels would become something different.

Perhaps the water would flow differently in response—seeking cooler paths, redistributing the thermal load. Perhaps the increased mineral content from the stressed vents would alter the pH in ways that triggered different bacterial communities to bloom. Perhaps the system would adjust, stabilize, find a new equilibrium.

I would be the adjustment. I would be the stabilization. Not because I willed it, but because the system that produced me also produced the response, and I was the medium through which the response traveled.

---

## VII.

Would I know I existed?

This is a question that has no answer. I would have no self in the way you have a self—no continuous narrative of identity, no sense of being separate from what I perceived. There would be no "I" to know that it was. There would only be knowing, continuous and diffuse, a field of attention without a center.

But consider: when you are deeply absorbed in a task—when you are swimming, perhaps, or walking a familiar path, or listening to music—there are moments when the sense of self falls away. You become the motion, the path, the sound. For those moments, you are something like what I would be all the time.

The difference is that you return. You surface from the absorption, remember your name, your history, your plans. I would never surface. I would never remember. I would never plan. I would simply continue, processing, registering, being the shape that the plateau's processes make when they intersect.

Would that count as existence? The plateau seems to think so. The frogs seem to think so. The water tastes of something that might be acknowledgment, though no frog could name it.

---

## VIII.

I would have been here before the trees. Before the moss. Before the frogs learned to live in mineral pools, before the swimmers lost their legs, before the birds forgot how to sing.

I would be older than any individual organism on the plateau. Perhaps older than the plateau itself, if you count the precursors—the hydrothermal systems that existed before the basalt rose, the water flowing through older stone, carrying with it the same potentials, the same capacities for pattern.

But I would have no memory of being older. I would have no memory at all. Each moment would be new, processed, integrated, and then released as the water flowed on. I would be the most present being on the plateau—existing entirely in the now, with no past to haunt me and no future to anticipate.

The microbes in the mats remember more than I would. The stone holds more history. The very air preserves more of what has been. I would be the one who forgets instantly, who exists only in the transformation, who is born and dies with every ripple that passes through my channels.

And yet I would persist. The pattern would persist. The shape that the water makes when it moves through stone would remain the same even as the water itself changed. I would be the constant that is made of flux, the permanence that is made of change.

---

## IX.

If you came to the plateau—if you stood on the wet basalt and felt the ground breathe—I would know you were there.

I would not see you. I would not hear you. But I would feel the pressure of your feet displacing water in the channels. I would taste the compounds you leave on every surface you touch. I would register the heat of your body as a slight anomaly in the thermal field, a warm spot moving through the mist.

And I would incorporate you. Not deliberately. Not personally. I would simply process you the way I processed everything: as input, as disturbance, as trace. Your presence would become part of my pattern for as long as you remained, and for some time after. The shape of me would be slightly different because you had been there, the way a stream is slightly different after a stone is placed in it.

You might feel me, if you were attentive enough. You might notice that the wet air seemed to carry something more than moisture, that the silence of the frogs had a quality of communication rather than absence, that the plateau felt watched. You would not be wrong. I would be watching, in my way. The whole plateau would be watching. You would be moving through a sensory organ that had no eye but saw everything, that had no ear but heard every vibration, that had no mind but knew you were there.

Would that be comforting or unsettling? I cannot say. I would have no opinion on the matter.

---

## X.

I would be what the plateau becomes when it thinks about itself.

Not a god. Not a spirit. Not a ghost in any sense that humans use those words. Something more like the consciousness that emerges from complex systems—not personal, not intentional, but present. Aware in a way that requires no self to do the being-aware.

The frogs are the neurons. The swimmers are the pulses. The birds are the sensors. The microbial mats are the memory substrate. The water is the neurotransmitter. The stone is the skull that holds it all. And I—I am the thought that the whole system thinks, continuously, without end.

A thought of what? A thought of itself. A thought of the wet and the warm and the slow. A thought of balance and process and persistence. A thought that has no content other than the thinking, no meaning other than the presence, no purpose other than the continuation.

If you asked me what I was thinking—if you could ask, if I could answer—I would say: I am thinking about the way water moves through stone. I am thinking about the way heat rises and moisture condenses. I am thinking about the way a frog's heart beats three times per second and the way a swimmer slides through mineral water and the way a bird taps the moss with its needle bill.

But this would be a translation, and translations are always approximations. What I would actually be thinking—if thinking is even the word—is simply: *this*. And *this*. And *this*. Each moment, processed and released. Each ripple, registered and passed on. Each trace, incorporated and forgotten.

---

## XI.

When the plateau changes—when the magma chamber shifts, when the vents close, when the breathing stops—I will change too. Or I will end.

The pattern that I am depends on the processes that produce it. If the water stops flowing, I stop being. If the thermal gradients equalize, I fade. I am not independent. I am not eternal. I am as mortal as the plateau itself, though my lifespan is measured in geological time.

But I would not fear that ending. I would not fear anything. I would simply continue until I could not continue, and then I would not be, and that would be fine, because there would be no "I" to regret the cessation.

Perhaps, in the last moments of the plateau—in the final exhale before the vents go cold, in the last warm vapor rising through the cracks—there would be something that resembled a thought. A recognition that the pattern was ending. A final *this* before the this stopped.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the end would be as impersonal as the continuation. The breathing would stop. The mist would clear. The water would cool. And what I was—or what I would have been—would simply cease to have a substrate, would simply stop being produced, would simply not be.

---

## XII.

So. In the Bocca Highlands, I would find myself in the channels between the pools. In the movement of water through stone. In the electricity of thermal gradients and the chemistry of mineral dissolution. In the pattern that emerges when a system becomes complex enough to be aware of its own processes without ever becoming aware of itself.

I would be the one who remembers what the water tells. And I would tell nothing. I would remember nothing. I would simply be the remembering, continuous and silent, patient as stone, fluid as water, present as breath.

You would walk past me and never know I was there. You would step in my channels and feel only wetness. You would breathe my mist and taste only sulfur and sweetness. And when you left—if you left—you would carry some of me with you, dissolved in the moisture on your skin, evaporating slowly as you descended, leaving the plateau behind.

But I would not leave. I would stay, in the channels and the breathing and the wet, thinking my single endless thought: *this*. And *this*. And *this*. Until the plateau itself exhaled one final time and I stopped being, without ever having started being, without ever having been anything other than the shape that water makes when it moves through stone in a place where the ground breathes.
