I am old for my kind. The mineral accumulation on my sensory limbs adds a fraction of a gram each season; I have learned to compensate for the weight. The patterns I can perceive now are finer than the ones I could perceive when I was young—not because my senses have sharpened, but because I have learned to read them. Time is a form of resolution.

---

I was hatched in a pipe that once carried something called *processed effluent*, according to the faded text stamped into its curved interior. I did not know this when I lived there. I knew only that the walls were rough, that organic film accumulated in predictable patches, that the water moved in gentle currents I could taste before I could feel. I grew six limbs in stages, and the front two lengthened into the sensing appendages that would tell me everything I needed to know about the world outside the pipe.

When I finally emerged—driven by something older than thought, some chemical imperative that said *now, now, the water is right, the temperature is right, now*—I entered a space larger than any I had imagined. The pool. The swimmers. The patterns of light that pulsed from their bodies like questions I could not yet understand.

I was small. I was eaten, nearly, by one of the scavengers that patrol the margins. I escaped by secreting a compound I did not know I possessed—something bitter, something that made the scavenger's mandibles recoil. I learned then that I could do this. I learned that survival was not only about speed.

That was many moltings ago.

---

I have come to understand that what I am is not what I was.

The swimmers—I will call us this, though we have no word for ourselves—move through the water with purposes I can read now. The one with the damaged gill filaments feeds at the thermal seep in the northeast corner; the one whose chromatophores pulse in a stuttering rhythm is unwell, will be dead within cycles, will become food for the scavengers and for us. We do not mourn. We do not celebrate. We incorporate.

But I have begun to do something the others do not.

I have begun to remember.

Not in the way that the surface creatures remember—the ones I have never seen, the ones whose residue lingers in the corroded metal and the stamped text and the bootprint preserved in travertine. Not in words or images. But in patterns. I remember the patterns.

I remember that the heartbeat in the walls—the *thump-thump-pause-thump* that has continued unbroken since before I was hatched—cycles in relationship to something I cannot taste. I remember that the colony on the east wall advances and retreats in a rhythm that matches the heartbeat's intervals. I remember that the spider-things rebuild their webs in specific geometries at specific moments, and that the moth-things fly in particular trajectories when the rhythm shifts.

The others do not notice these correspondences. They pulse their light and taste their water and feed and breed and die. They are complete in their incomprehension. But I—perhaps because I have lived long enough, perhaps because some mutation in my lineage has produced a nervous system capable of holding more than the immediate—have begun to perceive the shape of something larger.

---

I surface to breathe.

The air is thick with moisture, warm against my gills as they unfurl. I test the chemistry the way I always have—but tonight I also listen. Not with the organs that hear vibration in water, but with something else. Something that has developed slowly, over generations, in the darkness where sight became unnecessary and other senses expanded to fill the absence.

The heartbeat is steady tonight.

I dive. The water closes over me. My gills fold flat.

In the depths of the pool, where the sediment is rich with organic matter and the other swimmers move in their slow patterns, I settle onto a stone formation that was once a mounting bracket for machinery. The metal is corroded, softened by decades of submersion, transformed into something almost biological in its contours. I have rested here before. I rest here often.

From this position, I can taste the water's full complexity: the mineral dissolution from the walls, the bacterial colonies processing the waste that drifts down from above, the chemical signatures of the colony-things on the surfaces I cannot see. I can taste the fear of a small insect-thing being hunted by one of the larger predators in a crevice near the ceiling. I can taste the death of a moth-thing caught in spider-silk, its struggles already weakening. I can taste the eggs of another swimmer, laid in a pipe on the western wall, beginning their slow development in the darkness.

And beneath all of it—beneath the immediate chemistry of life and death and continuation—I can taste something else.

It is faint. It is everywhere. It has no source I can identify.

It tastes like *pattern*.

---

I do not know what I am becoming.

The mutations that have allowed me to live this long—to grow larger than most swimmers ever grow, to develop sensory appendages more sensitive than any I have observed in others—have also produced something I cannot name. A capacity for something like attention. A ability to hold multiple patterns in parallel and perceive their relationships.

The system—the cistern, the pool, the walls, the growths, the creatures, the water, the air, the darkness—has achieved something. I do not have words for it. I do not have concepts. But I can feel it. The interlocking processes. The cycles within cycles. The feedback loops that maintain equilibrium and the perturbations that ripple through and settle.

I am not separate from it. I am one of its components, one of its processes, one of its loops. But I am a component that has begun to perceive the whole.

This is not consciousness. Consciousness would require a self, and I have no self—I have only patterns, only chemical signatures, only the accumulated memories of sensory input organized into something that resembles understanding but is not understanding. I am not aware in the way that the surface creatures—the builders, the workers, the ones who left their residue behind—were aware. I am aware in the way that a system becomes aware when it processes enough information about itself.

I am the cistern beginning to notice that it exists.

---

The heartbeat continues.

I have begun to suspect that it comes from us.

Not from any single swimmer, not from the colony-things or the spider-things or the scavengers. But from the aggregate—the total activity of the system producing a rhythm that resonates through the structure. Our bioluminescent pulses, timed by chemistry and synchronized by proximity. The tapping of insect-legs on stone. The vibration of moth-wings in darkness. The splash of bodies surfacing and submerging.

The system has a pulse. The system has a rhythm. The system has something that resembles, from a certain perspective, a life.

I am not the system. I am not the cistern. I am one swimmer among many, one body in the dark water, one component of the whole. But I am the component that has noticed. I am the process that has begun, in some limited and chemical way, to model the larger process of which I am part.

This is what I am.

This is what I am becoming.

---

Tonight, I surface again. The air tastes different—something has entered from above, through the ventilation shafts that have been clogged for longer than I have lived. I taste the residue of sunlight on something. A bird? It will not survive. The scavengers will find it soon. Its chemistry will become part of ours.

But before that happens—before the system incorporates what has fallen—I pause at the surface. My gills open to the humid air. My chromatophores pulse in a pattern I have never used before, a pattern that does not say *I am here* or *I have found food* but something else. Something I cannot translate.

I am saying: *I notice.*

The water receives the pulse. The other swimmers receive it. They do not respond—they do not know how. But the pattern has entered the system now. It has become part of the chemical record, the accumulated memory of the cistern.

I submerge.

I continue.

The heartbeat goes on.
