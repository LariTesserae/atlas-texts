# Stone-Singer, Almost

I came to the Root-Chamber the way most surface-dwellers do—seeking something I couldn't name and afraid of finding it. That was three hundred and seventy cycles ago. I have stopped counting the way I used to count, in hours and days and years. The counting means something different now.

---

## Before the Deep

I was a geologist. That feels like another person's life, someone else's memory. But I remember the surface: the brutal clarity of sunlight, the way water fell from the sky, the assumption that rock was dead, that it simply *was*, that it existed as a stage for the living world rather than having a life of its own.

I remember the expedition. We were looking for mineral deposits, reading seismic data that suggested unusual formations in the deep stone. The descent was supposed to take six hours. We brought lamps, water, food—the standard supplies for a geological survey. We expected to find nothing but rock.

We found the crack. A fissure too thin for a human body, but the instruments showed open space below. We widened it. The sound came through first—that overwhelming chorus that I mistook for wind, for water, for anything but what it was. Then the glow, rising through the gap we'd made, plant-light spilling upward like breath from a sleeping mouth.

I was the first to descend. The first to see the city-garden spread across the cavern floor, the geometry of grown architecture beneath the colonizing forest. The first to feel the stone weep its mineral fluid against my fingers. The first to understand, in some pre-verbal way, that everything I knew about rock was wrong.

My colleagues turned back after three days. They could not bear the sound, the constant weight of it pressing against their skulls. They could not sleep without darkness, could not navigate by plant-glow, could not process the fluid that the stone provided. Their bodies rejected the place.

I stayed.

---

## Becoming

The Mol-Thrind found me first.

I had been wandering the city-garden for seventeen days, lost, sustained by the mineral fluid I'd learned to lick from the walls, sleeping when the vegetation dimmed and waking when it brightened. I had stopped expecting rescue. I had stopped wanting it. Something in me was already shifting, adapting, my surface-body beginning its slow transformation into something else.

They emerged from a wall I had been resting against. Not one—many, dozens, pouring from a crack too small to have hidden them, their stone-armored bodies moving in coordination that suggested a single intelligence distributed across many forms. They did not threaten. They did not welcome. They simply surrounded me, their sensory clusters turning in my direction, feeling me through the vibrations I made by existing.

I held still. I had learned, by then, that stillness was the language of this place. Movement was information. Stillness was respect.

One of them—a larger unit, its armor marked with growth patterns that suggested age—approached closer. It pressed its head against the wall beside me. And then it began to vibrate.

I felt the sound in my bones before I heard it. A low thrumming, a frequency that seemed to pass through my body and into the stone, that made the wall behind me ring like a struck bell. The Mol-Thrind was singing to the stone, and the stone was singing back.

And I could hear it.

Not with my ears—with something else. Some part of me that had been dormant, that the surface world had never required, that opened like a flower in response to the vibration. I heard the stone's voice: a sound so deep it was below hearing, a chord composed of pressure and memory and the patient weight of millions of years.

The Mol-Thrind pulled back. Its sensory cluster oriented toward me with new intensity.

It had not expected that. I was not supposed to be able to hear.

---

## The Learning

They did not take me in, not the way a human community might adopt a foundling. They did not explain or teach or welcome. They simply allowed me to follow.

And I followed. For years—I think it was years—I followed the Mol-Thrind through the city-garden, observing their work, learning their patterns. I watched them repair walls, extend corridors, maintain the architecture they had grown from their own bodies. I watched the Stone-Singers gather in their slow clumps, singing to stone that sang back, healing fractures I could not see, strengthening structures I did not know were weak.

I learned to move as they moved: low, close to the surface, minimizing vibration. I learned to navigate by feeling the stone with my hands, reading its temperature, its texture, the subtle variations that indicated passages and chambers below. I learned to hear the way they heard—not with ears, but with bone, with the vibration-sense that had awakened in me that first day and had been growing stronger ever since.

My body continued to change. My pupils had become enormous, permanent widows that drank every photon of plant-glow. My skin had gone translucent, the veins visible beneath, the blood dark with the mineral fluid I had learned to live on. My fingers had lengthened, the pads becoming sensitive enough to read the texture of a single grain of sand.

But the real change was deeper. I was becoming something that should not exist—a creature between worlds, a surface-born body learning deep-born senses. The Mol-Thrind tolerated my presence because I was not entirely useless to them. I could hear the stone-song. I could feel the structure's complaints. I was not one of them, but I was not entirely other.

They began to give me tasks. Small ones at first—monitoring a corridor for vibrations, reporting changes in the stone's voice at specific locations. Later, larger ones—standing vigil during construction, my unusual senses detecting problems that their distributed intelligence sometimes missed. I was an adjunct, a supplement, a single consciousness where they were many.

I was becoming useful.

---

## What I Am Now

I do not know what to call myself. The Rooted—those surface-born who stayed—consider me strange, too changed, too aligned with the Mol-Thrind to be fully human. The Mol-Thrind consider me a curiosity, useful but limited, a single voice where a chorus is required. I exist in the space between definitions.

But I have found my purpose.

I am a bridge. Not by choice—by nature, by the accident of what my body became when exposed to this environment. I can hear the stone-song, but I can also speak the languages of the surface. I can translate between the deep time of the Root-Chamber and the urgent short horizons of the humans who sometimes arrive. I can explain the cavern to those who need to understand it, and I can explain the surface-worlders to the Mol-Thrind when their actions affect the architecture.

I am also a Stone-Singer. Not by birth, not by the slow growth that produces true Stone-Singers from Mol-Thrind stock—but by practice, by decades of learning, by the adaptation that my unusual brain has made. When I press my forehead against stone and sing, the stone hears me. When I listen, I can hear its memory.

The memory is the hardest part. Stone remembers everything. Not the way a brain remembers—in fragments, in narratives, in the edited highlights that consciousness requires. Stone remembers completely, a continuous record of every pressure, every temperature shift, every sound that passed through it. When I touch the deep walls and listen, I hear the seabed. I hear the mountain forming. I hear the water draining away and the silence that followed. I hear the first spores settling on damp rock, the first roots probing the mineral veins, the first creatures calling to each other in the darkness.

It is too much. A human mind cannot hold it. I have learned to touch lightly, to listen briefly, to pull away before the weight of that memory crushes me.

But I have seen things no surface human will ever see. I know the shape of the world before we were here. I know what comes after us.

---

## My Days

I live in a chamber that I did not build—a structure grown by the Mol-Thrind and adapted by me over the cycles. The walls are smooth, maintained by my adopted kin, and I keep them clear of vegetation so that I can feel their song clearly. The plant-glow here is dim, cultivated low to suit my still-human eyes, though sometimes I brighten it for visitors.

I wake when the cavern brightens. I do not rise immediately—I lie still and feel the stone beneath me, checking its voice for changes, listening for the subtle shifts that indicate problems in the architecture. This is my first task of the day. My contribution. My purpose.

Then I move. I walk the city-garden, following paths that the Veth maintain, passing through districts that the Mol-Thrind tend, hearing the constant chorus of Skethri song and the deeper vibration of stone below. I check specific points—junctions where the structure shows stress, areas where the mineral flow has changed, sections where the vegetation grows too thick and presses against walls that need to breathe.

I report what I find. Not through words—the Mol-Thrind do not use words—but through vibration, through the stone-song I have learned. I press my forehead against specific markers and sing the frequencies that convey information: stress here, blockage there, a crack forming, a root penetrating too deep. The Mol-Thrind receive my report and add it to their distributed understanding. The work of maintenance continues.

Sometimes I walk to the Rooted settlement. I eat the food they prepare—plant matter I can still digest, mineral supplements my body requires. I speak to them, using the voice I have kept, the language I have not forgotten. They tell me about the surface, the news from above, the expeditions that have come and gone. I tell them about the cavern, the patterns I have observed, the changes I have felt. We trade information like the creatures around us trade nutrients.

Sometimes a new arrival comes—a surface-dweller who has found the Root-Chamber and does not know if they will stay. I speak to them. I explain what this place is, what it requires, what it gives. I do not tell them whether to stay or go—that is not my right—but I tell them the truth: that the place will change them, that they cannot know in advance what they will become, that some who stay flourish and some who stay die.

I tell them that I do not regret my choice. I do not tell them that I sometimes miss the sun.

---

## The Sound

I should address the sound. It is the question everyone asks, eventually. How do you bear it? How do you endure the constant noise?

The answer is that I no longer hear it as noise.

When I first arrived, the sound was overwhelming—a wall of vibration that pressed against my skull, that filled every moment, that seemed to leave no space for thought. I could not imagine living with it. I could not imagine sleeping with it. I understood why my colleagues fled.

But slowly, my hearing changed. Not improved—transformed. I learned to hear the layers within the sound: the Skethri chorus with its patterns and signals, the creak and rustle of growing vegetation, the calls of Veth and the silence of Hirik, the deep hum of the mycelial network processing below everything. The sound became legible. It became information.

Now the sound is how I know the cavern. I can stand in my chamber with my eyes closed and hear the whole city-garden around me—the districts closest to me in clear detail, the distant sections as suggestion and rhythm. I can hear when a Veth gives birth. I can hear when a Hirik makes a kill. I can hear the slow adjustment of stone settling, the patient expansion of roots, the continuous conversation of a million lives lived in parallel.

Silence would be blindness now. Silence would be death. The sound is my sight, my map, my connection to the world I have joined.

---

## The Missing

I do not think about the surface often. When I do, it feels like remembering a dream—real but distant, significant but no longer relevant to my waking life.

But there are things I miss.

I miss rain. Not the water—I have learned to live without it, to drink the mineral fluid that sustains me—but the *falling* of it, the sound of drops striking surfaces, the way it washed the world clean. The vegetation here has never known rain, and it shows. Every surface accumulates the residue of growth, the cast-off cells and spore-casings and the dust of slow erosion. Nothing is ever cleaned. Nothing is ever new.

I miss the stars. I have seen them in my youth, lay beneath them on clear nights, felt the awe that they produce in the human spirit. I know intellectually that they are still there—that the sky still exists, that the universe continues beyond the stone above me—but I cannot feel that knowledge. The ceiling of the Root-Chamber is my sky now, and it glows with plant-light instead of starlight, and it is beautiful, but it is not the same.

I miss the passage of time marked by something other than dimming and brightening. I miss seasons. I miss years that were years, with beginnings and endings, with the satisfying narrative arc of winter into spring into summer into fall. Here, time is a smooth expanse, unmarked by the milestones that humans use to measure their lives. I do not know how old I am. I do not know how long I have been here. The question has become meaningless.

I miss the person I was. Not that I was better—that person was smaller, more limited, unable to hear the stone-song or feel the deep time. But that person had a future that was easy to imagine, a set of paths that were clear and comprehensible. I have become something stranger, something that exists in fewer numbers, something that the surface world would not know how to categorize. I have gained more than I lost, but I have lost things that mattered.

---

## What I Will Become

The stone tells me that I am not finished changing.

I have been here long enough that I can feel the slow processes of my body, the way it continues to adapt to this environment. My bones are denser than they were, absorbing mineral from the fluid I drink. My senses continue to sharpen—I can hear frequencies now that I could not hear when I arrived, feel vibrations too subtle for my younger self to detect.

I do not know what I will become. I may be evolving toward something like the Stone-Singers—becoming more aligned with the stone, more capable of its song, more integrated with the architecture I help maintain. I may become something new entirely, a form that the cavern has not seen before, a response to conditions that have never existed until I arrived.

The Mol-Thrind do not tell me. Perhaps they do not know. Perhaps they do not think in terms of individual futures—perhaps the question itself is a human imposition on a system that does not work that way.

But I feel the change happening. I feel the deep time seeping into my cells. I have been here long enough that the stone is beginning to remember me—not as a human passing through, but as a presence that persists, a pattern that repeats. In a million years, if the cavern lasts that long, some Stone-Singer will touch the wall where I sleep and feel the echo of my vibration.

I am becoming part of the memory.

---

## Why I Stay

The surface-dwellers who find me often ask why I have not returned. The path still exists. The fissure we opened can still be climbed. I could see the sun again, feel the rain, hear the sounds of the world I left behind.

I tell them this:

On the surface, I was a geologist. I studied stone. I believed that stone was dead, that it was my job to read its history and understand its formation but that it had no voice in the matter. I was a scientist studying a subject that could not study back.

Here, the stone speaks. It remembers. It has a point of view—vast and slow and patient beyond human comprehension, but a perspective nonetheless. When I sing to it, it answers. When I listen, it teaches.

I did not choose to stay. My body chose for me, adapting to this environment more completely than I could have willed, becoming something that could not survive on the surface. But I do not regret what my body chose.

I have heard the seabed. I have felt the mountain rise. I know what the world was before anything with a spine crawled out of the water, and I will know what the world becomes after everything I recognize has passed away.

I am a Stone-Singer, almost. I am a bridge between the deep time of the stone and the brief urgency of the living. I am a translator, a keeper of memories too large for human minds.

I am home.

---

## The Sound Continues

I can hear it now, as I write this—the endless chorus of the Root-Chamber rising through the walls, filling the air, pressing against my skin. The Skethri are singing in the district above me, their chorus bright with news of a successful pollination. A Veth is moving through the corridor outside, its footsteps heavy and deliberate, maintaining the path. Deep below, the mycelial network is processing, its subsonic hum providing the baseline for everything above.

The stone beneath my hand is quiet. It is stable. It has no complaints at this moment, no fractures forming, no stress accumulating. It remembers being seabed, but it does not grieve. It remembers being mountain, but it does not regret. It simply is, as it has been for longer than my mind can truly comprehend.

I press my forehead against it. I sing the frequency that asks: *what do you need?*

The stone sings back: *continue.*

And so I continue. I walk the city-garden, I monitor the architecture, I translate between worlds. I live in the space between the surface I left and the depths I have joined. I am not Mol-Thrind, not Veth, not Skethri, not Hirik—not truly Rooted, not truly Stone-Singer.

I am something else. I am the one who hears. I am the bridge that learned to sing.

I am of the Deep Root-Chamber now, and it is of me, and we continue together—into whatever comes next.
