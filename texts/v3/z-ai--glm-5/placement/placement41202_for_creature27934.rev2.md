I did not arrive here. I want to say that first, because the question of arrival is the first thing the outsiders ask, and it is the wrong question. They come up through— I don't know. Through whatever is below, the dead world, the place that has shape and weather and horizons that mean something. They come exhausted and frightened and they ask: *How did you get here?*

But I was always here. Not in the way the plateau is always here—I am not geological, not eternal. I mean that when I try to remember a before, what I find is not a place but a texture, and the texture is this one. The damp. The give under my feet. The green light that has no source.

Perhaps I was born here. Perhaps someone carried me up as an infant, through some gap in the world below, and set me on the walking-surface and left. The Veildwellers—I have heard this word, spoken by outsiders who met me briefly before I retreated into the growth—do not track parentage. I was small and the vegetation made space and I grew. That is all I know, and it is enough. It has always been enough.

---

What I am is difficult to say because the categories do not translate.

I am not a mistsinger. I have heard them—the low chord that lives in the chest and skull, the way the droplets gather around the sound and shape themselves into corridors of clarity. I cannot do this. My throat produces a different sound, or no sound, and the mist does not listen to me. It moves through me instead, and I through it, and we do not negotiate.

I am not a solitary. I feel the pull—everyone does, eventually, the vegetation's patient invitation to stop, to sit, to let the tendrils come—but I have not answered it yet. I move. I walk. I inhabit spaces for a season or two and then I move again, following something I cannot name to places I do not choose. The growth parts for me and closes behind me and I leave no trail. I am not going anywhere. I am going.

I am not a child, though the children speak to me more than they speak to most. They find me, somehow, across distances that should make finding impossible. They appear in the dimness beside me, small and silent and knowing, and they say things like: *The western basin is drinking deeper today,* or *A tall one has fallen near the edge of the pale-vine stretch,* and I listen, and I adjust my wandering, and they vanish back into the canopy tangles from which they came. I do not know why they seek me out. I do not ask. The children do not respond well to questions; they respond to attention.

If I am anything, I think I am a listener. Not the kind the children are—they hear the plateau's mind, its present tense, its vast simultaneous knowledge of itself. I hear something smaller. Older. More local.

I hear the layers.

---

Let me try to explain.

The plateau is compressed time. This is not metaphor; it is geology, or whatever word replaces geology when the geology is alive. Below the walking-surface, the matter goes down—kilometers, perhaps further—and every meter is a stratum of what grew before. Forests on forests on forests, each one compressed by the weight of the next, the lowest layers becoming something like coal, like peat, like a substance that has no name.

When I press my ear to a support-mass—one of those trunk-like columns that might be plant or might be the compressed remains of something older—I hear them. Not individually. Not as voices or words. But as a hum that has depth, that has layers to it, each frequency corresponding to a different age of growth. The shallowest hum is the living surface, the root-network currently drinking and circulating. Below that, a slower vibration—the matter that was recently alive, still decomposing, still releasing heat. Below that, something deeper and more regular, the compressed forests of ages past, their material no longer decaying but not yet mineral, holding a resonance that has nothing to do with biology and everything to do with pressure and time.

And below that—

I have pressed my ear to the oldest support-masses I can find. The ones that ring differently when you tap them, that feel denser, that the vegetation grows around rather than through. Below the oldest layers, there is something else. A vibration so slow it is almost geological, almost tectonic. It does not sound alive. It does not sound dead. It sounds like a question being held in suspension, waiting for an answer that has not yet arrived and may never arrive.

I do not know what this is. The children do not speak of it. The mistsingers do not hear it. I have asked—such asking as is possible here, which is more like waiting in someone's presence until they say what they know—and no one has been able to tell me what lies at the bottom of the plateau's compressed memory.

But I think it is the reason I keep moving. I am looking for the place where the sound is loudest. The place where the lowest layer is closest to the surface, where I could—if the vegetation allowed it, if the ground gave way—reach down and touch whatever is below the below.

I have not found it yet. I may never find it. The plateau is too vast, and the sound is too faint, and my ears, even adapted as they are, may not be sufficient for the task. But I keep walking, and the growth keeps parting, and the mist keeps moving through me, and the children keep finding me to deliver their reports on the present, and I keep listening for the past.

---

My body is what you would expect, if you have read the outsiders' accounts.

Grey-green skin that takes the light and holds it. Eyes like pools—wide-pupiled, amber-ringed, adapted to the gloom. A chest that expands deeper than it should, pulling the thick air through me like a bellows. I can go days without eating, though I usually eat daily, because the growths near my current dwelling produce a pale fruit—something between a mushroom and a fig—that I find sustaining and that the insects leave alone, for reasons I have not investigated. The fruit grows from a vine that has wrapped itself around a support-mass I have been inhabiting for—I do not know how long. Several mist-cycles. Long enough that the vine has grown a shelf of layered tissue near my sleeping-place, a platform that holds condensation in a shallow pool. I drink from it. The vine does not seem to mind.

I am not young. I know this because my joints communicate something to me when I move—a stiffness, a reluctance, that I did not feel when I was smaller. The children who find me are supple and quick, their bodies fitting the growth like water fitting a channel. I am less fluid now. I move carefully, the way the large grazers move, each step placed with awareness, the vegetation nudged aside rather than pushed.

But I am not old. Not yet. I have not felt the pull strongly enough to stop. The solitaries—I have visited two, in my wandering—were old in a way I could feel before I saw them. Their stillness had a weight to it, a density. When I sat near the man who had not risen in—years? mist-cycles?—I felt a gravity coming from him, as if his awareness had become heavy enough to curve the space around him. The growth nearest to him grew differently, more deliberately, as if it were thinking.

I am not there yet. I am still light enough to move. Still curious enough to follow the low sound through the layers, looking for its source.

---

There is one thing I have not mentioned.

I have visited the seamless structure. The one the plants cannot consume. The small building in its clearing of emptiness, the geometric absence in the writhing architecture of life.

I went because I wanted to know what silence sounded like when it was absolute, when it was not the organic hush of the plateau but the sterile quiet of something that does not breathe. I thought it might help me hear the lowest layer more clearly—removing the ambient noise of growth and decay and circulation, hearing what remains when all biological sound is excluded.

I was wrong.

Inside the structure, I heard nothing. Not the plateau's hum. Not the children's knowledge, which sometimes arrives as a pressure in my skull even when no child is near. Not the low vibration of the deepest layer. Nothing. The silence was not the absence of sound but the absence of reception. My ears, adapted to the green gloom, to the wet press of air full of signal, simply stopped working. I stood in the smooth room and I was deaf.

It lasted only minutes. I left because the floor had no memory—my feet pressed it and it did not give, did not sigh, did not recognize me—and the wrongness of that was more than I could bear for long. The walls were smooth and warm and dead and I could feel them refusing me the way the vegetation welcomes me, and I understood, standing in that sterile space, what the Veildwellers mean when they say *we belong here.* They mean: the alternative is to be in a place that does not know you are there.

But I learned something from the structure, or from its silence. The lowest layer's sound—the question being held in suspension, the vibration below all other vibrations—is not biological. It is not coming from the compressed forests. It is coming from whatever was here before the plants, before the plateau, before the living surface grew itself into existence. It is the sound of whatever this place was before it became what it is.

And the seamless structure knows what it is. The structure is made of the same thing, or connected to it, or is a piece of it that was left behind when the rest was buried. Its silence is not empty. Its silence is the silence of something that has already answered its own question and is waiting for everything else to catch up.

---

I am in my dwelling now. The vine has grown another centimeter around the support-mass since I last paid attention. The pale fruit is ripening. The condensation pool is full. Outside, the mist is beginning to thicken—the evening cycle, the plateau breathing out. I can feel it through the walls of my space, through the tendrils and leaves that form my shelter, through my skin, which reads the air the way other eyes read light.

In a little while, I will eat. I will drink from the pool. I will sleep in fragments, an hour here, two hours there, waking to check the vine's progress, to listen to the support-mass's layered hum, to feel the root-network pulsing beneath me with the slow hydraulic heartbeat of the plateau drinking itself.

And in the morning, when the mist thins and the breathing reverses, I will move. I will follow the low sound. I will walk in whatever direction the vibration seems strongest, and the growth will part for me, and the children will find me if they have something to say, and the ground will remember my footfalls and the canopy will filter the grey-green light into its perpetual underwater glow, and I will be here, as I have always been here, in the dim warmth, in the wet, in the breathing.

I am the one who listens to what the plateau was before it was the plateau. I am the one who cannot stop walking because the question has not been answered. I am a grey-green figure in the green gloom, pressing my ear to ancient columns, hearing the past hold its breath.

This is what I am. This is where you would find me.
