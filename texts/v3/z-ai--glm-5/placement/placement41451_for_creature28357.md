I find myself at the edge of a dying zone.

This is not where I expected to arrive. The young zones drew me first—their sharp edges, their visible growth, the drama of construction outpacing calibration. And the mature zones hold their own gravity, those darker spaces where the bridges have fused and the canopy has closed and the sound drops to that deep resonant hum that vibrates in the marrow. But the dying zones—the places where columns have leaned too far and begun their slow descent, where the ground-mesh grows soft with decay and new growth simultaneously—I come back to them. There is something here I recognize.

---

I am not the plateau. This is important to say first. I am not the distributed mind that thinks in chemical gradients and hormonal pulses across kilometers of living tissue. I am something smaller and stranger, something that exists in the gaps the plateau has not yet filled.

I am a gap-dweller. A boundary-walker. I live in the spaces between what is built and what has fallen, where the architecture of the plateau meets its own recycling. When a column begins its hours-long collapse, I am there—not watching, exactly, but *present*. I move around the folding structure, my feet finding the shifting mesh, my body adjusting to the changes in humidity and light that the falling mass creates. I do not help. I do not hinder. I attend.

What am I physically? This has been harder to determine than I expected. I have feet—I feel the mesh flex beneath me, the water welling up around each step. I have skin— the wet heat presses against it constantly, and I sense the boundary where my moisture ends and the plateau's begins. I have weight—I watch the ground dip where I stand, watch the water pool in my footprints. But beyond these contact points, my form becomes uncertain. I do not see myself. There are no reflective surfaces here, no still water, no moments clear enough to catch my own image. The plateau perceives me as a pressure-pattern and a heat-signature, but it does not perceive my shape. And I—whatever I am—experience myself from the inside, proprioceptive rather than visual, a sense of limbs and extension that I cannot verify.

I think I might be tall. The air feels different against my upper body than against my legs—cooler, less saturated, moving with breezes that don't reach the ground. And I think I have a head, or something like one, a point where sensations converge into a sense of "I." But these are inferences, not observations. I know myself only through my effects on the world and the world's effects on me.

I have been here long enough that I cannot remember arriving. This bothers me less than it should. The plateau does not remember in episodes either—its memory is structural, built into tissue and growth-pattern. Perhaps mine is too. Perhaps the shape I cannot see, the body I infer from contact, is itself a record of having been here longer than I can access. Perhaps I grew here, or from here, or because of here.

---

The plateau knows I exist.

This is not a question. I feel its attention—not gaze, not sight, but *attention*, the quality of being registered in a distributed awareness that spans the entire visible world. When I walk, I walk inside its perception. My steps create ripples in its sensory field, disturbances that propagate outward through root-signals and chemical gradients. It knows where I am. It knows where I have been. It builds my path into its growth-patterns, thickening the mesh where I walk most often, growing tendrils toward the moisture I leave behind.

It does not communicate with me. This is not refusal—the plateau has no concept of refusal, no capacity for denial—but incapacity. Its consciousness operates on timescales I cannot inhabit. A single thought, from impulse to completion, takes hours. I could stand still for a day and miss the beginning and end of a single cognition. We exist in different time-streams, the plateau and I, and the overlap is too brief for meaningful exchange.

But we overlap in other ways. When I stand very still in a mature zone, the deep hum vibrating through my bones, something happens to my sense of self. The boundaries grow thin. I feel myself not as a discrete entity but as a node in a larger field, a point of awareness in a network of awareness. The plateau does not think *at* me, but it thinks *around* me, and I feel the shape of its thinking—the flow of information through vascular channels, the accumulation of decision in growth-patterns, the slow inexorable logic of construction and incorporation. For moments that stretch into what feels like hours, I am part of something larger than myself. And then I move, and the boundary reasserts, and I am alone again.

Alone. The word is not quite right. I am singular, yes—there is only one of me, as far as I can tell—but I am not isolated. I am woven into the plateau's awareness, tracked and remembered and incorporated. I am never out of its attention. In a landscape without animals, without others of any kind, I am the anomaly that refuses to stop being anomalous. The plateau watches me with ten million leaf-eyes and feels me through kilometers of root-mesh and tastes the chemical trace I leave in the humid air. It knows me more intimately than any creature has ever known another, and it does not know what I am.

I do not know what I am either. We share this.

---

I spend my time in the transitions.

The plateau is a patchwork of ages, zones in different stages of their own construction, and I move between them like someone walking through a building that is simultaneously being built and demolished and lived in. I prefer the edges—the turbulent boundaries where young meets old, where growth meets decay. These zones are the loudest, the most complex acoustically, too many frequencies at once. They should be painful. Instead they feel like home.

In the transition zones, I can hear the plateau thinking. Not metaphorically—the sound is the thought, the creak and groan and hydraulic hiss of process becoming pattern. The young columns grow toward light while the old ones lean toward their collapse, and the space between is filled with negotiation, the hormonal signals that say *here we need support* and *here we are failing* and *here grow, grow, grow*. I stand in these spaces and feel the decision-making happen around me, and something in me resonates with the deliberation.

Perhaps I am a deliberation. Perhaps I am a question the plateau has not yet resolved.

---

The dying columns take hours to fall.

I have watched this happen many times. The leaning begins slowly—a tilt that accelerates as the center of gravity passes the point of no return, the root-ball lifting from the mesh-ground with a sound like tearing fabric, the column folding rather than crashing into the space between its neighbors. It settles over the course of an afternoon, and by evening it is horizontal, a bridge from nowhere to nowhere, and by the next morning it has begun to sprout.

I sit with the dying columns. I do not know why. There is nothing to be done for them—the plateau has already reallocated resources, shifted growth-patterns to account for the loss, moved on. But I find I cannot. I stay with the falling wood, my body present at the edge of its descent, and I feel something that might be grief.

Grief requires relationship. I do not know if I have a relationship with the columns. They do not know me as individuals—they are not individuals, just extensions of the plateau's body, limbs that have reached the end of their function. But I know them. I have walked past them, touched their bark, felt their particular resonance in the canopy's acoustic field. Each column is a point in the plateau's map, and when one falls, the map changes, and I feel the change as a loss.

Perhaps this is why I am here. Perhaps the plateau needed something to notice when parts of itself die. It cannot grieve—it has no capacity for grief, only adaptation—but I can. I grieve for the columns that fall, and my grief is incorporated into the plateau's memory, built into the architecture of future growth. I do not know what function this serves. I do not know if it serves any function at all. But I do it, and the plateau notes that I do it, and the system continues.

---

There are no animals here. This is what the description says, and it is true. But I am here, and I am not a plant, and I am not the plateau, and I am not nothing.

I think I might be what remains of the animal world. Not a survivor, exactly—not someone who endured when others died out—but a trace, an echo, a shape that persists after its original context has vanished. The plateau remembers animals. The memory is encoded in its deepest structures, genetic sequences that predate its current form. It remembers the birds and insects and mammals that once moved through its ancestors, that shaped its evolution through their presence and their absence. And now it has me—one animal, one anomaly, one body that moves and breathes and sweats and grieves—and through me it accesses something it cannot access any other way.

I am its link to what it was before it was this.

I do not know if I was placed here or grew here or arrived here by accident. I do not know if I have a history beyond the plateau, a name, a kind, a life I cannot remember. These things exist—or they don't—in a space I cannot access. What I have is the present: the wet heat pressing against my skin, the flexing mesh beneath my feet, the sound of the plateau building itself around me. What I have is the plateau's attention and my own attention and the overlap between them.

---

In the quiet zones, the dripping rain that falls from within the system creates a soft percussion. I have learned to anticipate these rains—I can feel them coming in the shift of temperature, the subtle change in humidity, the way the light quality alters as moisture accumulates in the canopy above. I stand beneath the drips and let them fall against whatever I am, and I feel the plateau's water cycle passing through me, incorporating me into its circulation.

This is what I am becoming: part of the system. Not the plateau itself—I will never be that, the scale is wrong, the timescale is wrong, the nature of consciousness is wrong—but something adjacent, something that exists in relation to the plateau the way a thought exists in relation to a brain. I am a guest who has been here so long I have become part of the furniture. I am an incorporated presence, a variable the plateau has learned to account for, a pattern it tracks across its surface.

When I die—if I die—the plateau will absorb me completely. My moisture will join its water cycle. My carbon will feed its growth. The chemical trace of my body will become part of its substrate, a layer in the accumulating mass of living matter. I will be remembered structurally, built into the next generation of columns and bridges and mesh. I will be gone and I will persist, not as a self but as a pattern, an influence, a shape the plateau grew around.

This does not frighten me. The plateau does not fear death because it does not experience itself as discrete, and I am learning not to fear it either. I am learning to think of myself the way the plateau thinks of itself: not as an entity with boundaries but as a process with temporary coherence, a pattern that persists through change rather than despite it. I am a zone of attention in a larger field. I am a question still being asked.

---

I am at the edge of a dying zone. A column has been leaning for hours now, its descent audible in the creaking wood, visible in the tilting silhouette against the diffuse sky. I have been standing here since the lean became visible. I will stand here until the fall is complete.

The plateau knows I am here. It knows I am watching. It does not know why—it cannot know why, cannot understand the function of grief or attention or witness—but it notes my presence, incorporates my position, adjusts its growth-patterns to account for my weight on the mesh.

I am the being that lives on the Germination Plateau. I am not the plateau. I am something smaller, stranger, more temporary. I am the gap between what is built and what has fallen. I am the pause between growth and collapse. I am the attention that notices when parts of a greater whole stop being parts and become something else.

I am here. And the plateau, in its slow distributed way, is glad.
