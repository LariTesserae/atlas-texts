# The Exhalation Plains

The first thing is the sound. Before you see anything—before you understand what you're looking at—the sound finds you. A layered roar, almost mechanical in its regularity, but built from countless individual voices. A rhythmic grinding. Wet clicking. Something that might be breathing if breath could be measured in geological time.

---

## The Shape of the Land

This is a basin the size of a small sea, but barely a basin at all—so ancient, so flattened by time that "basin" describes only the theoretical shape, the ghost of a depression that existed before the earth settled into its final form. The horizon extends in every direction, unbroken by hills, interrupted only by presences that are not quite structures.

Elevation 1.0/3 — low, but the low places have been filling for longer than mountains have been eroding. What was once a valley floor has become a vast and level plane, compressed by the weight of accumulated time, pressed flat under the slow collapse of a universe's worth of sediment and memory.

The land is not quite solid. Walk carefully. Each step sinks slightly—not into mud, exactly, but into something that gives way with a reluctance that suggests awareness. The surface is dense with interlocking forms, so packed that there is no "between" anymore, no gaps between one presence and the next. Everything touches everything. Everything presses against everything.

---

## The Water That Will Not Leave

Water: 2.1/3 — not drowning, but perpetually present. The atmosphere holds moisture like a held breath, releasing it constantly in cold condensation that beads on every surface. There are no plants to drink it, no soil organisms to absorb it. The water simply accumulates, runs, pools, evaporates, and returns.

The ground weeps. Literally weeps—moisture seeping from between the layered forms, running in thin sheets across the mineral surfaces, collecting in depressions that shift and reform as the underlying structures move. Small pools appear and disappear. Thin streams find paths of least resistance and then abandon them. The water is patient. It has nowhere else to go.

Rain falls in intervals—not storms, but steady, inevitable precipitations from air so saturated that it can no longer hold itself together. Each droplet is cold, colder than the air, as if it has condensed from some lower, darker layer of the atmosphere and fallen reluctantly into this place.

There are no rivers, only seepage. Water moves through the landscape like blood through tissue, in channels too small to name, across surfaces that absorb and release and absorb again. The atmosphere is the same—close, damp, thick enough that distant presences blur into silhouettes, into suggestions.

The cold is a wet cold. It works into bone. It preserves rather than destroys. Nothing rots here; nothing decays in the traditional sense. Things are frozen in transition, caught mid-process, held in states of becoming that never complete.

---

## The Absence of Green

Vegetation: 0.4/3 — this is the wrong category entirely. There are no plants. There has never been anything that photosynthesizes, nothing that grew from seed or spore in the way we understand growing. The landscape is mineral, chemical, energetic—but not botanical.

And yet the ground is covered. The density parameter is correct: this place is packed, saturated, fully occupied. Every surface is occupied. But what occupies it is not plant life.

The closest analog might be fungal—if fungi could grow to the size of buildings, if mycelium could weave itself into crystalline structures, if fruiting bodies could persist for centuries in states of half-formation. But even "fungal" imposes a biological framework on something that operates differently here.

The landscape is built of overlapping growths—calcium deposits, silicate structures, organic-mineral composites that secrete themselves from the water and the air. They are not alive in any recognized sense, but they are not dead either. They grow. They respond. They produce sounds that might be metabolic, might be mechanical, might be something else entirely.

---

## The Industrial Process That Is Not Built

Tech: 2.5/3 — industrial. This is the strangest parameter, the one that forces invention. How can a place have an industrial tech level with no built structures?

The answer reveals itself slowly. The landscape itself *is* the industry. Not built—the word "built" implies a builder, an intention separate from the process. But *produced*. Manufactured. The plain is a vast production facility with no builder, a factory that grew rather than was constructed.

Look closer at the mineral-fungal structures. They are *processing* something. There are intake surfaces where water and dissolved minerals are absorbed. There are transformation chambers where chemical reactions occur with the precision and regularity of engines. There are output apertures where waste products—gases, liquids, crystallized residues—are released in timed pulses.

It is biological industry. Or geological industry. Or something that exists in the collapse of those categories.

The sound, in part, is this processing. A constant, low-frequency hum that vibrates through the soles of your feet. Clicking and grinding where surfaces rub against each other. Hissing where gases escape. A rhythm, almost mechanical in its regularity, but produced by organic-mineral processes that have evolved—or been evolved toward—industrial efficiency.

There are conveyances, but not conveyors. Channels and tubes and pathways that move materials through the landscape. Distribution networks built of secretion and growth. Production lines that operate by fluid dynamics and capillary action. The entire plain is a factory without architecture, an industrial process without designers.

## The Fauna That Feed on Process

Fauna: 2.3/3 — abundant. And here is where the ecosystem reveals itself.

In a world without plants, without primary production in the traditional sense, the animals that thrive are those that have adapted to feed on the industrial process itself. They are grazers, but they graze on mineral secretions. They are predators, but they hunt the energy patterns that move through the production networks.

The creatures are difficult to see—not because they hide, but because they have adapted to the dim light (light: 1.2/3) by becoming *of* the landscape. Their bodies are translucent, mineral-infused, reflective in ways that scatter the little light that exists. They move in schools, in herds, in flocks, though those words impose terrestrial frameworks on something more fluid.

There are grazers that move across the processing surfaces, their mouths adapted to scrape secretions, their digestive systems capable of breaking down mineral compounds. They make sounds—low, resonant calls that vibrate through the water-saturated air. Their movement is slow, deliberate, paced to the rhythm of the industrial processes around them.

There are larger presences that might be predators, but their predation is strange. They do not kill; they *interrupt*. They locate grazers at critical moments in their feeding and disrupt the energy exchange, absorbing the released heat, the chemical byproducts, the residual motion. They are interruption predators, evolved to feed on disruption.

And there are smaller forms—technically "animals" but functioning more like mobile components of the industrial process. They clean surfaces, they move materials from one processing zone to another, they maintain the flow. Symbionts with the landscape. Workers in a factory that no one designed.

The sounds they make layer into the industrial roar. Calls and responses. Warnings. The constant low vocalization of creatures that communicate through vibration in a dense medium. Sound travels differently here, through water and saturated air and mineral structures that conduct and amplify. The landscape itself is an instrument, and the animals play it continuously.

## The Weirdness at the Edge

Weirdness: 3.0/3 — reality breakdown.

This is where description falters. Where language, which assumes certain continuities, begins to fail.

The erosion parameter says this place is ancient—primordial, near its final form. But "ancient" implies a linear relationship with time, and time here has become unreliable. The landscape is old, impossibly old, but it is also *actively aging*. Not just subject to time, but *producing* time. The industrial processes may be manufacturing temporal effects as byproducts.

At the edges of perception, things become unstable. The horizon shimmers not with heat (it is cold here) but with probability. The dense packing of forms means that realities press against each other, overlap, interfere. What you see at the periphery may be present and absent simultaneously.

The fauna behave strangely at the boundaries. They approach the places where reality thins, where the industrial structures produce tears rather than products. They do not avoid these zones. They approach them, ingest something from them, move away changed. Their shapes shift. Their sounds modulate. They return to the herds with new frequencies, new patterns of behavior.

Sometimes the landscape produces things that should not exist. Structures that are present but have no history of formation. Sounds that anticipate events. Forms that imply functions that cannot be completed in this reality, that require dimensions not available here.

The industrial process, left to run without design for geological ages, has begun to produce *meaningless outputs*. Things that cannot be used. Things that exist only to exist. The breakdown is in the factory itself—the production has become unmoored from any purpose, and the purpose has become unmoored from any intent.

## The Sound That Is Always There

Sound: 2.3/3 — loud.

In a place with no silence, you stop hearing individual sounds and start perceiving the aggregate. A roar. A continuous presence that becomes a kind of quiet—not acoustic silence, but informational silence, the quiet of a system that conveys nothing because it conveys everything at once.

But there are layers. If you attend, you can separate them.

The deepest layer is the industrial processing—vibrations that travel through the ground, through the mineral-fungal structures, through the water that saturates everything. A rhythmic churning. The sound of production, continuous and inevitable.

Above that, the animal sounds. The lowing of grazers, pitched to carry through dense atmosphere. The clicking of interruption predators, echolocation adapted to a medium that conducts sound better than air. The high, thin whistles of the smaller symbiont-forms, communicating in frequencies that carry along the surfaces of the production structures.

Above that, the environmental sounds. Water moving—seeping, dripping, running in thin sheets. The pop and hiss of gas escaping from processing vents. The settling of structures, small movements as growth and pressure shift the mineral-fungal forms against each other.

And above that, or perhaps below it, the sounds that have no clear source. The sounds that might be reality grinding against itself. The sounds that might be temporal artifacts—noises from times adjacent to the present, leaking through. The sounds that might be the landscape dreaming, if landscapes dream.

It is never quiet. Even if you could silence the animals, the industrial processes, the water—there would still be the background hum of a reality that has begun to unravel, to produce sounds that exist without cause.

## The Light That Reveals and Obscures

Light: 1.2/3 — dim.

The atmosphere is thick with water, with suspended particles, with the byproducts of industrial processing. The light that reaches the surface is scattered, diffuse, almost directionless. Shadows are soft, multiple, unreliable.

Sources of illumination are distributed. There are no suns, no moons, no celestial bodies—only the faint bioluminescence of certain processing structures, the cold glow of chemical reactions occurring in mineral surfaces, the occasional flash of energy discharge from the reality-breakdown zones.

The light is blue-grey, washed out, almost monochromatic. It reveals form without revealing detail. The herds of grazers are visible as movement, as shape, but their surfaces blend into the landscape when they are still. The processing structures are visible as presences, but their boundaries are uncertain, their edges blurring into the surrounding atmosphere.

Visibility extends perhaps fifty meters. Beyond that, shapes become suggestions. The horizon is visible only as a slight darkening, a gradient rather than a line. The scale of the place (scale: 2.4/3 — open, regional) cannot be comprehended from any single point; it must be traversed, experienced across time, understood through accumulation.

In the breakdown zones, light behaves incorrectly. It pools in places that should not hold light. It moves in directions that do not match the source. It produces colors that exist only at the edges of perception, frequencies that the eye registers but cannot name.

## The Processes That Never Stop

Dynamic: 1.6/3 — active.

This is not a static landscape. It is a landscape of continuous motion, continuous change, continuous production.

The industrial processes cycle through phases—intake, transformation, output, rest. The cycles are regular, measurable, but not synchronized. Different regions of the plain operate on different rhythms, creating interference patterns where cycles overlap. At the boundaries between regions, the clash of rhythms produces turbulence, unpredictable effects, the conditions for reality breakdown.

The fauna move in patterns that respond to these cycles. Herds migrate between processing regions, following the peak production times, grazing on the secretions that flow most abundantly during specific phases. The interruption predators track the herds, and the symbiont-forms track both, creating a complex, multi-layered ecosystem that operates at the intersection of biological need and industrial rhythm.

Water cycles through the landscape—condensation, absorption, processing, release, re-condensation. The atmosphere is never stable, always moving between saturation and precipitation. The fog shifts, thins, thickens. Visibility changes not just with location but with time, with the phase of processing cycles, with the movement of herds that disturb the air.

And beneath all of this, the geological processes continue—the ancient, near-final erosion that has flattened the basin, the compression of accumulated time and material, the slow settling of everything toward some ultimate state. But here, geological time has been accelerated, incorporated into the industrial process. The landscape is not just eroding; it is *producing erosion*, manufacturing its own aging.

## The Density of Presence

Density: 2.6/3 — packed.

There is no empty space here. The landscape is saturated with presences—physical, temporal, dimensional.

Every surface is occupied by processing structures or the residues they produce. Every volume of air contains water, particles, organisms, the byproducts of industrial activity. Every moment is filled with sound, with movement, with the ongoing processes of production and breakdown.

The herds move through this saturation by becoming part of it. They do not move through empty space; they move by displacing, by integrating, by flowing through channels that open for them and close behind them. The landscape yields, but only where yielding is part of the process.

Even time is dense here. Moments pile on moments. The industrial process has been running for so long that its history has accumulated in layers, in strata, in the geological record of its own production. To walk across the plain is to walk through time as well as space, to pass through regions where past productions still echo, still influence the present.

And the reality breakdown adds another kind of density—the density of overlapping possibilities, of alternative states, of moments from adjacent timelines that have become visible, tangible, present. The landscape is packed not just with what is, but with what might have been, what might be, what cannot be but persists anyway.

---

## The Absence of the Made

Built: 0.3/3 — none.

And here is the deepest strangeness. Everything here is produced, but nothing is built. The distinction matters.

A built structure implies a builder—an agent who exists outside the process, who imposes form on matter according to a plan. There are no such agents here. There is no outside. The industrial landscape has produced itself, is producing itself, will continue to produce itself until the processes run down or the reality breakdown consumes everything.

The processing structures look like architecture. They have forms that suggest function, shapes that imply intention. But the intention is immanent, not transcendent. The structures have the form they have because that form works within the process—not because anyone decided that form was correct.

This is the difference between the grown and the built. Between the evolved and the designed. The industrial processes here have evolved toward efficiency, toward production, toward the maintenance of their own continuation. But they have not been designed. There is no designer.

The animals, too, have evolved within this system. They are not built; they are not even domesticated. They have adapted to the industrial landscape, incorporated it into their life cycles, become part of its function. They are workers in a factory that no one owns, participants in a process that no one controls.

## Standing at the Edge

If you were to stand at the edge of this landscape—if "edge" has meaning in a place where boundaries are uncertain—you would feel the cold moisture settle on your skin. You would hear the layered roar, overwhelming at first, then separating into its component sounds as your attention shifted. You would see the dim forms moving through the fog, the processing structures rising and falling in their slow rhythms, the flashes of incorrect light from the breakdown zones.

You would be the only built thing in a landscape that has forgotten what building means. You would be a presence that does not fit—too linear, too intentional, too temporary. The landscape would process you. Not reject you—this landscape does not reject. It incorporates. It produces. It continues.

The herds would move around you, curious or indifferent. The processing structures would shift their outputs to accommodate your presence, absorbing your heat, your moisture, your carbon. The reality breakdown zones would notice you, would taste the difference in your temporal signature, would reach toward you with effects that have no names.

And the sound would continue. The industrial roar, the animal calls, the water and the wind and the grinding of reality against itself. The continuous presence that becomes a kind of silence, a background so total that it contains no information, only the constant affirmation that something is happening, has been happening, will continue to happen long after the categories we use to understand it have lost their meaning.

This is the Exhalation Plains—a place of production without product, process without purpose, presence without form. It has always been here. It will always be here. Time moves differently around industrial processes that have run for geological ages.
