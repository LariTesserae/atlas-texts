# The Ascendant Mat

The ground here is not ground. You learn this in the first minute—your foot sinks not into soil but into a yielding, breathing substrate that gives three centimeters before meeting resistance. The Mat. Seventeen metres deep, they say, in most places. Deeper where it has found purchase in the fractures of the ancient stone platform beneath—stone that no living soul has seen in three thousand years, if anyone ever saw it at all, if the stone even exists and isn't simply the compressed remains of earlier Mat, earlier life, compressed into mineral mimicry by the weight of what grew after.

---

The heat arrives before dawn and does not leave until long after dark. It rises from below—a slow exhalation of thermal energy that the Mat has absorbed, transformed, and now releases in a continuous pulse. By mid-morning, the air shimmers. By noon, the distant columns of the Standing Giants waver and swim like dreams trying to remember themselves.

This is high country. The air should be thin, cold, clarifying. Instead, the Mat has created its own atmosphere. The moisture it traps in its upper layers—the morning's condensation, the sporadic rains that arrive from the distant cloudshelves to the south—evaporates in the heat, creating a humid envelope that presses against the sky itself. A vast bubble of life-breath held above the world.

You cannot see the edges of the place. The Giants march to every horizon, their spacing regular as a planned orchard, though no mind planned this—only the ancient logic of competition and cooperation encoded in cellular memory, each massive plant-tower exactly far enough from its neighbours to avoid direct shadow, exactly close enough that their canopy-branches interlace a hundred metres above, forming a partial ceiling that filters the light to a green-gold glow.

---

The sound begins in darkness.

Before true dawn—before the light has crested the eastern edge of the Mat—you hear it. A thrumming. A vibration in the air that you feel more than hear, pressing against your chest like a second heartbeat. Then the layered voices join: the chittering of the small burrowers in the Mat's upper layers, the territorial calls of the perch-dwellers in the Giants' lower branches, the far-off rumble of something large moving through the open spaces between columns.

By full dawn, the soundscape has become a wall. You cannot think around it. Your thoughts must compress, move sideways, find the narrow channels between the noise. This is why the Old Ones—the ones who built the scattered remnants you sometimes find, the low stone platforms and the carved handholds in certain Giants' bark-sheaths—this is why they left no writing, perhaps. Sound erases. Sound replaces. The mouth has better uses than words here.

---

The moisture comes unpredictably. Some mornings you wake to find the Mat slick with dew that has condensed from the air during the cold hours, the drops caught in the million tiny cups of the surface vegetation. You learn to press your canteen against these cups, to let them drain into the metal. The water tastes green. It tastes like intention.

Other days bring the rains—sudden, violent, horizontal. Storms that arrive with no warning from the cloudless western sky, moving fast across the vast plateau of the Mat. The Giants creak and groan in the wind, their massive forms swaying in unison like a congregation in prayer. The sound multiplies—vegetation groaning against vegetation, the Mat below you sighing as it compresses under the weight of absorbed water, the creatures screaming their alarm or their joy or their territorial insistence that the storm does not change what belongs to whom.

Then the rain passes. The heat returns. The Mat releases its captured moisture upward in a rising pillar of steam that the wind catches and spreads. For hours afterward, the air is thick enough to chew.

---

The structures are rare, and getting rarer.

In the first weeks, you found them every few days—a low platform of fitted stone emerging from the Mat's surface like a buried secret, or a series of carved handholds ascending a single Giant, or once, beautifully, a complete shelter wedged between two column-trunks, its framework made from the living branches of a parasitic vine that had been trained and shaped and bound until it formed walls, a roof, a door-frame. Inside: the faint impression of a sleeping-form in the dust that had accumulated on the floor, and nothing else. No tools. No offerings. No explanation for why someone built here, lived here, and then left no more trace than this.

You have begun to understand: the Mat eats shelter. Not quickly—never quickly—but with the patient hunger of something that has learned that time is on its side. The stone platforms slowly sink; the Mat's root-systems find their fractures and widen them. The trained vines grow wild again, forget their shapes. The handholds in the bark are absorbed as new growth covers them, heals them, makes them forget they were ever carved.

Nothing here is permanent. But nothing here is truly destroyed. The Mat remembers in its own fashion—every structure is taken in, incorporated, becomes part of the deep substrate where compression and time will transform it into something new.

---

The creatures are everywhere. You cannot see all of them, but you hear them, feel them, taste them in the air.

The Mat-scurriers—small, six-legged furred things with too many teeth—travel in bands of twelve to twenty, their motion visible as a ripple in the surface vegetation. They ignore you. They have no reason to fear anything that moves as slowly as you do. When they pass, you feel the vibration through your feet before you see their passage.

The perch-hunters work the middle heights of the Giants—reptilian, though the word means nothing here, where the categories of other places do not apply. Their scales catch the filtered light and flash green and gold. They call to each other in frequencies that hover at the edge of hearing—a whine that rises and falls, rises and falls, like breath turned into sound. You have seen them descend on prey, and you have learned not to watch. The Mat receives what falls. The Mat is patient.

Above the canopy, where the Giants interlace their crowns, dwell the drifters—gas-filled sac-creatures that ride the thermal currents, their trailing tentacles gathering moisture and whatever unfortunate flying things cross their paths. They sing in the deep frequencies, a sound you feel in your chest more than hear. At night, they descend slightly, and their underside glows with bioluminescence—a second sky of soft blues and greens floating above the Giants' canopy.

And then there are the deep-walkers. You have seen them only twice. They emerge from the Mat at irregular intervals—you cannot predict when, cannot find a pattern—rising from below with slow inevitability. Their bodies are long, segmented, covered in the very vegetation they emerge from, so that at first you think it is simply the Mat rising, folding over itself. But then the eyes open—dozens of them, ringing each segment—and the mouth-parts begin their slow work of ingesting everything in the path. You have learned to move. You have learned that the deep-walkers are not predators, not in any sense you understand. They are processors. The Mat grows too full, too dense, and the deep-walkers are how it eats itself, how it makes room for new growth. Their emergence is not hunting. It is digestion.

---

You have been walking for seventeen days. You do not know if you are any closer to the center—or if there is a center, or if the Mat has a center, or if "center" is a concept that applies to something that grows outward from itself, that expands at every edge simultaneously, that has been expanding for longer than anyone can remember.

You have begun to notice patterns that might not be patterns. The spacing of the Giants changes subtly every few days of travel—sometimes closer together, sometimes wider apart. The composition of the Mat shifts, too. In some regions, the surface layer is soft and springs back under your feet. In others, it is almost rigid, compressed into something like wood. The fauna changes with each shift—you see species in the tight-spaced regions that never appear where the Giants grow far apart, and vice versa.

Is this a single organism or a community? You cannot tell. The boundaries blur. The Giants communicate through their root-systems—the Mat vibrates with their chemical signals, their slow conversations about water and light and the position of the sun. The creatures are born from the Mat in some fashion—you have seen the small burrowers emerge from pupation-sacs that grow directly from the vegetation—and you have watched the perch-hunters lay their eggs in the Giant bark, where the trees' own systems absorb and protect them until hatching.

Everything here emerges from everything else. Everything here returns to everything else. The categories you brought with you—plant and animal, structure and organism, self and other—have softened, run together, become less useful than they once were.

---

On the nineteenth day, you find another structure.

This one is different. Larger. A platform of stone and compressed Mat-material, raised a full metre above the surface, its edges still sharp, its construction still visible. The Mat has not yet absorbed this one. On the platform: marks. Lines carved into the stone. You kneel, run your fingers along them, and feel the deliberate pressure of a hand, a tool, an intention.

The lines form patterns—circles within circles, crossed by diagonal marks. You do not know what they mean. But you recognize what they are: an assertion. Someone stood here. Someone built. Someone said, with stone and time and labor: *I am here. This is mine. Remember me.*

The Mat will swallow this too, eventually. In another century, perhaps, or another millennium—the deep-walkers will pass through, or the roots will find the fractures, or the surface will simply grow over it as the layers accumulate. But for now, for this moment, someone's assertion persists.

You sit on the platform as the afternoon heat builds toward its peak. The soundscape washes over you—the endless, layered, deafening chorus of life processing life. The Mat breathes below your feet. The Giants creak overhead. The deep-walkers move through their slow cycles in the darkness beneath.

And somewhere in the vastness, something new is growing.
