# The Inclosure

The first thing you notice is that nothing here was born.

---

The walls arrive before understanding does. They are close—too close for a landscape, just right for a room, and that tension never resolves. Stone, but not quarried; ceramic, but not fired; some third thing that holds the warmth of a kiln and the grain of mud but admits no origin story. They rise on all sides, meeting overhead in a vault that might be architecture or might be the inside of a skull. The scale is that of a modest chapel, a root-cellary kind of volume. You could touch both walls if you stretched. You could press your palms to the ceiling if you stood on your toes. The floor is the same material, warm as skin left in the sun.

There is no water. There has never been water. This fact becomes obvious not through absence but through the behavior of what grows: nothing here drinks. Nothing here *can*.

---

The vegetation is too lush for the space, and that is the second violation. Vines and fronds and fungal shelves climb every surface in a density that borders on suffocation—green and amber and a bruised purple that shouldn't exist in plant tissue, all of it pressing toward the center, toward you, though nothing moves. The leaves are waxy and cool and when you touch them they do not bend; they *give*, like leather, like something that was crafted to approximate flexibility. The stems have seams. Not veins—*seams*, the kind that appear where two pieces of material have been joined. You run your finger along one and feel the faint ridge, the almost-imperceptible mismatch where one side was attached to the other.

Someone made these. Someone with hands and patience and no knowledge of biology built every leaf, curled every tendril, stained every petal. The work would have taken lifetimes. The work would have taken more than lifetimes. And yet the toolmarks are crude—scrapes, gouges, the evidence of fingers and simple edges. No precision instruments. No metal. Just persistence and a kind of desperate thoroughness.

There are flowers. They are terrible. Each petal is a separate piece, lapped and layered and fixed with an adhesive that has darkened to the color of old blood. They do not smell. They do not wilt. They are permanently, aggressively in bloom.

---

The fauna is worse, because it moves.

Slowly—so slowly that you have to watch for a long time before you're sure—but it moves. Things the size and shape of lizards, except their scales are fitted like shingles, with the same tiny gaps, the same irregular hand-placement. Things like beetles that move on legs articulated with visible pins, their carapaces etched with decorative patterns that serve no survival purpose. A creature like a snake, if a snake were a hose of stitched leather filled with something that shifts independently of its skin.

They do not vocalize. They do not fight or mate or eat. They traverse the surfaces of the Inclosure with patient, purposeful routes, following grooves in the walls that might be wear-marks or might be paths laid out for them, and they do not acknowledge you. Sometimes two of them meet at an intersection and pause, and the pause lasts for hours, and then they continue. This might be communication. This might be collision resolution. There is no way to tell.

They also do not drink. There are no water sources. There are no waste products. The warm air—pleasant, blood-temperature, carrying a faint mineral smell like pottery dust—neither dries them nor sustains them. They are closed systems. They were *made* to be closed systems.

---

The quiet is not silence. Silence would be the absence of sound. This is the *suppression* of sound—your footsteps register as pressure changes in your ears but produce no echo; the leaves do not rustle when you brush them; the creatures make no sound even when their limbs scrape across the walls. It is as though the air itself has been *told* not to carry vibration, and it obeys with the same blank compliance as everything else here.

But if you stand still long enough, you become aware of a low, cyclic hum that doesn't come from anywhere. It comes from the walls themselves, from the floor, from the warmth in the stone. It is the sound of a process happening at a scale too large and a pace too slow for any ear to properly hear—a frequency you feel in your molars, in the joints of your fingers. Something is being maintained. Something is being *kept*.

---

The weirdness accumulates like sediment.

At first it's small: the way a shadow doesn't quite track its object, or the way a vine seems to have grown an extra leaf since you last looked. Then it's larger: a section of wall that was blank yesterday now bears a low relief of a flower that doesn't match any of the ones growing from it, and the edges of the carving are sharp and new, as though it was made while you slept. But you didn't sleep. You haven't slept. There is no day or night here, only the same steady, sourceless light—warm-white, shadow-soft, casting no hard lines—that illuminates everything with the evenness of a museum display.

And then there is the place where the wall meets the ceiling and the line between them is not a right angle, not a curve, but a *fold*—a crease, as though the surface of the room was paper and someone pinched it. You stare at it and it stares back. Your eyes refuse to focus on it. Your brain offers explanations—optical illusion, defect in the material—but none of them account for the way your hand, when you reach toward it, seems to approach from the wrong direction. As though the geometry of your arm is being rewritten as it gets closer.

You pull back. The fold remains. It was not there before. You are sure it was not there before.

---

The Inclosure is young. That is the hardest thing to accept. For all its density, its accumulated detail, its lifetimes of craftsmanship evident in every leaf-scale and carved stem, the space is *new*. The edges of things are sharp. The joins between wall sections haven't settled. The vegetation hasn't had time to find its own way—it follows the grooves and channels that were made for it with a fidelity that no living growth would maintain. The creatures follow their paths with the same blank obedience. Nothing here has had time to drift, to rebel, to make mistakes.

Which means this—all of this—was made recently. Made fast. Made with the urgency of a shelter thrown up before a storm, except the shelter is a cathedral-sized terrarium of hand-built life, and the storm is something you cannot perceive.

Or maybe the storm is you. Maybe the fold in the ceiling appeared when you entered. Maybe the new carvings on the wall are a response to your presence, the room *reacting*, building new features the way a body builds scar tissue. Maybe the weirdness isn't a property of the Inclosure but a symptom of its incompatibility with something that came from outside—something like you, something made of water and chemistry and spontaneous order, something that doesn't belong in a place where everything is made and nothing is born.

---

The warmth continues. The creatures continue their routes. The vegetation presses in, dense and lush and hand-crafted, and somewhere a new leaf is being built right now, or a new creature is being assembled in a crevice you can't see, or a new fold is forming in the geometry of the room.

It is patient. It is slow. It is very, very thorough.

And there is no door.
