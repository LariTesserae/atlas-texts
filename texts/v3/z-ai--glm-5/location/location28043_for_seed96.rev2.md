# The Terraces of Still Water

It begins as a wall.

Not a wall in the way you might expect — not a boundary or a fortification — but a wall in the way a cliff is a wall, sheer and unavoidable, rising out of the mist that clings to everything here. Except the mist isn't mist exactly. It's closer to breath. The air is so saturated that moisture condenses on any surface cooler than itself, and at this elevation, in this coolness, nearly every surface qualifies. So the wall breathes. The ground breathes. You breathe, and the air drinks you back.

The wall is made. Everything here is made. There is no natural substrate beneath it, no bedrock it was carved from — or if there once was, it has been so thoroughly covered that it no longer matters. The wall is constructed of small units: hand-fired bricks, each one slightly irregular, each one laid with a care that suggests someone understood this place would need to hold water for a very long time. The mortar between them is pale, almost white, and where moisture weeps through the joints — which is everywhere, constantly — it leaves mineral traces that have built up over time into thin ridges, like the growth rings of something patient and stony.

Behind the wall, the landscape opens. Or rather, it *levels*. A series of terraces step downward, each one perhaps a man's height below the last, broad and flat, stretching outward into a distance that the bright light and condensing air make difficult to judge. The light here is extraordinary — not warm, not cold, but *clear* in a way that seems to strip the film from everything. Shadows are hard-edged and dark. Surfaces that catch the light directly are almost too bright to look at. The sky above is pale, almost white, though whether from cloud cover or some quality of the atmosphere itself is impossible to say. There is no sun visible, only light, diffuse and total.

The terraces are paved. Not with stone — there is no stone here that wasn't first a brick or a tile — but with fitted slabs of fired clay, their surfaces smoothed by hand and then by water. Each terrace slopes almost imperceptibly toward channels that run along their edges, and the channels connect to downspouts that drop water to the terrace below, where it spreads across the paving, finds the next channel, drops again. The whole landscape is a slow, deliberate cascade. Water enters the system from somewhere above — from the wall, from seams in the highest terrace, from the air itself — and moves downward through it with a patience that makes glacier motion look hasty.

The sound is what stays with you. Or rather, the *almost*-sound. Water moving over clay at this pace barely murmurs. It's more of a texture than a sound — a continuous soft friction, like cloth drawn slowly across cloth. Here and there, a droplet falls from a downspout lip into a channel below, and each one lands with a small clear note that carries further than you'd expect in the dense air. But these notes are rare, isolated, and the silence between them has a quality of *attention* to it, as though the landscape is listening to itself.

There are structures on the terraces. Low structures, rarely taller than a person, built from the same fired brick and pale mortar. Some are cisterns — circular, open-topped, their interiors dark with still water that reflects the white sky. Some are shelters of a kind, though they don't seem designed for habitation: they have no clear entrance, or multiple entrances too narrow to use comfortably, or walls that don't quite meet at the corners. Their function, if they have one, is opaque. They might be markers. They might be memory. They might be the places where the makers rested, and the rest became permanent, and the resting became part of the construction.

Because this is the thing: this place was made by hand. Every brick was formed, fired, carried, and placed by someone using simple tools and their own body. The scale of labor is difficult to comprehend. The terraces extend in every direction, hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, stepping down and down toward some low point you cannot reach from here. Each one paved. Each one channeled. Each one edged with low walls and punctuated with structures whose purpose you can only guess at. And the work is *recent* — the bricks are sharp-cornered, the mortar only lightly stained, the mineral deposits thin. This is not an ancient ruin. This is a place that was finished, or nearly finished, not long ago. By hands that are no longer here.

No plants grow in the channels. No algae greens the still water in the cisterns. No insects skim the surface, no birds drink from the weeping walls. The absence is total and specific, as though life was considered and declined, or was never a possibility here, or was deliberately excluded by some property of the materials or the water or the light. The water itself is clear — not perfectly transparent, but carrying a faint mineral opacity, like very thin milk — and it has no smell, or rather, it has the smell of clean mineral: clay, calcium, the ghost of heat from the kiln.

The density of the place is its most overwhelming quality. There is no empty space. Every surface is occupied — by water, by structure, by the mineral traces of water's passage, by light reflected and re-reflected between wet surfaces until the air itself seems to glow. The terraces are broad, but they don't feel open, because the mist — the breath, the condensation — limits sight to perhaps a hundred meters in any direction, and within that hundred meters everything is *present*, everything is *there*, packed and saturated and fully itself. You cannot find a spot where nothing is happening. Water is always moving, slowly. Light is always reflecting. Bricks are always weeping.

And the place is young. That's the strangest part. The strangeness isn't dramatic — nothing here is grotesque or impossible or surreal — but there is a quality of *newness* to everything that sits oddly against the scale of the construction. The mortar hasn't weathered. The channels haven't smoothed. The mineral deposits are thin, tentative, just beginning their patient decoration. This enormous landscape of hand-fired brick and slow water was built recently, by hands that worked without machines, without metal more complex than simple tools, and then the hands left. And the water came. Or the water was always coming, and the construction was built to receive it, to shape its passage, to give it a path that meant something to someone.

Now the water moves through the terraces in its slow cycle — condensing, collecting, channeling, falling, spreading, condensing again — and nothing else happens. The light brightens and dims on some cycle you can't quite track. The temperature stays cool, steady, the kind of cool that isn't cold but never warms. The silence holds, broken only by the friction of water on clay and the occasional clear note of a falling drop.

It is a landscape that was made for something. A purpose was built into every slope, every channel, every structure. But the purpose is not apparent, and the makers are gone, and what remains is a place that does exactly what it was built to do — move water, slowly, through a vast hand-made landscape — without anyone to remember why.
