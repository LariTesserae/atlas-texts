The sound arrives before anything else—a low, continuous grinding that rises through the soles of the feet, fills the skull, presses against the eardrums until the whole body becomes a listening instrument. Not noise exactly. More like the voice of a throat that has never learned to shape air into words.

The plateau hangs at an altitude where air should thin to nothing, yet it doesn't. Something else thins instead. The horizon wavers, not from heat shimmer but from something pulling at the edge of visibility—a fraying. Standing here feels like standing at the threshold of a room you almost remember entering before.

Below, in the valley-scale expanse that stretches outward from the plateau's edge, the vegetation moves. Not in wind—there is no wind—but in response to something else. Fields of stalks, each one taller than a person stands, translucent and ribbed like the inside of a throat, colors ranging from bruised violet to the gray-green of old scars. They grow in precise geometric spirals that suggest intention without any clear intelligence behind it. No roots reach into soil. The stalks simply emerge from the rock face at intervals, drawing substance from some chemistry that has nothing to do with water. When examined closely, each stalk contains a slow internal pulse, fluid moving through capillaries that shouldn't exist in something that looks like plant matter. The fluid isn't sap. It gleams with the viscosity of something closer to mercury, though it carries no metallic scent. Whatever these things are, they drink from somewhere else—not from any aquifer or rainfall, but from the place where the horizon keeps threatening to unravel.

The lushness of it creates its own kind of wrongness. Lushness usually implies water, decay, the wet rot of organic cycle. Here the abundance feels desiccated despite its density—every stalk rigid, preserved, locked in a state between growth and mineralization. As if the vegetation is being remembered into existence rather than grown.

Scattered across the open expanse, structures stand at irregular intervals—too small to be dwellings, too deliberate to be geological formations. Each one rises from the stone in rough-hewn spirals, walls built from the same material as the plateau itself, excavated rather than imported. Hand-tools left marks. Chisel grooves that follow no standardized pattern, suggesting different makers working at different times, perhaps across centuries or across minutes—time does something peculiar here, bunching and stretching like cloth gathered in a fist. The structures appear to be markers of some kind. Or perhaps anchor points. Each one hums at a frequency just below conscious hearing, adding its thread to the vast droning chord that fills the valley.

The sound comes from everywhere and nowhere. A low-frequency pressure that vibrates in the chest cavity. Standing still long enough, the sound becomes visible—something at the edge of peripheral vision, a shimmering in the air that correlates to the deepest thrumming notes. The vegetation resonates in sympathy. The stalks shiver in waves that travel outward from each structure, creating interference patterns where multiple waves meet. At these intersection points, the air takes on a different quality. Looking through them reveals the landscape slightly askew, as if seen through flawed glass. Colors shift. Distances compress. A stalk that stands thirty paces away might appear suddenly within arm's reach, while the hand itself becomes uncertain, doubtful of its own dimensions.

The temperature sits in that range that feels neither warm nor cold but simply present—the kind of cool that suggests an absence of heat rather than an active chill. This is the temperature of stone left in shadow for millennia. Of places where no sun has ever fully committed to touching.

No birds call. No insects scratch against the stalks. No bones lie scattered among the geometric growth. The absence of fauna feels absolute—this is not a place where animals avoid. It's a place where the concept of animal life finds no purchase, as if the category itself has been revoked from the local lexicon of possibility. Even imaginary beasts feel wrong here. Trying to picture a creature moving between the stalks produces a mental static, a refusal. The mind skids away from the image.

The geology explains part of the strangeness. This is new rock—or rather, rock in the process of becoming. The plateau rises in what can only be described as a slow explosion, material welling up from below at a pace visible only through prolonged observation. Watch a single point for an hour and the surface rises by perhaps the width of a hair. The emerging stone carries with it traces of something that isn't quite mineral—veins of material that gleam wet in the dim light but evaporate within moments of reaching the air, leaving behind channels and pores that give the new surface a texture like bone marrow, like pumice dreamed by something that had never seen volcanic glass. The stalks favor these fresh extrusions, clustering most densely where the newest rock emerges.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. A permanent crepuscular glow that casts no shadows in any single direction. The plateau sits above the cloud layer, or what passes for clouds here—masses of vapor too thin to be called fog, too thick to be called air, moving in slow currents that carry no moisture. They diffuse whatever light reaches this altitude into a flat, even illumination that makes distances difficult to judge. Looking upward offers no help. The sky above has no sun, no stars, no moon—just layers of increasingly translucent atmosphere that eventually give way to something else. Something that looks like the inside of a eyelid seen from beneath. Or like a membrane stretched tight across a frame too large to comprehend.

Walking among the stalks, the sound changes pitch based on direction. Moving toward one of the structures causes the drone to deepen, to rattle the teeth. Moving away from any marker shifts the frequency higher, though it never becomes what could be called pleasant—always the same fundamental character, that grinding voice pressing against the limits of what a throat should be able to produce. After extended exposure, the sound stops being heard and starts being inhabited. The body adjusts. The drone becomes a kind of silence, and silence itself becomes the anomaly—something that startles when it appears.

One of the structures, different from the others, bears marks that suggest attempted communication. Grooves cut into the stone that might be writing, might be counting, might be madness given linear form. The marks are worn, partially regrown over by the stone's slow emergence, as if the plateau itself is healing over the wounds of inscription. Touching them produces a sensation in the fingertips—not pain exactly, but a wrongness, a certainty that the nerves are reporting something that doesn't match the visual evidence. The stone feels older than stone should feel. It feels like touching a memory of stone, or the idea of stone before stone was invented.

The structures weren't built all at once. Radiocarbon dating would be meaningless here, but the layering of techniques suggests multiple periods of construction—or rather, multiple periods of visitation. Some walls rise from bedrock that was already ancient when newer sections were added. The oldest sections show tool marks from materials that couldn't have produced them—chisel widths that suggest metal in a place where no metal exists, finishes too smooth for hand-tools of any kind. As if visitors came from elsewhere, bringing their technologies with them, and those technologies lingered in the stone like a scent. The newest additions are crude by comparison. Stone on stone, wedged together without mortar, relying on gravity and the plateaus peculiar physics to maintain their shape.

At the plateau's edge, where the land falls away into valleys that descend toward depths no instrument has measured, the vegetation stops abruptly. A hard line, stalks growing right up to an invisible boundary and then nothing. Beyond that boundary, the landscape breaks down. Not into emptiness, but into something worse—into fragments of landscape. Chunks of terrain floating in configurations that deny continuity. A mountain fragment hanging at a perpendicular angle to the valley wall. A section of what might be sky reflected in a surface that shouldn't be able to hold reflections. And between these fragments, regions where light itself gives up, where the eye finds nothing to interpret and fills the gap with whatever it can imagine—usually faces, usually mouths, usually things trying to speak.

The sound originates from these breaks. Or rather, it spills through them. A low, continuous moaning that varies in pitch across durations too long for the human ear to track without instruments—days, perhaps, for a single cycle from lowest to highest note and back. Combined with the constant mid-frequency drone, the result creates harmonics that produce the sensation of being addressed. The certainty that information is being conveyed. The frustration of being unable to decode it.

This is a place of emergence. The plateau is still being born, the stalks are still being designed, the structures are still being remembered into position. The process moves slowly—patiently—but it moves. Standing perfectly still for long enough, watching a single point on the horizon, the whole landscape seems to inhale. A subtle expansion. The vegetation leans outward by millimeters. The structures shift in their settings. The sound modulates toward something that might, given enough time, resolve into the first syllable of a word that might, given even more time, explain everything that's happening here. But the breath catches. The syllable never comes. The landscape settles back into its previous configuration and begins again.

The sound continues.
