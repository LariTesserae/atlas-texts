# The resonant steppe

There is no water here. This is the first fact that reorders everything you understand about what grows.

## The substrate

The ground beneath your feet is not soil — it is root. Not roots *in* something, but root as primary matter, layers upon layers of dead and living fiber compressed into something firm enough to walk on, springy enough to feel wrong. Each step sinks slightly and rebounds. The surface is a mat of material that has accumulated for longer than the concept of accumulation has existed. Where it breaks open — and it breaks open constantly, from the turbulence — you can see the cross-section: strata of growth, death, and regrowth, fibrous and particulate, in bands of amber, rust, near-black, and pale cream. No water moves through these layers. They are chemically dry in a way that your bones recognize as hostile, though the air itself carries a coolness that suggests otherwise.

The root-substrate extends downward for unknown depth. It is conjectured that this is all that remains of an older world — that the vegetation ate the stone, ate the soil, ate the water itself, and then learned to metabolize without it. The chemistry is not aqueous. Something else moves through these plant bodies: a thin, yellow-tinged compound that smells of sulfur and copper when it breaks surface. The plants drink metal, exude oxide, breathe a constant exchange of minerals into atmosphere. It is an industrial ecology in the profoundest sense — no distinction between the biological and the mineral processing.

## The visible forms

This is a high place. The elevation is not marked by mountains — the substrate itself rises, a continental shelf of vegetable matter that has accumulated so thickly it created its own altitude. The air is thin. The light is unfiltered and violent in its brightness, reflecting off the pale surfaces of the growth-forms until the world burns white at the edges of vision.

The plants here have no need for the strategies of water-world vegetation. There are no leaves in the traditional sense. What rises from the substrate are structures more akin to lattices, scaffolding, vast frameworks of fibrous material that grow upward in continuous extension. Some stand alone, singular towers of vegetation that reach heights you cannot measure — the scale defeats the eye. Others grow in clusters, their lattice-structures interlocking at intervals, forming something like cities of growth, though there is no design, no plan, only the reaching of many bodies into the same space.

The spaces between these growths are where the sparseness reveals itself. This is a place of vast distances. From any vantage point, you can see the next cluster of lattice-towers perhaps a kilometer distant, and the next beyond that, fading into atmospheric haze. The emptiness between forms is not empty at all — it is filled with motion, with sound, with the constant turbulence of air moving through and around these vast vegetable bodies.

## The sound

It is deafening.

The sound has no single source. It emerges from the place itself — the cumulative resonance of wind through uncountable lattices, each one a massive vegetable wind-chime tuned to frequencies that predate hearing. The sound is not a roar but a chord, harmonic and shifting, made from the air passing through hollow growth-structures that have evolved specifically to resonate. Some of the plants are instrument-like, grown into forms that catch wind and translate it into tone. The purpose is not clear — perhaps communication, perhaps some metabolic exchange that requires vibration, perhaps a byproduct of structural evolution that serves no function the plants themselves recognize.

In the near presence of a lattice-cluster, the sound becomes physical. You feel it in the chest, in the teeth, in the joints. It is cool and bright somehow, matching the light, a sound that suggests clarity rather than violence, though its volume crosses into violence. To stand near the largest growths is to lose the capacity for thought — the resonance fills the skull, leaves no space for interior voice. The fauna that lives here have evolved their own strategies: some lack hearing entirely, some have developed acoustic isolation, some communicate through the same frequencies, riding the sound rather than fighting it.

## The fauna

Life moves through this place abundantly, though you must learn to see it. The animals here are not separate from the vegetation — they live within it, on it, as part of it. The largest lattice-growth visible from where you stand — a structure perhaps four hundred meters in height, its base a tangle of interconnected fiber-columns — hosts an ecology in its scaffolding. You can see movement in the upper reaches: creatures that navigate the lattice, their bodies elongated and flexible, designed to slip through the gaps in the vegetable architecture.

They are not mammals. They are not reptiles or insects. The categories fail. They are something else entirely, the product of an evolutionary stream that diverged before the emergence of familiar forms. Their bodies appear metallic in the bright light, but this is exoskeletal deposit — they accumulate mineral compounds from the plants they consume and form them into external structure. Some have wings that are not wings but extensions of their mineralized bodies, membranes stretched between grown struts, allowing them to ride the turbulence between growth-forms.

The abundance is vertical. The ground-level is the quietest zone, though quiet here is still loud beyond enduring. Above, the lattice-structures host populations of these mineral-beings in numbers that would seem impossible if they did not move so fluidly through the spaces between growth. They are the pollinators, the grazers, the predators of this world. Some have grown so large they can be seen from the substrate below, drifting between growths like animate architecture themselves.

## The turbulence

Nothing here is still.

The dynamic parameter expresses itself as constant motion — the air never rests, the growths sway even at scale, the substrate shifts as underground structures expand and contract with some metabolic rhythm. The turbulence is not random but patterned beyond your capacity to read the patterns. This place is chaotic in the mathematical sense, a system so complex that prediction fails, but it operates according to its own rules.

The turbulence creates and destroys continuously. The sound you hear is the sound of the old falling — somewhere in the vast distance, a lattice-growth has reached the end of its structural integrity and is collapsing in slow motion, the sound of its fall traveling across the steppe for hours. But the turbulence also seeds new growth — the same winds that undermine the old structures carry propagules to new sites, deposit them in the mineral substrate, begin the slow upward reaching that will continue for centuries.

The vegetation grows upward and outward without end. There is no pruning, no harvest, no clearance. The dead growths fall and become the substrate for what follows. The living growths continue their extension, reaching into the bright air, catching wind, resonating. It is a Solaris-like state — creation and destruction happening simultaneously across a scale that defeats any single perspective.

## The industrial character

The technology reading — industrial — requires explanation. There are no factories here. No engines, no machines, no constructed structures. But this is an industrial world in the truest sense: it performs the functions of industry without distinction between the natural and the manufactured.

The lattice-growths are processing centers. They draw compounds from the substrate and atmosphere, metabolize them through systems that are recognizably industrial in function, and excrete the byproducts into the air and ground. The yellow-tinged compound that moves through the plants is a solvent, a catalyst, a medium of exchange. The mineral deposits visible in the substrate are the result of long-term processing — this place has been refining the earth beneath it for epochs.

The sound itself is industrial. It does not simply happen — the plants have evolved to shape it. The lattice-structures are engineered by evolution to produce specific frequencies when wind passes through them. These frequencies serve purposes: perhaps breaking down mineral compounds, perhaps communicating across distances, perhaps driving the metabolic processes of the plants themselves. It is an engine that runs on wind and light and the slow transformation of matter.

The scale is industrial as well. This is not a field or a forest — it is an operation of continental magnitude, a transformation of environment that operates continuously across the entire visible world. The growth-forms extend to the horizon in every direction, and beyond that, more of the same. This is not a place but a process, and the process has no edges.

## The light

Brightness here is a presence. The sun — or whatever star this world orbits — is not remarkable, but the atmosphere catches and amplifies illumination. The lattice-growths reflect and refract, the mineral-accumulated surfaces of fauna flash in flight, the substrate itself is pale enough to throw light back upward.

The brightness reveals everything. There are no shadows in the traditional sense — the lattice-structures create patterns of light and dark, but the overall illumination is so complete that darkness becomes texture rather than negation. To exist here is to be visible. The fauna have evolved strategies for this — some are transparent, some are reflective, some move too quickly to be seen clearly. The plants have no need for hiding. They simply are, vast and bright and undeniable.

## The rules of this world

The absence of water has shaped everything. Life here does not depend on hydration but on mineral exchange. The solvent-compound that replaces water is efficient but limited — it does not pool, does not flow freely, exists only within biological systems. There are no rivers, no lakes, no rain. The atmosphere carries moisture-like compounds in particulate form, and the plants capture these through specialized surfaces that have evolved for atmospheric extraction.

The high elevation means thin air, which explains the intensity of light but also the unusual behavior of sound. The thin atmosphere carries resonance differently — perhaps contributing to the overwhelming acoustic experience. The coolness is deceptive; without water, the temperature fluctuations are extreme between illuminated and shaded zones, but the plants regulate their own internal temperatures through metabolic processes that would be familiar to any industrial engineer: heat exchange, fluid movement, controlled oxidation.

The erosion reading — established, in balance — suggests that this is not a new state. The substrate has accumulated over time beyond reckoning. The growth-forms have reached a kind of equilibrium with their environment. The turbulence is not degradation but renewal — the constant change prevents stagnation without threatening the overall system. This place has been this way for longer than it has been observed, and it may remain this way long after observation ends.

## What it is to be here

To stand on this high, vast steppe of accumulated root-matter is to be surrounded by sound so total it becomes a kind of silence — the silence of being filled completely, of having no space left for anything else. To see the bright shapes rising into brighter air, to feel the coolness that carries no moisture, to watch the mineral-creatures navigate the lattice-work of vegetable cities — this is to understand that water is not necessary for abundance, that life can emerge from different chemistry, that the categories you know are local to your own experience.

This place does not need you. It does not need anything. It processes and grows and resonates and falls and grows again, and the sound of its own existence is so loud it drowns out the possibility of meaning, and yet it continues, and yet it is, and yet it may continue long after everything you recognize as life has been transformed into substrate for something else entirely.
