# The Hatchery

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before the light, before the warmth, before any sense of the walls. A roaring wet chaos of clicking and whistling, of many bodies rubbing against many bodies, of something like wind but wrong—gusty, directional, alive. The sound has layers. Underneath the high thin whistles there is a continuous low grinding, like pebbles being sorted by an unseen hand, and underneath that, something rhythmic. Pulsing. The stone itself seems to breathe.

You descend through a crack in the world—a gap that wasn't there a generation ago, that may not be there in another. The rock around you is warm to the touch, and damp. Not the cold seep of deep caves, but something closer to sweat. Condensation forms on the walls in beads that tremble and merge and run downward in erratic patterns, guided by irregularities in the stone that shift as you watch. The walls are still deciding what shape to be.

---

The space opens below you.

It is vast—a cathedral of unplanned geometry, a valley contained in stone. But "valley" suggests a floor, and here there is no single floor. Instead, the chamber drops away in shelves and towers and overhangs, a chaotic topography of pillars and bridges and isolated platforms, all formed from the same grey-brown stone with its sweating surface. The scale is difficult to parse because there is nothing familiar to measure against—no trees, no grass, no built structures of any significant size. Just stone and the shapes stone makes when it is still learning to be stone.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. A diffuse amber glow that seems to emanate from the air itself, or from the walls, or from something deeper. Looking up, you see the ceiling—a hundred meters above, perhaps more—mottled with darker patches that shift and roil like slow clouds. The ceiling generates weather. Moisture accumulates, gathers into droplets, falls as a fine warm mist that drifts more than plunges, caught by the turbulent air moving through the space.

The air moves because the space breathes. There are tunnels branching from the main chamber—dozens visible, perhaps hundreds—some wide enough to walk into, some barely large enough for a finger. They connect to other chambers, other passages, other systems. The whole structure extends beyond what any mapping could capture. And the connections are still being made. Somewhere in the dark, you can hear the crack and groan of stone separating from stone, new passages being born.

---

And the movement.

The floor—if you can call any surface "floor"—ripples. Not with water, not with wind, but with bodies. The space is carpeted in them. Cream-colored and segmented, like woodlice stretched to the size of a thumb, they flow across the stone in rivers of themselves. They climb the walls. They cascade over edges. They fill depressions like liquid, then unfill them, moving with a purpose that seems coordinated but isn't—each individual following only the simple logic of touch and chemical signal, the aggregate becoming something larger than any intention.

They are not alone.

The air above the crawling rivers shimmers with wings. Insect-like things, but not insects—too many joints in their legs, their bodies catching the amber light and throwing it back in metallic greens and coppers. They move in thick swarms that densify and thin according to patterns you cannot read, the sound of their wings a continuous desperate drone beneath the higher whistling. The whistling comes from other things. Larger. You see them on ledges above—pale shapes with too many limbs, opening and closing mouth-parts that produce the thin clear tones. Calling to each other. Or singing. Or simply existing aloud.

The density of life is absolute. Every surface hosts something. The corners where stone meets stone are packed with pupating masses, writhing slowly in their transformations. The mist from above settles on chitinous backs and is channeled into grooves, collected, consumed. The droppings of the flying things fall to the crawling things below and are absorbed. The dead are stripped to components in minutes. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is still.

---

You are standing on a shelf of stone, trying to understand what you are seeing, and you realize that the shelf itself is changing. Not quickly—on a scale you can only perceive because you are watching for it. The stone here is being eroded by the passage of the crawling millions, worn away in channels that guide more passage, which creates more channels. And at the same time, new stone is being deposited. You see it now—where the mist settles and dries, a mineral residue accumulates. The crawling things carry it on their bodies from one place to another. The structure is building itself and unbuilding itself at the same time, a slow-motion turbulence of geology mediated by biological process.

This is why the erosion parameter reads so low. The space is not being worn down from a previous state—it is being born. This is embryonic geology, stone in utero, a landscape still assembling its own rules. In a thousand years, this shelf will be a bridge. In ten thousand, a pillar. In a hundred thousand, dust and new chambers and different creatures, or perhaps the same creatures doing different work.

The warmth comes from below. Not from magma, not from any volcanic center, but from the sheer biological activity itself. The metabolism of millions of bodies generates heat. The chemical processes of decomposition and reconstitution release it. The air moves because warm air rises and cool air descends and the tunnels connect chambers at different temperatures. The whole system is an engine that runs on itself, or perhaps on something deeper—a slow oxidation of mineral veins, a geothermal sipping from heat far below, feeding into the biological matrix and being fed by it in turn.

---

You find the first structure on a wide platform overlooking a drop that disappears into darkness and movement.

It is built of the same stone as the walls, but shaped. Stacked. Fitted together without mortar, the joints imperfect but intentional. A cairn—waist-high, wide enough around to enclose a body curled tight. No, not a cairn. A shelter. There is a gap at the base, just large enough for the crawling things to pass through, but small enough to exclude the larger predators that move between the pillars on spindly legs. Inside, the stone is smoother. Hollowed by use. Someone has rested here.

Who? The construction is too crude to offer clues. No tools have been used—only hands, only leverage, only the carrying of stone from one place to another. Primitive does not begin to describe it. It is the most basic possible intervention: piling matter to make a barrier against other matter. And yet it is clearly intentional. Someone looked at this chaos of life and decided to carve out a space within it. To exist here, if only for a while.

There are other structures. You see them now, once you know what to look for. Another cairn on a distant shelf. A low wall, knee-high, built across a natural bridge—providing cover, perhaps, from the aerial swarms. A pit dug into a floor and lined with flat stones, its purpose unclear. The built environment is scattered, fragmentary, less a system than a series of isolated gestures. Someone is trying to live here. Someone may be living here still.

---

The creatures do not seem to notice you.

This is not indifference—you are simply outside their categories. They do not recognize you as food, as threat, as mate, as competitor. You are a stone that happens to move, a geological feature that rearranges itself. The crawling things part around your feet and merge again behind you. The flying things adjust their swarms to avoid your head. The whistling creatures on their ledges do not change their song. You are present but not meaningful.

This could change. If you touched one of the crawling things—if you picked it up, held it, interfered with its passage—the chemical signals would shift. An alarm would propagate. The meaning of your presence would be revised. But for now, you are permitted to witness.

And what you witness is not peaceful. The sound alone—the grinding and clicking and whistling and buzzing—would be intolerable if you had to sleep here. The turbulence is constant. Individuals in the crawling rivers are crushed by their neighbors, eaten by things that descend from above, caught in cracks that close as new stone forms. The air above the swarms is a battlefield of aerial combat—things eating things eating things, the fallen consumed before they can finish falling. This is not a balanced ecosystem. This is a system in continuous crisis, a permanent emergency of consumption and reproduction, each creature producing hundreds of offspring so that a handful might survive to reproduce in turn.

And yet there is a stability to it. A pattern. The numbers of each kind rise and fall in waves that interact and interfere, but the total mass of life remains roughly constant. The structure of the space—the shelves and pillars and bridges—channels the movement in ways that create local densities and rarities, and the creatures have evolved—or been designed?—to exploit these variations. The system is turbulent but not chaotic. It is a chaos with attractors, a storm that holds its shape.

---

You notice, eventually, that the crawling things are not all the same.

There are different sizes, different shades of cream and grey. And there are individuals—rare, but present—who move differently. Faster. Against the flow. They carry something in their mandibles, and where they stop, other crawling things gather. You follow one with your eyes and see it climb a ridge, deposit its burden—a pellet of compressed matter, dense and dark—and move on. Within minutes, the pellet is surrounded by a new structure. A shelter. A nursery. A cairn.

They are building.

The crude stone structures you found—the cairns and walls and pits—they might not have been built by human hands. Or not only by human hands. The same logic that guides the crawling things to their nurseries might have guided some previous presence to make similar shapes. Piling matter to make a barrier against other matter. The deep grammar of construction, expressed in bodies that share nothing with your own.

Or perhaps the builders were human, and the crawling things learned from them. Or perhaps both learned from something else—the stone itself, the slow architecture of emergence, the way that matter wants to accumulate in certain patterns when it is being born.

---

The weirdness of this place is not in any single element. The creatures are strange but not impossible—they follow biological logics that make sense once you observe them long enough. The stone is unusual but not supernatural—it is simply young, still forming, still responsive to the forces that act on it. The light has a source you cannot see, but absence of evidence is not evidence of magic.

No, the weirdness is in the combination. The density of life without vegetation. The warmth without sun. The light without fire or star. The construction without builders, or builders without construction. The way that everything here seems to be simultaneously creating and destroying, emerging and eroding, beginning and ending. The sense that you have arrived in the middle of a sentence that started before you were born and will continue after you die.

You are standing in a wound in the world, and the wound is healing, and the scar will be a landscape, and something will live there. Is living there. Will have lived there, in the tense that does not yet exist for places that have not yet decided what they are.

---

You should leave. You know this. The descent is difficult, the climbing dangerous, the creatures unpredictable in their tolerance. But you stay for a while longer, watching the rivers of bodies flow and divide and merge, listening to the whistling that might be song or might be navigation or might be something for which you have no category, feeling the warm mist settle on your skin and run in droplets to the ground.

This is a place that does not need you. Does not care about you. Will continue its turbulent becoming long after you have gone back to the surface world where the categories are clearer. And there is something restful in that. Something like the peace of being irrelevant.

You touch the wall. The stone is warm and damp under your fingers. Under your fingers, it is changing.
