# The Permamotive

The dark goes in all directions. That is the first thing. Not down from a ceiling, not inward from walls — in all directions, outward and down and up, a darkness so complete it has texture. You learn to read it with your feet before anything else.

---

You are high. Not mountain-high — there is nothing here that qualifies as a peak — but plateau-high, tableland-high, the kind of elevation that thins the air and makes you aware of your own breathing as a mechanical process. The ground underfoot is warm. Not sun-warmed. There is no sun to speak of, or if there is, it lives behind a permanent mass of cloud so dense and so dark that it might as well be a second sky, lower and heavier than the first. The warmth comes from below. From the stone. From something the stone is doing.

The plateau extends further than you can travel. Continental in scope. People have tried to map it and the maps become abstract exercises in repetition — the same notations appearing at intervals of hundreds of kilometers, the same warm dark stone, the same thin air, the same muffled quiet. It generates its own weather, or rather, it hosts a weather that has no interest in leaving. Cloud sits on it like a lid. Moisture condenses on the underside of that lid and falls in unpredictable patterns — not rain exactly, not mist, but something between: a persistent dampness that appears and retreats, appears and retreats. You walk through patches of wet air. Your skin beads. Then it dries. Then it beads again. The stone beneath you is dark when dry and darker when wet, and you learn to read the damp streaks as a kind of signage, a way of knowing where you are in relation to the cloud's hidden topography.

There are no structures. Nothing has been built here. And yet —

---

The first time you feel it, you think it's your own pulse. A low vibration in the warm stone, so subtle it lives at the edge of perception. You stop walking. You hold your breath. You press your hand flat against the ground.

It is not your pulse. It is the ground's.

The plateau is an engine.

Not a metaphor. Not quite. The stone itself operates. Something in the deep geology — a system of mineral strata and thermal gradients and fluid-permeable fault lines — functions as a mechanism. Heat rises from below, forced upward by pressures you cannot see. That heat moves through channelized formations in the rock — veins of a particular mineralogy, crystalline and ordered, that conduct thermal energy the way metal conducts electricity. The heat reaches the surface and dissipates into the thin air, and in its dissipation it drives secondary processes: the condensation cycles, the movement of gases through porous stone, the slow redistribution of mineral deposits from one layer to another.

It has been doing this for longer than anything alive can remember. The erosion parameter reads as *established* — settled, in its own state, weathered into balance. The system is not degrading. It is not building. It is simply *running*, the way a river runs, the way a heart runs. It found its operating rhythm eons ago and has maintained it since. The stone is weathered, yes — smooth, rounded in places, fractured in others along patterns so regular they look machined — but the weathering is part of the cycle, not a departure from it. Material erodes here and is deposited there. Channels widen and others narrow. The engine rebalances.

You cannot see any of this. The darkness is total. But you can feel it. The vibration is constant — not loud, not even truly audible, more a sensation in the bones of your feet and the flat of your palm when you touch the ground. A thrum. A turnover. The sense of something vast and slow and patient working through its cycles.

---

Sound is scarce here. The darkness absorbs it, or the thin air carries it poorly, or both. What sound exists is intimate and close. Your footsteps — a dry scrape, sometimes a damp crunch where condensation has gathered in the shallow depressions that pit the stone. Your breathing. Occasionally, from very far away, a low murmur that might be wind crossing a stretch of the plateau you haven't reached yet, or might be the engine's deeper registers surfacing briefly through the rock. The quiet is not empty. It is full of the *expectation* of sound. But the expectation is rarely fulfilled.

And then there are the animals.

---

You hear them before you know what they are. A clicking. A series of clicks, rapid and precise, coming from somewhere to your left and slightly below — which means they are coming from a depression in the stone, one of the broad shallow basins that dimple the plateau's surface. You move toward the sound. The ground slopes downward gradually. The clicking grows more complex, layered, multiple sources overlapping. You crouch. You extend a hand.

The floor of the basin is different from the surface stone. It is damp — properly damp, film-of-water-on-your-fingertips damp — and it is covered in a fine-grained material that is not quite sand and not quite silt. Something mineral, something the engine has processed and deposited here, in this low point, the way a machine deposits slag or a river deposits silt. It is warm to the touch.

The clicking comes from the surface of this deposit. You cannot see what makes it, but when you lay your palm flat, you feel motion against your skin — small, rapid, deliberate. Many small bodies, moving with purpose. You feel a delicate weight cross your knuckles. Another pauses on your wrist. A third investigates the tip of your thumb.

They are warm. Warmer than the air, warmer than the stone, almost as warm as the ground itself. Their bodies are hard and smooth — chitinous, perhaps, or mineralized, something between insect and crustacean and something that has no terrestrial analog. They are not large. The biggest might be the length of your finger. The smallest are barely visible — or would be barely visible, if there were anything to see them by.

There are *many*.

You sit in the dark basin and let them move over you and you begin to understand the ecology. The engine processes mineral from below. It deposits the processed material in basins like this one. The deposits are warm and damp and chemically rich. The animals — you start calling them the clickers, for lack of anything better — live in the deposits. They feed on the mineral substrate, or on the chemical gradients within it, or on each other. They are the fauna of this landscape, and they are abundant. Not just here — in every basin you come to, in every depression, in every channel where the engine's output pools and collects. The plateau is not empty. It is sparsely *occupied*, the life concentrated in these low points, scattered across the vast dark surface like settlements across a continent, separated by long stretches of bare stone where nothing moves and nothing lives and the only sound is your own footsteps and the engine's subliminal thrum.

---

The clickers are not the only ones. There are larger things.

You encounter the first one on the third day of walking — or what you assume is the third day; time is difficult in permanent darkness. You are crossing a stretch of bare stone between basins when the ground changes pitch under your feet. The vibration shifts. Not in intensity but in character — a deeper register, a slower cycling. You stop. You feel the stone through your boot soles. Something below you is moving. Not the engine. Something *in* the engine.

Then, ten meters ahead, the stone cracks. Not violently — slowly, deliberately, a lateral fissure that opens with a sound like a long exhalation. Warm air pushes up from the crack, carrying a smell you haven't encountered here — organic, dense, slightly sulfurous. Something is breathing down there. Something large.

You hear it move. A vast slow sliding, the sound of a heavy body pressing against stone, the grinding of a mineralized surface against a mineralized surface. It is below you, in the engine's channels, using the thermal veins the way a river uses a riverbed. It is part of the system. You don't know if it *knows* it is part of the system, or if the distinction even matters.

You stand still. The sliding sound passes beneath you and continues, growing fainter, moving in a direction you cannot predict. The crack closes behind it, slowly, the stone settling back into its previous configuration as if it had never opened. The engine's vibration returns to its normal register.

You never see it. You never see any of them. The darkness holds. But you begin to understand: the plateau's ecology is not only on the surface. It goes down. The engine is not merely geological — it is biological, or the biology has become geological, or the distinction eroded away long ago, the same way the stone erodes, the same way the channels widen and narrow, as part of the cycle.

The clickers in the basins are the surface expression of something that extends deep into the warm dark stone, a taxonomy of life that has organized itself around the engine's thermal outputs the way a forest organizes itself around sunlight. The large things that move through the channels are the grazers — or the predators — or the maintenance crew. You cannot tell. You do not have enough information. You have only the sounds, the vibrations, the warmth, the dark.

---

There is almost no vegetation. Here and there, in the dampest basins, among the clickers, you find something that might be plant-like — a fibrous, threadlike growth that mats across the mineral substrate, pale and soft and slightly luminous, though the luminosity is so faint you only notice it when your eyes have been open in total darkness for hours and begin manufacturing their own phantom geometries. The growth is sparse. It does not form stands or colonies or anything you could call a landscape feature. It is a trace. A rumor of photosynthesis — or chemosynthesis, more likely, driven by the engine's thermal and chemical output rather than any light. It does not ask for light. Nothing here asks for light.

You walk for a long time between basins. The stone is bare. The air is thin and warm and damp in intermittent patches. The engine hums beneath you. Sometimes you pass a place where the surface stone is worn into channels — shallow, smooth, organically curved — that once carried something: fluid, or gas, or the slow movement of mineral-rich sediment. The channels are dry now, or mostly dry, their function migrated elsewhere as the engine rebalanced itself over the millennia. They look like the bed of a river that stopped flowing, or the ductwork of a factory that was reorganized. They are beautiful in the way that functional things are beautiful — the beauty of something shaped precisely by what it needed to do.

You follow one of these channels for several kilometers. It leads you to a basin larger than the others — a broad amphitheater of depression in the stone, a hundred meters across, maybe more. The floor is wet. Not damp — wet. Standing water, shallow and warm, covers the mineral substrate. The clicking here is dense, a continuous rustle and chatter, the sound of a city at its most intimate scale. You kneel at the edge. You put your hand in the water. It is body-warm. Something large moves against your fingers and then away. Something small investigates your wrist and then moves on. The water is full of life. The engine feeds this basin more generously than the others, and the life has responded.

You sit here for a long time. The darkness does not change. The engine hums. The water laps gently at the stone edge, a sound so small and so close it might be the most beautiful thing you have heard. The clickers click. The large things move in the deep channels beneath. Somewhere out on the plateau, the same processes are occurring in other basins, separated by distances that would take days to cross. The life is scattered. The emptiness is vast. The engine connects them all — a network of thermal veins and mineral channels and processing zones, operating at a scale that makes the individual basins seem like cells in a single vast organism, or like nodes in a single vast machine, and you are not sure there is a difference.

---

The rules of this world, as you understand them:

The darkness is permanent. You adapt or you do not. There is no day coming.

The warmth is geological. It comes from below. It is the engine, and the engine has been running for longer than life has existed, and life organized itself around the engine's output, and now the life and the engine are parts of the same system.

Water is a byproduct. The engine processes mineral-rich substrates and one of its outputs is moisture — condensation,渗水, the slow weeping of processed fluid from the stone into the basins. The water is warm. The water is where the life concentrates. The water is temporary in any given location — the engine rebalances, and basins go dry, and new basins form — but it is permanent as a *process*. There is always water somewhere. You find it by following the sound of the clickers.

The stone is the medium. Everything lives in it, on it, through it. The stone is not inert. The stone is working.

You are the only thing here that was built. You are the only artifact. Your boots, your clothing, your body — all of it imported, all of it temporary, all of it foreign to the engine's cycle. The plateau does not acknowledge you. The clickers investigate you and move on. The large things pass beneath you and do not surface. The stone hums and does not change its rhythm for your presence. You are here and the system does not care, and there is something restful about that — something you have never felt before, the freedom of being entirely irrelevant to a world that is functioning perfectly without you and will continue to function long after you leave.

You stand up. You walk on. The basin falls behind you. The dark stone stretches ahead. Somewhere far away, you hear another basin — the clicking just barely reaching you across the bare stone, a sound like rain on a tin roof, like fingers on a keyboard, like the engine counting to itself in a language you will never learn.
