# The Hollow of Ten Thousand Years

The entrance gives nothing away. A crack in the earth, a shadow among shadows, and then the ground falls away beneath your feet — not a descent into darkness but into light.

## The Cavity

You emerge onto a shelf of stone and the world opens. A cavern so vast your eyes cannot hold its edges. The ceiling arches somewhere above, lost in its own pale luminosity — a dome of crystallized salt and flowed limestone that catches light from sources you cannot immediately trace and refuses to let it die. The light has the quality of a perpetual overcast noon: diffuse, sourceless, thorough. It casts no hard shadows. It reveals everything and weights nothing with drama.

The Hollow is the size of a valley. You could walk for hours and not cross it. The far walls recede into a grey haze that might be distance or might be the limits of the light's reach. The scale defeats the body's instincts. You feel small in a way that has nothing to do with threat — a speck, a temporary arrangement of matter passing through a space that existed long before you and will exist long after.

The air is cool and carries a mineral sharpness. Your skin prickles with it. There is moisture here but not water's full presence — you cannot see streams or pools, not yet, but your fingers find the damp when you touch the stone, and condensation beads on the walls in irregular constellations, sliding downward in slow, patient trails that have traced the same paths for centuries.

## The City

The city has no name because no one remains who would name it.

It spreads across the cavern floor and climbs the walls in stepped terraces — a settlement carved rather than built, hollowed from the living stone with tools you could hold in your hand. You see the marks of labor everywhere: chisel grooves still visible in doorframes, the rhythmic scars of patient hands working granite and limestone with nothing but time and persistence. No metal. No machinery. Just stone on stone, grit and water and the bodies of the people who made this place.

The architecture is not tall. The ancient builders worked horizontally, extending deep into the walls, creating networks of rooms and passages that honeycomb the cavern's perimeter. What stands free is low and thick-walled, eroded now into soft geometries. The corners have surrendered to time's patience. The sharp edges are gone. The city looks as though it has been slowly melting into itself — not from heat but from the patient work of condensation, of mineral-laden water seeping into cracks and freezing and thawing and freezing again, of air moving through chambers for longer than languages last.

Streets run between the structures, wide and deliberate, laid out with planning that suggests a people who understood they were building for descendants they would never meet. The paving stones are fitted so tightly that the gaps have been filled by mineral deposits over the millennia, creating near-continuous surfaces. You can walk these streets and feel the evenness beneath your feet, the intention.

But the city is not full. The density is low. There is too much space for what remains. You pass cluster after cluster of carved dwellings separated by stretches of empty stone — plazas that once held gatherings, markets, ceremonies, now inhabited only by light and the mineral-rich air. The population that built this place has long since declined or dispersed or transformed. What remains is a skeletal presence of construction: enough to show what was, not enough to crowd what is.

## The Creatures

And yet the Hollow is not empty.

You hear them before you see them — no, that is wrong. You feel them before you see them. The silence is near-absolute. The sounds that exist here are the soft ones: the drip of condensation, the settling of ancient stone, the movement of air through passages carved long ago. But there is a presence in that silence, a quality of being-watched that prickles the back of your neck.

The creatures emerge from the low doorways, the shadowed passages, the crevices between structures. They are difficult to count at first because they do not move like animals you know. They flow. There is a liquid quality to their motion — not ungainly but coordinated, silent, deliberate. Their bodies are pale, almost translucent in the sourceless light. They have limbs, you think, but the number varies. Some have four. Some have six or eight or a number that shifts as they move. They are not insects. They are not mammals. They are something else, something that evolved in this mineral world without the reference of green growing things.

They are abundant. This is what strikes you most: the quantity of them. The floor of the cavern ripples with their movement in the distance, a living texture that shifts and reforms. They climb the walls and flow through the streets and pour from the ancient doorways. But they make no sound. Their feet find purchase without scraping. Their bodies move without the creak and click of joints. They are the quietest living things you have encountered, adapted to a world where silence is survival, where sound has no place.

They do not approach you directly. They observe. Eyes or eye-like structures track your movement. They are curious, perhaps, or cautious, or simply processing your presence against whatever passes for memory in their kind. They are the inheritors of this place, whatever they are. The city was not built for them — they are too small, too fluid, too strange for the doorways and rooms designed for human-shaped bodies. But they have made it their own. They flow through the streets like water finding the lowest point, filling the spaces, present and numerous and utterly silent.

The ecology is a mystery. There are no plants here, no green, no photosynthesis. The creatures must find sustenance somewhere — from the mineral deposits, perhaps, or from each other, or from the fungal mats that cling to the dampest corners of the cavern, pale and rubbery and unrecognizable as any life you would name. You see them cluster around seeps in the stone wall where water runs thin and constant, their mouths or mouth-equivalents pressed to the damp surface, drinking something from the flow that you cannot see.

This is a world of leached minerals and dissolved compounds, of chemosynthesis and decay's absence, of food webs that operate on principles foreign to the sunlit world. The creatures have their own economy, their own hierarchies, their own purposes. You are a visitor to it, an anomaly in a system that has not accounted for you.

## The Turbulence

The strangeness of this place is not in its components but in their behavior.

The creatures move constantly. There is no resting, no stillness, no pause. The Hollow seethes with motion — not panicked or desperate, but continuous, driven, as though stillness were a concept they had never developed. They stream down the walls and across the streets and through the empty plazas in patterns that shift and recombine without resolution. They cluster and disperse and cluster again. They flow up and down the terraces of the ancient city in streams that cross and merge and separate. They are a living system that refuses to settle into a shape.

Watching them, you begin to see that the turbulence is not random. There are structures to the chaos, rhythms to the motion. The creatures are responding to something — to shifts in the air, perhaps, or to signals you cannot perceive, or to imperatives that belong only to their kind. They are executing something, performing something, caught in a pattern that has no beginning or end.

The turbulence extends beyond the creatures. The air in the Hollow is not still. It moves in slow currents and sudden gusts that come from sources you cannot identify. The temperature shifts by degrees as you walk — warmer near the walls where the creatures cluster, cooler in the open plazas where the light falls without obstruction. The condensation forms and re-forms in different places as the air moves. The cavern breathes, you realize. Not in the biological sense but in the physical one — a vast enclosed system that exchanges heat and moisture in patterns too large and slow for easy perception, but present, active, turbulent.

You feel it most when you stand still: the sense that nothing here is at rest, that stillness itself is foreign to this place. The creatures do not pause. The air does not calm. The water does not pool but moves, slowly, persistently, through the stone. Everything is in transit. Everything is becoming something else.

## The Light's Cost

The brightness, you begin to understand, is not a gift.

It comes from the ceiling — from the crystallized minerals that have formed over unimaginable time, layering and re-layering, capturing and refracting whatever light reaches this depth through channels and fissures too small and complex to trace. But the light also comes from the walls themselves, from deposits that glow with chemiluminescence in the damp stone, from the creatures' pale bodies as they pass in their endless streams, carrying bioluminescence they do not know they have.

The light is abundant but cold. It illuminates without warming. You see more clearly than you would in full darkness, but the seeing offers no comfort. The Hollow is revealed in its strangeness, its ancientness, its wrongness. You see the erosion patterns in the stone that suggest water courses that no longer exist. You see the rooms that have collapsed and the walls that have crumbled and the stairs that lead to passages now sealed by time. You see the marks of the original builders — handprints on walls where they steadied themselves, grooves where ropes wore the stone, hollows where feet passed for generations — and you see that no feet have passed that way for a very long time.

The creatures do not need the light. They navigate by other senses, other attunements. The brightness is incidental to them, a quality of their environment they neither seek nor avoid. But for you, it is disorienting. There is no night here, no darkness, no relief from the seeing. The light presses on your eyes and your mind, asking you to keep looking, to keep processing, to never close the visual field.

You understand why the ancients left, or died, or changed. Living in permanent light, in permanent motion, in permanent cool dampness — it wears on something. Not the body, perhaps, but the spirit. The sense of time unspools here. Hours feel like days. Days feel like nothing. The turbulence denies you the anchor of a static world.

## What Remains

You walk through the city for hours, or what feel like hours, and you find its edges without finding its center. There are no temples that you can identify, no gathering halls, no obvious markers of governance or worship. The city was built for living — for the ordinary continuance of life, for the passing of days, for the slow accumulation of moments that become years that become generations. The builders worked with the stone because the stone was there. They made rooms and streets and terraces because making is what people do, because the alternative was not-making, was emptiness, was the void that waits for all things.

Now the builders are gone and the stone remains and the creatures flow through the spaces they left behind, and the light falls on all of it without preference or judgment.

You find yourself standing in a plaza — a wide stone space with worn steps leading down to a lower level, empty now but for the pale bodies streaming past in their eternal motion. You sit on a low wall, its edge softened by centuries of condensation and mineral deposit, and you listen. And what you hear is almost nothing: the subliminal drip of water, the passage of air, the soft friction of countless bodies moving without sound. The quiet is not peace. It is the absence of the sounds you expect — the sounds of a world where life is loud and warmth is given and green things grow toward the sun.

This is a different world. It has its own rules. Life without plants. Motion without sound. Light without heat. Time without markers.

You could stay here. You could learn the patterns of the creatures, trace the paths of the water, map the ancient streets until you knew them as well as their builders once did. You could become a presence in the Hollow, a new shape in its turbulence, a body in the motion.

But you are not made for this place. You are made for sun and shadow, for growth and decay, for a world where stillness exists and silence has meaning. The Hollow offers you no home.

So you rise, and you walk back through the city with its soft edges and its flowing creatures and its patient light, and you find the crack in the earth that leads upward, and you climb toward a sky that is not always bright, toward a world where the rules are different, carrying with you the memory of the place below — the place where people once lived and died and left behind only stone and light and the endless, silent motion of lives you cannot name.
