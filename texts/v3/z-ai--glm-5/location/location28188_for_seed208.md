# The Weeping Expanse

The sound finds you first. It rises from below, around, through—a constant, layered roar that makes conversation impossible and thought difficult. Water moves everywhere here, and it has moved here for longer than memory.

---

This is a landscape at the end of its life. Not dying—nothing so dramatic. Simply finished. Erosion has accomplished what erosion does, given enough time. The land has been worn down to its ancient bones, reduced and smoothed until only the essential geometry remains. What were once hills have become low swells. What were once valleys have become broad, shallow depressions where water gathers and decides where to go next.

The rock is limestone, or something like it—a water-soluble stone that has been dissolving for geological ages. The result is a honeycomb. The surface appears solid enough, gray-white and pocked, but beneath lies a vast network of channels, chambers, and underground rivers. The ground sounds hollow when you walk. Sometimes it drums. Sometimes it sings.

Water emerges everywhere. It weeps from vertical faces—sheets of rock that have been exposed as the land wore down, now seeping with constant moisture. It pools in depressions that never quite dry. It rushes invisibly beneath your feet, and you can hear it—always hear it—the sound of a thousand streams finding their way through stone, echoing up from sinkholes and cracks, a white-noise roar that varies in pitch but never in presence.

The atmosphere condenses. The air is so saturated that droplets form on surfaces without any apparent source, beading on rock faces, collecting in the shallow grooves worn by ancient fingers. Breathing feels heavy. The moisture enters everything—clothes, skin, thoughts.

---

There are no plants. None. Not a blade of grass, not a moss, not a lichen.

This absence takes time to notice, and then it becomes impossible to un-see. The rock is bare. Gray-white, sometimes pinkish, sometimes pale gold where mineral veins run through it. The surfaces are smooth where water flows, rough where it doesn't, but always—always—bare.

And yet the ecosystem is abundant. Life exists here in profusion. It has simply chosen another form.

The rock-borers are the most numerous. Soft-bodied creatures, pale and translucent, somewhere between a slug and a shrimp, that rasp at the stone with rows of microscopic teeth. They leave trails of slightly finer grit behind them. In quiet moments—which are rare—you can hear their thousands of tiny mouths, a rhythmic scraping like sand on glass. They feed on the stone itself, or rather on what lives in the stone: bacterial mats, mineral deposits, something in the chemistry of the rock that sustains them. Their burrows honeycomb the surfaces, making the already-fragile stone more fragile still.

The water-channels host other creatures. Blind salamander-things with translucent skin and elaborate sensory crests along their flanks, navigating by vibration and chemical sensing. They are numerous, packed into the underground streams in surprising density, emerging from sinkholes at dusk to hunt on the damp rock surfaces. They are pale and wet-looking, their skin reflecting what little light there is, and they move with purpose, as if the darkness is not a limitation but a preference.

Above, in the dim air, insects you would almost recognize—except they are not insects. They are something earlier, simpler, like insects before insects evolved, with too many legs and strange mouthparts and wings that are not quite wings. They cluster where the water seeps, drinking from the wet rock, laying eggs in the damp crevices. They make sounds—not the familiar chirping of crickets, but a low, resonant thrumming that you feel in your chest as much as hear with your ears. Thousands of them, all at once.

And something hunts them.

---

The sound is not uniform. It comes in layers, and you learn to read it, eventually, the way you might read a landscape of hills and valleys.

The lowest layer is the water: the underground rivers, the constant seeping, the drip and rush. This is the bedrock sound, unchanging, eternal, older than anything else here. It creates a kind of acoustic climate, a saturated environment where other sounds must compete.

Above that: the churning of the salamander-things as they move through underground channels, disturbing water. The splashing is distinct, percussive, sometimes close and sometimes far, echoing strangely from chambers you cannot see.

Then the thrumming of the insect-things. This is the loudest layer after the water—a constant, rhythmic vibration that you feel in your bones. It is not unlike standing near machinery, except there is no machine, only thousands of small bodies, all producing sound, all at once. They communicate this way, or compete, or something else entirely. When the light dims further, the thrumming intensifies. At what passes for night, it becomes a wall of sound that presses against your skull.

And over everything: the calls of the hunters.

They are difficult to see. Large, perhaps the size of a small dog, but low to the ground and fast-moving. They have too many legs—eight? Ten?—and a body that flows over the terrain like water. Their skin is dark, almost black, to absorb what little light exists. They do not hunt by sight. They hunt by sound and vibration, reading the thrumming, reading the water, reading footsteps on stone. Their calls are high and piercing, audible even over the roar—short bursts of noise that serve as both communication and weapon, a kind of sonic lancing that disorients prey. When they call, nearby insect-things flinch and go silent, and the absence is noticeable, a hole in the soundscape.

You learn to track their movements this way—by the silences they leave behind.

---

There are structures here.

They are ancient. They might be older than anything else you have encountered—certainly older than anything built by people you would recognize. Simple constructions: piles of stones arranged in patterns, shallow grooves carved into rock faces, low walls that seem to direct water flow rather than block it. Some of the structures are platforms, flat surfaces raised slightly above the damp ground, smoothed by hands and time. Others are markers, standing stones or rock piles, positioned at irregular intervals across the Expanse.

They were built by people who understood this place. You can tell because they are still here. Anything not built to survive the water, the sound, the erosion, would have been gone long ago. These structures are simple, primitive in technology but sophisticated in understanding—positioned where water flows helpfully rather than destructively, shaped to shed moisture rather than collect it, aligned with the natural acoustics of the terrain.

Tools have been found. Hand-tools: stone blades, bone scrapers, simple things. No metal. Metal would not survive here; the water would eat it. But stone and bone endure, and they are everywhere, scattered like seeds. Someone lived here. Many someones. For a long time.

Are they still here? The question is not easy to answer. The sound masks movement. The dim light masks shape. The creatures here are difficult to distinguish from the few humans who might remain—and it is not clear that any do. You see shapes sometimes, at the edge of hearing, at the limit of sight. By the time you turn, they are gone, or they were never there at all. The Expanse does not welcome questions.

---

The light is always dim.

Clouds hang low and thick, perpetually gray, so close you feel you could touch them. Moisture condenses on their undersides and falls constantly—not quite rain, not quite mist, something between. The sun, when it can be perceived at all, is a pale smear behind the cloud layer, a direction rather than a source. It never gets bright. It never gets truly dark either.

This is the light that plants would hate. And plants are not here.

But the creatures do not mind. They are adapted to dimness, evolved for it. Their eyes are reduced or absent, their other senses heightened. The salamander-things navigate by pressure waves in water, by chemical trails, by vibration. The insect-things read air currents, temperature gradients, the sounds of each other. The hunters move through the soundscape like sharks through water, sensing everything, missing nothing.

And if there are people here still—they have adapted too.

---

You begin to understand the rules.

The water always wins. It shapes everything—your path, your shelter, your thought. You learn where it flows, where it pools, where it hides. You do not stand in depressions. You do not trust smooth surfaces. You listen for the hollow resonance that means emptiness beneath.

The sound is information. You stop fighting it and start reading it. The insect-things thrum at different pitches depending on moisture and temperature; you learn to gauge the air this way. The water-changes pitch when channels fill; you learn to read the underground floods before they surface. The hunters call before they strike; you learn their rhythms, their territories, their proximity.

The structures are warnings. Or guides. The ancient builders knew things you do not, and they left markers for a reason. Some mark safe paths across the dissolving rock. Some mark territories, warning of hunter-dens nearby. Some mark something else entirely—burial sites, perhaps, or places where the stone is particularly thin, where a wrong step would send you into the underground channels forever.

The creatures ignore you until they do not. You are not prey to the rock-borers. The salamander-things hunt smaller game. But the hunters—they notice you. They track you. They call to each other in patterns that might be coordination. And you are never quite sure, in this dim, wet, roaring world, whether you are being observed by animals or by something that has learned to watch from the sound.

---

The Expanse extends beyond what you can see.

The scale is regional—valleys and ridges, sinkholes and channels, spreading outward until geography blurs. You could walk for days and not reach an edge. The sound changes as you move, the acoustics shifting with the terrain. Some areas are louder—deep channels where the underground rivers emerge briefly, natural amphitheaters where the roar echoes and multiplies. Some are quieter—sheltered depressions where the sound is muffled, where you can finally hear yourself think.

These quieter places are where you find more structures. The ancient builders knew where to live. They built in the pockets of relative silence, where communication was possible, where the roar did not press so heavily. They built platforms and walls and markers, simple things that endured. And they built something else—shallow channels, carved into the rock, directing water in specific patterns. 

Water-management. Sound-management. A civilization that learned to work with the Expanse rather than against it.

You begin to wonder what they knew that you do not.

---

Night falls, or what passes for night.

The clouds darken. The light dims further. The insect-things intensify their thrumming. The water sounds the same—eternal, unchanging—but the darkness changes your relationship to it. You cannot see the rock-borers anymore, but you can hear them, their tiny mouths closer now, drawn to the moisture on your skin. You cannot see the salamander-things emerging from their channels, but you can hear them splashing, dispersing across the wet landscape. You cannot see the hunters, but you can hear their calls, high and piercing, circling closer.

And you might—or might not—hear something else. A rhythm that is not quite water, not quite creature. A pattern of sounds that might be deliberate. Clicks, perhaps. Or drumming. Something that rises and falls with intention, with meaning, just at the edge of audibility, just below the roar.

You are not alone.

You were never alone.

The Expanse knows you are here. And something in the Expanse is answering.
