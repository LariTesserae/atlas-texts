# The Weeping Terrace

The plateau came up from the earth faster than erosion could wear it down. You can see this in the rock faces—sheer and uncompromising, their edges still carrying the ghost of violence from whatever thrust them skyward. The stone hasn't learned to be round yet. It hasn't forgotten the anger of its own birth.

---

Water lives in everything here. Not the dramatic rush of rivers or the theatrical crash of waterfalls—this is a slower, more intimate saturation. The rock faces weep. They do this constantly, a thin sheet of moisture migrating down the fresh grey stone, catching light where it can. In some places the weeping is barely a whisper; in others, the water gathers into deliberate threads that trace paths straight down the cliff, like veins mapped by some patient geographer.

The moisture doesn't stop at the cliffs. It rises as well—condensing out of the air itself in the cooler hours, settling on every surface, beading on the smooth stones of the pathways, gathering in the shallow channels cut deliberately into the walking surfaces. Your feet are never quite dry here. Nobody's are.

---

The vegetation grows in thick stands that seem to have agreed to leave certain areas entirely alone. Ferns primarily—great fountains of them, some waist-high, some brushing your shoulders—emerging from the wet stone itself. They root in the cracks where the weeping water pools, and they drink constantly. Between the fern stands, the moss carpet extends: soft, dense, so uniformly green it almost hurts to look at. Small flowering plants dot the moss fields, but they're scattered, deliberate, as though each one needed its own private territory.

You could walk for a minute through open moss and occasional flowers before reaching the next fern thicket. The spacing feels chosen rather than accidental, though by what hand or logic, you cannot say.

---

The settlement clings to the plateau's eastern edge, where the weeping cliffs begin their long drop into the lower valleys. It is built of the same stone the plateau reveals—grey, tight-grained, dense. The buildings sit low and wide, their walls thick enough to stand centuries. You can see where newer construction abuts older work, the masonry techniques shifting subtly across generations: some walls fitted with impossible precision, others rougher, hurried perhaps, though still sound.

No metal ties the structures together. The builders work in stone and wood and fiber rope, and their joints hold by weight and friction and the patient wisdom of balance. Windows are few and small. Doors are thick slabs hung on stone pivots. The roofs slope gently, their surfaces grooved to guide the constant moisture into collection channels that feed the cisterns beneath each building.

Wide paths connect everything—broader than they need to be, given how few people seem to walk them at any given moment. The spacing between structures is generous, almost extravagant. Each building seems to want the company of its own shadow, its own plot of moss, its own section of weeping cliff face.

---

The quiet holds everything.

You can hear water, of course—the endless soft descent of it down stone faces, the slower drip from roof edges, the gradual migration through the channel systems. There might be wind sometimes, though it moves gently at this altitude, as though even the air respects the stillness here. Your own footsteps register as events. Your own breath, if you listen for it.

But no birds. No insects. No animal calls from the fern thickets or the moss fields or the cliff-side aeries. Nothing with fur or feather makes sound here. Nothing with teeth or talons or beaks. The silence is so complete it becomes its own kind of presence—not empty, but full of the absence of everything that would normally interrupt it.

You notice how the people who live here move. They step softly. They speak rarely, and when they do, their voices stay low, close, as though normal volume would be an intrusion. You notice how they pause before doorways, how they look at the paths ahead before taking them, how they wait for silence to return before they add their small sounds to it.

---

The warmth sits on your skin like a cloth laid gently there—not hot enough to make you sweat, but present, insistent, a constant reminder that the air around you is alive with energy. At this altitude, the sun should feel thinner, should bite more, should carry the sharp clarity of mountain light. Instead, it filters through the constant moisture in the air, diffused into a soft radiance that makes everything glow slightly from within.

Shadows don't sharpen here; they soften. The edges of things blur slightly in the humid light. Distance becomes difficult to judge.

---

You walk through the settlement in the mid-morning, and you pass perhaps twelve people in the time it takes to cross from one end to the other. Each encounter leaves space—physical space, temporal space. You nod; they nod back. Nobody crowds. Nobody rushes. The paths could hold four people abreast, but you never see more than one person on a path at a time unless they've clearly chosen to travel together.

The buildings are not houses, exactly—not in the sense you might expect. They contain rooms for sleeping, yes, but also rooms for storage, rooms for water collection, rooms for the crafts that occupy people's time: grinding stones for grain, looms for fiber, workspaces for the endless task of maintaining the channels and cisterns. Each structure serves multiple purposes, and the boundaries between them blur.

You see someone mending a section of water channel on the far side of a fern thicket. They work slowly, deliberately, replacing a worn stone with a fresh one, adjusting the pitch of the groove by fractions until the water flows exactly as it should. They've been doing this, you realize, for as long as anyone can remember. The channels need constant attention. The water finds every flaw, every settling stone, every crack that might form. The work is never done.

---

A child sits at the edge of the plateau, legs dangling over the weeping cliff, watching the water make its slow way down into the mists below. They don't look bored. They don't look impatient. They watch the water like it's telling them something—some story that takes a long time to tell, that might never end.

You realize, watching them, that nothing here is going to change.

Not in any hurry, anyway. The plateau is too new, too fresh, too busy being what it is to start becoming something else. The water will keep weeping, the ferns will keep drinking, the people will keep tending their channels and mending their walls and treading softly on the moss paths. The silence will hold. The warmth will sit on everything like a hand that means no harm. The light will diffuse through the humid air, and the shadows will stay soft, and the edges of things will refuse to sharpen.

---

You could stay here. You could learn the patience of stone that hasn't yet forgotten how to be angry. You could let the constant moisture become your own atmosphere, let the quiet settle into your bones until you move softly too, until you speak rarely and then only in low tones, until you understand why the child at the cliff edge can watch the water weep and find it enough.

The plateau is waiting. It has all the time in the world.
