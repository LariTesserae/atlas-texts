# The Cirque at the End of Time

## I. The Approach (What There Is of One)

You come to it through a crack in the rock — not a cave entrance, not a proper door, but a fissure in dark stone that narrows until your shoulders brush both walls and then, without ceremony, opens.

What it opens into is small. That is the first thing you understand. Not small like a room is small — small like a sentence is small. Contained. Complete. Everything that needs to be here is here, and nothing that doesn't need to be here has survived the finding of it.

The cirque is perhaps forty meters across at its widest. The walls rise on three sides — dark stone, wet, columnar in places, where some ancient geological process laid down fractures that now look architectural, like the ribs of a building that was never built but only suggested. The fourth side, the direction you came from, is the fissure. There is no other exit. You understand this without checking. The place tells you, the way a full cup tells you it is full.

The floor is not flat. It slopes gently toward a center that is slightly lower, a shallow basin, and in that basin water stands — not pools, not exactly, but lies. Covers the ground in a film that is sometimes a centimeter deep, sometimes only a wetness on stone. It doesn't flow. There is nowhere for it to flow to. It seeps up from below, condenses from above, and stays.

## II. The Light (What There Is of It)

You cannot see the sky. This is the second understanding. The walls rise high enough — ten meters, perhaps twelve, though measurement feels wrong here, as though the place resists being reduced to numbers — that whatever sky exists above the mountain is a rumor. What light reaches the cirque comes from two sources, and both are insufficient.

The first is the fissure itself. A faint grey spills from the crack you came through, falling across the nearest stone in a stripe that reaches perhaps three meters before the dark absorbs it. This is the brightest thing in the cirque. It is not bright.

The second is stranger. The stone walls — dark, wet, columnar — are not uniformly dark. In the crevices between the columns, in the hairline fractures that run through the rock, in the places where the stone has split just enough to admit moisture and time, something lives that glows. A weak, cold, blue-white luminescence, the color of moonlight if moonlight were wet. It comes from a lichen or a fungus — the distinction is unclear, perhaps irrelevant — that has colonized every vertical surface where water runs. It does not illuminate. It marks. It draws the architecture of the stone in faint lines of light, like a diagram of itself, so that you can see the shape of the walls but not the walls themselves, can see where the stone is but not what it is. The cirque is drawn in its own faint ink.

Between these two sources — the grey stripe from the fissure and the blue-white tracery on the walls — you can navigate. You cannot read. You cannot see color, except that the glow has a color and the darkness has a color, and the darkness is not black. It is brown. A deep, wet brown, the color of peat, the color of old wood that has been wet for longer than wood remembers being wood.

## III. The Water

The water is everywhere, and it is the reason for everything.

It comes from the stone. The walls weep — that is the right word, not "trickle" or "seep" but weep — from dozens of points, perhaps hundreds, each one a place where the rock has a fissure too fine to see and water too patient to be stopped. The weeping is slow. Each point produces a drop perhaps every thirty seconds, perhaps every minute, and the drops run down the stone in paths that have been worn into the rock over a span of time that you cannot estimate because the wearing is complete. The paths are deep enough to hold the water, to guide it, and the water follows them because it has always followed them. There is no decision here. The water goes where it has always gone.

The floor is the same. Water stands on it because the stone beneath the moss and the sediment is a basin — not carved by a stream, not eroded by flow, but a depression in the bedrock that has been here since the mountain rose, and the mountain is old. The water fills it to the brim and then stops, because there is no brim to overflow. The basin is the floor. The floor is the basin. The water is exactly as deep as it has always been, and it is exactly as wet as it has always been, and this is not a metaphor. The level does not rise. The level does not fall.

The air is wet. Not humid — wet. The moisture content is so high that the air feels like a substance, like you are breathing water that hasn't committed yet. Your skin begins to bead within minutes. Condensation forms on everything: on the stone, on the moss, on the chitin of the beetles, on your eyelashes. The air is doing what the walls are doing. Weeping.

And the temperature of the water — you learn this when you kneel, when your hand finds the floor — is the temperature of the stone, which is the temperature of the air, which is the temperature of the mountain at this altitude. Cool. Not cold. Not the cold of snowmelt or glacier. The cold of deep ground. The cool of a cellar that has never known a season. It is the same temperature at every point in the cirque, and you know without checking that it is the same temperature at every hour, in every month, and has been for longer than the concept of months has existed. There are no gradients here. Nothing is warming or cooling. Nothing is flowing toward equilibrium because equilibrium was reached long ago, and nothing has disturbed it since.

## IV. The Vegetation

The moss comes first. It comes before everything else in your understanding because it is the floor, or rather, it is what the floor is made of where the stone is not bare. *Sphagnum*, or something like it — a wet, absorbing, densely packed moss that holds water the way a sponge holds water, not by structure but by nature. It is dark green, so dark that in this light it reads as black, and it covers perhaps sixty percent of the floor, thick, wet, spongy under your foot in a way that tells you it has been here for a very long time, building, layering, each generation compressing the one below into peat, and the peat into something denser, and the dense thing into a substrate, and the substrate into a floor. The moss is the floor the way the stone is the floor. It is not growing on the ground. It is the ground.

The ferns come second. They rise from the moss in clusters, in thickets, in what can only be called groves — and this is where the cathedral scale announces itself, because the ferns are tall. Not tree-fern tall, not tropical, but a meter, a meter and a half, their fronds arching up and then bending outward, interlocking with the fronds of the next cluster, creating a canopy that is three feet above the moss, a green darkness within the brown darkness, a room within a room. The fronds are wet. Every frond is wet, and the wetness does not drip — it clings. The water holds the frond, the frond holds the water, and the droplets that do fall fall so slowly that you hear them land on the moss below as individual events, each one distinct, each one the same.

Liverworts cover the stone where the moss cannot gain purchase — the smooth faces of the columns, the walls where the water runs too fast for moss to root. They are dark, nearly black, glossy with moisture, and they follow the water the way the water follows the stone. Where the weeping is heaviest, the liverworts are thickest, and where the weeping is heaviest, the glow is brightest, because the lichen that makes the light grows where the liverwort grows, and the liverwort grows where the water runs, and the water runs where the stone has cracked, and the stone cracked when the mountain was made, and the mountain was made before anything alive existed, and so the chain of dependence runs backward to a time before dependence.

There are no trees. There are no flowers. There are no grasses. The vegetation is the vegetation of wet stone and deep shade — moss, fern, liverwort, lichen, fungus — and it is lush. Lush in the way that a saturation is lush, not the way a meadow is lush. Everything that can grow here grows here, and everything that grows here grows at its maximum possible density. There is no bare surface that could support life that does not support life. The stone that is bare is bare because it is too smooth, too vertical, too swept by water — and even there, the liverwort finds hold. The cirque is full. There is no room for more.

And it does not change. The moss is exactly as thick as it has been. The ferns are exactly as tall. The fronds are in exactly the position they were in yesterday, last year, last century, because the temperature does not change, and the water does not change, and the light does not change, and so the growing season is permanent and the growing season is complete. Nothing is growing. Nothing is dying. The vegetation is in the state that a fire is in when it has reached steady state — not the same molecules, perhaps, the individual cells turning over in their slow cycles, but the same form, the same shape, the same wet green darkness at every moment. The moss is always this thick. It has been this thick for a very long time.

## V. The Fauna

This is where the cirque becomes difficult.

You hear them before you see them, and what you hear is not sound — it is the absence of silence. The cirque is quiet, deeply quiet, the kind of quiet that in other places would be called silence, but it is not silence because it is full. Full of breathing. Full of the soft, wet sounds of bodies in contact with wet surfaces — the click of a foot on stone, the slide of a belly on moss, the imperceptible rustle of something adjusting its weight on a frond. These sounds are so soft that they register not as events but as texture. The quiet has a texture, and the texture is alive.

They are everywhere.

The salamanders come first to your attention because they are the largest and the most still. They are on the stone, on the moss, on the fern fronds, in the water, on the walls — everywhere there is a wet surface, there is a salamander, dark-backed, moist-skinned, the size of a hand or smaller, and they are not moving. They sit. They breathe — you can see the slow expansion of the throat, once every thirty seconds, once a minute — but they do not move. They are not hiding. They are not waiting. They are there, on their surfaces, in their positions, and they have been there, in those positions, for as long as the moss has been that thick. You count — not because you want to but because the density forces it — and in the small area you can see, which is perhaps three meters by three meters, you count eleven. In the whole cirque, at this density, there would be hundreds. Perhaps a thousand. The salamanders do not crowd each other. They are spaced — evenly, as though by design, though the design is not design but the result of each one finding its place and staying there, and the places being exactly the right distance apart because the resources are exactly the right amount for this many and no more. Saturation. The word comes to you and stays.

The beetles are next. They are smaller, dark-shelled, and they move — but their movement is so slow and so patterned that it does not register as movement at the scale of the cirque. A beetle on the moss walks three centimeters in a minute, and you see it walk, and you see that it is alive, but if you looked away and looked back, you would not know it had moved. They are on the moss, in the moss, under the liverworts, on the surface of the water, on the salamanders — and this is the moment you understand the density, because some of the beetles are on the salamanders, and the salamanders do not react, and the beetles do not bite, and this is not a parasitic relationship or a symbiotic one, it is simply that the salamander is a surface and the beetle needs a surface and the salamander does not mind. Every surface is occupied. Every surface has its occupant. The cirque is a mosaic of organisms on organisms on organisms, a fractal of occupation, and at no scale — not the scale of the cirque, not the scale of the fern, not the scale of the salamander, not the scale of the beetle, not the scale of the mite on the beetle — is there emptiness.

The spiders build in the ferns. Not webs — or not the kind of webs you associate with spiders. They build platforms, sheets of silk strung between fronds, and on these platforms water condenses, and the condensation attracts springtails, and the springtails are eaten. The platforms are everywhere in the fern canopy, every intersection of fronds has one, and they are all the same size, and they all have the same amount of water on them, and they are all catching the same number of springtails. The spiders sit at the center of each platform, and they are still, and the stillness is the same stillness as the salamanders' stillness — not waiting, not hiding, just being there, in the position that is theirs.

Snails — dark-shelled, wet-shelled — graze the lichen on the walls. You see their tracks in the glow, silver lines on the blue-white luminescence, and the lines are all the same length, and they all go in the same direction, and you realize that the tracks are not tracks of movement but tracks of maintenance — the snail follows the same path, eats the same lichen, and the lichen grows back at the same rate, and the snail comes back, and the cycle is so perfectly timed that the track looks fresh and ancient at the same time.

And in the water — the shallow, still, temperature-less water that covers the floor — there are other things. Not fish. There is not enough water for fish, and not enough flow, and not enough light. But there are creatures in the water that you cannot see clearly, that move at the boundary of perception — flatworms, perhaps, or larval something, or something that has no name because it has never needed one. They are there because the water is there, and they are in the water because the water is their place, and their place is the same place it has always been.

You try to find an empty surface. A stone with nothing on it. A frond with nothing under it. A patch of moss with nothing in it. You cannot. The cirque is not mostly full. The cirque is full. It is as full as it can be, and it has been as full as it can be for a span of time that would make your concept of time feel small.

## VI. The Traces

In the moss, near the fissure where the grey light falls, your hand finds something.

It is a stone. Not a stone from the mountain — the wrong type, the wrong color, brought here from somewhere else. And it is not a natural stone. It has been struck. You can feel the flake scars, the places where something hard hit something harder and took pieces away. It is a tool, or it was a tool, or it was the beginning of a tool — a core, a raw material, a thing that someone struck against another thing to make an edge.

It is old. The moss has grown over it, and the moss grows slowly, and the moss has been this thick for a span of time that you are afraid to estimate. The tool is not from this year. Not from this century. Someone came here — through the fissure, into the dark, into the wet — and they brought this stone, and they struck it here, and they either took what they made or left without it, and the moss grew over the evidence, and the moss has been the same thickness ever since.

You look for more. You find them — three more flakes, two of the same foreign stone, one of something else, a dark chert that could be local or could be from very far away. All in the moss near the fissure. All in an area of perhaps one square meter. Whoever came here came here more than once, or stayed long enough to work several pieces, and they worked where the light was, and the light was only here, by the crack in the rock, and so this is where they sat.

You try to imagine them — sitting in the grey stripe, striking stone against stone, the sound of flaking loud in the quiet, the salamanders watching with their unblinking eyes, the beetles crawling over their feet. You try to imagine what they wanted. A scraper for a hide. A point for a spear. Something to cut. Something to pierce. Something to make the world outside this place more survivable, and they came here to make it, here, where the light was grey and the water was patient and the creatures were so many that they must have sat on the visitor's legs like they sat on the stone.

Or maybe they came for the creatures. Maybe the salamanders were the point. Maybe this was a hunting place, a gathering place, and the stone tools were for the taking, and the taking was easy because the salamanders did not run, and the beetles did not flee, and the snails clung to the walls in their perfect rows. Maybe the visitors came and took what they needed and the ecosystem absorbed the loss and refilled the gap and by the time they came back, the next day or the next season, the salamanders were in the same positions at the same density, because the system does not change.

You find one more thing. Not a tool. A mark. On the stone wall, near the fissure, at a height that suggests a sitting adult — a scratch. A line cut into the rock, perhaps with the chert flake, perhaps with something harder. A single line, perhaps ten centimeters long. It could be a tally. It could be a mark of presence. It could be nothing — a natural scratch, a geological feature. But it is straight, and geological scratches are not straight. Someone made this. Someone was here, and they wanted to say so, and they said so with a line, and the line has been here ever since, and the lichen has grown around it but not over it, as though even the lichen knows.

## VII. The Stasis

You stay. You stay because the place asks you to, not by pulling but by not pushing. Nothing here wants you to leave. Nothing here wants you to stay. Nothing here wants anything. The wanting is not a property of this place. The wanting has been spent, burned off, exhausted in the long process of reaching this state, and what remains is not desire but equilibrium.

You sit. The moss accepts your weight. A beetle climbs onto your boot and sits there. A salamander — you did not see it arrive, and perhaps it was always there, perhaps you sat on it, perhaps it was under the moss and your weight pressed the moss down and the salamander up — adjusts itself and is still. You are now a surface. You are now part of the cirque's economy of surfaces.

The water drips. A drop falls from a fern frond to the moss with a soft sound — a tap, a click, a note. Another follows. You begin to count, and the counting slows your thinking, and the counting tells you what the place already knows: the drops are falling at a rate. Not a random rate. A rate. One every four seconds, or one every six, or one every some-number, and the number is consistent, and the consistency is not mechanical but biological — the fern produces water at the rate that the air deposits water on the fern, and the air deposits water at the rate that the walls produce water, and the walls produce water at the rate that the mountain feeds the walls, and the mountain feeds the walls at a rate that has not changed since the mountain stopped changing, and the mountain stopped changing a long time ago.

Everything here is like this. The salamanders breathe at a rate. The beetles walk at a rate. The lichen grows at a rate. The snails graze at a rate. And the rates are not independent — they are linked, coupled, entangled, each one depending on the others, each one set by the others, and the whole system is a clock that runs on its own time and has no mechanism to change its speed. The cirque is a clock that shows one time. The time is now. The time has always been now.
