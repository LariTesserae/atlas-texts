# The Condensation Works

It begins with the sound, except there is almost no sound. A low respiratory hum that you feel in the sternum before you hear it — and then you realize you've been hearing it since you arrived, that it was never silent, that the silence was just the absence of anything else. The hum is the pumps. They are still running.

---

You come up through the switchback road carved into pale scoria, and the plateau opens before you in a wash of dim amber light. The sky is not overcast exactly — it's *saturated*. A permanent cloud deck sits three hundred meters above the ground, not grey but the color of weak tea, and it bleeds light downward in a flat, directionless way that makes every surface look like the inside of a shaded lamp. Nothing casts a shadow. Or rather, everything casts the same shadow in every direction, which amounts to the same thing.

The city is called Keltănei in the old survey, though nobody lives here who would use the name. Nobody lives here at all, in the usual sense. But it is not abandoned. That distinction matters.

From the road you can see the full extent of the Works. The name is apt — everything here is working, still performing the functions it was built for, though the reason for those functions has become unclear, perhaps even to the machines themselves. The settlement covers a basin roughly four kilometers across, set into the high plateau like a wide shallow bowl. The elevation is enough that the air thins slightly at the edges of thought, enough that you notice your breathing the way you notice a clock after the power cuts out. It is hot. Not desert-hot — bathhouse-hot. The kind of heat that comes from below and from the air at the same time, so you can't tell which direction to escape it.

---

## The Water

Water is everywhere but not in any form you'd choose to drink. It comes up through the rock. The plateau is riddled with fissures — geological fractures that run down to something warm, something pressurized — and the water rises through them in a slow exhalation that never quite becomes a geyser. It reaches the surface and meets the air and the air is cooler than the rock but warmer than the water should permit, and so the water doesn't know what to do. It condenses. It beads on every surface. It runs down the walls of every structure in thin sheeting films that never quite gather into streams. The city weeps.

Every building is wet. Every pipe, every railing, every surface carries a gloss of moisture that reforms within minutes of being wiped. In the lower parts of the basin, where the water pools into broad shallow basins between structures, the surface is always trembling — not from wind, because the air is mostly still, but from the heat below, from tiny convective currents that rise and break and rise again. The pools are warm to the touch. Tepid. Body-temperature. This makes them more disturbing than cold water would be. They feel like something.

The water carries minerals. You can see this in what it leaves behind: pale travertine terraces forming on the lower walls, tufa mounds building slowly around the outflow points, a crust of calcium and silica and something else — something with a faint opalescent quality, a mineral the old surveys don't name, that forms in branching patterns like the nervous system of something that never had a body. These deposits have been building for as long as the Works have been running. They are the closest thing to growth here.

---

## The Structures

The city was built for the water. That much is clear from the layout, which makes no sense as a settlement and perfect sense as a machine. There are buildings, yes — but they are arranged not around streets or plazas but around *flow*. Channels cut into the basalt run between structures like capillaries, carrying the rising water in directed paths. Some of these channels are open, some are enclosed in pipe systems that run overhead and along the ground in a tangle that looks chaotic until you follow any single line and find it goes somewhere purposeful. The water is being moved. It is being moved through cooling galleries, through settling tanks, through distribution hubs, and ultimately out to the edges of the basin where it falls in wide thin curtains over weirs and vanishes into the fractures again, or evaporates into the cloud deck above. The system is a circuit. It has been running for — nobody knows. Long enough for the travertine to build up meters thick in places. Long enough for the original metalwork to be partly encased in mineral deposits, like bones fossilizing in real time.

The buildings themselves are industrial in origin. Blocky, functional, made from the same pale scoria as the road, mortared with a pale cement that has itself become coated with mineral flow. Some are large enough to be called halls — the cooling galleries, where water runs in shallow sheets over ribbed floors to maximize surface area for heat exchange. Some are small, shed-sized, containing pumps or valves or mechanisms whose purpose is not immediately legible. A few structures break the pattern: a tower, maybe twenty meters high, open at the top, from which steam rises in a continuous pale column that feeds the cloud deck. A long low building with no visible entrance, set apart from the others, from which a deep rhythmic vibration emanates — the loudest sound in the Works, and still it is barely audible from fifty meters away. You feel it more than hear it, a pulse in the ground, in the water, in the soles of your feet.

Everything is electrified. The cables are old, rubber-insulated, cracked in places, running between poles that lean at angles determined by decades of settling. The power still flows. You know this because the lights still come on at intervals — not reliably, not everywhere, but in a pattern that suggests automated systems still cycling through their routines. A sodium lamp on the corner of a pump house flickers amber. A row of indicator lights on a control panel visible through a broken window glows in a slow sequence: green, amber, red, green. Nobody is watching the indicators. They report to no one. They have been reporting to no one for a very long time, and they have not stopped.

---

## The Atmosphere

The air is heavy. The humidity is high enough that breathing feels like an effort of intention, not reflex. The heat sits on the skin and does not leave. Combined with the dim light and the altitude, this produces a state that is not quite dizziness but something adjacent — a looseness in perception, a sense that the boundaries between objects are softer than they should be. The clouds above are always moving, always reforming, but so slowly that you can only tell by comparing their shape to what you remember, and your memory feels unreliable.

The condensation is not passive. It is the dominant process here. The water comes up warm and meets the air and the air takes what it can and the rest condenses on surfaces, and those surfaces are mostly the city itself. This means the city is always wet because the city is always in the way. It has inserted itself into a natural cycle and become part of it — not disrupting the water but routing it, shaping its path, giving it work to do. The water does not seem to mind. Or if it does, its complaint is only the slow build of mineral deposits that will eventually seal the channels and stop the flow and bring the system to a halt. This has not happened yet. It may be happening now, too slowly to perceive.

---

## The Absence of Life

No plants. This is the first thing you notice, after the heat: the absolute absence of anything green. The mineral deposits are pale, the stone is pale, the water is clear-to-opalescent, the metalwork is rust-brown or corroded grey. The only colors are amber and off-white and the dull red of old iron. Nothing grows here. The water is too mineral, the ground too hot, the light too dim. Or perhaps the problem is more fundamental — perhaps this place was never meant for growth, was built precisely to exclude it, and the exclusion has held.

But there is something. In the deeper pools, in the channels where the water runs slowest, there is a film on the surface that is not mineral. It has an iridescence to it, a shifting oily sheen that rearranges itself in slow patterns that do not correspond to any current or breeze. When you look closely — and you have to look closely, because the dim light makes everything uncertain — the film appears to have structure. Layers. A complexity that is too organized to be chemical and too simple to be biological. It is not alive in any way you would recognize. But it is doing something. Reacting. Perhaps the water carries something it needs, and the film arranges itself to gather it. Perhaps it has been doing this since before the city was built.

In the long low building with no entrance, the one that vibrates, there are sounds that are almost organic. Not from inside the building — from the ground around it. A ticking, a clicking, like something shifting in the rock. It could be thermal expansion. It could be the mineral deposits cracking under stress. It could be something else. The sounds are arrhythmic and infrequent enough that you can never quite isolate them. They occur in the spaces between the pump-hum and the water-flow and the almost-silence, and they are gone before you can turn toward them.

There are animals here, or were. In the upper structures, near the tower where the steam rises, you find structures that might be nests — accumulations of debris, mineral fragments and old cable insulation and strips of corroded metal, arranged in patterns too regular to be accidental. Whatever built them is not present. The nests are old. The mineral crust has begun to form on their surfaces, which means they have been here for years, perhaps decades. Whatever made them either left or died or transformed into something that no longer builds nests. The only sign of current fauna is a sound, heard once, briefly, in the late dim hours when the light was at its lowest: a low chittering from somewhere in the high pipe network, like a mammal, like a bat, but lower and slower, as if the heat had stretched the sound out.

---

## The Rules

The Works operate on rules that were established by whoever built them and have been maintained by the systems themselves ever since. The pumps run. The water flows. The lights cycle. The power generates. These are the laws of this place — not laws of physics, though those apply, but laws of *function*. The city is a machine that has outlasted its purpose, and a machine with no purpose does not stop; it simply continues, and the continuation becomes the purpose.

The weather here is also a rule: the water rises, the air takes it, the clouds form, the condensation falls on every surface, the water flows back down, the cycle repeats. This has been happening for long enough that the geology has adapted to it — the fractures are wider than they were, the travertine is thicker, the pools are deeper. The landscape is being shaped by the city's presence, slowly, in the way that a river shapes a canyon. The city is the river.

And the dimness is a rule too. The cloud deck does not break. It does not thin. It regenerates as fast as it dissipates. The light that comes through is the color of weak tea and the strength of old memory, and it does not change with the hours. There is no day and night here, only the same perpetual amber twilight, and the only way to tell time is by the cycles of the automated systems — the lights that switch on and off in their slow pattern, the valves that open and close on some schedule that nobody programmed recently, the pumps that surge and quiet and surge again as if breathing.

---

## The Weirdness

There is something else. You find it when you stop moving and listen — not to the pumps, not to the water, but to the space between them. The silence here is not empty. It has a texture. It is the sound of something happening at a scale too large to hear, or too slow, or too regular to distinguish from the absence of sound. When you stand in the lower basin where the pools are widest and the water is warmest and the steam rises thickest, you can feel it: a sense of *attention*. Not directed at you. Not directed at anything. But present, the way a room is different when someone is sleeping in it — the same furniture, the same light, but something about the air acknowledges that it is occupied.

The film on the water does not help. The patterns it makes are too deliberate. When you move, the patterns shift — not away from you, not toward you, but *around* you, accommodating your presence the way water accommodates a stone. When you leave, they do not return to their previous configuration. They find a new one. You have changed something, or something has changed in response to you, and the distinction between those two possibilities is not one you can resolve.

In the deep building, the one that vibrates, the sound is strongest at night — if night is what you call the hours when the lights dim to their minimum. The vibration is not mechanical. It does not have the regularity of a motor or a pump. It has the quality of a voice heard through a wall, too low to make out words. If you press your hand flat against the wall of that building, you can feel it in your teeth. It says something. It has been saying something for a very long time. No one is listening, and it has not stopped.

---

The Works are not haunted. Hauntings require the dead, and the dead require a category that this place does not maintain. What is here is not past. It is ongoing. The water is rising. The pumps are running. The film is forming. The sound is sounding. The city is doing what it was built to do, which was — what? To manage water, perhaps. To generate power. To *process* something that no one processes anymore. The purpose has been forgotten by everyone except the systems performing it, and they do not forget because they cannot, and they cannot because forgetting is not among their functions, and so the purpose continues, embedded in the act of continuation itself.

You leave the way you came, up through the switchback road carved into pale scoria, and the plateau falls behind you, and the amber light fades, and the hum drops below the threshold of hearing, and you realize you can still feel it in your sternum. You will feel it for hours. You will feel it, on certain quiet nights, for years. It does not want anything from you. It is not calling you back. It is simply still going, and you were briefly near enough to catch the vibration, and the vibration does not know how to stop being in you, because stopping is not among its functions.

The road continues down. The sky clears. The shadows return, sharp-edged and directional, and the world becomes ordinary again, or seems to. But somewhere behind you, below the cloud deck, in the warm dim wet, the pumps are still running and the water is still rising and the film is still arranging itself in patterns that no one will ever decode, and the building with no door is still saying what it has always said, and the mineral deposits are growing, and the light is cycling, and the Works are working, and the Works are working, and the Works are working.
