# The Plateau of Amestris

The water here is ancient. Not old in the way that stone is old, but ancient in the way that silence is old—it has been still so long that it has forgotten how to move. And yet it moves constantly, pulled by currents that have been running the same courses for longer than this world has had names for them.

This is a high place. That takes a moment to understand, submerged as everything is. But the seafloor below—if one could call it a floor—drops away into darkness, into trenches that might as well be other worlds entirely. Here, the water is only perhaps two hundred meters deep, which is nothing, which is a breath, which is the difference between pressure that crushes and pressure that merely transforms. The plateau rises from the abyssal plain like the roof of a submerged continent, and the structures rise from the plateau like a city that was never built, only grown.

Everything is made. This is the first truth that any visitor—if there were visitors—would understand. There is no natural substrate beneath the constructions, no bedrock waiting to be revealed if one could somehow peel back the architecture. The architecture goes all the way down. Pillars and platforms and lattices that interlock in ways that suggest intention, design, some vast intelligence operating across timescales that make human civilization look like a mayfly's afternoon. But there are no tools here. No machines. No technology in any sense that would be recognized above the waves. The structures were not built. They were secreted, deposited, woven by bodies that have no concept of weaving.

The water is cold. Not the absolute zero of the deep trenches, but the steady, relentless cold of depths where sunlight arrives only as rumor, as a faint blue suggestion far above. The temperature holds at just above freezing, and it has held there for millions of years. The cold is a preservative. Things do not rot here. They calcify, crystallize, become part of the architecture slowly, over centuries.

The plateau is quiet, but the silence has texture. Listen: there is the deep thrum of currents moving through the lattice-structures, a sound so constant that it becomes a kind of silence, a baseline against which all other sounds are measured. There is the faint clicking and grinding of creatures moving across mineral surfaces. There is the occasional groan of something shifting settling eroding at the pace that mountains erode, audible only as infrasonic vibration felt in the body rather than heard by ears. And there is another sound, harder to identify—a whisper, a rustle, the sound of something being built, slowly, particle by particle, a structure accumulating itself out of the dissolved minerals in the water.

The light is dim. Above, somewhere far above, there is a sun, and it shines on water that eventually becomes this water, but by the time the light arrives here it has been filtered through hundreds of meters of ocean, stripped of its warmth and most of its visibility. What remains is a blue-gray twilight that never varies, a perpetual dusk lit only by the creatures themselves. The fauna here are abundant, and many of them glow—not with the dramatic bioluminescence of shallower waters, but with a faint phosphorescence, a low-level emission that marks their outlines against the dark. They drift and swim and crawl across the structures, and where they go, they leave faint trails of light that fade slowly behind them.

The vegetation rating is 0.2, which is generous. There is no plant life here. Nothing photosynthesizes at this depth. The ecosystem runs on chemicals instead of sunlight—on the minerals that seep from hydrothermal vents, on the organic particulate matter that drifts down from above (marine snow, it is called in other places, though here it seems more like a faint ash, a constant drizzle of dead matter from worlds away), on the structures themselves, which seem to harbor colonies of microorganisms too small to see but essential to everything that lives here. The vegetation rating might refer to these, or it might refer to the things that look like plants but aren't—the crinoid-like animals that root themselves to surfaces and filter-feed with feathery appendages, the long strand-like organisms that drift in the currents and seem to wave like kelp but are in fact colonies of simpler creatures, living together in shapes that serve them.

But the structures are what draw the eye, what hold it, what refuse to let it go. They are vast. Not vast in the way that a single building can be vast, but vast in the way that a landscape is vast. They extend past the limits of visibility in every direction, sprawling across the plateau in what might be a pattern or might be chaos—there is no way to tell the difference from any single vantage point. Pillars rise in clusters that become forests from certain angles. Platforms layer upon platforms in geometries that seem impossible until you trace the supports and find that they are solid, logical, rooted in structures below them which are rooted in structures below those, down and down until the eye loses the thread and the mind admits that this is not a building but an accumulation, an agglomeration, something that has been adding itself to itself for longer than any creature now alive can remember.

The construction material is calcareous, the same calcium compounds that form shells and coral and bone in other places. But here it has been worked differently—denser, more regular, forming shapes that echo organic structures without exactly mimicking them. There are chambers within chambers, passages that twist and double back, surfaces that are smooth at one scale and pocked with smaller openings at another. The structures look skeletal, but the skeleton is not of any single creature. It is the collected remains of a billion billion small lives, each one adding its contribution, building on what came before.

The fauna know these structures intimately. They have evolved with them, around them, inside them. There are creatures here that exist nowhere else—forms that have no names, that have never been seen by human or even terrestrial eyes. Crustaceans with bodies like glass and limbs like ornamental scrollwork, moving in precise dances across the surfaces. Soft-bodied animals that flow through the chambers like liquid, their translucent skins revealing internal structures of impossible delicacy. Swarming things that move in synchronized clouds, their collective motion creating shapes that suggest larger purpose. And larger predators, rarely seen, their presence marked only by the sudden absence of other creatures, the silence that falls when they pass through.

The ecosystem here is in constant motion—not violent motion, but the endless agitation of creatures feeding, breeding, dying, and becoming material for the structures. The turbulence rating is high because nothing settles here. The water is always moving, always carrying something—eggs, larvae, particulate matter, dissolved minerals. The structures themselves are always changing, growing in some places, eroding in others, being repaired by organisms that seem to exist precisely for that purpose. The plateau is not a static place. It is a slow riot, a perpetual reconstruction.

The weirdness is subtle. This is not a place of overt strangeness, of phenomena that violate the laws of nature. It is strange only in its particularity, in the way that it has evolved a system entirely unlike any other. The structures, if you study them long enough, begin to seem like they might have a purpose—not the purpose of shelter or storage, nothing so simple, but something else, some function that is difficult to name. Information storage, perhaps. Or communication across time. Or simply existence for its own sake, life accumulating because that is what life does, building because building is more natural than not-building.

The erosion rating is almost as high as it can be. This means that the structures have reached something like equilibrium—they are no longer growing rapidly, no longer in the first flush of their creation. They have been weathered, worn, shaped by the currents into their final forms. Or nearly final—there is always a little more erosion to be done, a little more accretion to be added. But the plateau feels settled, ancient, at peace with itself in a way that newer formations never are. It has achieved its shape. It is not trying to become anything else.

The scale is open—this is not a single feature but a region, a landscape, something that would take days or weeks to cross even if one could move freely through the water. The structures create horizons even in a medium where horizons do not naturally exist. They block sight lines, create canyons and valleys, form a topography entirely separate from the geological features beneath them. The plateau has its own mountains, its own plains, its own caves and cliffs and overhangs, all made by living things over millions of years.

And the density is sparse—there is much space between the features, much emptiness between the structures. This is not a crowded place, not a reef packed tight with life. The architecture is spread out, leaving room for currents to flow, for animals to migrate, for the slow deposition of new material. The emptiness is part of the design, or would be, if there were design. It is the result of growth patterns that maximize surface area, that create space rather than filling it.

There are no plants. The vegetation rating tells you this. But there are things that fill the niche of plants, things that anchor themselves and wait and gather what comes to them. Filter-feeders with bodies like ornamental glass, their feeding appendages spread to catch whatever the currents bring. Things that look like anemones but behave more like traps, waiting for the specific prey that they have evolved to capture. And in the chambers of the structures, hidden from the open water, there are organisms even stranger—chemotrophs that feed on the minerals that seep through the ancient walls, things that have never seen light of any kind, that live in the interior passages of the construction and never leave.

The atmosphere—if one can call it atmosphere, in a place entirely underwater—is thick with particulates, with the suspended matter that feeds the ecosystem. The water moves slowly in some places, quickly in others, its motion determined by the shapes it flows around. There are currents within currents, microclimates of temperature and flow rate, places where the water has been trapped so long that it has become chemically distinct from the water around it.

Sound behaves strangely here. The density of the water, the shapes of the structures, the strange acoustic properties of the construction materials—all of these create a soundscape that is difficult to interpret. Sounds seem to come from nowhere and everywhere. A click might echo through passages and emerge as a whisper on the other side of a platform. The constant background thrum of the currents is sometimes louder, sometimes softer, for reasons that have nothing to do with the actual movement of water. There are sounds that seem to be generated by the structures themselves—creaks and groans as the material expands and contracts with tiny temperature shifts, as minerals dissolve and precipitate in endless slow cycles.

The dynamic rating is at maximum. This place is change itself—not fast change, not change visible on any human timescale, but change nonetheless, constant and irreversible. The structures are being built and rebuilt and eroded and rebuilt again. The creatures that live here are dying and being replaced by their offspring, who are slightly different, who have adapted slightly better to conditions that have changed slightly since their parents' time. The water itself is changing, its chemistry shifting as minerals are added and removed, as organic matter drifts down from above or wells up from below. Nothing here is the same as it was a thousand years ago. Nothing here will be the same in another thousand years.

And yet the overall shape persists. The plateau remains a plateau. The structures remain structures. The ecosystem remains an ecosystem. This is the paradox of living architecture: it changes constantly and maintains itself simultaneously. It is always becoming what it has always been.

The light that exists here—the dim blue twilight filtered from above, the faint glow of the creatures themselves—is enough for the animals that have evolved to use it. Their eyes, where they have eyes, are adapted to the darkness, to the particular wavelengths that penetrate to this depth, to the bioluminescent signals of their own kind. There are creatures here that can see in ways that have no equivalent in terrestrial biology—sensing electrical fields, detecting pressure waves, reading the chemical traces left by other creatures in their passing. The world is visible to them in a fullness that would be alien to any surface-dweller.

The built rating is nearly total, and this is the strangest thing about this place, the thing that takes the longest to understand. There is no nature here, in the sense of something separate from the made environment. The nature IS the made environment. The animals and their constructions are a single system, a single vast organism spread across time and space, building itself continuously. The distinction between the living and the non-living breaks down. The structures are made of the remains of creatures, but they are also home to creatures, and they are also actively maintained by creatures, and they are also the product of biological processes that continue even now. What is this place? A city? A reef? A skeleton? A garden? It is all of these and none of them.

The weirdness rating is low, relatively speaking. This is not a place of impossible things, of violations of natural law, of phenomena that defy understanding. It is simply a place that operates by rules that are unfamiliar to those who know only surface worlds. The strangeness is in the particular combination of familiar elements, in the way that life has found a path that does not require sunlight or soil or any of the things that surface-dwellers think of as essential. It is in the scale of time, in the patience of construction that spans millions of years. It is in the unity of life and architecture, the way that the animals and their environment have become each other.

The rules of this world are simple, if you take the time to learn them:

Everything is part of the structure. The creatures live in it, on it, around it. They feed on the matter that drifts through it, on each other, on the microorganisms that colonize its surfaces. When they die, they become part of it—their shells, their bones, the mineral compounds of their bodies add to the construction, molecule by molecule.

Everything is connected. No creature here exists in isolation. The filter-feeders create currents that bring food to other filter-feeders. The predators keep populations in check, preventing any single species from overrunning the structures. The organisms that maintain the architecture create spaces that shelter other organisms, which in turn provide food for the maintainers. It is a web of dependencies so dense that to remove any single thread might unravel the whole.

Everything is slow. Growth happens at rates that are difficult to perceive. The largest structures might add millimeters in a century. The creatures that live here have life spans that range from days to millennia, but all of them exist in a rhythm that matches their environment. Nothing rushes. Nothing hurries. There is no need.

Everything is ancient and everything is new. The structures have been here for millions of years, but every part of them is being continuously renewed. The material that forms them is ancient, but the arrangement of that material is constantly changing. The species that live here have evolved over countless generations, but every individual is newly born, newly making its way in a world that its ancestors would still recognize.

The plateau stretches into darkness in every direction. The water is cold and moves in slow currents. The structures rise like the work of some vast and patient civilization, but there is no civilization here—only life, persisting, building, continuing. The creatures glow faintly as they move through the perpetual twilight, tracing paths through chambers that were old before the first human ancestor crawled onto land. And somewhere in the depths of the architecture, in passages that no surface-dweller has ever seen, the slow process of construction continues, particle by particle, adding another layer to something that might be a monument or might be a home or might simply be the shape that life takes when it is given enough time and enough silence and enough water to work with.

This is a place where time is measured in the deposition of calcium compounds. A place where the boundary between architecture and organism has dissolved. A place where everything is made and nothing is built. A place of cold water and dim light and creatures that have never known any other world.

It is enough.
