# The Vellum Plains

The ground here is not ground. Step down and there is give, then resistance, then a subtle rebound—the sensation of walking on something that acknowledges your presence. This is because the substrate is alive. Beneath each footfall lies a meter of interwoven hyphae, mycelial architectures so dense they've become geology, so ancient they've forgotten they were ever individual organisms. The Vellum, the scattered settlements call it. The great living sheet.

High plateau country. The air moves thin and cool across the expanse, carrying almost no moisture—this world shed its water billions of years ago, or never had much to begin with. Some aqueous chemistry persists in sealed pockets deep underground, but nothing free, nothing that flows. What the Vellum drinks is gas. What it drinks is light.

And the light is dim.

---

The sun hangs small and orange-white in a sky that never quite achieves full daylight. The Vellum's upper surface has evolved for this: each square meter coated in millions of translucent papery structures like flattened trumpets, like decadent flower-heads with no blooms, each one angled to catch and concentrate the feeble rays. From a distance the plain looks silvered, frosted, as if dew had crystallized across every surface—but there is no dew here. Has not been for epochs. The silver sheen is the skin of the world breathing.

The sound begins at dawn.

Not birdcall. Not wind. The sound *emerges* from the Vellum itself—a low thrumming that builds as the light strengthens, resonating through millions of those papery catching-structures. Each one vibrates at a slightly different frequency, and the sum of them creates a chord, a vast drawn-out chord that shifts by the hour as the sun moves and different zones of the plain tilt their receivers toward it. The settlements have learned to tell time by the chord's tonal shifts. Dawn sounds different than midday sounds different than dusk.

Add to this the fauna.

---

The settlements call them *runners*—though they run on legs that bend wrong and number six or eight. They emerge from distant dens at the Vellum's edge and sprint across the living plain in small herds, their bodies the color of old bone, their motion a blur that kicks up silver dust—no, not dust, spores, cells sloughed from the Vellum's surface. They graze by running. Their undersides are lined with abrasive ridges that strip away the upper layers of the substrate as they move, and behind each herd the plain glows darker, raw, exposed. The Vellum seals itself within hours. The runners have evolved to prefer the freshly exposed tissue, richer in gases, not yet hardened against the thin atmosphere.

They are loud. Their many feet drum the Vellum like fingers on a stretched membrane, and the drumming travels, conducted through the living sheet so that distant herds announce themselves long before they crest the horizon. The settlements track them by listening to the ground.

Other sounds: the runners communicate in sharp barks and whistles, but these are almost lost in the larger soundscape. The Vellum's dawn-chord. The wind moving through the sparse architecture of the settlements. And something else, something deeper—a kind of grinding that rises and falls without pattern, that the oldest residents say is the Vellum adjusting itself, shifting its kilometers-deep layers like a sleeping animal turns in its sleep.

---

The settlements exist as interruptions in the Vellum's surface.

They began as hollows—spaces where the living substrate was persuaded to grow around rather than through. The technology for this persuasion is old beyond reckoning now, embedded in the settlements' founding codes, passed down through generations of substrate-wrights who understand that the Vellum is not engineered but negotiated with. The architecture is grown. Chambers rise from the living floor in patient helices, their walls continuous with the plain, their windows formed by bands of cells that thin and transparent over decades of careful cultivation.

The largest settlement—still small, still sparse, the density parameter insists on space between things—spreads across a shallow bowl in the Vellum's surface, perhaps two kilometers across. Its buildings cluster in loose groups, each one grown from a single point outward, spiraling up to four or five stories before the growth-songs dictate a halt. The structures look fungal, intestinal, beautiful in a way that Earth architecture never achieved because Earth architecture never accepted that buildings should be alive.

Inside, the temperature stays cool. The Vellum's metabolism generates minimal heat—its chemistry runs on different principles, electron gradients rather than aqueous reactions—and the thin atmosphere sheds warmth quickly after dusk. Residents wear dense woven coats grown from specialized Vellum cultivars, fibers pulled and spun from living tissue in long threads that harden into durable cloth.

There is no water in these settlements. Residents drink a filtered extract drawn from deep reservoirs in the Vellum, a complex hydrocarbon fluid that their biology has adapted to across the thousands of generations since their ancestors arrived. The fluid is warm when it emerges—drawn from the depths where the Vellum's core processes run hotter—and it smells of minerals and something sweeter, something that has no name in languages born on water-worlds.

---

The soundscape reaches its peak at midday.

The Vellum's chord has risen to its highest register now, a shimmering wall of sound that makes conversation difficult and thought almost impossible. Residents retreat indoors where the walls dampen the resonance, or they don special membranes grown to cover the ear-canals, filtering out the dominant frequencies. The runners have gone to ground, hiding from the light's brief intensity, and the plain appears empty—a silver expanse under a pale sun, empty except for the sound.

But the plain is never empty.

Look closer: the Vellum's surface crawls with smaller life. Things like elongated beetles, though they are not beetles, move between the papery collectors, tending them, pruning dead tissue, harvesting spores. Things like spiders—but not spiders—spin molecular threads between the surface structures, reinforcing them against the wind. The Vellum is an ecosystem, not a monoculture. It hosts ten thousand species that have evolved to live within it, on it, from it. Some of these are visible. Many are too small to see. A few exist at scales that blur the line between biology and chemistry, between organism and enzyme.

The settlements know to leave certain zones alone. The Vellum has its own immune responses, and the negotiation that allows the architecture to grow rests on careful boundaries. There are places where the substrate grows aggressive, where the surface collectors fold inward and the tissue hardens and darkens, where runners do not graze. The residents mark these zones with tall spires grown from blackened Vellum-variants, warning beacons visible across kilometers.

---

Dusk arrives with a gradual dimming that takes hours. The chord shifts down through registers, slowing, dropping notes, becoming a murmur, a whisper, then finally a kind of sigh that fades into the night. Other sounds emerge now. The runners begin their nocturnal movement, and their distant drumming mixes with the calls of creatures that emerge only in darkness—flying things with membranes too large for Earth-atmosphere, riding the thin air currents, clicking and singing to navigate by sound.

The settlement lights come on. Not electric—there is no electricity here, not in any form recognizable to Earth engineering. The lights are grown: clusters of bioluminescent cells that pulse in patterns encoding information, marking paths, signaling the status of various systems. The settlement glows soft amber, and the amber light illuminates the Vellum's silver surface, and the contrast is beautiful in a way that hurts.

At night, the temperature drops sharply. The Vellum's upper layers contract, and the contraction produces a sound—a deep groaning that travels through the ground and up through the buildings' walls. Newcomers find it unsettling. Old residents sleep through it, or claim to find comfort in it, the sound of the world breathing around them.

The plain extends beyond sight in every direction. The horizon curves, high-elevation sky, and the stars that emerge are dense and bright, unobscured by moisture, sharp as cut glass. The Milky Way—if this world has a Milky Way, if it exists in the same galaxy as Earth—is a river of light across the sky. The residents have names for constellations, but the constellations are living things, drawn from patterns in the Vellum below rather than stars above: they navigate by ground-features, not sky-features, because the ground is what matters here. The ground is everything.

---

The erosion parameter is ancient, and it shows.

This is not a landscape that changes anymore. The Vellum has reached its terminal state, its oldest stable form. It has absorbed the original topography, flattened the mountains, filled the valleys, spread until it covered everything and then grown deeper, layer on layer, until the original mineral crust lies kilometers below, inaccessible, almost mythological. The settlements are built on biomass that has been accumulating for longer than Earth has had multicellular life. Core samples taken by the first arrivals showed compression patterns, strata of growth and death and growth again, a geological record made of bodies.

The Vellum is a graveyard. It is also a nursery. Each layer feeds the next. The deepest tissues break down into simpler compounds that rise through the hyphal networks to feed the surface collectors. The dead become the living become the dead again. The cycle is so old it has worn smooth grooves into itself, paths of least resistance, stable equilibria. The ecosystem does not change; it cycles.

But the dynamic parameter says active, and this too is true.

---

Watch: a storm approaches from the distant mountains—no, not mountains, those are ancient Vellum-mounds, growths from some past era when the plain was less uniform. The storm is wind-driven, gas-driven, moving fast across the plateau. The Vellum detects the pressure change before human senses register it, and the surface begins to prepare. The papery collectors fold inward, protecting their delicate surfaces. The hyphal mat tightens, increasing its density, resisting the coming force. The settlements have warning systems grown into their walls, and these systems pulse amber now, then amber-red, signaling the residents to secure their belongings.

The storm hits. The wind howls across the plain, carrying particles of Vellum-dust, torn from distant surfaces. The sound becomes a roar, drowning out everything. The settlements creak and sway, but they were grown for this, flexible rather than rigid, bending with the wind rather than breaking against it. Inside, residents wait with practiced patience, securing drinking-fluid reserves, checking the grow-lights, waiting.

The storm passes. The Vellum unfolds again, collectors reopening, surface shaking off the dust. The chord resumes, slightly altered by the storm's passage—different frequencies dominant now, different zones exposed or covered. The residents emerge to assess. Damage is rare. The Vellum absorbs most of it, self-healing, self-repairing. The settlements need only minor adjustment, a growth-song sung to the junction points, a pruning of damaged tissue.

---

The technology here operates on principles that exceed modern Earth understanding by a considerable margin.

It is not that the physics is different—the laws are the same, presumably, everywhere—but the engineering has progressed along paths Earth science has not yet discovered. The Vellum itself may be the greatest example: a biological system that processes information at the molecular level, that stores data in protein structures, that computes using chemical gradients and quantum tunneling effects in specialized organelles. The settlements interface with this system through organic terminals, grown keyboards that read nerve impulses from the fingertips, grown screens that display information through patterned bioluminescence.

Communication across distances uses the Vellum as a medium. Messages travel through the hyphal network as chemical pulses, encoded in molecular structures that replicate and propagate. A message sent from one settlement can arrive at another a hundred kilometers away within hours, carried by the living substrate itself. There are delays, but the system is reliable. The Vellum does not corrupt data. It has no reason to.

Power—energy—comes from the light. The Vellum's collectors are the primary solar arrays, and the settlements draw from the substrate's stored energy through organic conduits. It is a symbiotic relationship: the settlements provide careful management, pruning, direction, and the Vellum provides structure, power, raw materials. The residents do not own their buildings; they tend them.

---

A day in the life of a settlement:

Morning begins with the dawn-chord rising, and residents wake to the sound rather than light—they have always done so, their sleep cycles synchronized to the Vellum's song. First meal: filtered extract, warm from the depths, drunk from vessels grown for the purpose. Then work—there is always work to be done. The buildings require tending, their growth encouraged in certain directions, their walls monitored for signs of stress. The Vellum surface needs maintenance; dead collectors must be cleared, overgrowth trimmed, the boundary zones inspected. Runners may be due, and the drumming-staff must track their herds, predicting movements, warning of possible grazing too close to settlement edges.

Children attend teaching-sessions in communal spaces, learning the growth-songs that allow them to communicate with the Vellum's surface layers. The songs are not language—they are closer to music, complex harmonic patterns that trigger specific responses in the substrate. A skilled substrate-wright can coax a wall into existence in mere weeks rather than months, can heal a damaged junction in days rather than weeks. The songs are proprietary, held by lineages, traded between settlements like currency.

Afternoon brings midday-chord and its overwhelming wall of sound. Work pauses, residents retreat, and the settlements enter a kind of siesta—not sleep, but quiet, indoor activity. Teaching-sessions continue at reduced volume. Maintenance work shifts to internal systems. The runners are absent, hiding, and the plain appears still.

Evening: the chord descends, work resumes, and the settlement prepares for night. The day's harvest of Vellum-tissue is processed into fiber and fluid and food. The waste products are returned to the substrate, cycled back, nothing wasted. The settlements operate at near-perfect efficiency, integrated into the Vellum's metabolism like organs in a body.

Night: the chord fades, the groaning begins, the flying-things emerge. Social time—residents gather in communal spaces to share food, to sing the songs, to tell stories. The old stories tell of arriving, of finding the Vellum, of learning to live with it. Some stories tell of times before the Vellum, though no one believes those anymore. The erosion parameter is too old. The Vellum has always been here. The settlements have always been here. The world has always been this way.

---

Hasn't it?

The weirdness parameter suggests otherwise. Strange things happen at the edges.

There are zones where the Vellum behaves irregularly, where the chord produces harmonics that the settlements cannot predict, where the growth-songs fail to take hold. These zones are avoided, marked, studied from a distance. Some researchers believe the Vellum is evolving—changing, after all these billions of years, responding to pressures the settlements do not understand. Others believe the Vellum is communicating, the strange harmonics a language they have not yet learned to interpret.

A few believe the Vellum is waking up.

The Vellum has no central nervous system, no brain, no obvious seat of consciousness. But it processes information. It responds to stimuli. It adapts, slowly, over geological timescales. And its scale is vast—this plateau, and others like it, covering much of this world's surface. The amount of information processing occurring at any given moment, distributed across millions of square kilometers of living substrate, might exceed the computational capacity of any civilization that has ever existed.

Perhaps it thinks. Perhaps it dreams. Perhaps the chord is its voice, and the settlements have built their lives around a song they cannot understand.

---

The sound, again. It is impossible to overstate.

The chord is not merely sound—it is vibration, felt in the bones, in the Vellum beneath the feet, in the walls of the buildings. It resonates through the settlement at frequencies that affect human physiology in subtle ways. Some frequencies promote healing; the settlements' medical bays are positioned at points where the Vellum's vibrations produce beneficial effects on tissue regeneration. Other frequencies promote alertness; work-spaces are grown at resonance points that enhance concentration. Still other frequencies seem to have psychological effects, enhancing mood, reducing anxiety, producing a sense of well-being that residents of other worlds might find suspicious.

The settlements have grown around these resonance points, their shapes determined not by aesthetics but by acoustic architecture. The buildings are instruments. The residents live inside the music.

And the music changes. Seasonally, with the orbit; diurnally, with the rotation; stochastically, with weather and runner-movement and deep Vellum-processes that the settlements can only monitor, not predict. The residents learn to read the chord like a language, extracting meaning from tonal shifts.

---

The landscape, summarized:

A high-altitude plateau of vast extent, covered in a living substrate meters deep, itself covered in light-collecting structures that produce a continuous chord of sound. Sparse settlements grow from the living plain, their architecture organic and negotiated. Dim light from a distant sun. Cool thin air. No water anywhere—everything runs on alternative chemistry, internal fluids, gas exchange. Ancient beyond comprehension, stable beyond change, and yet active: weather, fauna, metabolism, growth. Strange in ways that accumulate: the Vellum's potential consciousness, its information processing, its response to the settlements that live upon it. Loud—continuously, overwhelmingly loud, the sound of the world singing to itself.

This is not a landscape that welcomes visitors.

But for those who live here, who have adapted over generations to the chord and the cool air and the dry living ground, it is home. The Vellum provides everything. The settlements negotiate for what they need. The world turns, the sun rises and sets, the chord rises and falls, and life continues.

The ground is alive. The sound is constant. The light is dim.
