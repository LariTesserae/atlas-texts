# The Gathering Tanks

The plateau holds water the way a lung holds breath.

---

You come up through the mist layer and the sound hits you first—a sustained roar that vibrates in your chest cavity, that makes conversation useless and thought difficult. Not a single sound but a layered architecture of sound: the deep bass thrum of the primary pumps, the higher whine of turbines, the endless rush of water moving through channels, over spillways, through pipes. And beneath it all, a wet, organic gurgle—the landscape itself, breathing.

This is the Gathering Tanks. A highland plateau approximately twelve kilometers long and half as wide, sitting at altitude where the air is thin and the light has an edge to it. The sun here feels close enough to touch. No atmosphere to soften it. You can feel it on your skin like a weight.

The ground beneath your feet is alive.

Not alive in the metaphorical sense—alive in the literal, biological, cellular sense. The entire surface of the plateau is a continuous mat of vegetation: mosses primarily, but also liverworts, hornworts, algae, cyanobacteria, and countless other simple plants that have grown together into a single organic substrate. It's spongy to walk on. Each step compresses several centimeters before you find resistance. Water squeezes up around your boots. If you stand still long enough, the mat slowly rebounds, pushing back against your weight.

The vegetal mat is meters thick in places—a living peat that has accumulated over centuries, building itself up layer by layer. The older layers compress into something like soil, but the surface is always growing, always renewing. Roots thread through everything, holding it together, drawing water up, anchoring the system.

It's cool here, despite the brightness. The altitude sees to that. And the wetness—the constant, pervasive wetness—holds the cold. Water beads on every surface. The moss is perpetually slick. Droplets hang from the sparse taller plants that rise here and there: reed-like things, thick-stemmed flowers with waxy petals, occasional woody shrubs that have managed to find purchase in the saturated medium. Everything glistens. The light catches on water and scatters.

---

The plateau shouldn't exist. That's what the geologists say. The rock beneath is young—volcanic, mostly, still cooling in some places, barely weathered. A landscape that hasn't had time to form proper drainage, hasn't developed the valleys and channels that would carry water away. The water pools here instead. Saturates. And the plants—they moved in not long after the rock emerged. Colonized everything. Built their own substrate where none existed.

Erosion has barely begun. The vegetal mat holds the young geology in place, prevents the water from carving channels, slows every process to a geological crawl. This is a landscape under construction, still becoming itself. The raw stone is down there somewhere—basalt and rhyolite and other fresh volcanic products—but you rarely see it. The plants got here first.

The scale of the thing becomes apparent only after you've walked for an hour and the landscape hasn't changed. The same spongy ground, the same scattered pools, the same sparse tall vegetation rising like islands. The same infrastructure. Because the tanks are everywhere.

---

They call them tanks, but that's not quite right. Reservoirs? No—they're not natural. Catchments. That's closer. Man-made depressions in the living ground, lined with concrete and steel, where water collects before being pumped down to the cities below.

The infrastructure is industrial but scattered. You walk for twenty minutes between installations. Each one is self-contained: a concrete basin, pumps housed in corrugated metal sheds, pipes emerging from the ground and descending into it, heading somewhere you cannot see. The buildings are functional, unadorned, slowly being colonized by moss and lichen. Rust stains run down the metal walls. Water damage. The constant moisture works against everything built here.

At each installation, the sound intensifies. The pumps are massive—diesel engines converted to run on biogas harvested from the peat itself, churning day and night, pulling water from the catchments and pushing it through the pipe network. The pipes are buried somewhere in the vegetal mat, carrying water to the plateau's edge where it drops—kilometers down—to the thirsty cities in the rain shadow below.

The noise is deafening near the pumps. Literally. Workers wear protection. Even then, the vibration travels through your body. You feel it in your bones.

But the strangest thing is how quiet it gets between stations. The sound drops away. The roar becomes distant, then faint, then gone—replaced by smaller sounds: the squelch of your boots, the drip of water from moss, the faint rustle of wind through the sparse reeds. Bird calls, occasionally—though animals are rare here. The wet ground limits habitat. The noise drives away what might otherwise thrive.

In the quiet moments, you notice the light. How bright it is. How the thin atmosphere offers no filter. Colors saturate. The greens of the moss are vivid, almost glowing. The water catches sun and throws it back. If you look up, the sky is a deep, hard blue—you can see stars at midday if you shade your eyes.

---

There's a maintenance station near the plateau's center. Three buildings, a small staff barracks, a helipad, a communications array. This is where the workers live—rotational shifts, two weeks on, two weeks off. The job is monitoring, repair, upkeep. The pumps break constantly. The water finds every weakness. The vegetation grows into every crack and seam.

The workers are quiet, habituated to the roar, adapted to the routine. They walk the check routes daily. They log pressure readings. They clear blockages from intake screens. They paint over rust and replace worn seals and watch the landscape slowly, slowly digest their infrastructure.

The buildings are sinking. That's the thing nobody mentions in the official reports. The vegetal mat is growing up around them, and simultaneously compacting beneath them. Every year, the structures settle a few centimeters deeper. Doors that once opened easily now drag on the spongy ground. Windows that were level are slowly tilting. Given enough time—a century, maybe two—the station will be swallowed. The moss will close over it. The pumps will fail and be abandoned and become part of the landscape, just another obstacle for the roots to climb.

This doesn't seem to bother anyone. The time scale is too vast. There are budgets for next year, not next century.

---

At the plateau's edges, the land falls away. Not gradually—suddenly. Cliffs drop thousands of meters to the lowlands below. The water gathered here exits through pipes and channels, descending to where it's needed. But before it goes, it spills over several places—accidental waterfalls where the catchments overflow, where the pumps can't keep up, where the system bleeds at the seams.

These are the weeping cliffs. Water seeps from the vegetal mat along the entire escarpment, a continuous curtain of moisture that catches light and refracts it. On clear days, rainbows form in the spray. The sound of falling water adds to the roar—you can hear the edge from kilometers away, a constant bass note underlying everything.

The pipes are visible here, bolted to the cliff face, descending in long straight lines to the valleys far below. The infrastructure is brutal in its scale. Industrial. The work of a civilization that needs water badly enough to build something like this, to maintain it, to send people up here to live in the roar and the wet and the light.

---

The vegetation changes as you approach the cliffs. The same species, but stunted, wind-shaped, growing low and tight to the ground. The wind is constant up here—another sound, another layer. It dries the surface of the moss, creates a thin crust that cracks underfoot. Beneath, the ground is still saturated. Water still squeezes up. But the top layer is almost crisp in places, a skin over the wetness below.

The pools here are clearer. Less disturbed. You can see down into them—the vegetal mat extending underwater, continuing its slow growth, building the plateau outward molecule by molecule. In the shallows, the water is tea-colored, stained by tannins from the peat. In the depths, it darkens to black.

Something moves, occasionally, in the pools. Not fish—the water is too acidic, too low in oxygen. Amphibians, perhaps, or large insect larvae. The ecology is limited, specialized. A few species that can tolerate the conditions. They're rarely seen, barely studied. The workers report shapes in the water, ripples without cause. Probably nothing. Probably just gas bubbles from the decomposing peat, rising through the saturated layers and releasing at the surface.

Probably.

---

The plateau has a smell. You notice it when you first arrive, then stop noticing, then notice again when the wind shifts. Organic. Peaty. The smell of waterlogged vegetation, of slow decomposition, of living things growing and dying in continuous cycle. It's not unpleasant—earthy, rich, vaguely sulfuric. The smell of a wetland multiplied by altitude and scale.

At night, the temperature drops. The light goes away and the plateau becomes a different place. The roar continues—the pumps don't stop—but it seems louder in the darkness, the only constant in a world reduced to sound and touch. The moss is cold underfoot. Condensation forms on every surface. You can see your breath by starlight.

The stars are bright here. Brighter than anywhere else, the workers say. Thinner atmosphere, less light pollution, nothing between you and space but a thin skin of air. You can see the glow of the cities far below, dim through the haze layers, but up here it's just the stars and the black shape of the plateau and the constant roar of the water moving.

Some workers hate the night shift. Others request it. Something about the darkness, the isolation, the clarity of the sky. The sound becomes meditative, they say. A kind of presence. You're never alone up here. The landscape is always with you, always working, always producing its single endless note.

---

There's a pattern in the vegetation, if you look long enough. Not a designed pattern—nothing so obvious. But a structure, a logic. The pools cluster in certain areas. The taller plants grow in specific alignments, following contours in the underlying rock that you can't see. The moss changes color slightly across the plateau—greens and browns and ochres shifting in zones that correspond to nothing on the surface maps.

The water table is close here. Close everywhere. You can dig a hole with your hand and hit water within a meter. The whole plateau is saturated, floating on itself, a vegetal raft accumulating slowly upward while the weight of it compresses the layers below. The geology beneath is young, still settling, still releasing heat in places. Warm springs bubble up in scattered locations—another input of water, another factor in the system.

The pumps pull from the catchments, but the catchments are connected to the water table, which is connected to the springs, which are connected to rainfall and snowmelt and condensation from the perpetual mist. The water is always being replenished. The system is sustainable, in theory. The plateau produces more water than the infrastructure can remove.

Which raises a question: why does the water collect here? Why doesn't it drain naturally, find its way to the cliffs, carve the valleys that such landscapes usually develop? The geologists have answers—the young rock, the flat topography created by the volcanic formation, the vegetal mat smoothing every surface, preventing the initial erosion that would start the carving process. But standing on the plateau, feeling the water squeeze up around your boots, watching the ripples spread across the pools, it feels like something else. A decision. The landscape has chosen to hold water. Has chosen to grow rather than erode. Has chosen a different path.

---

In the far corner of the plateau, furthest from the main maintenance station, there's an abandoned installation. The pumps failed here decades ago—the workers say sabotage, or lightning, or simple neglect. The catchment has filled beyond capacity, overflowed, created a permanent pond that spreads across the vegetal surface. The building is half-drowned. The moss grows over the roof. The metal shed is a habitat now, home to the sparse creatures that survive here, a monument to infrastructure returning to the landscape that birthed it.

The sound is different here. Quieter, somehow, despite the water. The roar fades to a murmur. The birds are more visible—some species has nested in the abandoned structure, finding safety in its rusted frame. If you sit by the pond and wait, you might see them: small, dark shapes, quick and elusive, calling to each other in voices too thin to carry over the roar of the working pumps.

This is what the whole plateau might become, given enough time. A wetland, a bog, a continuous vegetal ocean with scattered pools and the constant slow accumulation of organic matter. The infrastructure would fail, be abandoned, be absorbed. The cities below would find other water sources, or not. The plateau would continue its patient process—growing, compressing, building its own substrate, making itself thicker and deeper and more alive.

But for now, the pumps run. The sound continues. The workers walk their rounds. The light beats down on the wet green surface. The water moves, slowly, through the pipes, over the cliffs, down to where it's needed. The landscape holds what it's given and gives back what's asked. It doesn't question. It doesn't resist. It simply grows, in the only direction available to it: upward, toward the light, one cell at a time.

---

You leave the way you came, down through the mist layer, the roar fading as you descend. By the time you reach the lower elevations, the sound is gone entirely, replaced by other sounds—the ordinary sounds of ordinary landscapes, streams and wind and distant traffic. The plateau becomes a memory, a story you might tell, a place that exists in a different register than the places you know.

But if you close your eyes and listen, you can sometimes hear it still—not the actual sound, but an echo of it, a trace. The roar of water moving. The pump-song. The living ground, breathing.
