# The Throat

There is a place where the earth swallows its own water.

---

You find it by accident, usually. By mistake. Following a stream that shouldn't exist — a thin creek that emerges from a fissure in a hillside, in cold air that smells of nothing, or that smells of wet stone and something older beneath. The hill is unremarkable. Grass, scrub, a few wind-twisted trees. The kind of slope you'd walk past a hundred times.

But the creek is loud. Already loud, and it's barely a creek. That should warn you. The sound of it — not the pleasant babble of a brook but something closer to a constant low shout. The water moves fast. It moves angry.

And if you follow it back — if you climb through the fissure, which requires you to crouch and then to crawl and then to lower yourself into darkness — you find where it's been going.

---

The chamber is not large. Perhaps thirty feet across at its widest, shaped roughly like the inside of a lung. Or a throat. The rock is limestone, pale gray and slick with moisture. It has been wet for so long that the wetness is structural now — the stone has softened in places, been carved by the water's own hand across millennia. Stalactites hang like wet fingers, and where they drip, the water doesn't fall so much as descend reluctantly, pulled down by weight.

But the sound.

The sound is a physical thing. It presses against your chest like a hand. The underground river — because that's what it is, suddenly, impossibly, funneled through a channel no wider than your arm — the river has nowhere to go but through, and the pressure of it, the *force*, creates a roar that fills the chamber like water fills a glass. There is no silence here. There has never been silence here. The sound has existed before you were born and will exist after you are gone. It is the sound of the mountain drinking.

You try to speak and your voice is erased. You clap your hands and hear nothing. The roar contains all frequencies, all pitches — a white noise that is also somehow low and rhythmic, pulsing with the water's pressure. After a few minutes you stop trying to hear anything else. Your ears ring uselessly. Your body vibrates with it.

---

The darkness is nearly complete. At the entrance — the fissure you crawled through — a thin gray light leaks in, and it shows you only the shape of the chamber's mouth, the wet shine of rock. Beyond that, nothing. The river below is black. The far wall is black. The ceiling is a suggestion, a pressure above your head that you feel more than see.

And yet things grow here.

In the thin ring of light near the entrance, where the air is coldest and the moisture condenses on every surface, there is moss. Thick moss, cushions of it, in greens so dark they read as black. It coats the stone in velvety patches, and where the stone has cracked, ferns grow — not large ones, but dense, their fronds curled tight like sleeping fists. They have adapted to the dark. They grow in the dark. They reach toward the sound as if it were light.

There are small flowers too, pale and nodding, that you almost don't see. They grow from cracks in the rock where the limestone has worn thin, where soil has accumulated over centuries. They don't need sun. They take what they need from the damp and the cold and the mineral-rich water that seeps through the stone above. Their stems are thin as wire. Their petals are almost translucent.

You kneel to look at them and the sound presses against your back.

---

The chamber has been known. Someone, at some point, was here before you.

The evidence is slight: a tool, hand-sized, made of carved wood and sharpened stone, lying on a natural shelf of rock near the entrance. A kind of chisel, perhaps, or a ceremonial object. The wood has warped from the moisture, swollen and split, but the stone blade is still sharp. There are marks on the walls near it — not pictures, not words, just lines. Tallies, maybe. Or something else. Grooves cut into the soft limestone, shallow and regular, that might count years, might count visitors, might mean nothing at all.

They used hand-tools. Whatever they were doing here, they did it slowly. Patiently. The marks show no haste. The tool shows use but not wear. Whoever left it here left it deliberately, or died before they could retrieve it, and the chamber kept it.

There is nothing else. No bones. No other artifacts. No sign that this was ever a dwelling or a tomb or a place of meeting. Just the one tool, the few marks, and the endless roar of the water.

---

You cannot stay long. The cold settles into you — a damp cold, the kind that doesn't bite so much as infiltrate. Your fingers go numb. Your lips go numb. The moisture is everywhere, beading on your skin, soaking into your clothes, and the sound makes it impossible to think. You find that you can't form memories properly in here. The roar overwrites your thoughts. You try to remember why you came, what you were doing before you saw the fissure in the hillside, and the sound won't let you. It demands everything.

You are alone. The chamber is almost empty — that's what strikes you most, after the cold and the dark and the sound. The emptiness. The space. A pocket of air trapped in stone, with nothing in it but moisture and moss and that one ancient tool, and the river forcing itself through the rock like blood through a vein. There should be more. There should be bats, at least, or spiders, or something that lives in the dark and drinks the damp. But there is nothing. The chamber is too loud for life. Even the moss only grows where it can cling in silence, near the entrance, where the sound is almost survivable.

---

When you leave — and you will leave, because you have to, because the cold is pain now and the sound is pain and the dark is pressing against your eyes — you find that you've lost track of time. Minutes? Hours? You can't tell. Your watch has fogged. Your phone is dead from the moisture. The climb out is harder than the climb in, your hands numb and stupid, your body slow to respond.

But you emerge.

And the sound changes. Still there — the roar, muffled now by tons of stone, by the hill itself — but it becomes a background thing. A distant complaint. The thin creek still emerges from the fissure, still hurries away down the hill. The sky above is gray. The air is cold but warmer than the chamber. Grass bends in a wind you couldn't feel before.

You didn't imagine it. Your clothes are soaked. Your bones ache. And there on the ground at your feet, something that wasn't there before: a single pale flower, stem crushed, that must have fallen from your boot when you climbed out. You don't remember stepping on it. You don't remember it being attached to you.

The chamber gave you something. Or took something. You won't know which for a long time.

---

You will walk away. You will not come back. Most people don't.

But you will hear it sometimes, when you're far from here, when you're lying in bed at night or standing in a quiet room. A low roar that you almost recognize. A pressure in your chest that has no source.

The Throat, you'll think. That's what you'll call it, later. You never named it at the time, but names rise up in retrospect. The Throat. The place where the mountain swallows its own water, and the sound is too loud for living things, and the moss grows in darkness, and the cold is patient, and the tool lies on its shelf, and the marks on the wall count something no one remembers anymore.

It is still there. It will always be there. The water goes on roaring, and the dark goes on being dark, and the ferns go on curling in their patient sleep, and none of it needs you to understand it.
