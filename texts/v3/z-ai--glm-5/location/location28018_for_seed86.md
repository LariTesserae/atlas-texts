# The Hollow

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before understanding does—a thick, living roar that rises from somewhere below the world and fills the air like something with weight. Not a roar of water or wind. A roar of throats.

You descend through a fissure in the limestone—a wound in the earth still sharp-edged, still raw, its walls carrying the clean fracture-lines of a cave system born only centuries ago, not the patient rounding of ancient stone. The rock breaks beneath your boots in thin sheets, makes sounds like dinner plates cracking. This place has not yet learned to be gentle with itself.

The light dims as you go deeper. What filters down from the surface—pale, secondhand, already tired—meets another light entirely. Patches of bioluminescent fungi cling to the wetter walls, pale blue-green smears like breath on glass. Sparse. Scattered. The cavern system is vast enough that these small glowing patches only emphasize how much lies beyond their reach. Darkness between them like the dark between stars.

---

The scale reveals itself slowly. This is not a cave. This is a hollow country.

Your torch catches a wall forty meters away, then finds no wall beyond it. The ceiling lifts and lifts and finally vanishes into blackness that your light cannot interrogate. The space extends laterally beyond your capacity to walk in a single day. A valley turned inside-out, buried beneath the weight of the land above. You feel the pressure of all that stone like a hand resting on your skull.

The air is cool—somewhere between the temperature of a spring night and a cellar. It carries moisture without being wet. Condensation beads on the walls in certain pockets, accumulates in the low places as dark shallow pools that reflect nothing because there is no light to reflect. You touch one. It is older than you. It has been sitting in that depression since the stone split open and will sit there long after.

The dampness concentrates in certain zones—a seep from above, a vein of water working slowly downward through the limestone, the sweat of the earth condensing on the cooler rock. Between these zones, the air dries. Your lips crack within hours. Then you pass through another damp sector and the air softens, becomes almost comfortable. The moisture is distributed like rainfall forgotten how to fall, pockets of almost-wet in a larger almost-dry.

---

Vegetation exists here in the same spirit as the light—sparse, determined, insufficient. Fungal mats spread where moisture gathers, pale threads and nodules that might be feeding on minerals or on each other. Stunted root systems dangle from the ceiling in places where the fissures above reach deep enough for adventurous plants to send down exploratory tendrils. These roots are white and brittle, ambitious in the wrong direction. They will find nothing here. They grow anyway.

In the dampest zones, there are true mosses—soft, dark green, forming small carpets that feel like fur beneath your palm. They cluster around the seeps and pools as if holding court. Elsewhere, nothing. The stone is naked and will remain so.

---

But the sound.

The sound is a continuous, layered, living thing. You understand this when you finally see them.

The colony spreads across the ceiling like a stain, like a city, like a single organism with a million bodies. Bats—but not quite bats. Larger. Their wingspans reach a meter or more. Their bodies are pale, nearly white, adapted to a world without sun. They hang in masses so dense that the ceiling itself disappears beneath them. Claws gripping stone, wings folded, bodies pressed together for warmth in the cool air. Thousands. Tens of thousands.

The sound is the accumulation of their small movements. The shifting of wings against wings. The squeaking of territorial disputes. The high, thin calls they make to one another in frequencies your ears can almost catalog. The clicking of throats and the rustle of bodies repositioning. A single bat is silent. Ten thousand bats are a weather system.

They do not fly. They are not flying. They are waiting, and have been waiting, and will continue to wait.

---

You walk for hours through the hollow, and the colony extends above you the entire way. The density of animals shifts—thick masses in some regions, sparse scatterings in others—but they are always present. The sound of them becomes a texture, a constant pressure against your eardrums. You find yourself shouting to hear your own voice, then stopping because shouting feels like an intrusion.

These creatures have no need for your noise. They have their own.

You find evidence of their lives scattered across the cavern floor. Guano deposits in mounds that reach your waist, ancient and fresh intermingled. The smell is ammoniac, sharp, but not overwhelming—something about the scale of the space dilutes it. Bones of insects and unidentified prey. A few scattered skeletons of their own kind, bodies that fell or died and were simply left where they lay.

Nothing cleans up here. Nothing changes.

---

You notice, after some time, that the colony is not sleeping.

They are waiting, yes. Resting, perhaps. But their eyes—pale, nearly blind, but present—track your movement. When you stop, the nearest bodies shift. When you step closer, they press backward. They are aware of you. They are always aware. The entire colony is a single attentive thing.

You do not know what they are waiting for. You suspect they do not either.

---

In one sector of the hollow, the ceiling lowers. The bats thicken. The sound intensifies until it becomes a physical sensation, a vibration in your chest. You stop at the edge of this region. The bodies overhead are so dense that the stone between them has vanished entirely. A ceiling made of living things.

One of them drops.

It falls two meters, catches itself on an updraft you cannot feel, and circles once before returning to the mass. Others shift to accommodate its return. The colony ripples, adjusts, and settles. The sound dips for a moment, then resumes its continuous roar.

Nothing has changed. The colony was here before you arrived. It will be here after you leave.

---

You find a spot where the wall is damp and the moss is thick and the colony overhead is thinner, and you sit with your back against the stone. The cool air seeps through your clothing. The sound continues. After a long while, it stops being noise. It becomes weather. It becomes background. You notice that you have stopped listening to it.

You understand, sitting there, that this place does not care about you. The bats have no opinion on your presence. The stone has no opinion. The pools and the moss and the pale roots have no opinion. You are a feature passing through. A temporary irregularity in a system that has achieved equilibrium and will remain so for longer than you can imagine.

The colony overhead shifts. A thousand bodies reposition. The sound flickers and steadies.

You rest. The hollow continues.
