The light arrives before anything else.

Not because it is dramatic — though at this altitude it has a quality of exposure, a thinness, that makes everything it touches feel slightly more real than it ought to. It arrives because it is everywhere at once, scattered and bounced and re-bounced off so many wet surfaces that standing at the lip of the plateau, you seem to be standing *inside* the light rather than beneath it. The whole of the high bench glows. Not with warmth — the air is cool, perhaps eleven degrees, perhaps less, with the particular coolness of altitude and evaporating water — but with luminance, as if the stone itself were generating brightness. It is not. It is only wet. And the wet surfaces catch the high sun and throw it in every direction simultaneously, so that shadows exist but never fully consolidate. There are no dark places here. Only bright, and brighter.

Then the water. It does not arrive — it is already here. It saturates this place the way dye saturates cloth: through every fiber, in every layer, at every scale. You see it first on the surfaces — a thin, bright film coating every wall and basin and channel, so that the stone looks not wet in the way a puddle is wet but wet in the way a living thing is wet, from the inside out. Then you feel it in the air, which is heavy, close, dense with moisture, so that within minutes of arriving your skin is beaded with condensation, your clothes dampen, and you understand that you are not visiting a wet place — you are inside a system of water that includes you. Then you hear it, or almost hear it — because the sound is so quiet it sits at the threshold of perception, more felt than heard. A drip somewhere to the left, striking a basin with a sound like a fingertip tapping glass. A soft, continuous whisper, almost subliminal, of a film of water moving down a channel barely faster than breathing. A low hum, felt in the chest more than heard with the ears, where water fills a hollow inside a wall. These sounds do not accumulate into noise. They remain separate, layered, each one distinct, each one patient.

And then the construction, which is where the place begins to resist understanding.

Every surface has been made. Not modified — made. The plateau's original bedrock, whatever it once was, has been entirely reshaped. There is no natural ground anywhere — no exposed stratum, no unworked face, no surface that has not been touched by a tool. Every horizontal, every vertical, every angle and curve and channel and basin has been cut, carved, chiseled, ground, textured by hand. The tool marks are visible everywhere: individual chisel strikes, some as crisp as if they were made yesterday; the sweeping arcs of grinding stones; the flat, square impressions of a hammer face. The tools were simple. Stone on stone. Perhaps bone or antler for finer work. No metal — no file marks, no saw cuts, no drill holes. The entire landscape was made with hand tools, and the scale of the labor sits in the mind like a stone in a channel: it does not move easily.

The plateau extends perhaps a kilometer in each direction, and every square meter of its surface is constructed. This is the first shock — the completeness. But the second is the density. There is no open space. No courtyard, no path, no bare ledge. Every inch is occupied by some feature: a channel, a basin, a wall, a stair, a terrace, a carved runnel, a ridge, a lip, a groove. The features are packed so tightly that they seem to press against each other, jostling for room, each one flowing into the next without gap or margin. Moving through this landscape would require picking a careful path, stepping from carved ledge to carved ledge, turning sideways to pass between walls that are close enough to touch with both hands. It is not designed for passage. It is designed — and this is the word that sits at the edge of your thinking, not quite fitting — for flow.

The water enters from somewhere you cannot see. Perhaps it condenses from the air itself, collecting in the highest channels — narrow, finger-width runnels cut along the tops of the walls — and from there it flows downward through the network. And the network is vast. Channels converge and diverge, splitting into smaller channels, merging into larger ones, feeding basins that overflow into further channels that feed further basins, in a cascading sequence that covers the entire plateau. The water moves slowly. Very slowly. In some channels it is barely a creeping film that takes minutes to travel the length of your hand. In others it flows with a quiet, deliberate patience, filling a basin over the course of an hour, overflowing, finding the next channel, continuing. The terraces step downward in tight, packed levels, each one carved with its own channels and basins, each one wet, each one bright, and the water moves through them all, down and down, in a sequence so long and so complex that you cannot hold the whole pattern in your mind at once.

And the patterns — seen from the high ground at the plateau's edge, where the last of the natural rock gives way to the constructed surface, the waterworks trace designs that are not purely functional. There are curves that mirror each other across a central axis. There are basins arranged in spirals that tighten as they descend. There are channels that converge at precise angles and then diverge again, forming geometries that feel deliberate, considered, patient — but not useful. The water is not being filtered.
