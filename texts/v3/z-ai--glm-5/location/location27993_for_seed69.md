# The Black Plateau of Ymurra

There is no dawn here. There has never been a dawn. The concept arrives only through the collective memory of the acoustic fauna—a myth passed down in the vibratory languages of the deepwet, a story about a phenomenon called "seeing" that the ancestor species supposedly could do.

## The Atmosphere of Darkness

The darkness at Ymurra is not merely an absence. It has texture. It presses against the skin like a warm, wet cloth, saturated with water vapor that condenses on any surface cooler than the air—which is almost nothing, in this geothermally heated altitude. The condensation comes instead on living things, on the surfaces of the great vascular plants that tower in the black, their leaves like hands cupped to catch the moisture that the sky refuses to release as proper rain.

Instead, the water weeps. It emerges from the rock faces in countless places, running in sheets down the ancient stone, collecting in channels carved over millions of years. The plateau sits at an elevation where the atmosphere thickens below and thins above, creating a perpetual cloud sea that laps at Ymurra's lower edges but never rises to envelop the top. The plateau is an island of darkness above a floor of gray, and above that—nothing. No stars. No sun. The sky is a black void, an absence so complete it feels like a ceiling.

The warmth rises from below. Ymurra is heated from its bones, from hydrothermal networks that pulse with slow, rhythmic certainty, an immense heart beating beneath the stone.

## The Vegetation of the Black

The plants here have never known light. They are pale things, adapted to a world where energy comes from chemical exchange, from the mineral-rich waters that bleed from the rock, from the geothermal warmth that seeps upward through the soil. The lushness is impossible to see but immediate to touch—every surface is covered, layered, dense with growth that competes for space in a vertical domain without visual reference points.

The largest of them are the *sang-tubes*, vascular giants that rise up to forty meters in spreading clusters, their surfaces slick with condensation and bacterial films. They are structured like bundled reeds, each stalk as thick as a human torso, and they creak constantly as their internal fluids are pumped upward and distributed through the canopy above. The pumping creates a sound like breathing—deep, slow exhalations that join the ambient roar.

Smaller plants cling to the sang-tubes—epiphytic climbers with tendrils that taste the air for chemical signatures, spiral-fronded air-feeders that catch insects in their sticky curls, fungus-analogs that glow with the faintest bioluminescence, pale blue-green, barely enough to matter, barely enough to see. The glow is waste, really—a byproduct of their chemistry, not a signal. Nothing here signals with light.

Between the sang-tubes, the ground is a thick mat of low-growing moisture-trappers, broad-leaved plants that overlap like scales, creating a surface that squelches and releases water when walked upon. The water table is so close to the surface that in the lower channels, it rises openly, forming slow-moving streams that wind between the vegetation thickets before plunging over the plateau's edge into the cloud sea below.

## The Sound World

The sound is tremendous. Ymurra speaks in a language of vibration, and the conversation never ceases.

First, the water. The weeping of the stone faces creates a constant undertone—a rushing, gurgling bass note that varies in pitch depending on the mineral content of each spring. Some springs are narrow, high-pressure jets that hiss like steam vents. Others are wide, slow weepages that spread across stone in thin sheets, creating a flutelike whistling as the water finds channels and edges. The waterfalls that plunge over the plateau's edge create deep booms that rise from below, echoing off the cloud sea's dense surface, returning as distorted reflections that arrive seconds later, out of phase with their sources.

Second, the industry. The plateau's industrial systems are built into its geology, ancient and weathered, settled into the landscape so thoroughly that they have become part of the erosion cycle themselves. The great pumps and condensers that harvest water from the air run on thermal differentials, pulling energy from the gradient between the hot stone and the cooler air. They are massive, droning things—some still operating after centuries of continuous function, others fallen into states of partial activity that create arrhythmic clanking and grinding, mechanical wheezing that joins the water-chorus. The sounds travel through the stone itself, transmitted as vibrations that the listening creatures can interpret from kilometers away.

Third, the life. The fauna of Ymurra are acoustic specialists. The black has made them blind or nearly so, but their hearing has evolved into something extraordinary. The air is filled with clicks, chirps, resonant calls, and infrasonic pulses that travel through the ground. Some species communicate through the water itself, sending vibrations through the streams and weeping channels that other creatures receive through specialized organs in their feet or through the water that saturates their skin. The calls are territorial, sexual, competitive, cooperative—the acoustic ecology is as dense and layered as the vegetation, with niche frequencies and rhythmic patterns that different species occupy, a spectrum of sound as complex as any rainforest's visual architecture.

## The Creatures of the Wet Black

The fauna are abundant and strange, evolved for a world where light is not merely absent but foreign, a harmful radiation that no organism has needed to defend against for millions of years. Their skins are thin and permeable, designed to absorb water and dissolved minerals directly, making them constantly wet, constantly exchanging chemistry with their environment.

The *riphas* are the largest herbivores—lumbering, six-legged animals with wide, splayed feet that distribute their weight across the soggy ground. They move through the sang-tube forests in small herds, calling to one another with low-frequency rumbles that the plateau's geography amplifies and carries. Their bodies are covered in sensory filaments that read the air's chemical composition, tasting for the signature of ripe fruiting bodies, for the presence of predators, for the territorial markers of other riph herds. They are warm to the touch, their metabolism high, their breathing loud and regular, adding to the soundscape's respiratory quality.

The *striiders* are smaller, quicker—bipedal predators that hunt by sound triangulation, their auditory organs clustered in depressions along their sleek skulls like acoustic dishes. They can locate prey in absolute darkness by the slightest sound, and they communicate with each other through rapid clicks that the riph herds have learned to recognize and flee. The striiders do not hunt in packs, but they tolerate each other's presence in loose territorial networks, sharing information about prey movements through their click-languages, a cooperative system that falls short of true social organization but allows a degree of coordinated effort.

The canopy holds other things—*glider-weavers* with membranes stretched between elongated limbs, capable of launching from sang-tube to sang-tube, their passage announced only by the displacement of air and the faint rustle of leaves. They build nests in the upper stalks, weaving platforms from plant fibers, and they sing in frequencies that carry across kilometers, defining the boundaries of their territories, advertising for mates, warning of striider incursions into the upper zones.

And in the water—the *vithra*, amphibious creatures that live in the streams and weep-channels, emerging to lay their eggs in the moist soil beneath the ground-cover plants. They are the acoustic specialists of all specialists, their bodies essentially one large ear, capable of detecting vibrations in water, air, and stone simultaneously. The vithra are important to the industrial systems of Ymurra—they are drawn to the thermal pumps, gathering around them in dense schools, and their presence in the water helps to filter particulates and maintain the flow channels. They are tolerated, even cultivated, by the systems that tend the ancient machines.

## The Industrial Layer

The industrial presence is the strangest feature of Ymurra. The machines were not built by the creatures that now live here—they predate them, perhaps by millions of years. The erosion parameter suggests a landscape that has reached equilibrium, that has settled into its own state, and the machines have settled with it, becoming part of the geology.

They are extraction systems, primarily. The plateau's elevation and the condensing atmosphere create a natural water-harvesting opportunity—warm, moist air rises, meets the cooler stone, and sheds its moisture. The ancient engineers designed systems to maximize this process, building vast condensation surfaces, channel networks, and storage reservoirs. The water is drawn from the air and distributed through the plateau, feeding the vegetation, supporting the ecology—whether this was always the purpose, or whether the ecological abundance is a secondary effect of the infrastructure's original function, is impossible to determine.

The machines are maintained by other machines. Automated repair systems crawl along the condensation surfaces, clearing debris, sealing cracks, adjusting flow rates. They are powered by the thermal gradient, by the heat that rises from below, and they operate in darkness without difficulty, designed for environments where vision is irrelevant. They are not intelligent, not in any sense that would imply consciousness, but they are adaptive, capable of responding to changing conditions, of rerouting flows, of prioritizing certain systems over others based on parameters that may or may not still make sense.

The result is a landscape that feels inhabited by invisible presences—the maintenance machines are rarely seen, but their work is evident in the functioning infrastructure, in the channels that remain clear, in the pumps that continue to drone. The creatures of Ymurra have incorporated the machines into their understanding of the world. The riph herds drink from the collection reservoirs. The striiders avoid the active pump-stations, where the noise interferes with their hunting. The glider-weavers nest in the stable surfaces of the condensers. The vithra school in the processed water channels, finding the mineral content favorable.

The machines and the life have reached a kind of equilibrium—an industrial ecosystem, accidental but functional, in which the infrastructure provides services that the biology has evolved to exploit, and the biology, in turn, performs maintenance functions that the machines were never designed to implement. It is a partnership without agreement, a cooperation without intention.

## The Scattered Built Environment

The humans—or whoever built the systems—left little evidence of their presence beyond the infrastructure itself. There are occasional structures, small buildings at the intersection points of major channels, where the water is processed and distributed. These are weathered almost beyond recognition, their original purposes unclear, their interiors filled with the accumulated sediment of ages or colonized by fungi and moisture-loving plants.

One such structure, near the plateau's central rise, still functions as a control node. Its original operators are gone, but the systems respond to certain inputs—mechanical levers, pressure plates, acoustic signals that may or may not be passwords. The local fauna avoid it, not out of fear but out of indifference; the sounds it makes are unpleasant to them, the electromagnetic fields it generates disruptive to their sensory organs.

The structure is a rare point of visual interest in the darkness. Its exterior is coated with mineral deposits, calcified over centuries, forming irregular nodes and shelves that the condensation runs across. Inside, there are faint lights—indicator panels that still function, powered by the thermal systems, their glow a pale blue that barely penetrates the surrounding black. The light attracts insects, which attract insect-eaters, which attract predators, creating a micro-ecology that exists only in the immediate vicinity of the functioning tech.

## The Weather of the Black Plateau

The scale of Ymurra is vast enough to generate its own weather patterns, though they are unlike the weather of illuminated worlds. The thermal activity creates updrafts, columns of warm air that rise from the hot zones and spread across the plateau's surface. The moisture load is so high that these updrafts become saturated, the water vapor condensing into fog banks that roll across the sang-tube forests, dense and white—if there were light to see them by.

In the acoustic world of Ymurra's inhabitants, the fog banks are known by their dampening effect. Sound carries differently in the fog, its frequencies attenuated, its sources harder to locate. The striiders avoid hunting during fog conditions, their prey too difficult to find. The riph herds move more slowly, relying on chemical trails and the guidance of the vithra in the streams, whose aquatic hearing is less affected by the atmospheric changes.

The fog also brings changes in the weeping of the rocks. The thermal vents respond to atmospheric pressure, their flow rates increasing or decreasing as the weather shifts. Some of the deeper vents become active during low-pressure periods, releasing water that has been held in underground reservoirs, carrying minerals that change the taste of the streams, triggering spawning behaviors in the vithra and other aquatic species.

## The Sound of the Night

The term "night" has no meaning here, but the concept of "quiet" does, and Ymurra is never quiet. The sound level is high, a constant presence that the inhabitants have evolved to filter, to interpret, to use. For an outsider, the noise would be overwhelming—a wall of water-sound, machine-droning, animal-calling, stone-creaking, wind-rushing, a sensory overload that makes thought difficult, that demands attention simply to parse.

But the inhabitants do not experience it as noise. They experience it as information. The sound carries the shape of the landscape, the location of resources and threats, the movement of the air and the water, the activity of the machines, the presence of other creatures. It is a language that they read continuously, a map that is constantly redrawn in vibration and frequency.

And within the sound, there are patterns. The machines have rhythms that repeat, cycles that the biology has incorporated into its own timing. The riph herds move in response to pump cycles, migrating between feeding grounds as the water pressure rises and falls. The striiders hunt in the gaps between machine-clatter, learning the arrhythmic machines' unpredictable patterns, incorporating them into their ambush strategies. The glider-weavers sing in harmonic relationship to the drones, their calls finding frequencies that carry clearly through the mechanical backdrop.

## The World Below the Cloud Sea

The plateau is an elevated world, but it is not isolated from what lies beneath. The cloud sea, the perpetual fog layer that surrounds Ymurra's lower slopes, is not impenetrable—creatures move between levels, following resources, migrating with seasonal patterns that are marked by temperature shifts rather than light changes.

The sound carries through the cloud sea. Calls rise from below, announcing the presence of lowland species that occasionally climb to the plateau's edge. The riph herds know to avoid the perimeter when certain calls are heard—the predators of the cloud sea are different from the striiders, larger and less specialized, operating on different frequencies, hunting by different rules.

The water that falls from Ymurra's edge descends into the cloud sea and continues down, feeding the hydrological systems of the lower elevations. The plateau is a water source for the entire region, a high-altitude condensation engine that draws moisture from the atmosphere and releases it in controlled fashion, a function that the ancient machines were designed to maximize. The ecology below depends on the water that Ymurra provides, on the mineral content it carries, on the timing of its release.

## The Feeling of the Place

To stand on Ymurra is to stand in darkness so complete that the word "stand" loses meaning. There is no visual reference for orientation, no horizon, no sense of the ground extending beyond the immediate tactile radius. The warmth is constant, humid, wrapping around the body like wet cloth. The sound is a roar, a chorus, a symphony of mechanical and biological and geological processes, all layered into a single continuous presence that cannot be separated into components without concentrated effort.

The vegetation is everywhere, dense and slick, touching and overlapping, competing for space in dimensions that cannot be seen, only felt and heard. The water emerges from stone, runs in sheets and channels, pools in reservoirs both natural and artificial, drops into the void below. The machines drone and clank and wheeze, maintaining their ancient function, serving purposes that may or may not still be relevant. The creatures move through the black, calling to each other, hunting, fleeing, breeding, dying, their bodies returning to the wet soil to feed the plants that will feed their offspring.

It is a world without sight, but not without perception. It is a world without sun, but not without warmth. It is a world without obvious intention, but not without meaning—the meaning is everywhere, encoded in sound and chemical signature, in thermal gradient and water flow, in the patterns that emerge from the interaction of infrastructure and biology, of machine and creature, of the endless wet dark and the life that has learned to thrive within it.

The plateau is vast, and the darkness is deeper than distance. The sound never ceases. The water never stops. The life goes on, adapted to conditions that would be fatal to any creature evolved for light, and in the black, in the roar, in the warm wet air, Ymurra continues its ancient function—collecting water, distributing life, maintaining equilibrium in a system that no one designed but that functions nonetheless, a machine-ecology that has outlasted its creators and will likely outlast anything that remembers them.
