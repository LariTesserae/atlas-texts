# The Drowned Choir of Hálthörn

The water holds you before you know you've entered it.

There is no surface here—or rather, the surface is everything. A high-altitude lake, mountain-fed and ancient, fills what must once have been a valley but has become something else entirely. The water is cool but not cold, moving continuously without ever seeming to flow, and it carries light downward in columns that shift and reform like architecture designed by someone who understood illumination as prayer.

This is a place of drowning that drowns nothing. The water sustains. Breath moves through it as easily as through air—there is a wrongness to this, a violation of what bodies should do, and yet the body does it. The water wants you here. It has been waiting.

---

The vegetation begins thirty feet down, and it is not vegetation as forests understand the term.

Massive stalks rise from the lakebed—hollow, jointed, each one as thick around as an ancient oak. They are pale green bleeding to white at the internodes, and they grow upward not in the chaotic reaching of land plants but in deliberate, measured intervals, as if someone placed each segment with intention. The stalks do not end in leaves. They end in platforms—broad, flat surfaces of living tissue, smooth and slightly convex, radiating outward from the central stalk like lily pads designed for giants who never arrived.

Some platforms are small, barely large enough for a person to stand upon. Others spread twenty feet across, their edges overlapping with neighbors, creating irregular floors at various depths. The overall effect is architectural: a city grown rather than built, with levels that descend in unpredictable patterns, connected by the stalks themselves which serve as pillars, as stairways, as the bones of something that has no clear name.

These are the Elder Stalks. They are the substrate. Everything else exists because they exist.

The platforms are not passive surfaces. They breathe. Along their undersides, pale root-structures trail downward into the depths—fine as hair, dense as curtains, filtering the water for nutrients that travel up through the stalks and into the canopy-platform above. The roots are constantly moving in slow undulations that respond to currents and pressure and perhaps to something else, some signal that passes through the water from places unknown.

---

Between the platforms, the water is open space—sparse density made manifest. This is not a crowded place. Each Elder Stalk stands apart from its neighbors, with twenty or thirty feet of clear water between platforms. You can see across the entire settlement from any point within it, can count the stalks rising around you like pillars in an infinite cathedral that has no roof and no floor you can reach.

The scale is intimate. Perhaps forty stalks in total, arranged in rough concentric rings around a central emptiness—a plaza of pure water where nothing grows, where the depths fall away into darkness. The outermost stalks define a boundary that feels meaningful, as if crossing beyond them would mean leaving something essential behind. The entire settlement would fit within a modest hamlet, and yet it feels larger, stretched by the way light behaves underwater, by the way sound travels.

The architecture of emptiness matters here. The spaces between are not absence but presence—a different kind of presence, water as building material, as room and corridor and gathering place. The plant-city breathes through these gaps, currents moving through them like breath through lungs.

---

The built elements emerge gradually as you descend.

They are primitive in the truest sense—first tools, earliest intentions. Bundles of reed tied with strips of the Elder Stalks' own fiber, forming baskets and containers that rest upon the platforms. Sharpened stones wedged into cracks in the stalks, creating handholds for climbing. Nets woven from the trailing root-fibers, suspended between platforms, holding objects or perhaps fish or perhaps nothing at all, kept as potential.

Someone lived here. Someone may still.

The constructions follow the vegetation rather than imposing upon it. There are no cuts in the Elder Stalks, no harvested sections, no scars of removal. Every tool and structure works with the living architecture—hooks that loop around stalks without piercing, platforms built atop platforms by laying additional material on the existing surfaces, walkways made by stretching fiber-ropes between platforms and allowing aquatic moss to grow over them until they become solid paths.

The techniques are sophisticated in their primitiveness. Whoever built here understood that cutting is violence, that the plants will cooperate if asked correctly. The tools are asking-shapes. They request rather than demand.

---

Sound defines this place more than sight.

The water conducts and amplifies. Every movement creates sound, and the sounds do not dissipate—they reverberate between the Elder Stalks, bounce off platforms, echo down into the depths and back upward again. The result is a constant murmur, a tonal wash that rises and falls in cycles that feel deliberate, musical.

The plants themselves sing. Not continuously, but in sequences—a low thrumming that emerges from the stalks when light strikes them at certain angles, a higher piping from the root-curtains as they filter water. The sounds harmonize. They must be accidental, mechanical, the result of fluid dynamics and structural resonance, and yet they arrange themselves into something that resembles composition.

When something moves through the water—a fish, a falling particle of debris, your own body—the song incorporates the disturbance. Ripples in the sound-field, brief additions that the Elder Stalks absorb and transform. The settlement is an instrument that plays itself and everything within it.

The loudness is startling at first, overwhelming in the way of standing beneath a waterfall, but it becomes texture. Background. The water is never silent, and silence would feel wrong here, would suggest something broken or dying. Sound means life. Sound means the plants are breathing.

---

Life is abundant, and it moves through the water like weather through air.

Schools of silver fish navigate between the platforms, their movements synchronized in ways that suggest they are not merely swimming but dancing, responding to sounds and currents and perhaps to the Elder Stalks themselves. The fish are long-bodied and translucent, their internal organs visible as dark shadows, their scales catching the filtered light and scattering it into brief rainbows that fade immediately.

The fish do not eat the Elder Stalks. They eat the algae that grows upon the platforms' upper surfaces, keeping the living floors clean. In return, the stalks provide shelter—the fish sleep in the root-curtains' protection, their bodies tangled in the pale fibers, moving together in slow breathing rhythms.

Larger shapes move in the depths beyond the light's reach. They do not approach the platforms, but their presence registers as pressure changes, as subtle disruptions in the sound-field. Predators, perhaps, or custodians of a different sort. The ecosystem extends downward beyond easy comprehension, layers of life that interact with the settlement without entering it.

Amphibious creatures climb the Elder Stalks during the dark cycles—they have four limbs and soft, permeable skin, and their eyes are large and golden. They harvest something from the stalks' joints, rubbing them with their hands and collecting the residue that emerges, eating it on the spot or storing it in throat-pouches. They do not seem to communicate through sound. Their movements are choreographed, exact, suggesting a language of gesture.

---

The light here is normal in intensity but strange in quality.

The water filters it. Colors shift as you descend—red disappears first, then orange, leaving a palette of greens and blues that deepens toward violet at the lowest visible depths. The Elder Stalks absorb and reflect this limited spectrum, appearing more blue than green in certain angles, more white in others. The platforms glow faintly from within, bioluminescence that triggers when the ambient light dims, providing illumination that seems designed rather than incidental.

This is not the crushing darkness of deep ocean. This is mountain water, and light reaches everything eventually. But the light moves in columns and sheets, creating zones of brightness and zones of shadow that shift with the water's slow currents and with something else—a positioning that feels intentional, as if the light is being directed by surfaces you cannot see.

During brightest periods, the entire settlement is visible in a single glance, every platform and stalk and trailing root-curtain arranged in its deliberate pattern. During dimmer periods, the structures emerge from darkness as you approach them, materializing suddenly, their details crisp and immediate.

---

Time moves slowly here, and the dynamics are patient.

The Elder Stalks grow at rates measurable in inches per decade. Their platforms spread outward over seasons. The root-curtains lengthen almost imperceptibly, their undulations completing cycles that take days to finish. Everything in the settlement operates on scales that make human lifespans feel frantic, breathless, insufficient.

The water's movement is similarly slow—not currents but drifts, not waves but oscillations that complete themselves over hours. The weather of this place, if weather is the right word, changes across durations that feel geological. Light increases and decreases in cycles, but the transitions between states take so long that the present moment is always in transition without ever seeming to change.

This slowness is not stagnation. It is a different speed of being. The settlement has existed in this state for longer than the stones of surface cities have held their shapes. The erosion parameter is balanced—the Elder Stalks are weathered, settled into themselves, but not decaying. They have found an equilibrium that includes gradual change as a component of stability rather than its opposite.

You can feel this in your own movements. Hurry is impossible. The water resists rapid motion, and the settlement's design punishes impatience—platforms are too far apart for quick crossing, the stalks require measured climbing, the currents will carry you only if you surrender to their timing rather than fighting it.

---

The coolness is constant, comfortable, unchanging.

The water holds temperature the way it holds sound—uniformly, with minor variations that register as texture rather than difference. The mountain water enters from somewhere above, filtered through rock and root, arriving in the settlement already cooled to its stable state. Your body adjusts quickly, and the temperature ceases to register as sensation. It becomes environment, assumption, a quality of existence rather than a condition to notice.

This is not the shock of cold water. This is the embrace of water that has found its temperature and intends to keep it.

---

Something is unusual here, and the wrongness is difficult to name.

The settlement should not exist. Mountain lakes do not support vegetation of this scale, do not grow architectural structures, do not sustain ecosystems that feel designed rather than evolved. The Elder Stalks resemble no known plant family. Their geometry is too deliberate, their arrangement too intentional. They might be engineered except that engineering implies engineers, and no one engineered anything with pre-metal technology.

The water's properties are wrong. The way it sustains breath, the way it conducts sound, the way it interacts with light—these suggest a composition different from normal H₂O, with properties that border on the therapeutic, the psychoactive, the sacred.

The settlement feels occupied even when you see no one. The tools are maintained. The platforms show no decay. The root-curtains have been trimmed recently, the cut edges still fresh. Someone tends this place. Someone tends it slowly, across durations that do not require daily presence.

Perhaps the wrongness is simply that this place exists outside the categories that surface-dwellers use to organize experience. It is not quite city and not quite forest, not quite alive and not quite built, not quite underwater and not quite land. It occupies a category of its own creation, and it asks you to occupy it too, to become a different kind of body in a different kind of world.

---

You could stay here.

The water would let you. The Elder Stalks would grow platforms beneath your feet. The silver fish would eat the algae that accumulated on your skin. The sound-field would absorb your voice, incorporate it into the endless song, and you would become a note in a composition that has been playing for centuries and will play for centuries more.

This is what the settlement offers: not survival but belonging. Not shelter but transformation. Not home but a different category entirely, one you do not have vocabulary for until you have been here long enough to need it.

The drowned choir continues its singing. The platforms breathe their slow breaths. The root-curtains drift and filter and feed. And you float in the center of it all, held by water that wants you here, surrounded by architecture that grew from intention, part of an ecosystem that includes you now whether you choose to stay or not.

The choice was made before you entered the water.
