# The Pocket Inside the Root

You find it only if you have reason to look. A cleft in the submerged rootmass of something ancient and still-living, a tree or tree-analogue whose trunk is four arm-widths across and whose bark runs in braided ridges like the muscles of a forearm. The cleft is not at eye level. It is near the silt, where the root flares outward before plunging into substrate, and it is narrow enough that you must turn sideways to pass, and in passing you feel the root's interior graze your shoulder with a texture like wet felt.

Then inside.

---

The chamber is not large. You could kneel in it and touch both walls if you stretched. The ceiling is a hands-breadth above your head when you settle to the floor, which is silt compressed to something almost like clay — cool, smooth, giving slightly under your palm. The total volume of this place is perhaps the interior of a wardrobe, perhaps less. A pocket. An interior space that has no business existing inside a root.

But it does.

The water here is still. Not the stillness of a calm surface — there is no surface. The entire pocket is submerged, and has always been submerged, and the water does not circulate in any direction you can detect. It sits in itself the way a glass of water sits on a table, contained, untroubled, holding its temperature with a patience that feels less like physics and more like a decision. The cold is steady. Not biting — somewhere between the cool of a cellar and the cool of a stream in early autumn. It enters your skin slowly, evenly, until you cannot distinguish your body's warmth from the water's coolness, and that sameness is its own kind of warmth, or at least its own kind of comfort.

You expected darkness and you have it, but the darkness here is not the absence of light. It is the presence of something that was here before light was invented. It has weight. It rests against your open eyes like a soft fabric. You blink and the darkness blinks back, or seems to, or you lose track of whether you blinked at all.

---

And yet things grow here.

The vegetation is the first contradiction that becomes a fact. Along the walls, which are root-wood grainy and fibrous and wet, there is a moss that has no right to exist at this depth, in this absence of sun. It is not green. It is a color you would call black if you were being approximate, but in the moment of perceiving it your mind offers another word: *saturated*. The moss is so dense with pigment, so thick with whatever it uses instead of chlorophyll, that the color seems to pool in it the way water pools in a cup. Run your finger across it and the color transfers — not as stain, not as dye, but as a sensation. Your fingertip holds the darkness for several seconds after, a fading smudge of non-visibility, and you feel it tingle like touching a battery to your tongue, but gentler, slower.

The moss is not alone. Fern-fronds spiral out from the root-wall in clusters of three or four, each frond the length of your hand, each one so finely divided that the individual leaflets are nearly transparent — not clear, but thin enough that the darkness passes through them and they appear as outlines, as sketches of ferns drawn on tissue paper. They move without current. A slow unfurling, a slow re-furling. You watch one for what might be thirty seconds or three minutes and see it complete a single gesture, opening like a hand that is reaching for something it has already decided not to touch.

There are other growths. A fungus coats the ceiling in a crust that resembles frost, white-blue and crystalline, each crystal a slender needle that points straight down toward the floor. They are evenly spaced. Too evenly spaced. The regularity is not natural, or it is natural in a way that precludes your definition of natural, and when you breathe — you are somehow breathing, the water enters your lungs and exits your lungs and the exchange is not drowning, has never been drowning, you have stopped questioning it — the crystals vibrate. Not audibly. Not visibly. You feel the vibration in the chambers of your own sinuses, behind your cheekbones, a hum at the very lowest edge of perception, below sound, below hearing, a tremor that is to sound what the darkness is to light.

---

The built remnants are sparse but present. On the floor, half-buried in the silt-clay, there is a shard of something ceramic — a bowl, perhaps, or a tile — glazed in a deep blue that has survived its immersion with a stubbornness that borders on malice. One edge is clean. The rest is fractured. Beside it, in a depression you did not notice until you shifted your weight, there is a stack of three flat stones, each one no larger than a coin, placed with a precision that implies intention. Someone was here. Someone arranged these. Someone knelt where you are kneeling and set three small stones in a depression in the floor of a pocket inside a root at the bottom of a place with no light and no air and said, perhaps, *here*. Or said nothing. The stones might be the whole sentence.

There is a mark on the wall opposite the entrance-cleft. A line scratched into the root-wood with something sharp — a fingernail, a stone edge, a tooth. The line is straight. It runs vertically for the length of your forearm. At its midpoint it branches once, a single offshoot at forty-five degrees, and then continues. It looks like a map of a river. It looks like a diagram of a tree. It looks like a mark someone made because their hand needed to move and that was the direction it moved.

---

The density of this place is its defining quality. Everything touches everything else. The moss touches the ferns. The ferns touch the fungal crust. The crust reaches downward and the moss reaches upward and where they meet at the midpoint of the walls they blend into a band of material that is neither — a textile-like weave of plant and fungus, alive in a way that neither partner is alive alone, and this band is where the hum is strongest. Your cheekbones ring with it. Your teeth ring with it. You realize the sound is not coming from the crystals. The sound is coming from the junction, from the place where two forms of life negotiate their shared boundary, and the negotiation produces this sub-audible vibration the way a bow across a string produces a note, except the note is below hearing and above feeling and it occupies a register that your body interprets as weather, as atmosphere, as the mood of a room.

The water itself is dense with presence. Not with particles — it is clear, or would be clear if there were light to clarify it. Dense with *something*. The water has a texture. Moving your hand through it is not like moving your hand through water. It is like moving your hand through a substance that remembers every shape it has ever held and offers, very gently, a resistance that is not physical but historical. The water wants to stay where it is. It has been here a long time. It has memorized this chamber's contours and it does not wish to be displaced, and when you pull your hand back the water fills the space you made with a reluctance, a slowness, that is not viscosity but something closer to sorrow.

---

Here is where the world's rules begin to fray.

You notice it first in the ferns. You have been watching one spiral open and closed and you realize that the direction of the spiral has reversed — it was opening clockwise and now it is opening counterclockwise — and you cannot identify the moment of reversal. You try again, watching another frond, and this one opens clockwise, pauses at full extension, and then closes along the same path, which is not how spirals work, which is not how time works, and the frond does not seem to notice the error. It opens. It closes. It opens along the path it closed along. It is a loop, not a cycle. It is playing the same moment forward and backward and forward, and when you look away and look back the frond is in a different position than you left it, having moved while unobserved, having progressed through some sequence that requires your absence to proceed.

The darkness does not behave. You noticed this when you blinked, but now you notice it more fully: the darkness has depth. Not uniform darkness, not the flat black of a closed eye. There is a darkness that is farther away and a darkness that is closer, and the closer darkness sometimes moves, drifts across your vision like a cloud across a sky that has no sun, and when it passes you see the farther darkness more clearly, as though the closer darkness were an obstruction and the farther darkness were a thing worth seeing. But you cannot see it. You can only see *that* it is there, at a distance, a darkness that has depth and dimension and perhaps geography, and your eyes reach for it the way eyes reach for a horizon and find the same nothing in a different frame.

The wall is sometimes not the wall. You press your palm against the root-wood and feel its fibers and its damp and its grain, and then there is a moment — not a flicker, not a dissolve, more like a word you cannot remember, a gap in a sentence you are reading — and your palm is pressing against water. Not water *on* the wall. The wall is water. The wall has the texture of water, the give of water, the cool depth of water, and you push your fingers in and they enter the wall the way they entered this pocket, and you feel on the other side of the wall another space, another chamber, cooler, darker, denser with growth, and you pull your hand back and the wall is root-wood again and the other chamber is gone or was never there or is always there, waiting for the wall to forget that it is a wall.

---

The fauna are rare enough that each encounter is an event. A single organism passes through during your time in the pocket — something that moves along the ceiling with a motion like a leaf skittering across pavement, carried by wind except there is no wind. It is small. The size of your thumbnail. It has many legs or many projections that function as legs, and it is the same blue-black as the moss and it leaves a trail in the fungal crust as it passes, a furrow in the crystals, and the furrow fills in behind it at the same speed it is created, so that the creature is always at the leading edge of its own erasure, always walking on ground that has just healed over. It does not notice you. It travels the ceiling in a path that is not a straight line and not a curve but a pattern you recognize after a moment as the same pattern as the mark on the wall — the vertical line, the single branch. It traces the diagram. It is reading the mark or writing the mark or being the mark, and then it reaches the end of the line and drops from the ceiling to the water and is gone, not swimming, not sinking, simply *not there* in the way a thing is not there when it has never been there, and the water closes over the absence without comment.

---

The sound is almost nothing. Almost.

The hum from the fungal-moss junction is constant but below audition. The movement of the ferns is silent. The water's resistance produces no sound. What you hear, if you hold yourself still enough — and the chamber encourages stillness, the confined space and the dense water and the slow temperature all conspire to make movement feel like a rudeness — what you hear is a pulse. Not a heartbeat. Slower. A pulse that occurs once every twenty breaths, perhaps, a single low pressure-wave that travels through the water and through the root-wood and through your chest, and you feel it in your sternum and in the backs of your teeth, and it has a quality that is not mechanical, not the pump of a heart or the contraction of a muscle. It sounds like something breathing in its sleep. Something whose body is the root and the silt and the water and the moss and the chamber itself, a single organism that encompasses this entire pocket and is dreaming, and the pulse is its breath, and the hum is its dreaming, and the darkness is the inside of its closed eye.

You are inside something that is alive and asleep.

The three stones on the floor were left as an offering. The mark on the wall was left as a name. The ceramic shard was left as a handprint, a proof-of-presence, and the person who left it knelt where you kneel and felt the pulse and understood it and placed a blue shard on the floor and said *I was here too* and left, and the pocket closed behind them the way it will close behind you, the root-fibers drawing together, the cleft narrowing, the water settling back into its memorized contours with the slow relief of a thing that prefers to be undisturbed.

---

You will leave when you decide to leave, and the decision will feel less like a choice and more like being released. The cleft will be there. It will always have been there. You will turn sideways and pass through and the root's interior will graze your shoulder and you will emerge into whatever larger body of water contains this place, and the darkness will be different — thinner, merely dark, the darkness of depth rather than the darkness of sleep — and you will carry with you the hum in your cheekbones, fading, fading, gone by the time you reach the surface if you reach the surface, but not forgotten, the way a name is not forgotten even when the face is gone, the way a room is not forgotten even when the house is gone.

The pocket will remain. The ferns will spiral. The moss will hold its darkness. The pulse will breathe. The stones will sit in their depression in their careful stack and wait for the next one who finds the cleft and turns sideways and enters and kneels and stays still enough to hear the place dreaming, and leaves a mark, and is released.
