# The Lithic Gardens

The plateau came up whole.

Not gradually — not in the way mountains usually do, grinding centimeters per century against the sky. One season there was nothing here, and the next there was two kilometers of raw stone standing clean above the surrounding basin, flat on top like a table that had been slammed into place by something underneath. The edges are still sharp. You can cut your hand on them. No rain has come to round them, no wind has had time to sand them smooth, and the stone at the cliff-faces shows the marks of whatever forced it upward — long vertical striations, as if the rock was extruded through a die. It is warm to the touch. Not warm from the sun, though the sun is here too. Warm from below, the way a pan is warm before you put food in it. Residual heat. The kind that says *something just happened here, and has not finished happening.*

From the center of the plateau, you can see to the edges in every direction, and beyond them to the basin floor far below, and beyond the basin to hills that might be older mountains or might be dunes of a mineral so fine it behaves like sand. The scale is difficult. There are no landmarks, no trees of known height to calibrate against. The plateau is perhaps forty kilometers across. It feels larger, because there is so little in it.

---

The vegetation is the first thing you notice, and the thing you keep noticing, because it should not be here.

Not because of the altitude — warm air pools at this elevation in a way that suggests the plateau generates its own microclimate, a lens of trapped heat — but because there is no water. None. The stone is dry to a depth of meters. No aquifer beneath, no dew, no fog rolling in. The air tastes stripped. If you held a glass of water here it would evaporate in an hour, but there is no glass of water here and never has been.

And yet the plants are lush.

They grow from fissures in the stone — cracks that were present the moment the plateau emerged, as if the rock was born already fractured, already seeded. The roots do not seek water. They follow mineral veins, tapping into strata of sulfide and oxide that run through the stone like dark threads. The leaves are broad and waxy, blue-green in the normal daylight, and they are not quiet about their presence. Each plant is a riot of foliage, thick-stemmed, heavy with oils that gleam in the afternoon light. Some have grown to the height of a person. Some have grown larger. They stand in clusters along the fissures, and between the clusters there is nothing — bare stone, warm and clean, stretching in every direction.

The emptiness is the second thing you notice. The plants are islands. The spaces between them are oceans. You can walk for a kilometer over open stone without passing a single growth, and then you'll come to a fissure and find thirty plants standing together as if they've been having a long conversation and have only just stopped because you arrived. The density per cubic meter is almost zero. The plateau is a hall with pillars of vegetation spaced too far apart to be a forest, too close together to be accidental. Whatever placed them here — and something did, the pattern is too regular for chance — was working at a scale that doesn't mind the gaps.

---

There are no animals. None. The usual absence-feel of a landscape without wildlife is different here, more thorough. Not just no birds, but no insects. Not just no insects, but no sign that anything has ever eaten the plants. The leaves are unmarked. No curl of caterpillar damage, no browser's torn edge. The oils in the leaves might be the reason — they smell sharp, mineral, like the air around a battery. But it isn't only that. There are no burrows in the stone. No nests. No bones. No tracks in the dust, because there is no dust, because nothing has moved across this surface to make any.

The silence is complete. Wind moves across the plateau at intervals but the stone is too bare to catch it, and the plants are too widely spaced for their leaves to rustle against each other. You hear your own footsteps and the faint creak of your own breathing and after that — nothing. No distant water. No hum of insects. No machinery.

Except.

---

Except the ground has a sound. Not a sound exactly. A vibration, so low it sits below hearing and registers instead in the soles of your feet, in your sternum, in the bones behind your ears. It is constant. It does not change. If you press your palm flat to the bare stone you can feel it — a slow, even pulse, mechanical, steady, the kind of rhythm that implies a crankshaft, a reciprocating pump, something with a cycle.

Because there is something under the stone.

The fissures, if you follow them — and they all run somewhere, they are not random — lead to points where the stone has been cut. Not broken. Cut. Slots in the rock, too straight to be geological, too clean to be natural. Some are narrow, the width of a hand. Some are wide enough to drop into, though you would not want to. The walls of the wider slots show tooling marks — regular, machined, the pattern of a cutting head that moved in a spiral. At the bottom of one of these slots, far below, there is a faint orange glow and a sound that is almost a sound: a whispering of air being moved through a channel, the exhalation of a system that has been breathing for a very long time.

This is what the plateau is. Not rock that came up. Rock that was *raised.* Lifted, from below, by something that needed the surface — needed the altitude, needed the exposure, needed the flat expanse of stone open to normal daylight and warm air — and is still down there, still operating, in the silence and the dark, running whatever it runs.

---

The plants, you begin to understand, are not wild. They are an output.

The roots that follow the mineral veins don't end in the stone. They connect. Down in the fissures, at depths you can sense but not reach, the root systems meet channels — pipes, possibly, or the cut slots themselves serving as conduits — and through those channels comes not water but a fluid, dark and viscous, warm, carrying dissolved minerals in concentrations that would be impossible in any natural aquifer. The fluid is not water. It smells of sulfur and hot metal and has the texture of a light oil. The plants drink it. The plants have been designed to drink it, or have adapted to, or were always meant to — the distinction between agriculture and ecology doesn't quite apply here, because there is no one farming. There is only the system, below, and the plants, above, and the relationship between them, which is one of supply and growth.

The lushness of the vegetation is a sign of health. Not of the plants — of whatever is underneath. The plants are the visible portion of a process. Their waxy blue-green leaves are the surface expression of an industrial metabolism that operates in the dark, in silence, without any structure visible from above. No smokestacks. No buildings. No roads, no power lines, no fences, no yards, no doors. The plateau is empty of construction. The plateau is entirely built.

---

You sit on the stone near a cluster of the plants. The warmth comes up through your legs. The vibration comes up through your hips. The light is normal — sun, sky, the usual pale blue of afternoon at altitude, nothing strange about it at all, and this normalcy is itself strange, because it means whatever is happening here is happening in the spectrum of the ordinary, just below the surface, in a register you have to put your hand to the ground to feel.

The plants do not move. There is no wind to move them. The oils on their leaves catch the light and hold it. The stone stretches away in every direction, bare and warm, to the sharp edges of the plateau, and beyond the edges the basin drops away, and the basin is empty too, and the hills beyond the basin are too far to see clearly, and the sky above is the same sky that has been above this place since it was raised, which was not long ago, and which was also, in the frame of the system below, the first step in a process that is still beginning.

The plateau is embryonic. The stone is new. The plants are new. The fissures are new. The system below — the engine, the factory, the mechanism without a name — is old, or at least older than the stone, which means it was there first, which means it was waiting, which means the plateau was built for it and not the other way around, which means the process is not the plants and is not the machinery but is the *relationship* between them, and the relationship is just starting, and the landscape you are looking at is not a landscape but a first stage, and everything you see — the emptiness, the silence, the lush islands of foliage standing in warm light on bare stone — is the earliest expression of something that has not yet become what it will be.

You feel it in the stone. You feel it in the roots. The plateau hums, at the bottom of hearing, and the plants gleam, and the silence holds, and the warmth rises, and the system below continues.
