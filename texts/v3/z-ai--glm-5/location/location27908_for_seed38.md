# The Veldtmere

High. Unimaginably high. This place sits atop the world like a breath held too long—a plateau so vast it has forgotten it was once mountains. Time has worn them down to something approaching flatness, a process that took epochs no clock could measure. What remains is a surface that stretches to every horizon, and beyond the horizons, and beyond those too, until the concept of distance loses meaning.

---

## The Water

The water does not fall here. It emerges.

Every surface weeps. Rock faces—what little exposed stone remains—bleed from a thousand tiny pores where moisture presses up from within. The plateau does not hold water beneath it so much as it has become saturated all the way through, a vast sponge of vegetation and decay perched at the roof of the world. Step anywhere and the ground gives slightly, releasing a little sigh of dampness. Not enough to sink into—this is no swamp—but enough to feel that you walk on water held in suspension, waiting.

The atmosphere condenses constantly. In the dim half-light, droplets form on every leaf-tip, every spine, every trailing thread of growth, then slide down to be absorbed again before they can fall. A perpetual cycle of exhalation. The air tastes thick and green and wet. Breathing here feels like drinking.

Morning and evening, when temperatures shift, a fine mist rises from the ground itself—not from any body of water, but from the entire surface simultaneously. The plateau breathes out and then, hours later, breathes back in. Those who have lived here long enough (there have always been a few, scattered through the ages) time their movements to these rhythms, learning when visibility clears and when it thickens into white soup.

There are no rivers. Water moves instead through the vegetation itself, a slow hydraulic pulse traveling root-systems and vascular tissues. You can feel it sometimes, pressing your ear to the larger growths—a distant susurrus of movement deep inside, the sound of the plateau drinking itself, circulating, drinking again.

## The Vegetation

Plant life is not present here. Plant life *is* here. It has become the substrate, the architecture, the atmosphere made solid.

Nothing is not-plant. What looks like soil is compost across geological depths—the compressed remains of forests that grew on forests that grew on the ruins of older forests, going down perhaps kilometers, perhaps further. The first explorers who tried to dig found no bottom, only layer after layer of root-penetrated root-penetrated matter, each stratum more compressed than the last until the weight of ages turned the lowest layers into something like coal, something like peat, something that has no name because it exists nowhere else.

Walking here means walking on compressed time. Your feet press into surface that gives slightly—live roots threading through loose decay—before meeting the firmer layers beneath. A deceptive solidity. The ground remembers every footfall.

But above the walking-surface, plant life fills space with invention. There is no sky here, only layers of canopy, one stacked above another in receding planes of dim green. The lowest layer grows in the wet shadow of those above, pale and reaching, leaves the size of doors stretched horizontally to catch whatever light filters down. Above them, a middle tier of vertical growths—thick stalks, climbing tendrils, knots of epiphytes wrapped around larger supports that may themselves be plants. And somewhere far above, a crown layer exposed to what little light penetrates the perpetual atmospheric haze, growths you can barely see from below, their upper leaves bunched and furled, drinking the grey illumination.

The light never brightens beyond a perpetual dusk. Clouds hang close here, at this altitude—sometimes touching the canopy itself—and the combined filtering of mist and leaf reduces even noon to a submarine gloom. Things grow strangely in this light. Colors shift toward the blue-green spectrum; reds and yellows appear only as deep shadow. The darkest growths have evolved to pure blackness, absorbing every photon, their surfaces velvety and still.

And everything connects. No plant stands alone here. Every stem, every trunk, every spreading mat of surface-growth links to its neighbors through a web of runners and root-grafts and parasitic attachments and symbiotic bindings. Step into a clearing (such as they are—small openings where something fell and has not yet been overgrown) and look down: the ground is a woven mat of overlapping tissues, each edge blurring into the next. Look up: the canopy is a single sewn fabric of interlocking leaves.

This is one organism. Or it is a million organisms that have agreed to become one. The distinction has ceased to matter.

## The Scale

A traveler could walk for months and see the same landscape repeat in endless variation. The plateau extends beyond any reasonable measure—a remnant of an ancient continent that simply stopped eroding at this height, or perhaps a formation that grew upward as the plants accumulated, adding mass century by century, rising on their own ruins.

From certain elevated points (and there are a few, where harder stone resisted the wearing-down), the view stretches until curvature intervenes. The surface ripples gently—no true flatness at this scale, only the illusion of it, gentle swells and declines rolling across distances that take days to cross. In the far distance, the canopy haze merges with atmospheric haze, and the horizon becomes a guess.

There are no landmarks. Or rather, the landmarks are too vast to see as such. What looks like a uniform surface reveals itself, upon approach, to contain regions: a hundred-kilometer stretch where pale vines dominate, their runners weaving a fabric you could walk on top of for leagues. A deep basin where the canopy dips, descended into by growths that have learned to harvest moisture from the mist-clouds that pool there. A section where flowering bodies have colonized the middle layers, their pale blooms opening only during the mist-cycle, releasing scent that travels for kilometers.

Weather happens here, but it happens slowly. A rain-cloud might take days to cross a region the size of a small country. The mists follow temperature-gradients that shift with the patience of geology. Wind rarely reaches below the upper canopy, and when it does, it is a gentle thing, a swaying of the highest leaves that communicates itself downward in attenuated waves—a slow dance in which the whole plateau participates, each movement taking minutes.

## The Animals

They are abundant, but you will not see them.

Not at first. Not without patience. The fauna here have evolved to be indistinguishable from the plant-life that surrounds them—camouflage pushed beyond simple mimicry into something closer to biological identity. There are creatures whose skin photosynthesizes, who can go days without eating because they carry their own food-production on their backs. There are hunters who wait so still they have been mistaken for growths until their prey—unsuspecting herbivores browsing what they took for leaf-matter—wandered within reach.

The largest of them move slowly by necessity. A massive grazer—six meters long and nearly as wide, flat-bodied to navigate the dense growth—might take an hour to travel a hundred meters, each step careful, deliberate, parting the vegetation with patience. They communicate in frequencies below hearing, vibrations through the root-network that carry for kilometers, messages of territory and migration and warning.

Smaller creatures live faster lives in the lower layers. Pale things, eyeless, navigating by chemical sense and touch. Gliding forms that never descend to the ground, living their entire life-cycles in the canopy tangles, drinking condensed moisture from leaf-cups and hunting the insects that breed in the pools of water collected in plant-axis joints. And everywhere, everywhere, the insects—though insects is the wrong word. Arthropod-ancestors of every description, segmented and jointed, some microscopic, some palm-sized, clicking and rustling through the substrate, processing waste, aerating the root-layers, carrying pollen from growth to growth.

The sounds they make are mostly below notice. A low hum from the canopy-dwellers, audible only in the quietest moments. The rustle of passage through dense growth. Clicks of territorial display. This is a silent world in the way a library is silent—full of activity, all of it hushed, respectful of the listening vegetation.

## The Remains

People have been here before.

Not many. Not often. But the traces exist if you know where to look. The plateau preserves its own history in compressed layers of matter, and occasionally something resists compression enough to remain recognizable.

A foundation. A wall-section. In one place, a tower's base, the rest long since overgrown and collapsed into the mulch of ages. These are not recent ruins. They have been here longer than memory—longer, perhaps, than the peoples who built them. The vegetation has incorporated them, roots wrapped around stones, tendrils threaded through cracks, growth pressing from within until the masonry split and surrendered.

Who built here? No record remains. They left no writing, or none that survived. What tools they had were organic and have long since decayed into the soil they helped till. The plants themselves may be their only legacy—cultivars that escaped cultivation, species that were once crops or ornamentals and have since gone feral, expanded, conquered.

There is a single complete structure, smaller than a hut, that survived because it was built from something the plants cannot consume. The walls are smooth, seamless, a material that repels root and tendril and climbing vine. It stands in a clearing of its own making, an emptiness where nothing grows, and inside it—

Inside it is only space. A room with no furniture, no markings, no purpose visible. It might have been a tomb. A temple. A storage-unit for something that has since been removed. Whatever its function, the building now simply *is*, a geometric absence in the writhing architecture of life.

Travelers sometimes shelter there, though few stay long. The silence inside is different from the silence outside—not organic but absolute, sterile in a way that feels wrong. The walls have no give. The floor has no memory. It is the most frightening place on the plateau because it is the only place that does not breathe.

## The Atmosphere

The warmth is constant.

At this altitude, it should be cold, but the vegetation holds heat the way it holds water—absorbing, storing, radiating slowly. The plateau has created its own climate, a bubble of warmth and moisture that insulates it from the thin cold air that must exist somewhere above the canopy. Nights cool slightly, enough to prompt the mist-cycle, but never enough to threaten. This is a greenhouse that evolved by accident, a closed system that stabilized into permanence.

The humidity presses. Visitors from elsewhere feel it first as resistance—the air has substance here, weight. Movement takes effort, not from terrain but from atmosphere. Speech carries strangely, sounds dampened and shortened. A shout dies within meters, absorbed by the soft surfaces, while a low rumble travels through the ground itself, detectable by feet before ears.

The smell is green and thick—chlorophyll and decay, moisture and growth, the sweetness of fermentation in the lower layers where matter breaks down. It coats the throat. After days, you stop noticing it; after weeks, you begin to taste variations, can read the landscape by scent alone—water-nearby, canopy-gap, blooming-cycle, presence of creatures.

The light never changes quality, only intensity. Morning and evening are slightly dimmer than midday, but the grey-green cast remains constant. Time passes without obvious markers. Sleep is difficult for newcomers—their bodies cannot find the rhythms. The plateau's inhabitants, human and otherwise, have learned to sleep in fragments, an hour here, two hours there, responding to internal cues rather than external ones.

---

## The Experience

To walk here is to walk inside something alive.

The path—if there is a path—moves between growths that brush against shoulders and arms. Roots shift beneath feet. The canopy filters light into something that is not quite darkness, not quite day—a perpetual underwater glow. The temperature presses against skin like a warm palm. The air fills lungs with wet sweetness.

And all around, everywhere, in every direction, the plants continue their patient growth. They climb toward what light they can find. They reach toward moisture. They expand into gaps and create new ones by overshadowing what grew before. They die and are overgrown and become the ground on which their descendants rise. They have been doing this, will continue doing this, for longer than any framework of years can encompass.

This is the terminal state. The ancient equilibrium. The plateau has arrived at the end of its erosion, the end of its becoming, and now simply *is*, a living surface atop a dead world, breathing and drinking and growing and decaying in a loop so slow it feels like stillness.

In the dim green light, with the mists rising and the silent creatures moving through the rooted gloom, you understand that this place will outlast everything that sees it.
