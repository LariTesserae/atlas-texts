# The Weeping Heights

The first thing you notice is the sound. It comes from everywhere and nowhere—a resonant, harmonic drone that seems to vibrate in your chest rather than meet your ears. The sound of water moving through stone, though that description fails to capture its vastness. It is the sound of a mountain range breathing through a throat of wet rock.

---

The plateau sits at an altitude where air grows thin and the sky deepens to a violet-blue that hurts to look at. Up here, above the cloud layer most days, the light falls hard and unfiltered—sunlight that feels physical, pressing against your skin. There is no warmth in it. The light illuminates but does not heat. Temperatures hover just above freezing, and your breath hangs in the air.

Water is everywhere, yet nothing grows.

This is the paradox that unsettles: moisture without green, wetness without life. The plateau should be a bog, a marsh, something teeming. Instead it is mineral. The water has nowhere to go. It emerges from the rock faces in slow seeps and sudden weeping cracks, running in sheets down angled stone, pooling in depression after depression. The ground beneath your feet makes a squelching sound but holds your weight—compressed mineral layers, dense and patient. In some places the water has cut channels inches deep; in others it spreads across the surface in thin sheets that refract the harsh light into scattered diamonds.

The rocks themselves are the vegetation. What you first took for fallen boulders, for geological debris, reveals itself as something more deliberate. The stones grow. Not as plants grow—no, nothing so soft or linear—but in a different mode entirely. The minerals here accrete, layer by microscopic layer, fed by the water that carries dissolved compounds up from deep within the plateau's core. You see it now: the spreading fans of deposit, the bulbous accretions that resemble coral but shine with an internal gleam, the tapering spires that have grown upward from the wet ground seeking—they must be seeking—the light.

They catch the light. They sing.

The sound, you realize, comes from the stones themselves. The water moves through them—not over but through, permeating their porous structures, and the passage creates tone. Each formation sings at its own frequency, and the plateau holds thousands upon thousands of them, packed close, shoulder to shoulder, a dense forest of mineral growths that chime and drone in an endless, shifting chord. When the wind shifts, the harmonics shift with it. When you move, the sound changes—the stones respond to your presence, perhaps, or your body merely alters the acoustics of a space that was never meant for visitors.

---

The plateau extends beyond sight in every direction, though distance becomes difficult to judge. The light is too bright, the air too clear, the horizon too sharp. Features that seem close reveal themselves as distant when you try to walk toward them. The scale of the place keeps slipping. What you took for a valley wall might be an outcrop; what seemed a boulder might be, upon approach, a hill.

Density defines this landscape. The stone formations crowd each other, layer against layer, new growth atop old, accreting in shapes that seem almost meaningful. Towers of deposited mineral lean together like conspirators. Basins of still water reflect towers that rise from other basins. The whole plateau is a maze of wet mineral and trapped moisture, and you cannot take five steps without brushing against something, without your presence interrupting some slow process that was here before you and will continue after.

There are paths, though. Narrow ways between the growths. They might be natural—water-channels that have widened over centuries—or they might not be.

---

Because someone has been here.

You find the first structure an hour into your exploration, though time moves strangely in this place. It is a cairn of sorts, a stack of smaller stones placed with obvious intent atop a flat-topped formation. The stones are held together with something—a crude mortar made from the same mineral-rich water that births the formations, perhaps, accelerated and shaped by hands that understood its properties. The structure is small, waist-high, and weathered. It has been here a long time.

Further on, more signs: a ring of stones surrounding a pit that has filled with perfectly clear water. A single upright slab, too regular to be natural, its surface carved with grooves that might be decoration or might be function. A low wall, fragmentary, that seems to mark a boundary between one section of the plateau and another.

The technology is primitive. Hand-tools only, if tools at all—hands shaping water, hands placing stone, hands that worked slowly in a place where nothing moves quickly. Whoever built here worked *with* the plateau rather than against it. They used what they found. They understood, or learned to understand, how water moves through this place, how mineral accretes, how to shape the growing stone by guiding rather than forcing.

The structures feel like offerings. Or prayers. Or warnings.

---

It is difficult to remain certain of what you see.

The plateau resists coherence. The mineral formations, you realize, do not hold still—not in the way that rocks should. They grow, yes, but they also *move* in ways that growth cannot explain. A formation you passed an hour ago seems closer now, though you have walked away from it. The path behind you looks different than the path ahead, though it is the same path. The light, constant and bright, casts shadows that do not align with the objects before you.

Reality thins at the edges here. You feel it in moments of inattention: your hand passes through a formation before the formation solidifies; a basin of water reflects a sky that is not quite the sky above you; the sound shifts to a harmonic that makes your teeth ache and then resolves back to the droning baseline. The plateau exists in a state of continuous becoming, never quite settling into itself.

You begin to understand why no plants grow here. Plants require stability, require a world that holds still long enough to put down roots. Here, roots would find no purchase—or would find purchase and then be rearranged, the ground shifting beneath them, reality reconfiguring around the fact of their existence. Plants cannot survive in a place that will not stay itself.

But the mineral formations persist. They are not alive—they cannot be, not in any sense you recognize—and yet they endure. Perhaps because they *are* the plateau, extensions of its slow processes rather than interlopers. They do not grow *on* the stone; they grow *as* the stone, accreting from within, singing the plateau's song, moving when it moves, staying when it stays.

---

The loudness of the place presses against you.

It is not painful, exactly—the sound is too harmonious for pain, too organized—but it is relentless. You cannot escape it. The drone fills every space, emanates from every surface, accompanies every step. When you press your palm against a wet formation, you feel the vibration humming through it, the water passing through its pores, the slow crystalline growth at its edges. The stone is warm with motion, with process.

This place is not stagnant. The water cycles endlessly, rising from somewhere deep, spreading across the plateau, sinking back down to rise again. The mineral formations grow in patient layers. The structures left by whoever came before slowly accrete their own mineral coating, slowly becoming part of the plateau, slowly joining the song. Even the air moves, thin and cold and bright, carrying moisture from one wet surface to another.

You walk for hours—days, perhaps—and the landscape continues, valley-sized and larger, dense with presence, dense with sound. You see no animals, no insects, nothing that moves of its own volition except you. Even your own movement feels borrowed here, a disturbance in processes that do not require you and will not remember you.

---

Eventually you find a structure that is not a structure.

It is a doorway. Or rather: it is a rectangular opening carved into the side of a massive formation, too regular to be natural, too precise for primitive tools. The edges are sharp. The interior is dark, though light should penetrate—and does not. The sound changes here, drops to a lower register, a bass-note you feel rather than hear.

You do not enter. Something tells you that entering would be a different kind of journey, one that might not end on the plateau.

Instead, you sit beside the doorway, your back against wet stone, and you listen. The sound surrounds you, harmonics shifting as the water shifts, as the light shifts, as the plateau breathes its slow mineral breath. You feel the dense presence of the place, its thousands of formations standing close, its water moving in invisible channels, its reality settling and unsettlng in continuous cycles.

And you understand, finally, what this place is.

It is a throat. A mouth. An organ of vocalization on a geological scale. The plateau is not merely singing—it is *saying* something, has been saying it for millennia, will continue saying it for millennia more. The sound is a word or a name or a call, repeated endlessly, vibrating through water and stone and the thin bright air.

You cannot understand the word. You were not meant to.

But you can hear it. You can let it move through you, resonant and vast, and for a moment—just a moment—you become part of the plateau's endless utterance, your own small voice joining the choir of wet stone and bright light and water that has nowhere to go but through.

---

When you leave, the plateau does not notice. The song continues unchanged, the mineral formations continue their slow growth, the structures left by previous visitors continue their slow transformation into stone.

But something in you has changed. You carry the resonance now, a hum in your bones that will never quite fade, a sense of the vastness and strangeness and *aliveness* of a world that exists without needing to be understood.

The Weeping Heights. The Singing Stone. The place where water finds no exit and reality finds no edge.

It will be there long after you.
