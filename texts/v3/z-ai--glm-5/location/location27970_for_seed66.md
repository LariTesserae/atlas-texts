# The Condensation Cities

The plateau goes by many names. Up here, four kilometers above the lowland seas, it exists in a state of perpetual dampness that has nothing to do with rain.

## The Water That Rises

Water does not fall here so much as it materializes. The warm, moisture-laden air rises from below, meets the cooler strata of the high plateau, and surrenders. Condensation beads on every surface. The buildings weep. The streets gloss over with a sheen that never quite dries. Stand still long enough and you feel your own skin grow clammy, your hair begin to curl in the humidity.

In the early hours, before the machines reach full throttle, you can watch it happen: a collecting tank's surface rippling as droplets materialize out of seeming nothing, the air itself compressing into liquid. The condensers—great skeletal towers of rusted steel and copper tubing—work continuously, pulling moisture from the atmosphere, channeling it into the vast cistern networks beneath the city. Some districts run entirely on harvested condensation; their tap water tastes of metal and altitude.

The rocks here are porous limestone, eaten through with caves and channels over geological time. Water saturates the stone. Press your palm against a tunnel wall and it gives slightly, like squeezing a soaked sponge. In places the rock surface actually moves, a slow vertical creep of water emerging from within, sliding down in sheets that collect in the gutters and run along the channels cut into every street.

## A City of Horizontal Towers

They built outward, not upward. The plateau's fierce and steady winds made vertical architecture impractical generations ago. Instead, the city sprawls—a dense, layered footprint of low structures extending to every horizon. From a distance it resembles a mineral reef, a calcified organism spreading across the highland. Up close, it reveals itself as streets and passages, factories and housing blocks, all hugging the ground like something holding on against a gale.

The oldest districts were cut directly into the stone. You descend into them rather than climb. They are dry by comparison—the deeper chambers protected from the condensing air—and house the administrative cores, the ancient archives, the seeds of what this place became. The newer construction spreads across the surface: warehouses with corrugated walls streaked with mineral deposits, housing blocks of pale concrete gone green with age and chemical exposure, the refineries with their constant exhaling of steam.

Movement here is lateral. You walk streets that stretch longer than any lowland avenue, punctuated by crossways, the grid extending until atmospheric haze blurs the details. The longest thoroughfare—Processional Way, a name from another era—runs for nineteen kilometers without a significant bend.

## The Animal Ubiquity

There is no escape from them.

They are not dangerous, exactly. The plateau's fauna have lived alongside the city for longer than any record remembers. They occupy every gradient of niche, every gap in the built environment, every square meter of open space. They are the true inhabitants; the human population are latecomers who built on top of an ecosystem that simply absorbed them.

The sky-vermin come first: small, quick-winged creatures with membrane-stretched bodies that congregate in the thousands. They roost in the machinery, beneath the eaves, inside the ventilation shafts. Their droppings pockmark every horizontal surface, a calcium-rich glaze that requires constant removal. In the morning they burst from their roosts in synchronized clouds, the sound of their wings like a sudden wind. They eat the aerial plankton—tiny organisms that also thrive in the moisture-rich air—and the waste from the processors, and occasionally, in a nuisance that has defeated generations of exterminators, the insulation on electrical cables.

Then there are the crawlers. Six-legged, somewhere between an insect and a reptile in form, they emerge at dusk from the gaps between buildings, from the gutters and the subterranean channels. They are not aggressive. They simply exist in overwhelming numbers, their hard-shelled bodies clicking and scraping across the pavement as they forage for minerals. They love concrete—the treated aggregate provides something their metabolism requires—and they can reduce a new installation to pitted ruin within a decade if left unchecked.

In the water channels, the aquatic colonies thrive. Pale, blind fish-kin, evolved for the dark, swim in the cisterns and the condensation channels. They are harvested for protein and for their oils, but their populations persist. They breed continuously. They have to. Everything here breeds continuously. It is the only strategy that works in an environment where predation, competition, and the occasional industrial accident take such tolls.

The larger fauna stay to the margins—or rather, what used to be the margins before the city expanded to meet them. The shaggy, low-slung grazers that roam the plateau's stony grasslands (such as they are—the "grass" here is a lichen-like crust, inedible to humans but sustaining to those adapted for it) sometimes wander into the outer districts, where they stand in the condensation runoff and drink from the gutters. The processers tolerate them; their waste provides nitrates for the synthesizers.

But it is the birds that define the sound.

## The Noise That Never Ends

The plateau is never quiet. Even at the deepest point of the nocturnal cycle, when the machinery runs at minimum capacity and the human population sleeps, the animals continue. Chittering, clicking, the low-frequency thrum of territorial displays. The sky-vermin roosting vocalizations—a constant murmurous backdrop, thousands of tiny voices producing a white noise that locals no longer consciously hear.

During the active hours, it becomes genuinely loud. The factories run continuous shifts. The generators, housed in concrete bunkers at regular intervals throughout the city, produce a bass thrum that vibrates through the stone itself, felt as much as heard. Steam releases with sharp hisses. The crawlers emerge with their clicking. And above it all, the sky-vermin wheels and calls, a layered soundscape of shrieks and whistles that carries for kilometers.

New arrivals report it as maddening. They cannot sleep. They cannot think. Within a month, they have adapted or they have left. Locals describe silence—the true, ringing silence of the deep archives or a sealed chamber—as distressing, unnatural, almost dangerous.

## The Industrial Atmosphere

The machines are old. Not primitive—this is sophisticated industrial technology, the product of centuries of iterative improvement—but old. The aesthetic is one of functional persistence: steel that has been replaced and patched so many times that no original surface remains, but the form persists. Pipes that carry condensation, steam, and various industrial byproducts snake along the exteriors of buildings, crossing streets via overhead gantries, descending into the subterranean networks. The air carries a smell of machine oil, of ionized minerals, of the faint sulfur taint from the deeper processing vents.

Everything is damp. The machines are damp. The electrical systems, sheathed in waterproofing that requires constant maintenance, are damp. The workers are damp. Their clothes never fully dry; they acquire a permanent stiffness, a chemical patina from exposure to the industrial atmosphere.

Light is diffuse. The condensation in the air softens everything, reduces contrast, creates a permanent slight haze. On clear days you can see for kilometers—but clear days are rare. More commonly, visibility extends to perhaps three kilometers before the world dissolves into a gray-white blur. The sun, when it burns through, does so as a bright patch rather than a distinct disc. Shadows are soft-edged, mobile, never quite trustworthy.

At night, the city generates its own illumination. Gas lamps along the major thoroughfares—the municipal system, burning processed gas from the digesters. Electric lights in the industrial sectors, harsher, bluer. The glow carries, reflected off the condensing mist, so that even the unlit areas are never truly dark.

## The Absence of Green

New visitors always remark on it, usually with discomfort: there are no plants. Not here. The plateau's altitude and the constant moisture would, in theory, support vegetation, but something in the soil composition, in the water chemistry, in the accumulated industrial output of centuries, has rendered it impossible. The few attempts at rooftop gardens require imported soil and sealed, climate-controlled environments; they are luxuries for the wealthy, curiosities, not a feature of daily life.

Instead, the biological world here is entirely animal (if you count the crawler-kin and sky-vermin as animals, which their six-limbed body plans challenge) and fungal. The fungal layer is invisible but essential—deep in the substructure, in the water channels and the waste processing systems, fungal mats digest and process and render the city's output into forms that can be cycled back. The synthesizers, the digesters, the breakdown tanks—all rely on fungal colonies that have been cultivated and refined for generations. They are not seen. They are not spoken of. But they are there, doing the work that plants might do elsewhere, breaking down the old into nutrients for the new.

This is not a dead world. It is simply a world that has found a different arrangement.

## The Slow Present

The city changes slowly. A building might take a decade to construct, its foundation cut by hand-tools and early mechanical borers, its walls raised section by section. A factory might operate for two centuries before its processes become obsolete, at which point it will be repurposed rather than replaced—retrofitted with new machinery, its bones remaining, its purpose shifting.

The erosion that shaped the plateau continues, at its geological pace. The water that seeps through the limestone will, over centuries, open new channels and close old ones. A street might settle slightly, require reinforcement. A foundation might shift by centimeters over a human lifetime. The city adjusts.

But the cycles here are patient. The sky-vermin populations rise and fall on a seven-year cycle, overwhelming some years and merely abundant in others. The condensation rate varies seasonally, with the wet months bringing near-constant fog and the dry months (a relative term) bringing mere dampness. The crawlers swarm cyclically, their numbers governed by pressures and resources that no one fully understands.

Human memory, in this context, operates differently. Generational knowledge persists: the locations of the best water, the timing of the sky-vermin migrations, the safe routes through the tunnels, the danger zones where the air quality drops below breathable. These are not written down so much as absorbed, passed through proximity, reinforced through daily practice. The city knows itself.

## The Plateau Extended

The scale of the place is difficult to grasp from within. The city covers an area larger than many small nations. Walking from one edge to the other—assuming you could walk in a straight line, which the street grid does not permit—would take weeks. The municipal boundaries are administrative fictions; the reality is a continuous built environment that shades gradually into the stone-y wastes at the margins, where the condensers thin and the animal populations return to their un-managed states.

There are districts within the city that have their own dialects, their own customs, their own microclimates created by the density and arrangement of their construction. The Industrial Quarter—never mind that the entire city is industrial—houses the primary processing infrastructure, its air thick with steam and chemical output, its population adapted to the noise and the routine. The Archives District, cut deep into the stone, maintains its own water systems and its own quiet. The Margin Towns, at the city's edge, operate almost as separate entities, their economies tied to the plateau's animal resources rather than its industrial output.

Transport between districts takes time. The municipal trams run on set schedules, their routes determined decades ago and rarely altered. Freight moves via the underground canals, pulled by cable systems powered by the central generators. Messengers on foot still carry urgent communications; the electrical message system covers only the central districts and is prone to interference from the atmospheric conditions.

But mostly, people stay where they are. The city is vast; a single life fits within it easily, contained within a few square kilometers of streets and passages, a known set of faces, a routine that extends from waking to sleep.

## The Life That Persists

Stand on a rooftop at the transitional hour, when the night shift ends and the day shift begins, when the condensation is at its heaviest and the light has that diffuse, sourceless quality that means morning is coming.

Listen. The machines hum. The sky-vermin are beginning to stir. Somewhere below, a crawler clicks across a wet street. The water runs in its channels, mineral-rich, warm from the processes that have cycled it through the city's systems.

This is not a hostile place. It is a place that has simply found its equilibrium—a state in which water, stone, machine, and animal have settled into mutual persistence. It is damp and loud and alive. It has existed, in something close to this form, for longer than anyone can remember.

It will likely exist, in something close to this form, for longer than anyone can predict.

The condensation continues to form. The animals continue to breed. The machines continue to turn. The plateau endures.
