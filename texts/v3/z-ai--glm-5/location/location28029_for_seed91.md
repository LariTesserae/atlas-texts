# The Hollow Garden

The first thing is the sound. It arrives before you understand where you are—a low, mechanical roar that vibrates in your chest, that you feel in your teeth, that becomes a constant you forget only because there is no silence to contrast it. This is the kind of noise that restructures thought. After hours here, your inner voice begins to shout just to be heard.

---

You have descended. The passage down was cut recently—explosive charges, judging by the scorch marks still visible on the raw stone walls. The tunnels here are young. Edges haven't softened yet. Fracture lines run in violent directions, following no geological logic, only the path of whatever industrial bore punched through. The walls weep. Condensation gathers on the cold rock in heavy drops that slide down and collect in thin, mineral-rich rivulets. The air is wet in the way of caves, but colder—near freezing, with a chill that settles into bone.

Then the tunnel opens, and you understand why they cut through.

---

This is a subterranean valley, a cathedral-sized void beneath the surface. And it is filled with plants.

Not covering the walls. Not growing in patches. The plants *are* the walls. Great columns of something like bamboo but bundled tight and woody, ribbed and segmented, rise from a floor you cannot see to a ceiling lost in darkness above. Between them: vast empty space. The density is sparse. Each column stands apart from its neighbors by twenty meters, thirty, fifty. You could walk between them for an hour and pass only a dozen. But their scale is regional—the forest (if it is a forest) extends beyond headland visibility, columns fading into dim blue haze in every direction.

The light is minimal. Bioluminescence, faintly blue, pulses slowly from nodes at intervals along each column's height. It's barely enough to walk by. Your pupils stay dilated. Shadows pool in the distances between.

---

The columns are alive. You can see it now—the slow pulse of fluids moving within, the subtle flexing of segments, the way condensation doesn't just collect on their surfaces but is actively exuded, drawn from some deep reservoir below. The air tastes of oxygen and moisture. This whole place breathes.

Fungi carpet the lower portions—pale, shelf-like growths in cream and grey that layer over each other in elaborate terraces. Mosses form continuous sheets across the ground between columns, thick enough to walk on without ever touching stone. Small, vine-like tendrils link the columns at intervals, carrying nutrients, signals, or something else. The vegetation is the substrate. There is no soil here, only layers of older growth compacted into something walkable, something that yields slightly underfoot like peat.

---

And threaded through this living architecture: industry.

Pipes run between columns, bolted into the woody bark, carrying fluids you cannot identify. Some are warm to the touch—condensation steams off them in the cold air, feeding the cycle. Generators hum in distant chambers you can hear but not see, their vibrations transmitted through the plant-matter itself. Cables snake along the ground, half-buried in moss, carrying power to lights (the harsh yellow sodium ones must be recent additions, half-dead, flickering) and to machines whose purpose is unclear.

This place was built. Or is being built. The industrial presence is scattered—small structures, single rooms, platforms half-grown into the plant columns. Some look abandoned. Others still operate. You can see the functional ones by the steam they vent, the rhythmic clanking that joins the greater roar.

The roar. It comes from deeper in. The valley extends past what you can see, and somewhere far off, something massive runs. The sound is mechanical but has become geological—a noise so sustained it feels like weather, like atmosphere, like the room itself is producing it.

---

There is almost no animal life. You've seen one small pale creature, insect-like, moving between moss layers. Something that might be a bird crossed with a bat drifted past a distant column and was gone. But this is a plant economy. The living mass here is almost entirely vegetal, and it has solved the problem of the underground. The columns draw water from below. The fungi break down waste. The mosses filter the air. The whole system is a body, and you are walking through its tissues.

You find remains of scaffolding. A rusted container with equipment inside—presses, tanks, tubes—coated in dust and thin mold. A cutting machine, its blade still sharp, designed to harvest segments from the columns. This is an extraction operation, or was. Someone learned that the columns produce something useful—oil, sap, fiber, fuel—and they came to take it. The plants tolerated this. Or perhaps the plants are the operation now. It's hard to tell if the industry is exploiting the garden or serving it.

---

The cold is constant. No sun warms this place. The heat comes only from the machines and the biological processes of the plants themselves—the columns are slightly warm to the touch, slow metabolic fires burning inside their woody skins. Condensation is everywhere. The air cycles between damp and drier as the columns breathe, as the machines vent, as water moves through the system.

You walk for what might be an hour between the columns, following no path but the spacing of the plants. The noise never changes. It becomes a kind of silence—your mind edits it out, and you notice only when a machine in the distance changes rhythm, a new clanking joining the chorus, something starting up or shutting down.

The dynamics are slow. You see the evidence: a column that has grown around a pipe, a platform being lifted by decades of vegetal growth, a machine whose base is buried in accumulated moss. This place changes, but on timescales that make your presence here feel sudden, almost violent. You are the fast thing. The noise is the closest the garden comes to speech—a continuous, mechanical utterance that means nothing to you but means *something*, surely, to whatever tends it.

---

At the far edge of the section you've explored, the columns thin. The ground slopes down. You can hear running water now, beneath the roar—the first distinct sound, a stream moving fast over rock. The moss gives way to actual stone, cold and wet, and then a channel appears: water running in a cut channel, diverted from somewhere deeper. It feeds into a pipe system that leads back toward the columns. The garden irrigates itself, or is irrigated.

Beyond the channel: another chamber, another section of the valley. The columns there are different—paler, thinner, with a different pattern of bioluminescence. You don't cross. The roar is louder here, and the machines cluster thicker, and you sense that whatever this place is doing, it intensifies in that direction.

---

You turn back. As you climb toward the tunnel you entered through, you pass a column that has been cut—a clean segment removed from near its base, the wound calloused over with new growth. The harvested sections must be valuable. The plant recovers. The industry continues. The roar goes on.

At the tunnel entrance, you pause. From here, the valley looks like what it is: a vast room defined by its contents, dim and cold and wet and loud. The columns stand like pillars in a temple dedicated to some process you don't understand. The machines are its priests, or its parasites. The condensation falls like a slow rain that never reaches the ground.

You ascend. The sound fades—gradually, then suddenly, then not at all, until you emerge into daylight and silence so complete it rings.

Behind you, the garden continues its work.
