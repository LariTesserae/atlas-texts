The Velostrade

High altitude does things to water. Up here, where the air goes thin and cold, the moisture doesn't know whether to be ice or rain or fog, so it becomes all three at once—a perpetual state of indecision that soaks everything through without ever quite committing to falling.

The plateau extends beyond sight in every direction, what must have once been a mountain range before time ground it flat. Three billion years of erosion will do that. The rock beneath—if you can still call it rock—has been worn down to something like geological exhaustion: sedimentary layers compressed so thoroughly they've become a kind of stone-flesh, porous and waterlogged, weeping from a thousand tiny fissures whenever pressure is applied. Walk across it and it gives slightly beneath your feet, releasing thin streams of ancient water that seep up from reservoirs trapped who-knows-how-far-down. The whole plateau is a sponge that's been sitting in a cold bath since before multicellular life existed.

And the plants.

The plants are the first thing you notice, and the last thing you understand.

They are not growing *on* the plateau. They are the plateau—or they have become it, over epochs of patient colonization. What looks like groundcover from a distance reveals itself, on closer inspection, to be a rhizomatic nervous system of unbelievable complexity. Mattes of root and stem and something like leaf have woven themselves into the stone-flesh, replacing mineral with vegetable cell by cell, until the distinction stopped mattering. The substrate itself is alive—or was alive, or lives in such a slow alien timescale that "alive" doesn't quite capture it. A cubic meter of this ground, if you could excavate it (you can't; the roots go down forever and hold everything together), would contain more biological information than a rainforest, compressed into a crawling, creeping horizontal density.

But sparse. The word seems wrong. How can a place be sparse when plants are the ground itself? And yet—above the surface layer, above that thin interface where root becomes stem and geology becomes biology, the plateau feels empty. The living mats spread for kilometers between features, between growths, between the things that rise up from the horizontal infinity. Great distances separate the vertical presences. You can walk for half an hour across the damp, slightly yielding surface without seeing anything change, and then come upon a sudden congregation of structures that shouldn't exist.

They look like factories. They hum like engines. They produce something—heat, vibration, low-frequency thrumming that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. Tall cylinders of woven plant-matter, reinforced with silica drawn from the stone-flesh below, rise in clusters, their surfaces pocked with circular openings that vent steam or mist or sometimes a fine particulate dust that settles on everything nearby. Pipes—actual pipes, though grown rather than forged—connect cylinder to cylinder, carrying fluids that pulse visibly through their semi-translucent membranes. The structures are warm to the touch, warmer than the cold air, and they generate a constant low industrial drone that the wind carries for kilometers.

No one built them.

This is the thing that takes the longest to accept. The cylinders, the pipes, the venting towers, the processing units—they grew. Not deliberately, not with intention, but through some convergent process that the plants stumbled into hundreds of millions of years ago and have been refining ever since. The plateau's ecosystem discovered chemistry, discovered production, discovered industry, and integrated these processes into its biological substrate. The factories are organs. The pipes are vascular systems. The steam and particulate are metabolic byproducts. The whole plateau is a single vast organism running some process whose purpose no human science has deciphered.

Some researchers think it's soil production—the plateau constantly regenerating its own substrate, breaking down stone-flesh into something more biologically available. Others argue it's atmospheric processing, the plant-mats extracting something from the perpetually foggy air and concentrating it in ways that serve some ecological function. Still others claim the entire system is purposeless now, an evolutionary vestige that long ago stopped serving its original function but continues to run because stopping would require more energy than continuing. The factories hum and pulse and produce their output, and the output dissipates into the cold wet air, and nothing anywhere seems to benefit.

The noise is constant. The industrial drone from the factory-growths, yes, but also the water—squeezing up from below, dripping from overhangs, running in thin streams across the living ground—and the wind, which never quite stops up here, and the animals. The animals are everywhere, once you learn to look.

They've evolved to match the place. Low-slung, furred or feathered in dense layers against the cold, moving in quick bursts between the sparse vertical structures. Some graze on the living ground itself, though carefully—they've learned which surface-mats will regenerate and which will die if disturbed, which areas are safe to feed upon and which will release toxic compounds when damaged. Others hunt the grazers, or hunt each other, or subsist on the particulate output of the factories, having evolved some metabolism that can process whatever the cylinders produce. There are things that look like insects but aren't, things that look like mammals but have the wrong number of limbs, things that move too fluidly to be anything that evolved under normal circumstances.

The plateau has been isolated long enough that its fauna operate on different rules. Up here, three billion years from the nearest sea, five kilometers above what used to be continental shelf, the normal constraints of biology have loosened. Not everywhere, not for everything—but in patches, in regions, at certain times of day or certain phases of the moisture cycle, the animals behave in ways that suggest they've slipped partially free of ordinary causality. A grazer will be feeding on the living ground one moment and then, without transition, be twenty meters away, still feeding, having never moved. A predator will catch prey that was behind it. A creature will give birth to offspring that appear to be different ages simultaneously, one kit nursing while another version of the same kit explores nearby, both equally real, both equally present.

The researchers have a word for it: *phase-slippage*. The plateau's extreme age, its unique biological substrate, its industrial-organic processes, its isolation—some combination of factors has worn thin the normal boundaries of here and now. The factory-growths produce something that affects local reality. Or the living ground accumulates temporal distortion like sediment. Or the animals have simply evolved to exploit instabilities that exist everywhere but are only visible, only tangible, in places like this.

Fog pools in the low areas, thick and cold, and sometimes shapes move in the fog that don't correspond to anything physical. Not hallucinations—multiple observers see the same shapes, describe the same movements—but not material either. They pass through solid objects. They leave no tracks. They appear and disappear without pattern or predictability, and the animals ignore them entirely, as if they've learned that some phenomena simply don't interact with ordinary matter.

The sound never stops. Even at night, even in the rare moments when the wind dies, the plateau hums and drips and pulses with its industrial-biological processes. The factory-growths operate on no cycle that matches any known system—they run continuously, have run continuously for longer than the fossil record shows, will likely run continuously long after everything else here has changed beyond recognition. Their drone becomes background, becomes inaudible, until you leave the plateau and notice the absence more than you ever noticed the presence.

Water weeps upward here. The stone-flesh and plant-substrate have compressed so tightly that the water table has been squeezed into strange configurations—sometimes forcing moisture up against gravity, up through cracks and pores, up into the cold air where it becomes fog or frost depending on the temperature of the moment. Ice crystals form on the factory-growths during the coldest hours, then melt and run down the cylinders in patterns that almost look intentional, almost look like writing in some script that no human has ever learned to read.

The plateau is not hostile. That's the wrong word. The plateau is indifferent in the way that only something unimaginably old can be indifferent—it has seen so many millions of creatures live and die across its surface that individual lives register as barely distinguishable from the background noise of biological process. The animals who live here have adapted to its strangeness, learned to navigate its phase-slippages and avoid its toxic zones and exploit its industrial output. Humans can survive here, briefly, with preparation. The cold is manageable. The dampness is uncomfortable but not lethal. The living ground supports weight, mostly, though you learn quickly to test before committing.

But you wouldn't want to stay.

The plateau operates on different assumptions than the rest of the world. Time moves differently—not faster or slower, but *differently*, accumulating in some places and depleting in others, pooling and flowing like the water that seeps up from below. Distance is unreliable; walk in a straight line for an hour and you might travel one kilometer or five, depending on conditions no one has successfully measured. The factory-growths process something that may be physical matter or may be something else entirely, and their output may be feeding the phase-slippage or may be trying to correct it or may be unrelated entirely.

What the plateau is, fundamentally, is old. Older than anything should be. Old enough that the normal rules have started to wear thin, started to fray at the edges, started to let other things through. The plants that became the ground and the ground that became the factories and the factories that became the atmosphere and the atmosphere that became the conditions for the animals and the animals that learned to phase and slip and move through spaces that shouldn't exist—this is what happens when a place persists long enough. It develops complications. It accumulates strangeness like sediment. It starts to operate on logic internal to itself, consistent but opaque, comprehensible only on its own terms.

The Velostrade—that's what the researchers call it, a name that's stuck despite no one remembering who coined it or what it was meant to mean—has no center and no edge, no feature that could be called its heart or its boundary. The living ground extends past the horizon in every direction, the factory-growths rise and hum and vent their steam, the animals graze and hunt and phase through moments, the water seeps up from reservoirs that have been trapped since before the atmosphere had oxygen, and the whole vast cold wet ancient system continues its processes without reference to anything outside itself.

It was here before us. It will be here after us. And in between, it hums and drips and waits, patient beyond human comprehension, for whatever comes next.
