# The Perpetual Cradle

The heat comes first. Not from above — there is no direct sun here — but from below, rising through saturated ground that has never known a season, never tilted toward warmth or away from it. The basin sits low, nearly at sea level, held in a shallow geological depression that might, in another world, become a river delta or a lakebed or a coastal plain. But this landscape does not progress toward becoming anything. It rests in the state of its own first morning, and has rested here for longer than anything in it can remember.

The rocks weep. That is not metaphor. Pale, soft stone — calcareous, chalky, still warm from some deep source — pushes up through the dark muck in low shelves and knuckles, and from every fissure, water slides in thin, constant films. The stone has not yet learned to erode. It surfaces raw, almost fetal, its mineral surfaces still bright with the moisture of their own emergence. In some places the rock is so new it has the texture of wet chalk, leaving white residue on anything that touches it. In other places it has already been colonized so thoroughly by moss and liverwort and the rooting tendrils of ferns that the stone itself is barely visible — a pale ghost beneath its green covering, weeping quietly underneath.

Between the rock shelves, the ground is a saturated dark loam, almost liquid in places, holding the warmth like a bath. Water does not flow here so much as it seeps, rises, condenses. The atmosphere is so close to saturation that moisture appears on every surface — on leaves, on stones, on the backs of the creatures that move through the understory — without any rain having fallen. Dripping is constant, arrhythmic, coming from the canopy above where broad leaves channel their own condensation down petioles and stems to fall in fat, irregular drops onto whatever is below. The air smells of mineral water, of warm stone, of green things respiring in the dark.

Because it is dark. Not night-dark — this is a permanent condition, a filtering that happens above. The canopy is layered three, four, five times over, and each layer catches and holds its share of light until what reaches the floor is a greenish, submarine glow, enough to see by but not by much. Shapes lose their edges six or seven meters out. The trunks of the largest plants — they are not quite trees, these things; their stems are too soft, too thick, too fast-growing, more like woody ferns or giant herbs — rise straight and pale out of the dark ground and disappear into the ceiling of foliage where their leaves spread in overlapping tiers. The leaves themselves are enormous, some of them, broad and deep green, designed to catch what little light filters down and to hold the moisture that condenses on their surfaces. Everything is glossy. Everything is wet. Everything reflects the dim light just enough to give itself away.

The vegetation is not uniform but it is overwhelming. Ferns of every scale — from tiny, translucent filmy ferns growing in the weeping cracks of the stone to massive tree ferns with crowns three meters across — dominate the understory and midstory. Between them, thicker-stemmed plants with pulpy trunks and broad, simple leaves crowd upward, their wood too soft to ring, their growth too fast to harden. Vines and climbing epiphytes connect everything, draping from frond to frond, rooting in crotches and crotches-within-crotches, so that the whole space is a web of living material, near-touching, layered, saturated with itself. There is almost no gap. To move through this landscape would be to push through it, to part curtains of fern frond and spongy stem and hanging moss, to step over and around roots and fallen logs already half-consumed by the next generation of growth. The density is not aggressive but it is absolute. The space is full. Every cubic meter holds something living, and most cubic meters hold several things.

And the animals.

The sound tells you about them before you see them. The sound is constant — a layered, droning, pulsing wall of biological noise that has no beginning and no end, no variation, no pause. Insect song dominates, a shimmering, frequency-layered hum that sits in the warm air like a physical thing, vibrating in the chest. It is not one insect but thousands, tens of thousands, each in its own register, each in its own rhythm, and the rhythms have been the same for so long that they have locked together. The mosquitoes — and there are mosquitoes, clouds of them, breeding in every standing pocket of water, in every hoofprint-shaped depression in the saturated ground — whine at one pitch. The cicadas, broader-bodied, darker, clinging to the trunks of the giant ferns, drone at another. Something smaller, something in the leaf litter, ticks and rattles in a third. These sounds have been measured by no instrument and would not need to be. They are simply the sound of this place, as constant as the heat, as constant as the weeping stone.

Above the insects, frogs call. Not the sporadic, conversational calling of a temperate pond but a continuous, overlapping chorus that never rests, the same phrases cycling and recycling through the same frequencies at the same intervals. There are dozens of species in the chorus, some of them tiny — the small, jewel-colored frogs that sit on the broad leaves above the water, their skin glossy in the dim light, their throats pulsing — and some of them large, deep-voiced, sitting half-submerged in the warm pools that collect between the rock shelves. Their calls do not change. They have called like this, in this pattern, in this place, for as long as the stone has wept, for as long as the canopy has filtered, for as long as the insects have hummed.

Other sounds layer in and hold their positions. A low, rhythmic thumping — something large, something heavy, something in the deeper green beyond sight — beats at an interval of perhaps four seconds. It has been beating at four-second intervals. It will continue. Birdsong, such as it is, comes in short, bright phrases that repeat and repeat, not with the improvisational quality of songbirds but with the mechanical precision of something that has found its pattern and will not depart from it. The birds themselves are visible in glimpses — small, dark, fast-moving things that dart through the understory and disappear, their wings humming, their paths apparently deterministic, the same routes flown at the same intervals. Larger birds, if they are birds — the shapes are hard to resolve in this light — move higher up, in the canopy, their calls deeper, more resonant, rhythmic, patterned.

And on the ground, in the leaf litter, in the shallow water, on the surfaces of the weeping rocks, the movement is continuous and dense. Beetles with iridescent carapaces move in lines along the fallen fronds. Centipede-like things, pale and long, navigate the edges of the water. Something that might be a crab — dark-shelled, flat, moving sideways — occupies the wettest patches of ground, its claws clicking against the soft stone. Small lizards with translucent skin cling to the lower trunks, their throats pulsing in time with the frogs, their eyes tracking the insects that move past them at distances too close to be cautious. Snakes — thin, dark, wet-scaled — thread through the undergrowth in patterns so regular they might be following grooves worn by their own previous passages.

The abundance is staggering. Every surface is occupied. Every niche is filled. The air holds insects; the water holds larvae; the leaf litter holds grubs and worms and beetles; the bark holds lichen and moss and the small creatures that live in moss; the spaces between leaves hold spiders and their webs, and the webs hold the remains of what has been caught, which is immediately consumed and replaced. The landscape is at carrying capacity — and it has been at carrying capacity so long that the populations have stabilized into precise, unshifting equilibria. Nothing increases. Nothing declines. Nothing colonizes anew. Nothing goes extinct. The system has found its numbers and holds them the way the rock holds its moisture.

This is the strangeness of the place, and it is a subtle strangeness, not visible to a quick glance. A quick glance would see only a hot, wet, lowland fern-forest teeming with life, dim under its canopy, loud with its creatures. But to stay — to watch and to listen — is to notice that the light never changes. The dimness is permanent. The temperature never shifts. The humidity never drops. The insects never vary their pitch. The frogs never alter their phrase. The thumping in the deep green never speeds up, never slows down, never stops. The dripping from the canopy falls in the same irregular pattern — the same irregular pattern, the same drops striking the same surfaces at the same intervals that have never repeated until now and are repeating now and will repeat. The condensation on the leaves forms in the same droplets, swells to the same size, falls at the same moment, down the same paths, to the same ground.

Nothing is changing because nothing has ever changed. This is not an equilibrium that was reached. This is an equilibrium that was always here. The stone emerged weeping and has wept since. The ferns grew to this height and have grown to this height. The animals found their numbers and have kept their numbers. The light was filtered to this level and has been filtered to this level. The world was assembled in this configuration — hot, low, wet, dense, dim, loud — and it holds this configuration the way a crystal holds its lattice.

The heat is always exactly this hot. The water is always exactly this wet. The creatures are always exactly this many.

In the far distance — and the landscape does extend far, opening out beyond the immediate denseness into more of the same, valley-sized, regional, horizon-less under its low ceiling of green — the thumping continues at its four-second interval. The chorus continues at its set frequencies. The condensation falls.

The stone weeps, softly, brightly, as it has always wept, in the first and permanent morning of a world that does not move forward.
