# The Weeping Alcove

The first thing is the light. It hurts. Not warm—never warm—but white and absolute, bouncing off every surface until the eye has nowhere to rest. This is mountain light, thin-air light, the kind that makes shadows seem cut from something denser than darkness itself.

The place sits hollowed into a peak I cannot name. Perhaps it had no name before someone carved it. Perhaps the carving named it. The Weeping Alcove, they might call it, or something older, a word that means both "sanctuary" and "wound."

**The walls are wet.** Not dripping—weeping. The stone holds moisture the way skin holds tears: a constant, slow exudation from pores too small to see. Run your hand across the granite and it comes away glistening. The water doesn't freeze. It should, here, at this altitude, in this cold that settles into the bone like a quiet tenant, but something in the mineral composition—the pressure, the faint warmth from some deep fracture—keeps it liquid. It sheets down the carved walls in transparent rinds, gathers in channels cut centuries ago, pools in hollows worn smooth by palms and copper vessels.

The erosion is old here. Not the erosion of wind or ice, though both have worked, but the erosion of use. Everything shows the patience of generations. The steps descending into the Alcove have dips worn into their centers where feet have fallen ten thousand times. The basins carved into the walls have lips worn thin from the scrape of pots. Someone designed this place for water, and someone else improved it, and someone else after that, until the original intention became sediment, layers of purpose compressed into something like geology.

**There is so much life.** This seems wrong. The vegetation outside is minimal—lichen mostly, small hardy succulents that cling to rock faces in stubborn clusters, a few grasses that grow in protected crevices where heat collects. Nothing taller than a hand's breadth. Trees cannot survive here.

But life has found other forms.

The pools swarm with it. Not fish—too cold, too shallow—but crustaceans the size of rice grains, pale and eyeless, their shells translucent enough to show the dark threads of their digestion. They cluster at the edges where the water meets stone, feeding on something in the film that forms there. And feeding on them: spiders that walk on the water's surface, their legs dimpling the meniscus, patient as saints. And feeding on those: birds that nest in the carved alcoves above the pools, small swift things with plumage the color of wet slate, their bodies dense with the heat of constant motion.

They are never still. This is the turbulence of the place—not wind, not water, but *movement*. The birds spiral and bank, catching insects that rise from the pools, landing only to launch again. The crustaceans pulse through the water in visible currents, thousands of bodies making the liquid seem to move with intent. The spiders trace geometries across every still surface.

And yet: silence.

The cold dampens sound. The stone absorbs it. The water's constant movement makes no splash—just a sliding, a seepage, the softest possible disturbance. The birds open their beaks and what emerges is a whisper, a thin piping that the air carries only a few feet before swallowing. When the wind outside howls across the peak, the Alcove receives it as a distant moan, muffled as if heard through water. Inside, the dominant sound is breath. Your own. The shallow rhythm of bodies pressing against bodies in the crowded space.

Because this is a small place. A cathedral in miniature, perhaps, or a shrine, or something older than shrines—just a room, carved with intention, that holds perhaps thirty people pressed close, or a hundred if they squeezed. The ceiling arches overhead, smooth from centuries of condensation, and the walls angle inward as they rise, giving the sense of being inside something organic. A throat. A heart chamber. The space beneath a giant's bowed head.

**The density is remarkable.** Every surface serves multiple purposes. The walls are carved with niches—some hold small copper bowls for collecting water, some hold offerings of stone and bone, some hold the mummified remains of creatures too small to identify, preserved by the cold and the mineral-rich air. The floor is not flat but stepped, terraced in a way that makes the water flow through channels from level to level, and each step is also a seat, a shelf, a sleeping platform. People—or something—have nested here. The corners hold stacked collections of objects: feathers arranged by size, shells from some distant sea, fragments of stone that might be tools or might be debris, pieces of copper beaten into rough shapes.

The technology is simple. Copper, cold-hammered. Bone needles. Stone tools with edges worn to smooth curves. Thread spun from something fibrous—perhaps the tough inner bark of the sparse mountain plants, perhaps the sinew of animals taken in some hunt that happened here long ago. No metalwork requiring fire. The Alcove holds no hearth, no char, no smoke-stain. Whatever warmth exists comes from bodies, from movement, from the mysterious mineral heat that keeps the water from freezing.

And the place is *busy*. This is what the turbulence means: not chaos but purposeful motion, the anthill energy of a space designed for work. People would have come here—perhaps still do—to collect water, to offer something, to observe the creatures that live in the pools, to harvest them. The birds leave feathers. The crustaceans leave shells when they molt. The spiders leave egg sacs that look like pearls strung in the corners. Everything produces something. Everything is used.

The bright light makes it strange. The Alcove faces east, and the morning sun enters directly, flooding the space until the water gleams like liquid silver and the walls seem to glow from within. In this light, the creatures in the pools become visible in their thousands—every drop of water holds life, every surface crawls or swims or clings. The density of being becomes overwhelming, beautiful, almost frightening.

But the strangest thing: **the water remembers.**

Not in any mystical sense. Perhaps not in any sense at all. But if you watch long enough—and the silence, the brightness, the cold encourage watching—you begin to notice patterns. The way the water flows over certain stones in spirals that repeat. The way the crustaceans cluster in shapes that match the carved designs above them. The way the spiders' webs mirror the cracks in the ceiling, as if each is reading the other.

Something carved these patterns. Something deliberate. And something in the water—or the creatures, or the light—keeps finding them again.

The Alcove sits in its high place, cold and bright and wet, full of life that moves in silence, full of water that weeps from stone, full of patterns that repeat and repeat and repeat, as if trying to remember what they were meant to be.
