# The Hollowing Works

The stone is still warm from the fracture.

---

This is a place being born. Not grown—that would imply patience, organic rhythms, the slow consent of living things. No: this place is being *carved*. Every chamber, every passage, every roughly rectangular dwelling exists because someone swung a tool against raw geology and *removed* what didn't belong.

The rock is limestone, pale and dense, shot through with veins of something darker—perhaps shale, perhaps compressed ancient mud that never quite became proper stone. When newly exposed, the surfaces gleam wet, as if sweating. The dampness here is condensation, mostly: the cool stone meets warmer air that filters down from above, and moisture beads on ceilings and walls, collects in the tool-marked grooves, drips eventually to channels cut deliberately into floors. Someone has thought about where the water wants to go. Someone has been thoughtful about it.

There are no rivers here. But there is wetness—a pervasive, atmospheric humidity that settles on everything, that makes the dust stick to skin and cloth, that keeps the newly carved surfaces from fully drying. In some deeper chambers, the condensation is thick enough to create permanent dampness underfoot, a thin sheen of water that reflects torchlight and makes the floor treacherous. Workers learn to recognize the feel of stone that is merely cool versus stone that has become slick.

---

The settlement extends across multiple levels—though "levels" suggests too regular a geometry. Rather, the hollowing has followed the stone's natural weaknesses and gifts. Where the limestone was thick and sound, broad chambers opened with each strike of the pick. Where imperfections ran—the dark veins, the pockets of softer material—the carvers adapted, creating narrow passages that twist and drop and climb according to their own accidental logic. A person could walk for an hour through these connected hollows and never quite be certain whether they were moving deeper or shallower, whether they had traveled far or merely in tight circles through the packed and busy dark.

And it is packed. The density of occupation here presses in from all sides—dwellings carved cheek-to-jowl with storage hollows, with working spaces, with chambers whose purpose has shifted three times as the settlement expanded around and through them. A family might live in a room whose back wall was, six months earlier, the front wall of someone else's home—until the hollowing broke through and both spaces became one, and now the two families share a courtyard that neither family intended. The settlement grows like a fungus spreads: by accumulation, by encounter, by the slow filling of every available void.

There is no room between presences. Every carved space serves at least one purpose, usually two or three. A sleeping hollow becomes a tool-sharpening workshop during waking hours; a passage becomes a place for children to play when the foot traffic is light; a chamber originally cut for grain storage now holds three families displaced when their own hollows flooded last season. The damp has a way of remaking decisions.

---

The light comes from above and from fire. In the upper levels—those nearest the surface, where the original hollowing began—vertical shafts pierce the stone at irregular intervals, their openings carved wide enough to admit sunlight during the day, narrow enough to limit the rain that falls during the wet seasons. The quality of illumination shifts throughout the day: gray and diffuse in mornings, direct and warm at midday, thin and amber as afternoon lengthens toward evening. Near the shafts, moss grows in patches—pale, slow-growing things that have adapted to the uncertain light, drawing what they can from dampness and stone.

Deeper, where the shafts cannot reach, the settlement burns oil rendered from something—fat from animals rarely seen, perhaps, or pressed from the few plants that survive in cultivated pockets near the light sources. The oil burns with a steady, close flame that smells faintly of whatever it was made from. The light is warm and alive, but it costs something. Every deeper chamber that remains occupied represents a continuous expense of fuel, of effort, of the labor needed to keep the wicks trimmed and the reservoirs full. Some passages have been abandoned because the cost of lighting them exceeded the value of what they contained. You can tell which ones: the unlit stretches smell different, older, and the darkness there feels less like an absence and more like a presence waiting to expand.

But the settlement is still growing. Still being carved. The sound of tools against stone is the dominant rhythm of this place—metal striking rock, the crack and ring of it, the slower scraping of debris being cleared away. The work happens in shifts, because the noise is tremendous when multiple crews operate in adjacent chambers. The concussion of pick against stone travels through the rock itself, so that even in a quiet hollow three chambers distant from the working face, you feel each strike as a faint throb in the floor, the walls, the bones of your own skull.

They have organized themselves around this sound. Certain hours are for sleeping, and during those hours the hollowing stops; the silence afterward feels like pressure released. Certain hours are for work, and the whole settlement rings with it; you learn to hold conversations in the brief pauses between strikes, to time your words to the rhythm of the tools.

---

The vegetation is sparse but present. Near every light shaft, someone has planted something—hardy herbs that tolerate the damp and the uncertain seasons, small crops that can grow in the thin soil that accumulates when debris and waste and the slow breakdown of stone dust combine with the persistent moisture. There is a cultivated courtyard, three levels down, where a single shaft has been carefully widened over years, and where the light is enough to sustain a small grove of trees—stunted, twisted things that bear a bitter fruit no one would choose but everyone accepts. The fruit is fermented into something drinkable, or mashed into paste, or simply eaten raw when there is nothing else.

In the lower levels, where the light is artificial, nothing grows intentionally. But life persists in crevices and damp corners: pale fungi that need no light, thin threads of something that might be mold or might be root systems seeking blindly through the stone. The workers clear these away when they find them, but the settlement is too dense, too packed, for total vigilance. In the abandoned passages, the fungi spread unchecked, forming pale colonies that feel like velvet when touched and that release a faint, sweet smell when disturbed.

---

The fauna is rare but not absent. The settlement has its share of the usual companions of human habitation—small creatures that live in the margins, that eat what is discarded, that move unseen through passages that humans have forgotten or never knew. There are insects that drink the condensation, that cluster near the oil lamps, that breed in the standing water of the drainage channels. There are small four-legged things that hunt the insects, their eyes catching the torchlight, their bodies lean and quick. No one knows exactly where they came from; they were not brought intentionally. They simply appeared, as life does, when the conditions were right.

Larger animals are almost unknown. These incidents become stories, repeated until the animal grows in the telling: the goat becomes a strange beast, the fox a creature of ominous intelligence. The settlement has its myths already, born of the dark and the enclosed press of existence. It does not need more, but it collects them anyway.

---

The coolness is pervasive. The stone retains the temperature of deep time, and no amount of human activity has managed to change it. In the upper levels, near the shafts, the temperature shifts with the surface weather—warmer in the dry season, cooler when the rains come. But below, in the settled dark, it is always cool, always damp, always the same. Bodies adjust. Newcomers shiver for their first weeks and then stop noticing. The cold becomes a constant, like the darkness, like the damp—something that no longer registers because it has always been there.

---

The place has rules. They are not written—there is no writing here, not yet—but they are known, and they are enforced.

Do not dig without approval. The settlement expands according to plans that exist only in the minds of the carvers, but those plans are detailed. A chamber cut in the wrong place can breach a water-bearing seam, can collapse an adjacent hollow, can undermine a load-bearing wall that was not meant to be load-bearing. The carvers know the stone; they have been reading it for years. When they say that a passage must turn left rather than right, they are not offering an opinion. They are describing reality.

Do not block the drainage channels. The water wants to move; if it cannot move where the carvers intended, it will move where it chooses, and where it chooses is often through someone's dwelling, someone's workshop, someone's stored food. The channels are kept clear by designated workers who move through the settlement's lower reaches with scrapers and buckets, clearing the mineral deposits and debris that accumulate. It is not prestigious work. It is necessary work.

Do not extinguish a lamp in a public passage. The cost of fuel is shared; the cost of someone falling in the dark is borne by the fallen. Lamps are tended, refilled, trimmed, but not put out until daylight filters down through the shafts and the upper levels grow bright enough to see. Even then, the deeper passages remain lit. There is no darkness more absolute than the darkness underground, and the settlement has learned to respect it.

Do not speak of what was found in the deep. This rule is newer, and it is followed less consistently. The hollowing has broken through into spaces that were not made by human hands—natural caverns, perhaps, or something older. The carvers sealed them quickly, but not before people saw what lay beyond: tunnels that extended beyond the reach of torchlight, formations of stone that looked too regular to be natural, a current of air that suggested vast spaces waiting to be discovered. Or perhaps they saw nothing at all. The stories vary. The rule exists because someone thinks the stories should stop. The stories have not stopped.

---

The atmosphere is thick—literally, in the deeper levels, where the air grows heavy with moisture and the faint residue of oil smoke and human breath. Ventilation is a concern that grows more pressing with each new chamber carved. The carvers have begun cutting vertical shafts not just for light but for air, connecting the lower levels to the surface through narrow passages that allow the damp, used air to rise and fresh air to descend. It works imperfectly. In the deep hollows, during busy times, the air can become close enough to make breathing difficult. People learn to move slowly, to conserve their wind, to take breaks in better-ventilated spaces when they feel the pressure building in their chests.

---

There is a central chamber—not the largest, but the most deliberately shaped—that serves as the settlement's heart. It is near the surface, well-lit by a shaft that was widened deliberately over years, and it is here that decisions are made, disputes resolved, plans discussed. The walls have been smoothed more carefully than most, and the floor has been leveled with packed stone dust that hardens into something like a pavement. There are benches cut into the walls, and a raised platform at one end where the carvers stand when they explain their plans. The space feels almost like the inside of a skull—rounded, enclosed, echoing slightly with whatever is spoken. When the settlement gathers here, which it does when decisions must be made collectively, the density of bodies makes the air warm and close. People tolerate it because they must. There is nowhere else large enough.

---

The settlement has no name, or rather, it has several, and none of them are official. Some call it the Hollow. Some call it the Works. Some call it the Womb, which some find too intimate and others find too morbid. The carvers have their own term, spoken only among themselves, which translates roughly to "the patient waiting." They understand something that others do not: the stone is not passive. The stone is not simply being removed. The stone is present, is watching, is aware in some mineral sense of what is being done to it. The carvers strike the rock with respect. They clear the debris with care. They seal the spaces they are not meant to enter. They do not explain why. The rules are enough.

---

At night—or what passes for night, when the shafts dim and the oil lamps become the only light—the settlement grows quiet. Not silent: there is too much life, too much breath, too much activity for true silence. But the quiet is dense and textured. You can hear the drip of condensation falling into channels, the soft sounds of people sleeping behind curtain-covered doorways, the occasional footstep of someone moving between chambers on necessary business. The quiet has layers. Closest, the sounds of your own dwelling. Beyond that, the sounds of the neighboring hollows. Further still, the sounds of the upper levels, where night is more clearly defined. And beneath it all, or perhaps above it, the slow sounds of the stone itself: the creak of pressure adjusting, the faint settling of newly carved spaces learning to hold their shape.

---

The settlement is young. It has existed for perhaps three generations—not long enough for the stone to fully accept it, not long enough for the spaces to feel permanent, not long enough for the people to forget that they are living in something that is still being made. The children who are born here know nothing else, and they treat the carved walls and the dripping ceilings as simply the way the world is. Their grandparents remember the surface—the open sky, the feeling of wind, the sight of horizon. They do not speak of it often. There is no point. The descent was necessary, even if the reason has been obscured by time. The settlement grows because it must. The stone yields because it chooses to. And somewhere below, in spaces that the carvers have not reached, something waits that has been waiting for a very long time.

The erosion is zero here because the stone has not yet been given time to erode. The surfaces are fresh. The hollows are new. The settlement presses against the boundaries of what it knows, expanding slowly, carefully, into the dark.
