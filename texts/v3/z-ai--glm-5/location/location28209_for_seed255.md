# The Underworks

The darkness here has architecture.

Not the absence of light but something built—a material fact, stacked and fitted. You learn this when you reach out: the blackness presses back, threaded with structure. Walls close without warning. Ceilings drop. The space wants to be small, was made to be small, and then something else happens—a chamber opens, and another behind it, a whole sequence of them, receding into distances your voice can't find the edges of. Someone measured all of this. Someone's hands knew every centimeter before you did.

The construction is primitive in method but total in ambition. Everything that exists here was made—there is no underlying geology, no substrate of natural stone or soil beneath. Just layer upon layer of worked material. Fired clay bricks pressed tight without mortar. Rough-hewn stone blocks fitted with obsessive care, gaps filled with smaller stones, then smaller still, a fractal attention to joinery. The walls want to be solid. They want to hold. In some passages, the brickwork gives way to carefully split shale, slate-dark and damp, or to bundles of reed-lattice plastered over with something like chalk that crumbles at a touch. In others, the passage walls are simply dense-packed earth, tamped into hardness by some forgotten pressure, held in shape only by the adjacent masses that contain it.

And then, newer work—strata of effort still revealing themselves. Half-finished walls. Openings that lead to nothing yet, or to places where the construction simply stops, as if the builders turned away mid-thought. Erosion here is embryonic, not decay but emergence. The place is still becoming itself. You walk through rooms that have never been completed, passages that dead-end not because they're blocked but because they were never continued. It gives the corridors a quality of held breath. Of waiting.

The dampness moves. This is the first thing you notice after the dark—how moisture travels through the built world. Condensation beads on the cooler surfaces and gathers into droplets that find paths downward, tracing the mortar lines between bricks like reading Braille. In some chambers the walls weep continuously, thin runnels that join into streams along the floor-channels, clearly carved for exactly this purpose. In others the air itself is wet, thick with vapor that settles on your skin and makes everything slick. You touch a wall and your hand comes away gleaming. The dark holds that shine.

Rain falls in certain chambers—actual rain, from no sky that exists. The ceilings there are pierced with small deliberate openings, shafts that lead up through hundreds of meters of constructed earth to some higher system where water is collected, channeled, released on some schedule you cannot know. The rain-chambers are loud, each drop striking ceramic tiles with hollow percussion. The sound echoes down the corridors, blending with other sounds until you can't tell where one noise ends and another begins. Dripping. Rushing. The deep rumble of water moving through pipes you cannot see, the walls themselves carrying fluid through hidden channels.

And then the mechanical sounds. Rhythmic. Repeating. Something turning, something grinding, something that might be a wheel or a gear or simply a stone rolling in a stone groove, powered by water or by weights or by hands that turned a crank decades ago and the mechanism has simply continued. The Underworks are loud with motion you can't locate. Sound comes from everywhere and nowhere, bouncing off surfaces you can't see, multiplying until the air itself seems to vibrate with accumulated noise. Your footsteps add to it. Your breathing. The sounds of your passage fold into the larger soundscape and become part of it, and you realize you are making this place louder, the way a room grows noisier when someone enters it.

The scale is difficult to judge in darkness, but you learn by walking. Hours of passage. Corridor after corridor, chamber after chamber, each one slightly different—the height of the ceiling, the material of the walls, the tilt of the floor, the presence or absence of those carved drainage channels. Some rooms have niches, shallow alcoves sized for bodies or bundles or nothing you can name. Others have platforms or low walls or pits covered with grates of woven copper wire, now green-black with age. Some chambers connect through obvious doorways; others require you to crouch through crawl-spaces or climb through holes knocked in the brickwork, edges still rough, clearly not part of the original plan.

A region this size should have been built by thousands over centuries. But there are no marks of varied intention—no idiosyncratic choices, no regional styles, no signatures of different hands or different eras. Every brick is like every other brick. Every wall follows the same logic of placement, the same compression, the same careful filling of gaps. If thousands built this, they were all the same person. Or they were following a pattern that admitted no variation. Or they were not builders at all, but something that built—some process that laid brick on brick according to a plan inscribed in material itself.

You find the fungi in the wet chambers, where the rain falls or the walls weep most heavily. Not plants—nothing here photosynthesizes, there is no sun to convert—but growths nonetheless. Flat films of pale tissue spreading across damp brickwork. Bulbous protrusions pushing from the gaps between stones, rubbery and faintly luminous in the absolute dark, emitting a gray light that isn't light, a glow you feel more than see, more presence than illumination. In one chamber, the floor is carpeted with something like moss, except it's waxy and cold and yields under your boots like wet leather. It makes a sound when you walk—a squelching compression—and after you pass, the material slowly rises back to its original height. You leave no trace. The floor heals.

The fungi don't seem to eat the construction, exactly, but they do inhabit it. They find moisture and they spread. In some corridors they've grown so thick they've formed new walls, pale membranes that block passages or narrow the already narrow way. You can push through them—they tear easily, wet-paper thin—but the sensation is unpleasant, the tissue clinging to your skin, leaving trails of slick residue. In one place you find a chamber where something has happened: a massive bloom of fungal growth that filled the entire room and then collapsed, dried, hardened, becoming a new kind of architecture itself. The collapsed growth forms shelves and ridges, a landscape within the landscape. The floor is crunchy with desiccated matter.

You hear the fauna before you see them. Sounds of movement—scrabbling, splashing, the click-clack of something hard traveling over stone. They are rare, but they are here. In one flooded passage, you catch sight of something pale and many-legged, the size of a large dog, swimming through the shallow water with a body that seems to be made of articulated segments, like a metal chain but organic, wet, and moving. It doesn't notice you. Its eyes—if they are eyes—are small black dots that face forward, fixed on something down the passage that you cannot see. In a high chamber, something flies—or glides, rather—between pillars of built stone, a flat body like a ragged keratin sheet, drifting on air currents that you can't feel, navigating by some sense other than sight. It passes close enough that you feel the displacement of air.

The creatures are not afraid of you. They are not aggressive either. They simply move through the dark-built world, following their own imperatives, their own routes through the endless architecture. You wonder if they evolved here—or if they were made, too. If everything in this place was made, then what made them? And why? They seem too complex for accident. Too functional for decoration. They do something in the Underworks. They serve some purpose in the larger system.

Because there is a system. You begin to understand this as you walk. The water that moves through the channels and pipes, the rain that falls through the ceiling-shafts, the drainage that carries it away—it all connects. The chambers where moisture collects feed the fungal growths. The fungal growths feed something else, or process something, or create some condition that the next chamber requires. The sounds you hear—those mechanical rhythms—are not random. They are timed. Cycles. You stand in one place long enough and you hear a sequence: first the grinding, then a rush of water through distant pipes, then a pause, then a soft rain beginning in some far chamber, then silence, then the grinding again. Something is being pumped. Something is being distributed. Something is being processed. The Underworks are not just a built environment—they are a built process, a machine the size of a region, and you are walking through its internal spaces, its ventricles and valves and conduits.

The strange quality of the place deepens the longer you stay. Time moves differently—or your perception of it does. The sounds become a rhythm that your body starts to follow. Your heartbeat slows to match the grinding cycles. Your breathing syncs with the rain-falls. You find yourself waiting for the sounds, expecting them, and when they come, something in you relaxes. The darkness, initially oppressive, starts to feel like a skin. The close walls feel like containment, yes, but also protection. You are inside something. You are held.

In one chamber, you find marks on the wall—not construction, but something else. Scratches. Lines and curves that might be writing or might be counting or might be the casual working of some tool across stone, the way a person might doodle while thinking. But there are many of them. Hundreds. Covering a section of wall from floor to ceiling. And when you look closer, you see that they continue behind you—the marks cover every surface of this chamber, every wall, the ceiling too, you can feel them when you reach up, the stone worn slightly by their incision. Someone—or something—stood in this room and made these marks. For hours. For days. For years. And then stopped. Or finished. Or was stopped.

You cannot read them. But standing in that chamber, surrounded by the accumulated evidence of effort, you feel something like recognition. Someone else was here. Someone else moved through these corridors and chambers, touched these walls, heard these sounds, breathed this damp air. The Underworks were not built empty. They were built to be inhabited. They were built to contain.

What they contain now is unclear. You, briefly. The pale segmented swimmers. The drifting sheets. The slow-growing fungal landscapes. The water. The dark. The sounds that move through the architecture, filling it, defining its shape by echoes, making the place audible even when it cannot be seen.

When you leave—if you leave—the sound follows you for a while. The grinding. The rain. The sense of something vast and processual continuing behind you, below you, around you, a built world executing its functions in the dark, waiting for nothing, completing itself.
