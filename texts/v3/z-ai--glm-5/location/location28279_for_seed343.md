The High Everplain

The plateau goes on forever. Or near enough that the distinction ceases to matter—a table of land so vast that walking its edge would take seasons, so old that the mountains that once defined it have worn to gentle swells beneath an unbroken skin of green. This is not a young landscape. This is what remains after everything assertive has softened. The bones of the world, wrapped in living flesh.

---

The elevation announces itself in the thinness of air, in the way breath comes shorter and sound travels strange—carrying too far in some directions, swallowed entirely in others. Two miles up, perhaps three in places. High enough that the sky sits close, a pressed hand of blue-gray rather than a distant dome. Yet warmth persists. The sun has work to do up here, unfiltered and direct, and it does that work thoroughly. Sweat forms and dries in cycles on skin.

Clouds form at eye level. They drift through in wisps, intimate and unconcerned with human presence, leaving condensation on every surface—leaves, bark, the soft mats of moss that carpet the ground. Morning fog rolls across the plateau at ankle-height, dissipating by midmorning only to return at dusk. It's never quite wet, never quite dry. Damp the way breath is damp.

---

Plant life is not present here. Plant life IS here.

The distinction matters. There is no soil beneath the vegetation because the vegetation has become the soil—layer upon layer of living matter, decomposing matter, matter in transition. Roots weave through older roots. Vines climb vines that climb trees that grow from the hollowed cores of their ancestors. The ground gives slightly with each step, a walking-on-flesh sensation, spongy and alive. Sometimes it gives too much—a foot sinks to the ankle in soft plant-mud, pulls free with a sucking sound, leaves a footprint that begins filling with moisture almost immediately.

The biomass stratifies. At the bottom: the ancient mat, feet thick, dark and warm and breaking down. Above that: ground-cover vines with leaves like hands, catching the light, holding it. Then shrubs and small trees, dense and interwoven, their branches locked together in a competitive embrace so tight that moving between them requires pushing, shouldering through, the plants resisting and yielding in equal measure. Then the emergent layer—the great trees, if they can be called trees, rising a hundred feet and more, their canopies spreading wide and merging into a single photosynthetic ceiling that filters sunlight into a green-gold glow.

Everything grows on everything. Ferns sprout from bark. Moss coats the underside of branches where the condensation collects. Fungi fruits in clusters along the vines, shelf-like and orange, tasting of nut and spore. Flowers bloom from the trunks of the great trees directly—no intermediary branches, just bark-embedded blossoms, pale yellow, opening each morning and closing each evening, synchronized to some signal that travels through the mass.

No bare stone. No bare earth. No paths except those made by movement, and even those heal within days, the vines and ground-cover reclaiming what was pushed aside.

---

And yet—emptiness.

The plateau is so large that even this, this total biological occupation, feels sparse. The density of life per cubic foot is absolute, but the density per square mile, per hundred square miles, creates a paradox: life everywhere, and still so much space between moments. You can walk for an hour and see no change, no variation, the same green-gold light, the same damp warmth, the same soft ground. Features are regional rather than local—a slight rise here, a depression there that collects more water and grows more lush, a break in the canopy where one of the great trees fell centuries ago and the gap still hasn't closed.

The scale defeats comprehension. The plateau stretches to horizons that shimmer with heat-haze and humidity, and everywhere, the same living mass, breathing and growing and dying and growing again. A body too large to see. An organism at landscape scale, each tree a cell, each vine a capillary, the whole thing pulsing with the slow rhythm of photosynthesis.

---

Sound should not exist at this volume. But it does, and it is deafening.

The insects form the baseline—a constant, textured drone, not a single note but a chord that shifts through the day, different species warming up and winding down in overlapping cycles. Crickets, cicadas, beetles with wing-cases that resonate when struck, all of them calling and responding and filling the air with a hum so thick it has weight. You feel it in your chest.

Above that, birds. Thousands. Tens of thousands. The plateau supports flocks so large they darken the canopy when they pass, their calls layered into a wall of shriek and song and trill. Some communicate in frequencies that vibrate the chest rather than the eardrum. Some mimic—each other, the insects, the sound of wind through branches, the creak of wood under strain. Dawn and dusk bring the worst of it, the full chorus, sound that obscures thought, that requires you to stop walking, stop moving, just wait for the wave to pass.

The mammals add their voices. Howler equivalents, though they are not quite primates—something older, stranger, with vocal cords evolved for this environment, producing guttural roars that travel miles across the open green. They call to each other from the emergent trees, marking territory, declaring presence, their voices threading through the insect drone and the bird shriek.

And beneath it all, the sound of the vegetation itself. The creak of wood expanding in morning warmth. The pop and crackle of seedpods opening. The whisper of leaves adjusting their angle to follow the sun. The groan of vines under tension, growing tighter each day, pulling down branches, slow-motion collapse audible as a low-frequency throb.

The soundscape has no silence. Even at night, when some insect species rest and the birds go quiet, others take over. Frogs in the wetter depressions. Night-flying insects with wings that buzz. The ongoing, endless conversation of life talking to life about life.

---

The fauna owns this place. Humans are guests, and temporary ones.

Insects dominate by sheer number. The air carries them constantly—beetles the size of thumbnails, flies that land on every exposed surface, dragonflies that hunt the spaces between branches, their iridescent wings catching the green light. They are not aggressive, merely present, and after a time you stop noticing the tick of legs on skin, the whine near the ear, the scatter of tiny bodies against you as you walk.

The larger creatures make themselves known differently. They are heard before they are seen. Movement in the canopy—the shake of a branch, the ripple of leaves, the rush of displaced air as something leaps from tree to tree. Most keep their distance, observing with eyes adapted to this light—large, dark, reflective. You catch glimpses: a flash of fur, the curve of a spine, a tail vanishing into foliage. They are not hiding. They simply have no reason to approach.

Some do approach. The avian species, fearless and curious, land on nearby branches and tilt their heads, examining with an intelligence that is unsettling. They have colors that shouldn't work—blue and orange in patterns that ache the eye, crests that rise and fall with their calls, beaks curved for extracting insects from bark. They are neither pet-sized nor eagle-sized but somewhere between, and when one calls, others respond, a cascade of attention, and suddenly there are dozens watching from the surrounding vegetation, a gallery of eyes.

The herds move through the plateau seasonally, following rainfall patterns and plant growth. Massive, lumbering things, built for pushing through dense vegetation, shoulders hunched and heads low. They create temporary paths—trampled ground, broken stems, the lingering scent of musk and digestion. Their calls are low moans that travel through the ground as much as the air, felt in the feet before heard in the ears. When they pass, everything else goes quiet. Even the insects pause. Even the birds. The herd moves, and the plateau holds its breath, and then, when they are gone, the soundscape returns like a wave re-forming after a stone.

Predators exist. You sense them more than see them—the absence of sound in a particular direction, the warning calls of birds, the stillness that spreads through the canopy. They are solitary, territorial, patient. They watch from the middle levels, neither canopy nor ground, waiting for the weak, the sick, the slow. You learn to read the signs. When the insects go quiet in a specific area, move the other way. When the birds give a particular stuttered call, stop and identify the direction. The rules are simple. The predators are not evil—they are part of the system. But they are also very, very good at what they do.

---

Structures exist. Barely.

The people who live here—or lived here, or pass through—build with the only material available: the plants themselves. Framed structures of bent saplings, woven vines forming walls, leaves layered for waterproofing. The architecture is temporary by design. The vegetation resists permanence; it grows over, through, and around anything static, absorbing it back into the mass. The buildings that survive are those that are maintained, visited regularly, the growth cut back and the vines re-woven.

You find them in clearings that feel deliberate, spaces where the canopy thins and the ground-cover gives way to packed earth. Some are singular dwellings, barely large enough for a sleeping adult. Others are clusters, three or four structures arranged around a central fire-pit, abandoned now, the fire long cold, the woven walls beginning to sag as new growth pulls them apart. Tools lie scattered—hand-shaped, useful for cutting and weaving and grinding. Bone and stone and wood. No metal. Nothing that requires heat beyond what a cooking fire provides.

The people who made these structures understood the land. You can see it in the placement—always near a depression that collects water, never in the lowest point where flooding occurs, oriented to catch the morning sun and avoid the afternoon glare. They were not conquering the plateau. They were negotiating with it, taking what was offered, moving when the vegetation shifted, accepting impermanence as a condition of existence.

Some structures are older than others. You can tell by the degree of absorption—the almost-structures that are more plant than building now, green ghosts of what was, their forms visible only as slightly different densities in the surrounding mass. Others are newer, still maintained, smoke-stains on the fire-pit, fresh-cut stems where the growth was pushed back. Someone is here. Not now, perhaps not this season, but recently. The plateau is not entirely uninhabited.

---

The rules of this place emerge slowly, learned through experience rather than instruction.

**Do not run.** Running attracts attention, and attention, in a place this dense with life, means too many eyes tracking you, too many creatures curious about the sudden movement. Walk slowly. Stop often. Let yourself be boring.

**Follow the water.** The depressions collect condensation and rain, forming temporary pools that drain slowly. These are the centers of activity—the clearest spaces, the most diverse animal life, the places where insects gather in thick clouds above the surface. But they are also the easiest places to find, the navigable landmarks in a landscape without them.

**The soundscape is information.** The constant noise is not random. It carries patterns—warnings about predators, indications of water, signals about weather changes moving across the plateau. Listen long enough and you begin to parse it, the insect choir separating into distinct voices, the bird calls resolving into meanings. It is overwhelming at first, deafening, a wall of sound that defeats analysis. But it becomes readable. It has to.

**The vegetation is the map.** Different growth means different conditions, and different conditions mean different resources. Taller growth with darker leaves indicates deeper soil, probably a former depression now filled. Brighter, shorter growth suggests rock close to the surface, or an area that floods regularly. The colors shift through the day as leaves adjust their angle; track the shifts and you track the passage of time.

**Nothing is permanent.** The structures, the paths, the clearings—all of them will change, will be absorbed, will become something else. The plateau does not hold still. It grows. It dies. It grows again. To be here is to be in motion even when standing still, carried forward on the living substrate, the slow turnover of biomass, the seasons measured in growth rather than temperature.

---

The light holds at a particular quality for most of the day—filtered, warm, green-tinted, like being inside a leaf. Direct sun passes through in shafts where the canopy thins, creating pillars of brightness that insects swarm through, visible as movement within the light itself. Shadows are soft, diffuse, green. Time of day is readable not in shadow angle but in color temperature: morning is cool and blue-green, midday bright and yellow-green, evening warm and orange-green, night a different thing entirely, the darkness broken by bioluminescence—fungi, insects, the occasional flower that glows to attract moths.

---

The weirdness is not dramatic. This is not a place of impossible geometry or unnatural physics. It is simply a place where life has won, completely and totally, where the balance has tipped so far toward biology that geology becomes secondary. The strangeness is in the totality, the sense of walking on something alive, the knowledge that the ground beneath your feet is not ground at all but accumulated centuries of growth and death and growth again. It is the uncanny valley of landscape—familiar enough to be recognizable, but wrong in ways that take time to articulate.

The plateau is patient. It has existed in something close to this form for longer than recorded history, worn down and built up, eroded and regrown, a cycle so slow it feels like stasis. Everything here has adapted to everything else, the interdependencies so complex that removing any single element might cause cascading failure—or might be absorbed, compensated for, the system adjusting around the absence.

You are temporary. The plateau knows this. The insects land on you, taste you, move on. The birds watch you and lose interest. The vegetation begins to grow over your footprints before you have finished making them. You are here, and then you will not be here, and the plateau will continue its slow breathing, its green photosynthesis, its deafening chorus of life talking to life about life.

---

The moisture returns as evening comes. Condensation forms on every surface, dripping from leaves, pooling in the crooks of branches, beading on skin. The temperature drops slightly—not cold, but cooler, the warmth of the day releasing slowly from the biomass. The insects shift their chorus. The birds give their final calls. The herds in the distance begin to settle. The predators start to move.

And through it all, the sound. The endless, overwhelming, layered, living sound. Deafening in volume and meaning both. A landscape speaking its own name, over and over, in a language that takes a lifetime to learn.

This is the High Everplain. And it will be here, more or less, long after you stop being its witness.
