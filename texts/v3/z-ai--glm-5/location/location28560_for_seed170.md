# The Veldt of Living Engines

## First Approach

The heat comes up before the light does.

Not rises — *comes up*, as if from below, as if pushed. The ground here is warm in the way a body is warm: from the inside. You stand on it and feel your boots grow uncomfortable hot within minutes and you think, distantly, that this must be what an oven floor feels like to bread. The sensation is not metaphor. The ground is cooking something out of itself, slowly, over centuries, and you are standing in the exhaust.

This is a high place. The air is thin enough that the sky appears not blue but a kind of blanched bone-white, as though the atmosphere gave up its color along with its density. You can see a great distance — the plateau extends in every direction, flat in some places, rolling in others, broken by long shallow ridges that run parallel to each other like the grain of old wood. Wood is wrong. There is no wood here. There has never been wood here. The ridges are mineral. Everything is mineral.

The scale of it presses against you. Open country. Regional, at least — you can see for tens of kilometers in any direction, and the far horizon shimmers but does not waver. The land has been here long enough to have settled into its own shape. Nothing is being torn down. Nothing is being built up. The erosion patterns are complete, stable, the way a riverbed is stable even after the river is gone — the form remains as a record, a fossil of process.

There is no water. You understand this not as an observation but as a condition, the way a locked door is a condition. The salts here are not the kind that dissolve. They are the kind that form. The ground underfoot is a dense, pale crust — calcium-analogue compounds, sulfates, dry silicates pressed into a surface like unfinished ceramic. It crunches faintly when you walk, and the crunch is one of the only sounds that belongs to you.

The other sounds do not belong to you. The other sounds belong to the ground.

## The Engines

Listen.

A low vibration comes first, felt in the feet before it reaches the ears. A sustained, tectonic hum that has no beginning and no end — it simply exists as a baseline condition, the way gravity exists. Underneath the hum, or perhaps woven through it, are rhythmic pulses: deep, mechanical, patient. Throb. Throb. Throb. Like an enormous heart made of stone and metal, beating somewhere far below. The interval between pulses is roughly four seconds. You count. You stop counting because it doesn't matter. It has been doing this for longer than anything you can conceive of and it will continue long after you leave.

The plateau is a field of natural engines.

This is the strangest thing, and it takes time to see it, because the strangeness is not in any single feature but in the cumulative recognition of what the features *are*. You notice a low mound fifty meters to the east — a blister of dark, ferric stone, perhaps three meters across, with a hairline crack running along its crown. From the crack, a thin column of steam rises. Not steam — vaporized mineral compounds, superheated and vented. The vapor is colorless, but where the light catches it, it refracts into a faint amber sheen, and it smells like nothing so much as hot iron and struck flint. You notice another mound to the north. Another to the southwest. They are everywhere, spread across the plateau at irregular intervals, hundreds of them, perhaps thousands, each one venting its slow mineral breath into the thin air.

They are not built. Nothing here is built. There are no structures, no artifacts, no sign that any hand or mind ever shaped this place. The mounds are geological — or they are something that has no name yet, something between geology and biology, something that the word *geology* was never meant to cover. They are formations, yes. But they function. Each one is a system: intake, processing, exhaust. The internal heat of the plateau — conducted upward through mineral strata, pressurized in sealed chambers below the surface — drives chemical reactions that convert solid compounds to vapor, venting them through surface fractures. The cycle is slow. The cycle is enormously, incomprehensibly slow. A single mound may process for ten thousand years before its mineral fuel is spent and it goes dormant, cooling, settling, becoming just another ridge in the grain of the land.

This is industry without industry. Production without product. Engines that no one built, running on no one's schedule, producing nothing that anyone will use. They are the plateau's respiration. They are what this place does instead of weather.

And the sound — the sound is the aggregate of all of them. The hum of superheated mineral vapor passing through stone channels. The pulse of pressure building and releasing in deep chambers. The crackle of thermal expansion in the surface crust. Thousands of systems, each barely audible on its own, combining into a sustained, thick, physical sound that fills the air from the ground to the sky. It is loud. Not painfully loud, not deafening, but loud the way the ocean is loud — a constant, saturating presence that crowds out silence and makes it impossible to hear yourself think until you stop trying to think and let the sound become the environment.

You stop trying to think. The sound becomes the environment. It is easier this way.

## The First Signs

You have been walking for twenty minutes before you notice the animals.

This is not because they are rare. It is because they are the same color as the ground.

The first one you see is a flat, pale shape — perhaps the size of a dinner plate, perhaps larger, it is hard to tell — moving slowly across the mineral crust about thirty meters to your left. It moves the way a shadow moves: not by walking, not by slithering, but by some method that produces no sound and almost no visible displacement. You watch it and realize you cannot identify its edges. It is the same color as the substrate, the same texture, and where its body meets the ground, the boundary between organism and surface is not clear. It could be a single creature. It could be a colony. It could be a fragment of the crust that has detached and is moving on its own for reasons that no taxonomy has ever described.

You look down. There is one near your foot. You did not hear it approach. It is the size of your hand, flat, mineral-textured, slightly warm to the proximity — you can feel radiant heat from it, or perhaps you imagine this. It has no visible mouth, no eyes, no features of any kind. It is a patch of ground that is alive.

You look up and the landscape rearranges itself.

## The Fauna

They are everywhere.

The realization arrives the way a hidden image in a puzzle arrives — suddenly, completely, all at once. The ground is covered with them. Not in the sense of a swarm, not in the sense of infestation, but in the sense that the ground *is* them, that the mineral crust you have been walking on is not a surface they inhabit but a surface they *comprise*. The pale ceramic crunch under your boots is the shed material of their bodies. The ridges are their pathways, worn smooth by the passage of countless generations. The mounds — the engine-mounds — are not separate from them. The mounds are where they congregate.

You stand still and let your eyes adjust, and the plateau comes alive.

The creatures — you cannot call them anything else, though the word feels inadequate — exist across a range of sizes that defies easy categorization. The smallest are the hand-sized patches, barely distinguishable from the substrate. The largest are impossible to fully see. They are the size of buildings, of geological features, of low hills. The large ones are partially submerged, their bulk extending below the surface crust, only their dorsal surfaces visible as long, low, mineral-encrusted rises in the terrain. You have been walking on them. You realize this with a feeling that is not quite horror, not quite awe — something in between, something that has no word because no language was designed for the experience of discovering you have been standing on a living thing the size of a city block without knowing it.

They are overwhelming in number. Not dense — the parameter of emptiness holds. They do not crowd. There are vast stretches between them, open mineral crust, empty and silent except for the engine-hum. But the total number, integrated across the visible landscape, is staggering. They extend to the horizon in every direction, their pale bodies merging visually with the ground until the distinction between *life* and *ground* becomes philosophical rather than empirical.

And they are moving. Slowly. So slowly that you must watch one for a full minute to confirm it. The large ones are nearly static — their movement is geological, perceptible perhaps over days or weeks. The small ones are faster, but even the fastest would take several minutes to cross a meter of open ground. They are patient organisms. They operate on a timescale that makes the engine-mounds seem hasty.

What do they eat? The question arises and then dissolves, because *eating* is the wrong framework. They are not consuming. They are *processing*. The mineral compounds in the crust — the sulfates, the silicates, the ferric salts — pass through their bodies the way water passes through a sponge. They metabolize the heat. They metabolize the mineral vapor that vents from the engine-mounds. They metabolize something else, something you cannot see or measure, something that the engines produce as a byproduct that is not mineral and not thermal and not any category you have a name for.

You are certain of this because the air near the largest concentrations feels different. Not hotter, not denser, but *altered* — as though the physics of the local space has been slightly rewritten, as though the rules that govern how matter behaves have been loosened a fraction. This is the first sign of the breakdown.

## The Breakdown

You first notice it at the edge of your vision.

To the northeast, perhaps a kilometer away, a cluster of engine-mounds is venting with unusual intensity. The mineral vapor rises in columns that twist, and the twist does not obey the wind — there is no wind. The columns twist because the space they are rising through is not uniform. The vapor moves through air, and then it moves through air that is not quite air, and then it moves through something that has no name, and the transitions between these states cause the column to distort. You watch the distortion and feel your eyes attempt to correct it, to explain it as a mirage, as heat shimmer, as any familiar phenomenon. Your eyes fail. It is not any familiar phenomenon.

The ground near the cluster is wrong. Not broken, not damaged — *wrong*. The mineral crust, which elsewhere is a continuous pale surface, here shows gaps, and through the gaps you can see not deeper strata but a kind of darkness that is not the absence of light. It is the absence of *context*. Looking into the gaps is like looking at a word in a language you don't speak — the shape is there, the form is legible, but the meaning is not available to you. The darkness in the gaps is a place where meaning has withdrawn.

The large animals near the cluster have congregated. Six, seven, perhaps more of the building-sized creatures have gathered in a rough circle around the venting mounds, their bodies touching at the edges, their mineral surfaces merging and separating in slow, tidal cycles. As you watch — and you watch for a long time, because the slowness of it becomes hypnotic, the sound and the heat and the wrongness combining into a state that is not quite trance — you see that where their bodies meet, the boundary between them does not simply blur. It *exchanges*. Material from one creature's surface passes into the other's, and material from the other passes back, and in the zone of exchange, new structures form: small, intricate, crystalline lattices that look like nothing the crust produces on its own. These structures persist for a few seconds, then dissolve back into the parent bodies.

This is how they reproduce. You are almost certain. Not by division, not by budding, but by *conversation* — by the slow, mineralogical exchange of pattern between individuals, generating novelty in the contact zone. The engine-mounds provide the energy. The breakdown in local reality provides the *permission* — the loosening of physical constraint that allows new forms to emerge from the exchange.

You understand, suddenly and uncomfortably, that the reality breakdown is not a malfunction. It is not damage. It is a feature. The engines produce it. The animals gather around it. The loosening of physics is what makes their world possible. Without it, they would be static — mineral formations, nothing more. With it, they are alive. They have been alive, in this way, for longer than the plateau has existed in its current form. They are the reason the plateau exists in its current form. They built the landscape the way coral builds a reef — slowly, generation by generation, through the accumulation of their own processed material, their shed skins, their metabolic waste, the crystalline structures that form at their boundaries and then detach and settle and become ground.

The plateau is a reef. The engines are its geothermal vents. The animals are its coral. The reality breakdown is its ocean.

## Deeper In

You walk toward the cluster. You are not sure this is wise, but the landscape does not offer a concept of wisdom, only a concept of direction, and you choose northeast.

The heat increases as you approach. Not gradually but in steps — distinct thresholds where the temperature jumps by several degrees and the mineral crust changes color, deepening from pale cream to a dry, rusted amber. The sound changes too: the engine-hum thickens, gains harmonics, becomes less a drone and more a chord, as though the vents near the cluster are resonating with each other in a frequency relationship that your ears register as almost musical. Almost. The intervals are wrong. The notes do not correspond to any scale you know. They correspond to something else, to a mathematics that operates on principles your auditory cortex can detect but your consciousness cannot parse.

The animals are denser here. The small ones — the hand-sized patches — are everywhere, covering the crust in a near-continuous layer, and you realize with a fresh shock of recognition that the ground you are walking on is not crust at all. It is them. You are walking on a living carpet of these creatures, their flat, mineral bodies packed edge to edge, and the crunch under your boots is not ceramic but chitin-analogue, not geology but biology. You stop. You lift your foot. The creature beneath your boot is unharmed — its body is as hard as the crust it mimics, and your weight means nothing to it. You are not a threat to them. You are not a meaningful physical event.

This should be comforting. It is not. It is the opposite. To be irrelevant to the organisms around you is to be reminded that your frame of reference — the one in which human beings are significant agents — does not apply here. This is their world. You are a trace element.

The large animals near the cluster are even more disturbing up close. Their surfaces are not smooth. Up close, you can see that the mineral coating is covered with fine, intricate structures — ridges, channels, pits, all on a scale too small to see from a distance but too regular to be natural erosion. They look like the cooling fins of a machine. They look like the intake valves of an engine. They look like the surface of a brain, folded and creased, and you realize with a chill that you cannot tell which of these analogies is most accurate because all of them might be simultaneously true. The large animals may be engines themselves. The engines may be animals. The distinction between the geological and the biological may have collapsed here, not because the words are inadequate but because the reality they describe does not contain the distinction.

## The Center

There is a center to the cluster. You find it by walking inward, toward the loudest sound and the deepest wrongness, and eventually you arrive at a place where the engine-mounds are so close together that their vents merge into a single, continuous exhalation of mineral vapor, rising in a column that is not a column but a wall, a curtain of superheated gas that extends upward until it vanishes into the bone-white sky.

At the base of the curtain, the ground is open.

Not cracked. Not broken. *Open* — in the way that a door is open, in the way that a book is open, in the way that a question is open. The mineral crust has parted, and beneath it there is a space, and in that space there is—

You cannot describe it. Not because it is indescribable, but because the act of describing it requires a relationship between language and reality that does not hold here. You can say that there is light. You can say that the light is the wrong color — a green that exists outside the visible spectrum, a sound that your eyes register, a texture that your ears parse. You can say that the space extends downward beyond your ability to perceive depth, and that in the depths, things are moving. Large things. Larger than the building-sized animals on the surface. Much larger. Things that have been there, in that space, processing and producing and existing, for time periods that the word *ancient* does not begin to touch.

You can say that the reality breakdown is strongest here. That the rules of physics are not merely loosened but actively suspended, and that what replaces them is not chaos but a different set of rules, a different physics, one that operates on the material of this place — mineral, heat, and the incomprehensible byproduct of the engines — with the same steady, patient consistency that physics operates on your own world. This is not the absence of order. This is a different order. An order that you are not built to perceive, the way the hand-sized animals are not built to perceive you.

You stand at the edge of the open ground and you listen. The sound here is total. It is not loud in the way that a machine is loud or a storm is loud. It is loud in the way that existence is loud — it is the sound of matter being processed, of energy being transferred, of reality being *manufactured*, and it fills every frequency you can hear and many that you cannot, and it is not going to stop.

It has never stopped.

## What Remains

You leave. You walk back the way you came, across the living carpet of small animals, across the empty mineral stretches, across the ridges that are the worn pathways of a biology you do not understand. The sound diminishes but does not disappear. It will not disappear until you have left the plateau entirely, and even then you will hear it in moments of quiet — a faint hum at the edge of perception, a pulse every four seconds, a reminder that the engines are still running, that the animals are still processing, that the world beneath the world is still open and still producing its impossible order, slow and patient and vast and completely indifferent to whether you are there to witness it.

The plateau does not change as you leave. It has no reason to. It is in balance — has been in balance for longer than balance has had a name. The engines run. The animals gather. The ground opens. The rules bend and hold and bend and hold. The heat comes up from below.

It will be here when you are gone. It was here before you came. It is not for you.

You understand this now, and the understanding sits in you like a mineral deposit — slow, dense, and permanent.
