# The Hollow

The first thing is the light.

It falls from somewhere you cannot see—a great wound in the ceiling of the world, far above, where the rock gave up and opened to sky. The beam slants down through the chamber's single vast emptiness, a column of pale gold that seems to hold the space together by pure presence. Without it, this would be a tomb. With it, the Hollow becomes a temple without doctrine.

You are below the surface. The walls curve upward in smooth sheets of limestone, water-shaped across millennia, their surfaces worn into something like frozen waves. The erosion here is old, settled, patient. This is not a place that changes anymore; it has become what it will remain. Drip marks trace vertical paths down the stone where condensation gathers and releases, gathers and releases, in cycles too slow to watch. If you pressed your palm flat against the rock, you would feel dampness—the mineral skin of a creature that breathes once per century.

The scale is difficult to judge. The Hollow is perhaps the size of a cathedral nave, perhaps larger; the emptiness plays tricks. Distance stretches here. A pebble dropped on the far side might land in perfect silence, the air too still to carry sound. There is almost nothing in the space—a scattering of pale rocks, a fallen stalactite shattered into segments, a few dark pools no wider than your hand gathered in low places where the condensation runs. That is all. The density is near-zero. The emptiness itself has presence.

And yet.

The light falls, and in its path, things live.

They come from the shadows at the chamber's edges. First you notice movement—a stirring in the crevices where rock meets rock, where darkness pools against the walls. Then they emerge. The fauna of the Hollow.

They are small. Most are pale, almost translucent, their bodies shaped by generations spent far from true sun. There are flat many-legged things that move like water spilling across stone; there are delicate aerialists with wings like cellophane that drift up into the light column and catch the glow; there are blind burrowers that surface in the damp pockets near the pools, their faces a mess of sensitive bristles. They do not fight. They do not hurry. In this small world, they have found equilibrium. The ecosystem is too sparse for competition; there is enough for all because there is so little demand.

One creature captures your attention. It perches on a jut of stone near the light's edge—a thing like a frog, but wrong. Its forelimbs are elongated, almost hand-like, and it uses them to manipulate a shard of limestone, turning it over, scraping at its surface. You realize it is shaping the stone, thinning one edge, creating something that might be a tool. The behavior is primitive, patient, instinctive. The creature does not understand what it does, not in any way you would recognize, but it does the work anyway, guided by some ancient imperative. When the shard is sharp enough, it uses the edge to pry open a crack in the rock, revealing pale fungal threads that it consumes with slow precision.

Nearby, others of its kind practice the same ritual. They have no structures, no shelters, no culture you can discern. They exist in the moment of their tool-use, in the damp and the light, and then they return to stillness.

The sound is minimal. A faint wet clicking when the tool-users work; the rustle of cellophane wings; the occasional plink of condensation releasing from the ceiling. No wind. No distant water. The silence has texture, a softness like felt.

The warmth settles around you. It rises from the stone itself—geothermal heat bleeding upward through the limestone, held in by the enclosed space, kept gentle by the vast emptiness that allows it to diffuse. The temperature has been stable for longer than any measuring. The Hollow exists in its own climate, sealed away from the weather above, touched only by the light and the slow breath of the earth.

You notice now that the light column moves. Not obviously—nothing so violent as a sunbeam tracking across a floor—but over hours, as the sun above traverses its arc, the golden blade sweeps laterally across the chamber. It is moving now, brushing toward a cluster of vegetation you had not registered. The plants are sparse, pale, their leaves broad and translucent to catch whatever illumination reaches them. They cling to a ledge where condensation pools, a garden perhaps six feet across, and as the light reaches them, you see them turn. The movement is slow—imperceptible unless you watch for long minutes—but it is real. They track the sun. They have learned to anticipate it.

The strange thought occurs: this is not a cave. Not really. This is a wound that healed into something else. The collapse that opened the ceiling was violence, but the life that followed found ways to exist in the aftermath. The tool-users, the light-tracking plants, the aerialists who ride the warm air rising from the stone—none of this was meant to be here. And yet the Hollow holds them all, has held them for unknowable time, in balance so deep it has become geology.

The light begins to shift toward amber as afternoon advances somewhere far above. In a few hours, it will fade entirely, and the Hollow will return to its natural state: true dark, true quiet, a space defined by absence. The fauna will retreat to their crevices. The tools will rest on the stone. Nothing will change until the light returns.

This is the way of things here. Slow cycles. Patient processes. A single room at the center of the world, almost empty, almost silent, bright for a fraction of each rotation, and utterly, profoundly itself.
