# The Rendering Floors

A complex of three low buildings connected by covered walkways, sitting in a shallow depression where the land dips toward what was once a river. The river is gone now, or rather, it has been replaced by something else. The buildings are concrete and corrugated steel, windowless, painted a green-gray that has faded to the color of wet bone. They have been here for a long time. Decades at least. Possibly longer.

---

The moisture comes up through the floor, mostly. Condensation forms on the undersides of the concrete slabs and drips into the crawl spaces, where it pools and stagnates and slowly evaporates back up through cracks, maintaining a constant dampness that never quite becomes wet. The air tastes mineral. Iron and calcium and something else, something organic but not vegetal — there are no plants here, have never been plants here, will never be plants here. The place exists outside the green world entirely.

Rain falls sometimes, and the water moves through the buildings in predictable patterns. Down the interior walls, across the sloped floors, into the drainage channels cut into the concrete, and out through the grates into the settling ponds outside. The system was designed for this. It still works. Everything here still works.

---

The creatures came later, or they were always here and simply became what they are now. Nobody knows anymore. Nobody remembers who built this place or what it was meant to do. The machines run because they run. The creatures stay because they stay. The noise is constant.

It is a particular kind of loud — a textured loud. Layers of it. The deep rhythmic thudding of the rendering presses on the bottom floor, operating at thirty-two cycles per minute, every minute, every hour, every day. The high whine of the conveyor belts carrying material through the ceiling apertures between floors. The wet clicking sounds from the settling ponds outside where the moisture collects. And beneath all of it, the chittering.

The chittering is the creatures. They fill this place. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, maybe more — they move through the ceiling spaces and the crawl spaces and the spaces between machines, pale and smooth and eyeless, the size of large rats but longer in the body, with too many joints in their legs. They do not behave like ordinary animals. They move in synchronized waves, the entire population flowing in the same direction at once, then stopping, then flowing again, as if responding to a signal that only they can perceive. Their chittering rises and falls with the machine cycles, and it is impossible to tell whether they are imitating the machines or the machines are imitating them.

---

The three buildings are not identical. The first, the largest, houses the rendering presses. Massive iron frames bolted into the concrete, each one surrounding a hydraulic piston that descends and rises, descends and rises, pressing whatever is fed into them through the fine mesh screens at the bottom. The material that comes out is a thick gray paste, uniform in texture, which moves along the conveyor belts to the second building.

The second building is where the paste is dried. Long racks of heated trays, steam rising from them constantly, the air thick and warm despite the general coolness of the complex. The dried flakes fall into collection bins that are never emptied because the creatures eat them. This is what they eat. This is what they have always eaten. The system sustains itself.

The third building is where it goes quiet.

---

The third building is not silent. Silence would imply an absence of sound, and the third building has plenty of sound — the hum of electrical transformers, the distant vibration of the machines in the other buildings, the settling noises of the structure itself. But the presses do not run here. The conveyors do not carry material. The creatures do not enter this space. They stop at the threshold between the second and third building and will not cross it. They crowd against the doorway, filling it with their pale bodies, pressing against each other in their endless synchronized motion, but they do not enter.

The third building is where the control room is. Or what was the control room. A raised platform with a curved desk facing a wall of gauges and switches, most of which no longer function. The needles on the functioning gauges hold steady. They have held steady for as long as anyone can remember. The same pressure, the same temperature, the same flow rate, the same humidity. Nothing changes.

There is a chair on the platform. A standard industrial chair, metal frame, worn padding, adjustable height. It has been there so long that the floor has settled around its feet, concrete flowing like slow ice, and now the chair cannot be moved without breaking the floor itself. It faces the gauges. It waits.

---

Nobody sits in the chair now. Nobody comes to read the gauges or throw the switches or watch the presses through the observation windows. The system runs itself. The creatures eat what the system produces. The moisture collects and evaporates and collects again. The noise fills the air like a physical presence, dense and resonant, a sound you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. The place has reached equilibrium. It does not need anyone.

The creatures know every inch of the first two buildings. They have worn paths into the concrete floors, shallow grooves where thousands of bodies have passed over thousands of days, following the same routes, the same rhythms. They do not fight each other. They do not mate, as far as anyone can tell, but they do not die either — or rather, when they die, their bodies are carried by the others to the input chutes of the rendering presses, and they become part of the gray paste, and the system continues. Nothing is wasted. Nothing changes.

---

Outside, the settling ponds stretch across the low ground, three rectangular basins lined with concrete, filled with still water that reflects the gray sky. The surface is covered with a film of something that is not algae, not moss, not any plant — a bacterial mat, perhaps, or a biofilm of some kind, smooth and faintly iridescent, rippling when the wind touches it. The water smells of iron and something sweeter, something that might be pleasing in a different context but here reads as wrong.

The creatures drink from the ponds. They come out through small openings in the building walls and cluster at the edges, dipping their heads below the film, drinking in the same synchronized way they do everything else — all at once, then stopping, then all at once again. It is not a natural rhythm. Natural rhythms vary, hesitate, respond to conditions. This rhythm is mechanical. They drink at intervals determined by something other than thirst.

---

There is a kind of beauty here, if you can see it. The symmetry of the system, the elegant balance of production and consumption, the way every element has found its place and holds it. The buildings settle into the earth. The machines wear into their patterns. The creatures become what the system requires them to be. Everything is accounted for. Nothing is wasted. The noise becomes a kind of silence, after a while — a silence that contains sound, a stillness that contains motion. The place has become what it was always trying to be.

But it is not a human place anymore. It may never have been. The chair sits empty. The gauges hold steady. The presses descend and rise, descend and rise, thirty-two times per minute, every minute, every hour, every day, in the cool damp mineral air, under the constant chittering of the creatures, in a room where no plants grow and no plant ever will.
