# The Plateau of Engines

The dark here has texture. It presses against the eyes like felt, dense and fibrous, warmed by something below. Not the warmth of sun on skin — this is a slower heat, patient, rising through stone from depths where pressure still remembers when the world was soft.

Stand still and the plateau extends in all directions, flat as a sentence that has finished being spoken. The elevation registers first in the lungs — air thin enough that breath comes deliberate, conscious, each inhalation a small decision. This high, with no water anywhere, with nothing growing, the body notices its own moisture. Lips crack within hours. The inside of the nose stings. The plateau takes what it needs and offers nothing back.

**The city** — if a scattering of structures across a hundred square kilometers can be called a city — sprawls across this ancient flatness in patterns that suggest purpose without revealing it. No walls, no clear boundaries. The buildings rise like isolated thoughts, each one separated from its nearest neighbor by distances measured in long minutes of walking. Between them: nothing. Compacted dust, mineral-hard, unmarked by wind or water. The erosion rating tells the truth — this place reached its final form so long ago that even the concept of change seems foreign here. Nothing has moved in geological ages. The dust lies exactly where it settled.

The structures themselves defy easy categorization. Industrial, certainly — the bones of mechanization run through them like metal marrow. Pipes that could be circled by both arms. Exhaust stacks wide enough to walk inside, their interiors blackened by processes that stopped before the current silence began. The technology speaks of engines, of factories, of production at a scale that required this vast horizontal sprawl. Whatever was built here was built in quantities that suggest a world's appetite.

But no lights. The darkness parameter anchors everything. Even at nominal noon — if such a concept exists here — the light never rises above a deep twilight. Perhaps the plateau sits beneath an eternal haze layer. Perhaps this world orbits something dim, or nothing at all. The buildings themselves emit no illumination. No lamps, no windows glowing, no indicator lights on machines that might still hum with power. If electricity moves through the industrial veins of this place, it does so invisibly, warming the pipes but providing no photons to the eye.

**The warmth in the dark** becomes the primary sensation. Tactile, not visual. The buildings radiate heat — not the sharp burn of active industry, but the steady seep of ancient systems in equilibrium. Thermal inertia. Something deep below, perhaps a formation of radioactive minerals, perhaps a geological process too slow to name, keeps the foundations warm. Heat rises through the structures and bleeds into the air. On this high plateau, under whatever dims the sky, the warmth feels almost protective. A mothering quality, though nothing maternal ever existed here.

Sound: almost nothing. The acoustic ecology of this place has settled into a silence so complete that the human ear invents tones to fill it. A high whine, maybe, or the pulse of one's own blood. But external sound is rare here. The air moves so slowly, the distances between surfaces so vast, that even if wind existed — and it barely does — it would have nothing to whistle through, nothing to push against. The buildings stand too far apart to create wind tunnels or echo chambers. They simply stand.

Which raises questions that the sparse density refuses to answer: Why build this way? What purpose demanded such isolation between structures? Were they once connected by something — cables, bridges, lines of transport — that has since been removed or consumed? The erosion parameter suggests removal is impossible. Nothing changes here. So the isolation was original. Intentional.

**The weirdness** accumulates in details.

The buildings, for all their industrial character, have no doors. Not in the conventional sense. Openings exist — ragged apertures at various heights, some suggesting entrances, others leading nowhere — but no frames, no thresholds, no evidence that anything ever opened or closed. The spaces are simply there, cut into the material, or perhaps formed that way. The construction technology, whatever it was, didn't operate on the logic of entry and exit.

And the material itself — not stone, not metal, not concrete. Something between organic and mineral. The warmth affects it. Over long periods, the substance flows, slowly, like glass over centuries, but faster. A building might subtly reshape itself across decades. Not erosion — this place is beyond erosion — but flow. Internal redistribution. A pipe that once angled sharply might now curve. A stack that once rose vertical might lean by almost imperceptible degrees. The dynamic parameter says this place never changes, and it's true in the short term. But over spans longer than human observation, everything here migrates, settles, rearranges. The plateau is a slow liquid. The city is a viscous ocean of structures.

**The fauna parameter** — rare — manifests in a single organism type, if organisms they are.

Deep in the warmest structures, where heat concentrates, something moves. Not often. Perhaps once per solar cycle, or once per some longer rhythm the plateau recognizes. The sound of movement — a scraping, deliberate and heavy — emerges from within the industrial architecture. Whatever makes the sound stays hidden, deep inside, where the heat forms pools. They've never been seen clearly. The darkness and the sparse arrangement mean that when one moves, there's no vantage point close enough to observe.

Theoretical constructions: they might be machines that have outlasted their builders, still executing maintenance cycles on systems that no longer need maintenance. They might be biological descendants of workers, evolved across millions of years to live in the dark and warmth, processing something — the thermal energy itself, perhaps, or minerals dissolved from the walls. They might be something else entirely, native to this world, using the structures as shells, as hosts, as homes they didn't build and can't understand but nonetheless occupy.

Their rarity is absolute. Walking for days across the plateau, a visitor might encounter no sign of them. But the sound, when it comes, confirms presence. Life. Or life-adjacent. Something that exists without water, without plants, without light, sustained only by warmth and mineral and time.

**The rule** of this place, if rules can be derived from landscape, is equilibrium. The plateau exists in a state so stable that it borders on death, yet the warmth moves, the structures slowly flow, the hidden fauna persist. It's not dead. It's not alive. It's something else — a mode of being that the standard categories don't accommodate. This is a world that has found its final form and settled into it, comfortable in the dark, warm from below, unchanging across time scales that make mountains seem transient.

Visitors, if visitors ever came, would face a choice. Leave within hours, before the body's moisture depletes. Or stay. Commit to the plateau's logic, find a warm structure, let the darkness become the only visibility, listen for the rare sound of hidden movement in the pipes. Let the lungs acclimate to the thin air. Let the skin acclimate to the dry. Let the mind acclimate to the absence of change. The plateau offers nothing and takes slowly, over time, as everything here takes — by persistence, by being what it is, by having been this way long enough that resistance becomes meaningless.

In the end, the city on the plateau might be understood as a kind of monument to equilibrium. Not a memorial to what was, but a testament to what persists when all other possibilities have been exhausted. The industrial technology isn't abandoned. It simply finished. The structures aren't empty. They're complete. The darkness isn't oppressive. It's the natural state. The warmth doesn't threaten. It welcomes — slowly, patiently, as everything here is slow and patient, to a form of existence that the rest of the universe might not recognize as existence at all.

This is the Plateau of Engines. It was here before you arrived. It will be here after. The only question that matters is how long you're willing to stand in its dark, breathing its thin dry air, feeling its patient heat rise through your feet, listening for whatever moves in the deep warm spaces.
