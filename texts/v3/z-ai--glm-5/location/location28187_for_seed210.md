# The Engines of the High Plateau

## First Approach

The path—if it can be called that—rises sharply through air that tastes of copper and ozone. Somewhere around three thousand meters, the trees change. They become something else entirely.

This is a young landscape. The rock beneath your feet is basalt and something darker, something that shouldn't be stone at all, and it breaks in facets rather than weathering smooth. The cliffs ahead look like they were sliced yesterday, or perhaps ten thousand years ago—time has a way of refusing to commit here. The sharp edges catch water that seeps from within the stone itself, weeping from horizontal fissures at regular intervals, as if the mountain were built in layers with plumbing already installed.

No one built this place. That's what makes it wrong.

---

## The Clearing

You emerge onto the plateau expecting a view. What you find is a room.

Not a constructed room—there are no walls, no ceiling, no architecture of any kind—but the space declares itself with the authority of a cathedral. The boundary is felt before it's seen: a change in the quality of air, a thickness, a sense that you have entered somewhere rather than arrived somewhere. The scale is intimate despite the elevation. Perhaps forty meters across. Perhaps infinite. The two measurements somehow coexist.

The light is perpetual dusk. Not the dusk of evening, but the dusk of a room with drawn curtains, the color of old photograph paper. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. Clouds hang below the plateau's edge, and sometimes they drift through the space like smoke, but they never obscure the dim light because the light isn't coming from the sky.

It's emanating from the vegetation.

---

## The Growth

Each plant stands alone.

This is the first strangeness of many: the density is both lush and sparse, a paradox that shouldn't hold but does. Ferns rise from the wet black stone in perfect isolation, each one separated from its neighbors by meters of bare ground, as if they've agreed to give each other space. Mosses coat the rocks in patches with crisp edges, like countries on a map. Nothing crowds. Nothing touches.

But within their isolation, the plants are extravagant. The ferns are tall as a person, their fronds dripping with moisture that isn't quite water—it's thicker, faintly luminescent, and it runs down the stems in deliberate streams rather than random rivulets. The moss glows with that same gray-green light, just bright enough to see by, just dim enough to feel like memory rather than presence.

And everywhere, the water. It doesn't flow so much as express itself. It beads on every surface, pools in the sharp-edged hollows the young stone provides, rises from below through those evenly-spaced fissures. The air is saturated. You breathe in water and breathe out something that tastes different. Condensation forms on your skin immediately, but it doesn't roll or drip—it stays, as if your body has become just another surface for the plateau's slow expression of itself.

The temperature hovers at a level that bypasses sensation. Not cold enough to shiver, not warm enough to comfort. A temperature that erases itself, that makes you realize you've stopped noticing temperature entirely.

---

## The Machinery

The sounds begin before you understand what you're hearing.

A low, rhythmic thrumming. The bass-note rumble of enormous engines turning over somewhere far below. The hiss and clank of pistons. The shriek of metal under stress, though no metal is visible. The sounds are loud—not painfully so, but present, insistent, as if they're trying to tell you something in a language you almost recognize.

Because there is no machinery here.

Or rather, the machinery is the place itself.

Look closely at the stone and you see it: the basalt is streaked with threads of something that looks like iron but flows like water when you don't watch it directly. The weeping fissures aren't natural cracks—they're too regular, too purposeful, opening and closing by millimeters with the rhythm of the subterranean engines you can hear but cannot find. The vegetation isn't growing; it's being *produced*. The ferns unfurl in increments you can almost perceive, their movements synchronized with the clanking rhythm, as if they're components of something larger that's assembling itself very slowly.

This is industry without industry. Mechanization without makers. The plateau is a factory that produces only itself, and has done so forever, and will continue doing so forever, because the concept of "before" and "after" has no purchase here.

---

## The Inhabitants

The animals are what finally break you.

They move through the cathedral-space with the confidence of creatures who belong. You see them clearly despite the dimness—that gray-green glow provides enough light for seeing, once your eyes adjust to its strangeness. There are so many. Too many for a space this size, except that they don't crowd either, because nothing touches anything here.

Small mammals that resemble shrews but move with mechanical precision, their legs pumping like pistons, their whiskers vibrating at frequencies that harmonize with the engine-sounds. Beetles with carapaces that look like stamped metal, crawling in straight lines across the wet stone, following invisible tracks. Birds that might be birds—they have wings, they fly—but their motion is wrong, too steady, their bodies emitting a faint clicking that syncs with the clanking below.

A deer-like creature stands at the clearing's edge, drinking from a pool that forms and reforms as you watch. Its legs are jointed in places legs shouldn't joint. Its eyes reflect nothing. When it raises its head, water streams from its mouth in a continuous thread, unbroken, connecting it to the pool for long seconds before the connection finally separates with an audible snap.

You realize: none of them are breathing.

Or rather, they are breathing, but their breathing doesn't change the air. They inhale and exhale the same saturation, the same thick wet light, and their bodies absorb what they need and express what they don't through pores that look like vents. They are part of the machinery. They were produced here, like the ferns, like the water, like the sounds.

## The Equilibrium

You sit on a stone that hums beneath you, and you wait for something to happen.

The clouds drift through. The water seeps. The engine-sounds continue their endless cycle. The shrew-thing passes your left boot at precisely the same interval it passed your right boot seventeen minutes ago. The fern you've been watching has unfurled three centimeters, and when you measure it against a mark you scratched in the stone, you realize it will unfurl another three centimeters in exactly seventeen more minutes.

This is the hardest thing to understand: nothing changes because everything is already complete.

The plateau isn't waiting for anything. It isn't developing toward a goal or declining from a peak. It exists in a state of perpetual present, each component cycling through its function in perfect rhythm with every other component. The machinery runs because that's what machinery does. The water expresses itself because that's what water does here. The creatures live their compressed mechanical lives because living is what they were produced to do.

Time passes—or seems to—but the plateau doesn't notice. You could watch for an hour or a century and see the same seventeen-minute cycles, the same water-beading, the same dim luminescence, the same clouds drifting through at intervals their own logic determines.

You begin to notice that your thoughts have started to sync with the engine rhythm. Your heartbeat has aligned with the clanking. Your breath has matched the weeping of the fissures.

---

## The Lesson

This is what the plateau teaches, if you stay long enough to learn:

Some places are not for humans.

Not because humans are forbidden, but because humans are the wrong shape for them. We are creatures of change, of before-and-after, of growth and decay and story. We need time to mean something. We need cause to lead to effect. We need to touch things, to crowd, to matter.

This place doesn't need any of that. It is complete without us. It was complete before we arrived and will remain complete after we leave, and "before" and "after" are only words we brought with us, concepts that dissolve in the wet gray air like sugar in hot water.

The machinery runs. The water expresses. The creatures cycle. The light glows from nothing and illuminates everything.

You stand at the edge of the plateau, where the young stone drops away into clouds that never clear. The sound of the engines is very loud now, or perhaps your ears have finally learned to hear what was always there. Below you, hidden by those clouds, the rest of the world continues its messy changing business. Wars. Growth. Decay. Story.

Here, there is only this. This that is not waiting. This that is not lonely. This that is not anything except itself.

You turn to leave, and you notice something: there are no footprints behind you. The wet stone has already expressed itself over the places you stepped. The water has already beaded over your path. In seventeen minutes, the shrew-thing will cross where you stood, and it will not notice your absence because it never noticed your presence.

This is mercy, of a kind.

You descend through the copper-tasting air, and the engine-sounds fade, and the light begins to come from the sky again, and you carry with you the knowledge of a place that is perfectly, terribly, eternally itself.

---

*Some landscapes forgive you for being temporary. This one simply didn't require your forgiveness.*
