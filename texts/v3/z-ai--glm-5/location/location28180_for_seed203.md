# The Root-Cathedral

You descend into it. There is no other way to arrive. The passage down—whether through crevice or old mine shaft or the collapsed roof of some forgotten cavity—ends not in darkness but in light so total it has weight.

---

The space below the earth is not a cave. Caves imply stone, and stone implies permanence. This is something else. A hollow in the world's flesh. A chamber so vast that when you look upward, you see what might be clouds forming far above—mist and vapor organizing themselves into weather patterns that move slowly across a ceiling you cannot see. The air is cold, and your breath fogs and rises to join that distant atmosphere.

Everything here grows. The floor—if it can be called that—is a dense, pale mycelium spreading across raw mineral substrate, climbing over the sharp, uneroded rock faces of this newly-formed void. The rock is recent. You can feel it in the edges, the way fractures still catch light with the gleam of fresh breaks, surfaces not yet smoothed by time or water. This place opened recently. Decades ago, perhaps. Centuries at most. The earth split or hollowed or simply revealed itself, and the growing things rushed in.

They are not plants as you knew them above. They are pale, branching structures—somewhere between root and fungus and coral—that reach upward in sparse, deliberate formations. Each organism stands alone, separated from its nearest neighbor by hundreds of meters of empty space. The density is deceptive; from a distance, the white trunks and branching canopy create an illusion of forest, but as you walk among them, you realize how much nothing lies between each presence. You could walk for an hour and pass only three or four of them.

But each one is immense. The smallest you've seen rose two hundred meters before branching. The largest—a distant silhouette against the ambient glow—might be a kilometer tall. They do not crowd. They do not need to. Every specimen commands its own territory, its own silence.

---

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The fungal-trees glow with a cold luminescence that increases near their branching tips, as if the growth itself generates brightness. The pale mat of mycelium beneath your feet holds a dimmer glow, enough to walk by, enough to cast shadows. The light is blue-white, tinted faintly green where it passes through the occasional translucent membrane or spore-cloud.

The air is damp but not wet. Condensation beads on the cold surfaces of the growths, runs in slow, intermittent trickles down the pale bark-equivalent, pools in shallow depressions in the mycelium mat. Here and there, these pools have grown large enough to become small lakes—still and bright, reflecting the glow from above, their surfaces perfectly undisturbed. There are no currents. No inflow or outflow visible. The water seems to accumulate from the air itself, the slow exhalation of the growing things, and sits waiting in clear, cold stillness.

---

You walk for what feels like a day. The silence is profound but not absolute. Your footsteps compress the mycelium with a soft, dry crunch—like walking on decades of accumulated leaves, except the mat is alive, slowly springing back behind you. Occasionally, a creak or groan echoes from somewhere in the canopy above, the sound of massive structures adjusting their weight, settling, growing.

Once, you heard a distant crash—a distant giant finally reaching the end of its structural integrity, collapsing inward on itself in slow motion. You couldn't see it happen, but the sound traveled for what might have been minutes, rolling across the empty distances. This is a turbulent ecosystem in its own way, but the turbulence operates on a scale that makes it seem still from below. Growth happens visibly here; you can almost watch the pale tendrils extending, seeking, climbing. Death happens visibly too—great structures softening, darkening, collapsing into material that the mycelium mat absorbs and transforms.

The ground shifts. Not in any dangerous way, but constantly. The mycelium is active beneath you, thickening here, thinning there, sending up pale stalks that grow to knee-height in what feels like hours and then retract just as quickly. The surface you walked across an hour ago may already be different. The landscape is alive in its substrate, even if the great trees stand still and distant.

---

You found a structure on your second day of walking.

It was small—no larger than a shelter—built from the pale material of dead growths, lashed together with some kind of fibrous cord. The construction was primitive but careful. Hand-tools had shaped the beams, leaving clean cuts that still showed the marks of stone or bone blades. Whoever built it understood the material, knew how to work with its strange grain, how to join pieces so they would hold against the slow expansion and contraction of the living floor.

Inside: remnants. A sleeping platform raised on legs to stay dry. A hollowed depression that might have been a fire bowl, though no fire had burned there in a long time—nothing here seems flammable in any conventional sense, and the air is too damp, too cold, for open flame to thrive. A few objects left behind: a grinding stone worn smooth on one face, a pile of pale fibrous material that might have been bedding or clothing, a handful of small implements whose purpose you couldn't determine.

No bones. No sign of violence or sudden departure. Just the sense that someone had been here, had lived here for a time, and then had left. Perhaps they had gone deeper. Perhaps they had found what they sought and ascended. Perhaps they had simply moved on, following some migration pattern that made sense in the logic of this underground continent.

You found two more structures in the days that followed. Each different. Each abandoned. Each showing the same careful, primitive craftsmanship, the same understanding of the material, the same patient adaptation to a place that offered neither metal nor fire nor the familiar resources of the surface world.

---

The creatures here are rare. You glimpsed something moving between two distant growths on your fourth day—a shape too large to be an insect, too small to be a threat, moving with a fluid grace across the empty space. It was pale, like everything here, adapted to the light and the cold. It did not approach you. It may not have seen you. When you looked again, it had vanished into the mycelium forest.

Later, you found traces: shallow depressions in the mat where something had bedded down, scratches on the bark of a fallen trunk, droppings that broke apart into material the mycelium quickly absorbed. Life exists here, but it moves softly, stays hidden, makes itself scarce. This is not an ecosystem that favors abundance. There is too much space between meals, too much emptiness between encounters. Survival here requires patience, efficiency, stillness.

---

You began to notice the weather on your sixth day. The clouds above had thickened, and a faint mist began to fall—not rain exactly, but a fine precipitate of condensed moisture, droplets so small they seemed to float rather than fall. The mycelium mat darkened as it absorbed the water. The pale trees seemed to brighten, drinking the moisture through their bark-equivalent, swelling visibly as you watched.

The temperature dropped. Not dangerously, but noticeably. Your breath fogged thicker. The condensation on surfaces ran faster, pooling in new depressions, forming new small lakes. The weather lasted perhaps six hours, then cleared. The clouds thinned. The mist stopped. The underground continent returned to its steady-state brightness.

You realized then that this place was complete in itself. It had its own cycles, its own seasons perhaps, its own slow weather patterns forming far above the floor. It was not a cave but a world, sealed and separate, operating on principles that had nothing to do with the sun or the moon or the familiar mechanics of the surface.

---

The strangeness of the place accumulated slowly. It was not any single thing but the aggregate: the way the mycelium seemed to respond to your presence, thickening in areas where you walked frequently as if trying to repair itself; the way the pale trees all leaned slightly in the same direction, toward some distant center you had not yet reached; the way the light seemed to pulse very faintly, on a cycle that might correspond to something but you couldn't determine what.

You found, on a slope of raw stone not yet fully colonized by the mycelium, a series of marks. They might have been natural—scratches from some ancient geological event—but they seemed too regular, too deliberate. Grooves cut into the stone in parallel lines. A shape that might have been a handprint, weathered by the damp until only the suggestion remained. Something about the age of it. Something about the way it predated the growths, suggested a time when this place was emptier, rawer, and someone had been here to see it.

---

You never reached the center. You walked for what might have been two weeks, following the lean of the pale trees, and the space unfolded around you, endless and patient. The growths grew larger as you went. The mycelium mat grew thicker, more resilient. The structures you found grew more complex—one had been large enough to house a dozen people, with storage hollows and ventilation shafts and a system for collecting condensation that ran through the walls like veins.

You understood eventually that you would not cross this place. It was too vast. The scale defeated you—not through hostility or obstacle but through sheer extension, through the patience of distance. You turned back, or what you thought was back, following some internal compass that may or may not have been reliable.

The way out was there, when you finally needed it. The same crevice you'd descended through, or one like it. The climb was long and cold, and when you emerged, the surface world seemed strange—too bright, too warm, too dense with life and noise and the busy certainty of things that lived and died on human scales.

You remember the Root-Cathedral sometimes. The patience of it. The slow turbulent stillness. The way life could fill a space without crowding it, could exist in vast emptiness without loneliness, could grow and collapse and grow again in cycles that made your own life seem brief as a breath.

The structures are still down there. The tools, the marks, the remnants of whoever came before. The pale growths are still climbing toward a ceiling they will never reach, still drinking the light they generate themselves, still leaning toward a center that may not exist.

The damp air still condenses on cold surfaces. The mycelium still shifts and thickens and repairs. The weather still forms far above the floor.

It is still growing. It has not finished becoming what it will be.
