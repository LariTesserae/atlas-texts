# The Tierworks

The mountain has no stone beneath it.

This is the first thing you learn when you climb high enough to see the seams — not the scars of quarrying, but the honest edges of fabrication. What looks like granite from the valley floor reveals itself, up close, as accumulated industry: layer upon layer of manufactured substrate, compressed and weathered until it impersonates geology. The Tierworks rises three thousand meters above the coastal shelf, and every centimeter of it was laid down by hands, by machines, by processes no one fully remembers anymore.

**The Structure of False Stone**

From the observation platforms at Midspire, you can trace the original architecture beneath the living skin. Massive buttressing ribs — corroded iron now fused with mineral deposits into something harder than either — form the skeleton. Between them, cellular chambers filled with graded aggregate, ceramics, and the compressed remains of older constructions create the bulk. The outer surface was once smooth, perhaps sealed, but millennia of tropical condensation have etched it into elaborate relief. Every groove holds water; every shelf accumulates soil; every crack becomes a foothold for something growing.

The elevation creates its own weather systems. Warm air rises from the coast below, moisture-laden, and condenses against the Tierworks' many faces. By afternoon, cloud-wreaths drape the middle levels, feeding an endless slow drip through the channel-works. These are not random erosion patterns — the grooves running vertically down the mountain-side follow an engineered logic, branching and rejoining like vessel systems. They are vessel systems: the circulatory architecture of a structure designed to distribute water from condensation zones to growing zones, from growing zones to collection cisterns, from cisterns to the great reservoirs hidden in the mountain's hollow core.

**The Engineered Rainforest**

The lushness is not an accident. It is the system working as designed.

Vegetation carpets every horizontal surface, drapes every vertical face, roots in every pocket of accumulated humus. But this is not the chaotic forest of a tropical mountainside — it has a taxonomic discipline that becomes visible once you know what to look for. The lower slopes grow broad-leafed collectors: plants bred (or built) to trap condensation on their surfaces, channeling it down their stems to the channel-works. Mid-level terraces hold production zones: food plants, fiber plants, medicinal cultures arranged in neat grids beneath the drifting clouds. The upper slopes and peaks expose the infrastructure more plainly — the green thins to reveal the grey substrate beneath, and the machinery of the Tierworks emerges from its vegetative camouflage.

Hydraulic systems thread through everything. Pipes of brass and ceramic (the iron long corroded, replaced, corroded again) carry water along pressure gradients. Some are ancient, their original purposes obscure. Others are clearly still maintained — not by human hands, but by the automated systems that have outlived their makers. Maintenance drones, insectile and patient, crawl the channel-works removing debris, patching leaks with a biological cement secreted from their ceramic bodies. They have been doing this for so long that they have evolved with the structure; some have developed specialized forms for specific micro-climates, their components interchanging in slow industrial generations.

**The Sound of Function**

The Tierworks is loud, but it is not chaotic.

The soundscape has a rhythmic quality — the industrial heartbeat of a structure still performing its original purpose. At dawn, the pressure valves release accumulated condensation in deep resonant exhales, a process that takes hours and resonates through the entire mountain. Throughout the day, the clatter of automated maintenance creates percussion. Water moves constantly: the gurgle in the channel-works, the rush in the larger conduits, the roar where spillways release overflow during heavy condensation periods. The vegetation creaks and rustles in the thermal winds that rise along the structure's face.

And over it all, the fauna.

**Life in the Machine**

Animals have colonized the Tierworks as they would any vertical island. But these are not wild animals in the ordinary sense — they are descendants of stock originally introduced, bred, or designed to support the structure's function. Pollinators — some biological, some mechanical — move between the production terraces in iridescent clouds. Small mammals work the compost systems, breaking down organic matter into usable substrate. Birds have evolved to navigate the vertical shafts, their calls echoing in the hollow spaces within the mountain.

Larger animals occupy specific niches in the engineered ecosystem. The Tierworks goat (descended from domesticated stock, now feral and specialized) climbs the vertical faces with impossible ease, grazing on the terraces and maintaining the boundary zones where vegetation threatens to overwhelm infrastructure. Monitor lizards patrol the channel-works, eating the insect larvae that would clog the water systems. In the upper levels, where the air grows thin and cold, specialized fauna — some clearly derived from laboratory stock — maintain the condensation collectors and solar arrays that power the ancient machinery.

The relationship is mutualistic. The animals are fed, watered, and housed by the structure; in return, they perform the maintenance that the automated systems cannot. Humans could do this work, and perhaps once they did. But humans have not lived in the Tierworks for a very long time.

**The Absence of Makers**

The structure shows its age in the subtle ways.

There are spaces clearly designed for human occupation — residential chambers with windows overlooking the terraces, communal halls, workshops — but they have been empty for centuries. Moss grows in the living quarters. The great kitchens that once fed thousands now serve only the resident fauna, automated systems still producing nutrient feed from the production terraces. The temples (for the Tierworks has temples, places clearly designed for reflection or worship or something like either) have been colonized by birds, their roosts built into the architectural niches.

The technology remains, maintained by machines that maintain themselves, powered by systems that harvest energy from light and water and heat. But the purpose — the original purpose — has become obscure. The production terraces still produce, but there is no one to consume. The reservoirs still fill, but there is no one to drink. The structure operates on autopilot, patient and precise, waiting for instructions that may never come.

**The Light**

Brightness defines the Tierworks.

The elevation places it above the coastal cloud layer for much of the day, exposing it to harsh tropical sun. The vegetation has evolved (or been bred) to thrive in the intense light, with waxy coatings and specialized pigments that capture every wavelength. The built surfaces — where they are not covered in growth — reflect and redirect, channels and mirrors and collectors that turn the mountain into a massive solar receiver.

In the early morning and late afternoon, when the light slants, the Tierworks reveals itself as architecture. Shadows accentuate the edges of the manufactured stone, the ribs of the hidden structure, the precise angles that no natural erosion would produce. At noon, when the sun stands directly overhead, the light bleaches everything into a white-hot wash, and the mountain seems to dissolve into pure brightness, its surfaces indistinguishable, its details swallowed by glare.

**The Slow Time**

Nothing happens quickly in the Tierworks.

The condensation cycle follows the diurnal rhythm: night brings cooling and moisture capture, morning releases the accumulated water through the channel-works, afternoon sees evaporation and condensation, evening begins the process again. The animals move through this cycle in their accustomed patterns, untroubled by change. The maintenance drones follow programs set long ago, their operations measured in decades, their repairs so gradual that a human observer would see only stillness.

This is not stasis — the structure is alive with activity — but the activity operates at a pace that human perception struggles to register. Plants grow. Water flows. Light changes. The machinery turns over, cycles completing in measures longer than attention spans. If you stand still on an observation platform, watching, you might eventually notice the slow pulse: the mountain breathing, the water circulating, the light tracking across surfaces that were designed to receive it.

**The Weirder Edges**

The Tierworks is mostly comprehensible — a structure serving a function, populated by life, operating on principles that could be understood. But there are elements that resist understanding.

Some chambers deep within the mountain are sealed, their purposes unknown. Sensors suggest spaces larger than the visible architecture should allow — interior voids that do not appear on any accessible plans. The very bottom of the structure, where it meets the coastal plain, has been buried by accumulated debris and growth for so long that no one remembers what lies beneath. The original entrance (if there was one) has been lost to time and tide.

Occasionally, the automated systems produce something unexpected — a light in a window that should be empty, a sound from a sealed corridor, a reading from a sensor that monitors nothing visible. These anomalies are rare, minor, easily dismissed as malfunction or misunderstanding. But they accumulate in the imagination of anyone who spends long enough in the Tierworks, suggesting depths that remain unplumbed, purposes that remain unspoken.

The structure continues its patient operations, indifferent to interpretation.
