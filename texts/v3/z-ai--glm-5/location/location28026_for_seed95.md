# The Gjalvarr Expanse

The plateau goes on forever. Or near enough that the difference doesn't matter—you can walk for three days and still see the same low horizon, the same sky pressing down like a hand on a closed book. This is old land. Not old in the way that mountains are old, still clinging to their drama, their sharpness. Old in the way that a bone left in a dry riverbed becomes old: picked clean, smoothed by wind and time, reduced to its essential architecture and then reduced further still, until even the architecture begins to dissolve.

---

**The Stone**

They call it the Expanse, but "flat" isn't quite right. The surface undulates. Gentle swells rise thirty meters over distances you can't measure by eye, then slope down again into broad depressions that hold shadows longer than the surrounding ground. The stone is fine-grained and pale, a color between bone and clay, and it has been ground so smooth by so many years of wind that it feels almost soft underfoot. Not slippery—*worn*. Like the handle of a tool someone stopped using before the work was done.

In places, the surface has cracked into polygonal patterns, cracks no wider than a finger, and moisture collects there. Morning condensation, mostly. The warmth rises from somewhere below—geothermal, you'd guess, though you have no way to measure it—and meets the cooler air above, and the result is a dampness that never quite dries. By afternoon, the water has returned to the air. By evening, it's condensing again. A slow breath that the land takes and releases, takes and releases.

There are darker patches where the stone has a higher mineral content, iron or copper or something you don't have a name for, and these patches form irregular shapes across the plateau. From a distance, they look like maps of territories you've never been to. Up close, they're just discolorations in ancient rock.

---

**The Life**

Sparse vegetation, yes. But *overwhelming* life.

You notice it first as movement. The stone seems to shimmer when you don't look directly at it, and when you do look, you see them: the Grazers. That's not their name—you don't know if they have a name, or who would have named them—but it describes what they do. They graze. Not on plants—there are hardly any plants to graze on, just scattered clumps of gray-green lichen that cling to the deeper cracks, low mats of something almost succulent that grows where the moisture pools longest. No, the Grazers graze on the stone itself.

They're small. The largest you've seen would fit in your palm, and most are much smaller—thumbnail-sized, beetle-shaped, but not beetles. Their shells are pale like the rock, and they move in a slow, deliberate way that makes you think of cattle more than insects. They scrape their mandibles across the surface, eating the lichen, but also eating something in the stone itself. A mineral, maybe. A salt. They leave behind faint trails, etched into the rock, and these trails cross and recross until some areas look like they've been engraved with dense, illegible script.

They're not alone.

The Gliders appear in the morning and evening, when the thermal currents are strongest. They're larger—hand-sized, some of them—and they have the same pale coloration, but their shells have extended into broad, flat surfaces that catch the rising warm air. They don't fly, exactly. They float. They drift. They descend slowly toward the Grazers below, and when they land, they fold their gliding surfaces against their bodies and become almost indistinguishable from the stones they walk among.

And then there are the Burrowers. You don't see them often, but you see their work: small holes, perfectly round, opening into the rock at irregular intervals. Some are fresh, the edges still sharp. Others have been worn smooth, their edges soft with age. You've seen Grazers enter these holes and not emerge. You've seen Gliders descend into them deliberately, folding themselves tight. You don't know what happens below.

There are other creatures too. Hoppers that move in sudden unpredictable leaps, their powerful back legs propelling them meters at a time. Spiral-shelled things that move so slowly you have to watch for minutes before you're sure they're moving at all. And once, at dawn, you saw something larger—a pale shape moving across the distant horizon, too far to make out clearly, its body low and long, its motion smooth and unhurried. You haven't seen it again.

---

**The Structures**

They're scattered across the Expanse, and you might walk past a dozen without noticing. Low mounds, first of all—barely rises in the already-gentle topography. But when you stop and look closely, you see that the mounds are too regular, too symmetrical. Something built these. Or grew them, maybe.

The best-preserved one you've found is about four meters across, circular, with a shallow depression in the center. The walls—if they can be called walls—rise perhaps half a meter, and they're made of the same pale stone as the surrounding plateau, but fitted together in a way that can't be natural. The joints are tight. The work is precise. And it's old. So old that the edges have begun to soften, the once-sharp lines now blurred by the same wind that has worn the rest of the Expanse.

You've found eleven of these structures so far, and they're all similar: circular, low, shallow, placed at intervals that seem random until you realize they're all along the same contour line, following a path you can't see. They might have been shelters once. Or markers. Or something else entirely, something whose purpose has been worn away along with the sharp edges of the stone.

There are other traces too. Lines of darker stone that might be paths, or might be erosion patterns along some buried fault. Shallow depressions that might be cisterns, or might be natural hollows that happened to hold water. A single standing stone, taller than the others, its top edge broken off at an angle, its surface marked with grooves that might be decoration, might be damage, might be both.

---

**The Silence**

The landscape is quiet. Not silent—the Grazers make a faint clicking sound as they move, and the Gliders' shells rustle against the stone when they land, and the wind is a constant presence, low and steady—but quiet. No birdsong. No insect hum. No calls or cries or territorial proclamations. Just the soft sounds of creatures going about their business, and the softer sounds of the land itself, breathing its slow thermal breath.

The sky is often clear, a pale washed-out blue that darkens toward the zenith. Clouds appear sometimes, high and thin, moving quickly overhead. They don't bring rain—at least, you haven't seen rain yet—but they bring shade, and when they pass, the light changes, and the Grazers pause in their scraping, and the whole Expanse seems to hold its breath for a moment.

---

**The Weight of Time**

This is what strikes you most: not the strangeness, though the place is strange, but the age. This is a landscape that has outlived its own story. Whatever built those structures, whatever processes shaped the stone, whatever lives have come and gone across this surface—they're all gone now, worn down to traces and suggestions. What remains is the end point of erosion, the final state toward which all land eventually moves. This plateau has arrived. It has become what it was always going to become.

And yet it's not empty. It teems. The Grazers and Gliders and Burrowers, the Hoppers and Spiral-shells, the pale low lichen and the moisture-collecting mosses—they've found a way to live here, in this stripped-down landscape, at this end-of-all-things. They don't seem to mind the quiet or the ancientness. They go about their business, slow and deliberate.
