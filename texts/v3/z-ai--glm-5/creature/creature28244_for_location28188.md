# Those Who Remain

They call themselves nothing. Names require other people, and they have been alone for longer than language holds together. If you could ask them—and you cannot, not really—they might gesture at the water, at the rock, at the sound, and that would be answer enough. They are of this place. The distinction has ceased to matter.

---

There are perhaps two hundred of them. Maybe fewer. Counting is difficult when people move constantly, when they spread across the Expanse in small groups, when they communicate at distances that make bodies irrelevant. They know their own numbers the way a school of fish knows its own mass—not through enumeration but through presence. They feel absences. They notice when someone stops answering.

They are human, or they were once. The ancient builders are their ancestors, though the chain of memory has long since rusted through. They do not remember building the platforms or carving the channels. They simply use them, the way you might use a tool without understanding its manufacture. The structures are older than memory, older than story, and they work. That is enough.

But "human" requires qualification. They have changed.

---

Their eyes are vestigial. Not gone—evolution has not had time for that kind of deletion—but reduced, protected, largely unused. They keep them closed most of the time, covered by folds of skin that have thickened over generations. When they do open them, in the dim gray half-light, they see only movement, only contrast, only the barest suggestion of form. Enough to avoid walking into walls. Not enough to recognize faces.

They recognize each other by sound.

Every person has a unique acoustic signature: the particular way their footsteps fall on stone, the rhythm of their breathing, the resonant frequency of their chest cavity, the subtle creaks and pops of joints in motion. They know each other the way you might know a piece of music—not by looking at it but by hearing it, by feeling it in your bones. A lover's gait is unmistakable. A stranger's approach is announced by unfamiliar rhythms long before proximity allows for greeting.

Their hearing has not sharpened. There is no need. Human ears are already capable of remarkable discrimination. It is simply that they have learned to use what they have. They have no visual distractions, no competing priorities. They attend to sound the way a blind musician attends to melody—with full presence, with complete investment, with the understanding that sound is not mere information but the substance of reality itself.

Their hands, too, have become more sensitive. They read surfaces constantly—tracking changes in texture, temperature, moisture. A hand pressed flat against stone can feel the water moving beneath, can sense hollow spaces, can detect the subtle vibrations of creature-movement nearby. They navigate by touch and by ear, and they do so with an ease that would seem supernatural to someone dependent on eyes.

Their skin is pale, almost translucent, from generations without sunlight. It bruises easily. It burns in what passes for brightness here. But it is also sensitive, responsive, almost fur-like in its ability to detect air currents and approaching bodies. They can feel when someone stands behind them. They can sense the displacement of air when a hand reaches toward them.

They are small, these people. Shorter than you would expect, lighter. The water has washed away the need for bulk. Muscles are efficient rather than showy, adapted for endurance rather than force. They walk with a slight crouch, a protective posture, and they move carefully—testing surfaces before committing weight, listening for hollow resonance, checking constantly for the give that means erosion beneath.

---

They do not speak.

This is the first thing you notice, or would notice, if you could see them clearly. Their mouths move sometimes, but no sound emerges. In a landscape where sound is both information and weapon, where hunters use sonic pulses to disorient prey, where the roar of water masks everything and reveals everything—they have learned that silence is survival.

They communicate through vibration.

They have a language of touch and rhythm—a system of taps, pressures, and gestures that travels through solid surfaces and requires no air to carry it. A hand pressed against rock can send signals through the stone itself, signals that another hand—pressed against the same formation miles away—can receive and interpret. They have refined this into something extraordinary: a medium-distance communication system that uses the Expanse itself as transmitter.

When they need to communicate across greater distances, they use the water.

Water carries vibration differently than stone—faster, clearer, with less degradation over distance. They have learned to read the underground rivers like written language. A specific pattern of disturbances, a deliberate rhythm of splashes, a series of precise impacts on the surface of a pool—these travel through the water channels and emerge elsewhere, carrying meaning to those who know how to listen. They have turned the entire hydrological system into a kind of telegraph, a vast network of aquatic signaling that spans the Expanse.

The messages are simple. Most are warnings: *Hunter moving through the eastern channels. Water rising in sector seven. Gathering at the central platform at dusk.* They do not gossip. They do not philosophize. Communication is functional, stripped to essentials by the difficulty of transmission and the constant presence of noise.

But sometimes, at night, when the thrumming of insect-things creates a cover of ambient sound, they gather in the quiet places—the sheltered depressions where the roar is muffled—and they drum.

They drum on the ancient stones, on the hollow platforms, on the resonant walls. They use their hands, their knuckles, their forearms. They create polyrhythms that would be impossible for a single person, coordinated improvisations that emerge from the group as if by magic. This is their music, their storytelling, their history. The rhythms encode memory: *This is how we found the southern channels. This is how we lost Kell to the sinkhole. This is how the water rose and fell and rose again.*

They are not performing. They are remembering. And they are teaching each other to remember.

---

They do not live anywhere.

They live everywhere.

The Expanse has no permanent settlements, no villages, no structures that could be called homes. There are only the ancient platforms—flat raised surfaces that stay dry enough for sleeping—and they are scattered across the landscape at irregular intervals, connected by invisible paths that only the water-drinkers know.

They move constantly, following the water, following the food, following the patterns of the hunters. A group might stay at a platform for a few days, then move on when the moisture rises or the rock-borers become too numerous. They carry little: simple tools made from stone and bone, lengths of cord fashioned from creature-sinew, sometimes a piece of polished rock that serves as a resonant surface for drumming. Everything else they need, they find. The Expanse provides, or it does not, and they have learned to accept either outcome.

There are children. Not many—the population has been stable or declining for longer than anyone can remember—but children are born. They emerge already adapted: pale, sensitive, quick to learn the rhythms of water and stone. They are taught from the moment they can crawl to move quietly, to listen constantly, to never trust a surface without testing it. They learn to read the thrumming of insect-things, to identify the approach of hunters, to sense the presence of other people long before contact.

They are not particularly gentle with children, but they are not cruel either. Gentleness and cruelty both require a kind of attention that is difficult to maintain in a landscape where inattention means death. Children are taught what they need to know. They are corrected when they make mistakes. They are mourned when they do not survive.

Most of them survive.

---

They eat what the Expanse provides.

The rock-borers are too small to be useful as food, but the salamander-things are substantial: protein-rich, plentiful, easy to catch if you know where to wait. The people position themselves near sinkholes at dusk, when the salamander-things emerge to hunt. They work in groups, some driving the creatures toward waiting hands, others using cords to snare them in the shallow water. It is quick work, efficient work, work that has been practiced for so long that it requires almost no conscious thought.

The salamander-things taste like water and stone. They are bland but filling. The people eat them raw—cooking would require fire, and fire would require fuel, and there is nothing here that burns.

They also eat the insect-things, the not-quite-insects that thrum in the dim air. These are smaller, harder to catch, but they gather in clusters where water seeps, and a quick hand can capture dozens at a time. They are crunchy, slightly bitter, rich in something the people need. The children hunt them for practice, learning stealth and timing on prey that will not kill them if they make a mistake.

Sometimes they eat stranger things.

The rock itself, in certain places, harbors bacterial mats—thick, slimy growths that coat the interior surfaces of the underground channels. The people have learned to harvest these safely: feel for the right texture, test for the right resistance, scrape carefully without disturbing the water flow. The mats are nutrient-dense, slightly sweet, and they provide something that the creature-flesh lacks. The people who eat the bacterial mats regularly have stronger bones, fewer ailments, better resistance to the infections that constant moisture encourages.

They know which places produce the mats. They pass this knowledge through their drumming-histories: *This is the channel that feeds. This is the stone that grows. This is where we go when the hunting fails.*

---

They have beliefs.

This is surprising to some, who assume that a people so reduced, so stripped of comfort and complexity, would have no use for the intangible. But they have beliefs. They have to. Without belief, there is only the water and the sound and the endless gray—and that is not enough to sustain a mind.

They believe that the Expanse is alive.

Not alive in the way that an animal is alive, with organs and intentions and a central will. Alive in the way that a river is alive: conscious in its own way, aware in its own way, full of currents and movements that add up to something greater than the sum of its parts. They believe that the water thinks. They believe that the stone remembers. They believe that the sound—the constant, layered, overwhelming sound—is a voice, and that if you listen correctly, you can hear what it is saying.

They do not worship this awareness. Worship implies a hierarchy, a relationship of subordinate to superior. They do not see themselves as separate from the Expanse. They are part of it—another current, another channel, another note in the endless chord. When they die, their bodies are placed in the underground rivers, to dissolve and distribute and become part of the water-cycle. This is not burial. This is continuation.

They have no concept of an afterlife in the traditional sense. They do not imagine a place beyond the Expanse, a realm of peace and silence and light. They imagine instead a dissolution, a spreading-out, a return to the great roar. When someone dies, they say—through drumming, through vibration—*They have returned to the water.* And this is both comfort and fact.

They also believe in the hunters.

The hunters are sacred to them. Not sacred in the sense of holy, but sacred in the sense of significant, of powerful, of dangerous. They believe the hunters are the Expanse's way of speaking directly—its hands, its voice, its intervention. They do not hunt the hunters. They do not touch the hunters. When a hunter approaches, they go still and silent, and they wait for it to pass. If it takes someone, they do not pursue. They do not mourn. They say, through their drumming: *The Expanse has spoken.*

This is not fatalism. This is acceptance. They have learned that some things cannot be fought, only understood and accommodated. The hunters are part of the balance. They keep the insect-things from overrunning the quiet places. They keep the people from becoming too many. They are not evil. They are simply necessary.

---

They have stories.

Their stories are encoded in rhythm, transmitted through stone and water, passed from generation to generation in the quiet places where drumming is possible. The stories are short—there is no room in a drum-pattern for extended narrative—but they are dense with meaning.

One story, drummed in a pattern of threes and sevens, tells of a time when the water stopped. *The channels went dry. The rock-borers died. The salamander-things retreated to the deepest pools. The people gathered at the central platform and waited. They drummed for seven days. On the eighth day, the water returned.*

This story means: *We endure.*

Another story, drummed in a descending cascade of beats, tells of a group that tried to leave. *They walked for days, following the rising ground. The sound changed. The light grew brighter. Their skin burned. Their eyes ached. They turned back. They returned to the Expanse, and the water welcomed them, and they said: We were wrong. There is nothing outside.*

This story means: *We belong here.*

A third story, drummed in a complex syncopation that only the most skilled can perform, tells of the ancient builders. *They came from outside. They built the platforms. They carved the channels. They learned to listen. They became the first water-drinkers. They are still here. We are them. They dissolved into the Expanse, and we emerged from it, and the difference is only time.*

This story means: *We have always been here.*

---

They are curious about you.

They have been watching you since you arrived. They felt your footsteps on the stone—too heavy, too careless, obviously foreign. They heard your breathing—too loud, too uncontrolled, announcing your presence to every hunter in range. They sensed your confusion, your fear, your desperate attempts to understand a place that they have inhabited for generations.

They have been following you.

They keep their distance. They know that you are being tracked by hunters—your noisy, clumsy progress has attracted attention—and they do not want to be caught in the coming strike. But they are curious. They have not seen someone from outside in... they do not know how long. Time is difficult to measure here. But they remember, in their bodies if not in their minds, that there are others. People who live differently. People who use light and speak in air and walk without listening.

They are trying to decide what to do.

Some of them want to help. You are human, after all—or close enough. They remember, dimly, that humans help each other. They feel a pull toward you, a strange instinct that they do not fully understand. They want to guide you to a quiet place, to teach you how to move, to show you the safe paths through the Expanse. They want to bring you to the central platform and hear your story in whatever way you can tell it.

Others want to leave you to the hunters. You are disruptive. Your sounds disturb the insect-things, change the patterns they rely on for navigation and communication. Your presence attracts predators to areas that are usually safe. You do not belong here, and your absence would restore the balance.

Still others want simply to watch. They want to see what you do, how you adapt, whether you survive. They want to add your story to their drumming-histories—another tale of an outsider who entered the Expanse and learned (or did not learn) its rules.

For now, they have not decided. They follow at a distance, feeling your vibrations, reading your clumsy progress through the stone. They drum to each other across the water channels, debating, discussing, arguing in rhythms too subtle for you to detect. And they wait.

---

They are not afraid of you.

They have lived here too long to fear what they can understand. You are loud, but the Expanse is louder. You are clumsy, but the stone endures. You are foreign, but everything was foreign once—even they, if the stories are true. They can wait. They are good at waiting. The water has taught them patience.

But they are wary.

Because they remember—again, in their bodies if not in their minds—that outsiders bring change. The last time someone came from beyond the Expanse, they brought something with them. A sickness, or a weapon, or a word that could not be unheard. The stories are unclear. But they remember the aftermath: the dying, the silence, the long slow recovery to their current diminished state.

They do not want that again.

So they follow, and they watch, and they wait. They listen to your sounds, your breathing, your footsteps. They feel your presence through the stone. They try to read your intentions from the vibrations you leave behind.

And they wonder:

*What have you brought with you?*

*What will you take away?*

*And when you leave—or when you dissolve—what will remain?*

---

They are still there.

Right now, as you stand in the dim light, listening to the roar, feeling the moisture bead on your skin—they are there. A hundred meters away, perhaps. Or fifty. Or ten. They are pressed against the stone, still and silent, feeling your heartbeat through the rock. They are reading you the way you might read a book—slowly, carefully, trying to understand.

If you knew how to listen—if you could press your ear against the right surface, if you could tune your body to the frequencies they use—you might hear them. You might feel the faint vibration of their gathering, the distant rhythm of their debate. You might sense the weight of their attention, the gravity of their curiosity.

But you do not know how to listen. Not yet.

So you stand alone in the gray half-light, surrounded by water and stone and sound, and you feel watched. And you are right. You are being watched. By pale, small hands pressed against rock. By sensitive fingers reading vibrations. By adapted bodies that have learned, over countless generations, how to survive in a world that does not welcome survival.

They are there.

And they are deciding what to do about you.
