# The Inhabitants of the High Plateau

## The Creatures Called Drifters

They are the first to notice you. Not because they are aggressive—they are the most docile of the plateau's large fauna—but because they are the most observant. A drifter will freeze mid-step on some high rock shelf, head turned, nostrils testing the air, and will hold that position for an duration that makes you uncomfortable. They are watching. They are always watching.

The drifters occupy a niche that seems at first contradictory: they are both the most visible of the plateau's animals and the most enigmatic. You see them constantly, basking on warm pipes, gathered in groups of three or five on prominent outcrops, gliding on the thermals that rise from the canyons. But their social structure, their communication, their purpose—these remain opaque. They do not flee. They do not approach. They simply... observe.

Their bodies are built for patience. Low to the ground, splayed-legged, with a wide stance that distributes their weight across the irregular stone. Their skin is a fine mosaic of grey and rust, perfectly matched to the volcanic rock, and they can hold so still that you might mistake them for weathered formations until one blinks—a slow, deliberate closing and opening of eyes that are surprisingly dark, surprisingly deep. Not the eyes of a simple reptile. There is something behind them. Some processing.

They feed on the condensation, lapping at the gutters with long, grey tongues, and they eat the insects that swarm around the warm machinery. But they also eat something else. You will see them gathered at certain vents, certain outflow points, licking at the residue that accumulates there. The industrial compounds. The processed minerals. They have adapted to a diet that would kill most organisms, and they have done so completely enough that they now depend on it. Remove the machinery, and the drifters would starve within a season.

What is unusual is that they seem to know this. You will see them, occasionally, nudging a pipe with their snout, pressing against a valve, as if testing its function. Not using tools, not truly maintaining the system, but attending to it. Monitoring. As though they understand that their survival is bound up in something they did not build and cannot repair.

They are not alone in this understanding.

## The Hunting Ridges

The drifters are prey. This becomes clear when you see them scatter.

The first time you witness a hunt, it is over before you understand what happened. A shadow detaches from a canyon wall—a shape you had mistaken for a protrusion of rock, a shelf, a boulder—and drops thirty feet in a single controlled fall, landing with a sound like a whip crack on the stone below. By the time you focus, it has already caught something, and it is retreating into a crevice with quick, efficient movements.

This is a ridgeback. Named, presumably, by whoever came before, though the name fits. The ridge of scales along its spine catches the light differently than the rest of its body, creating a false silhouette that breaks up its outline against the stone. It is large—larger than you expect, the size of a man crouching, and far more massive—and it is solitary in a way that suggests not preference but necessity. The plateau cannot support many predators of this size. Each ridgeback claims a territory of several square kilometers, and it patrols that territory with a patience that borders on geological. It knows every shelf, every hiding spot, every escape route. It has to. The prey here are quick and alert and numerous. A ridgeback that chases will starve. A ridgeback that waits will eat.

They are ambush predators, which means they are masters of stillness. You might walk within arm's reach of one and never see it, never sense it, until it moves. And when it moves, it is fast—not the speed of a cheetah or a falcon, but the speed of something that has been storing energy for hours, for days, releasing it all in a single explosive motion. The attack is efficient. No wasted movement. The ridgeback does not play with its prey. It kills quickly, eats quickly, and returns to stillness.

They have no predators of their own. Nothing here hunts a ridgeback. But they are not invulnerable. They are vulnerable to heat, to dehydration, to the same pressures that shape all life on the plateau. You will find their skeletons in the deeper canyons sometimes, picked clean by the smaller scavengers, bones weathered to the same grey-black as the stone. The ridgebacks die. They just do not die easily.

They mate rarely. Once every few years, perhaps, when the conditions align, when the condensation is heavy enough and the prey are abundant enough. The females lay eggs in the warmest crevices they can find, often near the industrial exhaust vents, and they abandon them there. No nesting, no guarding. The young emerge on their own or they do not emerge at all. Those that survive their first season are already half-grown, already skilled at stillness, already patient.

The ridgebacks do not interact with the industrial system the way the drifters do. They use it incidentally—the shade, the warmth, the way the infrastructure shapes the movements of prey—but they do not depend on it. If the machinery failed, the ridgebacks would persist. They are older than the system. They will outlast it.

## The Swarmers

The ridgebacks eat the drifters, when they can catch them. But mostly they eat the swarmers.

The swarmers are small—not much larger than your hand—and they are everywhere. They are the reason the plateau feels occupied even when you see no other animals. They are the movement in the corners of your vision, the skittering in the crevices, the nervous energy that pervades every canyon. Where the drifters are patient and the ridgebacks are still, the swarmers are in constant motion. They have to be. Stillness is death for them.

They live in collectives. This is the wrong word—"hive" suggests a structure, a nest, a center. The swarmers have none of these. They are distributed across the terrain in loose, shifting aggregations that number in the hundreds, sometimes the thousands, and they communicate through a system of touches, postures, and chemical signals that you cannot parse. They move together, feed together, sleep in piles for warmth—but they are not eusocial. There are no castes, no queens, no specialization. Each swarmer is an independent organism that has simply learned that survival is more likely in proximity to others.

They eat almost anything. Condensation, insects, the residue from certain industrial processes, carrion, each other (when food is scarce), and a kind of bacterial film that grows on the shaded rock faces where the moisture collects. They are the cleanup crew of the plateau, the recyclers, the ones that ensure nothing goes to waste. When an animal dies, the swarmers find it within hours. They reduce it to bones within a day.

They are nervous. They have reason to be. Everything eats them. The ridgebacks, the gliding hunters, even the drifters on occasion—the swarmers are the baseline food source, the grass equivalent in an ecosystem where there is almost no grass. And so they have developed a suite of survival strategies that would be impressive if they were not so desperate.

A swarmer on its own is vulnerable. It will freeze, hoping to go unnoticed, and if that fails, it will bolt for the nearest crevice with a speed that belies its small size. But swarmers are rarely alone. When one detects a threat, it signals—not with sound, but with a sudden change in movement pattern, a specific zigzag sprint that the others recognize. The signal propagates through the group, and suddenly the entire collective is aware. This does not mean they flee. Sometimes they scatter. Sometimes they mass, rushing toward the threat in a tight cluster that confuses and overwhelms. Sometimes they do nothing at all, letting the predator take one or two while the rest continue feeding. The swarmers make collective decisions, and you cannot predict what they will decide.

They use the industrial system extensively. The pipes provide warmth at night, the gutters provide water, the junction boxes provide shelter. They have been observed gnawing on certain valves—not damaging them, precisely, but wearing them down in ways that seem almost intentional. No one knows why. Perhaps they are extracting some trace mineral. Perhaps they are sharpening their teeth. Perhaps they simply like the taste.

The swarmers are not intelligent. But they are numerous, and they are adaptable, and they have been here longer than anything else. When the ridgeback skeletons weather to dust, the swarmers will still be skittering through the canyons, eating what can be eaten, surviving what can be survived.

## The Gliders

The sky above the plateau is not empty.

You might think it is, at first. The air is thin, the sun is harsh, and you see nothing moving in the expanse above you. But if you wait—if you find a high point and sit still long enough—you will notice that some of the shadows are wrong. They do not match the clouds. They move against the wind. They are cast by something that is riding the thermals, circling in slow spirals, watching.

The gliders are the most alien of the plateau's inhabitants. Not because they are strange in form—though they are, their bodies stretched between thin membrane wings, their heads small and pointed, their tails long and ruddered—but because they occupy a niche that has no easy equivalent elsewhere. They are predators, yes. They hunt the swarmers and the drifters and anything else small enough to lift. But they also scavenge, and they also harvest, and they also perform some function that you cannot quite identify.

They are drawn to the industrial system. Specifically, they are drawn to the heat. The machinery, the pipes, the exhaust vents—these create columns of rising warm air that the gliders use to gain altitude without effort. They spiral up these thermal columns, climbing thousands of feet, and then they glide for hours, crossing the entire plateau on a single updraft, watching.

They nest on the highest pinnacles, the isolated spires that rise above the canyon maze. These nests are messy affairs, built from sticks and bones and scraps of industrial material—wire, cable fragments, pieces of pipe insulation. The gliders return to the same nests year after year, generation after generation, and some of these structures are enormous, accumulations of centuries. They are the closest thing the plateau has to monuments.

The gliders communicate through postures and displays, but they also use sound—not in the conventional sense, but a kind of resonant thrumming that they produce by vibrating their wings. This thrumming carries for kilometers in the thin air, and the gliders use it to signal territory, to warn of threats, to coordinate movements. It is not language. But it is communication.

They are intelligent enough to recognize patterns. A glider will learn the rhythms of the industrial system—the cycle of the pumps, the timing of the vent releases—and will use these patterns to hunt. When a valve opens, when steam erupts, the prey animals scatter. The glider anticipates this. It is already diving before the valve opens, already closing its wings to fall faster, already reaching for the fleeing swarmer or drifter before the animal knows it is exposed.

They have no natural predators. Nothing hunts a glider in flight. But they are not invincible. They fight among themselves—territorial disputes, mating competitions, squabbles over carrion—and they sometimes fall victim to the machinery, pulled into intake vents, tangled in power lines. The plateau is indifferent to them, as it is indifferent to everything.

## The Tenders

You asked who lives here. You did not specify *animals*.

There are people here. Not many. Not enough to call a population. But they exist, and they have lived here long enough to become something other than what they were.

They call themselves tenders, or they do not call themselves anything at all. The distinction is unclear. They were not the original builders of the industrial system—that much is obvious from their relationship with the machinery. They do not understand it, not fully. They know how to maintain it, how to read its signals, how to perform the rituals that keep it running. But they did not design it, and they cannot modify it, and they do not know what it is ultimately for.

They live in the canyons, in the crevices that the animals do not use. Their shelters are minimal—overhangs with scrap metal walls, caves with rudimentary doors, small rooms carved into the rock where the thermal vents provide warmth. They move through the terrain like the drifters do, with the same quiet purpose, the same awareness of every surface, every shadow. They have adapted.

They are not from here. You can tell. Their features, their stature, their skin—all wrong for this altitude, this heat. They are descendants of someone who came from elsewhere, who was perhaps stranded or stationed or simply left behind. Generations of living on the plateau have changed them—they are thinner now, heat-adapted, their lungs enlarged for the thin air, their skin darkened against the sun—but they are still recognizable as human. Or human-ish.

Their diet is the same as the animals. Condensation water, insects, small creatures they trap or hunt, industrial residue that they have learned to process. They are not cannibals—this is important to note, because the question arises—but they have been observed eating the dead of other species, and they have been observed feeding certain animals to others, and they have been observed performing exchanges with the drifters that look almost like trade.

They do not speak. They may have lost the capacity, or they may have chosen silence, or they may communicate in ways you cannot perceive. They gesture—large movements, arm sweeps, head tilts, a kind of sign language that seems improvised rather than formal. They understand sounds; they respond to clicks and whistles and the mechanical noises of the industrial system. But they do not make meaningful vocalizations themselves.

They maintain the machinery. This is their function, or the function they have adopted. They walk the pipes, checking for leaks. They climb the cable routes, tightening fastenings. They grease bearings and replace panels and clear blockages in the condensation gutters. They do this methodically, thoroughly, following routes that are probably ancient, probably passed down through generations of teaching. They wear faded coveralls that might have once been uniforms, and they carry tools that might have once been standard issue, and they move with a competence that suggests deep familiarity.

But they do not know why.

Ask them—through gesture, through mimicry—what the system does, and they will look at you with eyes that are not puzzled, exactly, but resigned. They know what they know. They know the motions. They know that if they stop, the condensation traps will clog, the valves will seize, the pumps will fail, and the plateau will change. They do not know what is being extracted or processed or transported. They only know that the work must continue.

They are not unhappy. This is hard to explain to someone who has not spent time with them. They do not seem oppressed or desperate or trapped. They move through their routines with a kind of contentment, a satisfaction in competence. They have enough to eat, enough to drink, shelter from the worst of the heat. They have each other—there are perhaps thirty of them total, scattered across the plateau in loose family groups—and they have the animals, and they have the machinery. They do not have what they lack. They do not seem to miss it.

They are protective of the system, and they are protective of the animals. You will see a tender scolding a drifter that has damaged a pipe—not harshly, but with a kind of weary patience, like a parent with a child. You will see a tender scare off a ridgeback that has gotten too close to a work site, not by fighting it, but by making noise, by throwing stones, by standing ground until the predator retreats. You will see tenders and drifters sharing the same condensation trap, the same patch of shade, the same moment of rest.

They are not the owners of the plateau. They are its custodians. And they know the difference.

## The Deeper Relationship

The plateau is not random. The ecosystem is not accidental. And the relationship between the animals, the tenders, and the machinery is not merely one of opportunistic use.

Something else is happening here. You feel it. You cannot prove it.

The industrial system was built for a purpose. Extraction, probably. Processing of something that rises with the geothermal heat—minerals, gases, energy in some form. The original builders are gone. They left behind the machinery and perhaps a population to maintain it, and that population became the tenders, and the tenders became part of the ecosystem.

But the machinery was not designed for the animals. And yet the animals fit.

The drifters drink from condensation traps that were sized for industrial cooling, but the overflow gutters—the ones that prevent flooding during heavy condensation—are positioned at heights that small animals can reach, with lips that are easy to drink from. The ridgebacks nest in the warmth of exhaust vents, but those vents are angled in ways that provide shelter, that reduce wind exposure, that create the specific conditions a nesting predator might prefer. The swarmers move through junction boxes that have gaps just large enough for their bodies, and the gliders perch on transmission towers that have cross-bracing at exactly the right intervals for taloned feet.

It could be coincidence. It could be adaptation—generations of animals learning to use what was available, evolving to fit the infrastructure. But the fit is too precise. The integration is too complete. The system and the ecosystem have become one thing, and the boundary between natural and artificial is no longer visible.

The tenders know this. Or they sense it. You will see them pause in their work, looking at the animals, looking at the pipes, looking at nothing at all. You will see them perform maintenance that the machinery does not strictly need—clearing a gutter that is already clear, checking a valve that was checked an hour ago, walking a route that serves no functional purpose. They are not wasting time. They are performing rituals. They are acknowledging something.

The plateau is alive. Not in the metaphorical sense—in the literal sense. The machinery and the stone and the animals and the tenders and the thermal processes and the condensation cycles are all parts of a single organism, a single system, a single vast slow consciousness that is too distributed and too unlike you to recognize as consciousness. But it is there. It is watching. It is waiting.

It does not have a plan. It does not have a purpose. It has only continuance—the same continuance as the stone, the same continuance as the heat rising from below. It will persist until something fundamental changes, and then it will adapt, and then it will persist again.

## What Lives Here

You ask what lives on the plateau. The answer is:

The drifters, patient and observant, adapted to the machinery they did not build.

The ridgebacks, ancient and solitary, predators of stillness and patience.

The swarmers, nervous and numerous, the baseline life that everything else eats.

The gliders, alien and aerial, riders of the thermals, watchers from above.

The tenders, human and not-quite-human, custodians of a system they do not understand.

And underneath all of it, woven through all of it, present in every pipe and every paw print and every breath of hot thin air:

The plateau itself.

It does not have a name. It does not need one. It knows what it is, even if you do not.
