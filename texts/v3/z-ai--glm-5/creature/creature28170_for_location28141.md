What Lives in the Hatchery

The crawling things do not have names. They have functions.

They are born soft and white, emerging from nursery cairns in clusters of dozens, their segments not yet hardened, their mandibles not yet capable of the work they will need to do. At this stage they are food—eaten by their siblings, eaten by the things that descend from above, eaten by each other in moments of confused proximity. The mortality rate approaches ninety percent in the first hour. Those who survive begin to darken within minutes of their first breath, if breath is the word. Their exoskeletons oxidize in the warm wet air, cream hardening to tan hardening to the grey of old stone.

They have no parents. They have no language. They have no memory of any place other than this. And yet they work.

An observer—one of those rare individuals who moves against the flow, carrying a pellet of compressed mineral—does not know that it is an observer. It does not know that its passage deposits trace chemicals that other crawling things follow, that its choices become architecture, that its rest periods become gathering points. It knows only this: the weight in its mandibles, the warmth of the stone beneath its legs, the subtle gradient of moisture that tells it up from down. It is building the future of the Hatchery. It will never know this. It will die within a cycle—a period of time that has no measure here, but that corresponds roughly to the period during which the ceiling completes one exhalation of mist—and its body will be carried by others to a pit, deposited, covered in the secretions that begin the process of decomposition and reconstitution. Its matter will become wall. Its matter will become floor. Its matter will become the substrate from which new stone emerges.

This is not death, exactly. This is incorporation. The Hatchery does not distinguish between the living and the geological.

---

Above the crawling rivers, the swarms thin air.

The flying things—if flight is the word, for their movement is more like swimming through the thick atmosphere—exist in a state of continuous emergency. Their lives are measured in hours. They hatch from pupating masses in the corners and cracks of the chamber, emerge damp and trembling, and must achieve altitude before their wings dry and stiffen. Those who fail fall to the crawling things below and are absorbed. Those who succeed join the shimmering clouds that drift and densify according to patterns invisible from below.

They feed on the mist. This seems impossible—there is not enough substance in water vapor to support bodies of their size—but they do. Their mouths are complex structures, filters and grinders and chemical processors, and they extract something from the air that is not water. Some dissolved mineral. Some biological residue from the process of stone formation above. Their excretions fall to the crawling things, and the crawling things carry those excretions to the nurseries, and the nurseries produce secretions that rise in the warm air and become the mist that the flying things consume.

They are not insects. They have no ancestors that ever lived above ground, ever saw the sun, ever experienced a world where light came from a single source. Their eyes—if the clusters of light-sensitive cells on their frontal segments qualify—are designed for the diffuse amber glow of the Hatchery. They navigate by gradients: warmer this way, cooler that way, the chemical signature of their own kind thick here, thin there. They do not see shapes. They do not see distance. They experience the world as a continuous field of stimuli, and they move within it like fish in a current.

The males and the females are indistinguishable to human observation. They may not be distinct at all—some may be both, some may be neither, some may change function over the course of their brief adult lives. Reproduction happens in the swarms. Bodies meet, align, exchange genetic material through processes that leave both participants weakened. They fall afterward. Most do not rise again.

And yet there are always more. The pupating masses in the corners pulse with new life. The system has achieved a equilibrium of desperation: enough die to feed the processes below, enough survive to produce the next generation, and the whole swirling cloud maintains its density like a weather pattern that refuses to resolve.

---

On the ledges above, the whistlers wait.

They are patient in a way that the other creatures are not. The crawling things move because movement is their nature; the flying things move because stillness means death. But the whistlers can hold position for what seems like hours, their pale bodies pressed against the warm stone, their many limbs folded beneath them in configurations that suggest prayer or rest or something for which there are no words in any surface language.

They are related to the crawling things. This is visible in the segmentation, in the jointed legs, in the mouth-parts that can produce sound. But where the crawling things are compact, designed for the horizontal, the whistlers are elongated, designed for vertical terrain. Their legs end in grippers that can hold stone surfaces at any angle. They can climb the ceiling. They can descend walls head-first. They can hang motionless in positions that would cause blood to rush to the wrong places in any vertebrate body.

Their sound is not speech. It is not song. It is perhaps location—a continuous announcement of presence that allows others of their kind to navigate the space without collision or conflict. Each whistler produces a slightly different frequency, a slightly different pattern of clicks and tones. They can recognize each other. They can recognize strangers. When a new whistler emerges from its pupation somewhere in the depths of the Hatchery, it must find its way to an unoccupied ledge, and it does so by listening to the chorus of those already established, identifying the gaps, moving toward silence.

They are territorial. They are not violent—there is no space for sustained conflict in a system this dense—but they are insistent. If a young whistler attempts to claim a position too close to an established elder, the sound changes. The elder's whistling intensifies, incorporates new frequencies, becomes a wall of noise that the younger cannot pass without disorientation. The younger must retreat, find another path, locate another gap. Most do not succeed. Most fall from their climbs and are consumed by the crawling things below.

Those who survive can live for a long time. The whistlers are the longest-lived of the Hatchery's creatures—they have been observed maintaining position for entire cycles, barely moving, barely feeding, their sounds continuous but not desperate. They consume the flying things that come too close, their mouth-parts snapping out with surprising speed to catch passing bodies, digesting them while continuing to whistle. This is perhaps the only violence in the Hatchery that is not mass destruction: the careful, targeted predation of the established on the ignorant.

They are not intelligent. They are not building anything. But they are watching, in their way—observing the flow of bodies below, the movement of swarms above, the slow shifting of stone that marks the Hatchery's growth. They may know more about this place than any other creature. They may be its historians. They may simply be its background noise.

---

And there are others.

The spindly-legged predators that move between the pillars are rarely seen clearly. They are faster than anything has a right to be in this density of life, their bodies designed for sudden dashes and sudden stops. They hunt the whistlers, sometimes—climbing the walls with a different kind of grip, making no sound, their approach invisible against the background roar until the moment of contact. They also hunt each other. They are the only creatures in the Hatchery that seem to experience anything like fear.

The things that live in the cracks, too small to see clearly, too fast to track. They might be larvae. They might be a completely different order of being. They might be the same creatures at different stages of development—the Hatchery does not distinguish clearly between young and old, between one species and another. The categories that surface biology depends upon do not hold here.

And the deep-dwellers. The things that live in the pits, in the places where the light does not reach, where the warmth becomes oppressive and the air becomes thick with chemical signatures that no surface-dwelling creature could parse. They are rarely seen. They are perhaps the size of dogs, perhaps larger—mass determines shape in the Hatchery, and the deep-dwellers have mass. They consume the waste that accumulates at the bottom of the chambers, the bodies that have been stripped by the crawling things, the mineral deposits that the aerial swarms cannot reach. They are the Hatchery's digestive system. They may also be its reproductive system—there are suggestions, in the chemical traces they leave, that new chambers begin in the deep, that the process of stone formation originates in bodies like theirs.

But these are speculations. The deep-dwellers do not come to the light, and the light-dwellers do not go to the depths. The Hatchery has layers, and the layers do not mix.

---

The builders remain a question.

Someone made the cairns. Someone piled the stones into shelters with gaps just large enough for the crawling things to pass through but just small enough to exclude the spindly-legged predators. Someone dug the pits and lined them with flat stones. Someone built the low wall across the natural bridge, providing cover from the aerial swarms.

The crawling things build, yes—but their structures are nurseries, growing from the pellets that the observer-individuals deposit. They are not intentional in the way that the cairns are intentional. They are not designed for a body to rest in.

So there must have been builders. Or there must still be builders.

There are no bones in the cairns. No remnants of clothing, no tools, no markings of any kind. If the builders were human, they left nothing behind but the shapes of their absence. If the builders were human, they are gone now—or they have become something else.

Unless they are still here.

There is a figure, sometimes, on the highest ledges. Too distant to see clearly, too still to be a whistler. It does not produce sound. It does not move when the swarms pass. It is there, and then it is not there, and no evidence remains to suggest that it was ever present at all.

It might be a hallucination—the product of prolonged exposure to the Hatchery's strange atmosphere, the amber light playing tricks on eyes evolved for a different kind of seeing. It might be a whistler that has learned a different kind of patience. Or it might be one of the builders, or a descendant of the builders, still present in the space that they shaped, watching the creatures that may have learned from them.

If it exists—if the figure is more than illusion—it does not interact with the other inhabitants of the Hatchery. It does not eat. It does not sleep in any visible way. It simply observes. This would make it unique in the Hatchery: a creature without a function, a presence without a role. If the Hatchery has a place for such beings, it is not visible. If the Hatchery needs them, their purpose has not yet emerged.

---

But perhaps the builders were never human at all.

Perhaps they emerged from the Hatchery itself. Perhaps the same forces that produce the crawling things, the swarms, the whistlers—the same forces of emergence and pattern and slow accumulation—also produce occasional shapes that mimic intention without containing it. The cairns might be accidents of process. The walls might be residue. The pits might be the beginning of new tunnels that were never completed.

This is the horror of the Hatchery, if horror is the word. Not that it is alive, but that it might not be. Not that it has intention, but that it produces the appearance of intention without any mind behind it. The crawling things build. The stone forms. The patterns emerge. And none of it means anything.

But there is another possibility. The Hatchery might be a single organism. The crawling things might be its blood, the swarms its breath, the whistlers its nerves. The stone might be its body, still forming, still growing, still learning what shape it wants to take. The whole vast chamber and all its tributaries and all its layers and all its creatures might be a single entity in the process of becoming. A god in utero. A world being born.

In which case, the builders—the ones who made the cairns, the ones who still might watch from the highest ledges—would be something like its first thoughts. Its first attempts to understand itself. Its first glimmerings of a category that does not yet exist in the Hatchery's lexicon: the category of *I*.

---

And what lives in the Hatchery, in the end, is the Hatchery itself.
