# The People of Ashiin

## The Bodies They Inhabit

They are soft in the way things become soft in water.

Not weak—never make that mistake. But held in moisture for so many generations that their skin has learned to breathe through itself, porous and accepting, no longer fighting the damp. Visitors prune within days, their fingertips wrinkling, their skin protesting. The Ashiini do not. They have adapted to be permeable. They take in the air and the water and the spore-heavy breath of the place, and they let it pass through them, and they remain.

Their feet tell the first story. Broad and calloused, yes, but calloused differently than walkers on solid ground. These feet grip. Toes spread wide, almost prehensile, the webbing between them more pronounced than in lowland folk—not gills, not yet, but something moving in that direction over the slow centuries. They climb constantly, and it shows in the ankle-strength, the calf-density, the way they can suspend themselves from vines by their feet alone, hands free to work.

Their hands are the hands of people who negotiate with living things. Every adult carries the marks: thin scars from thorns that learned not to grab them, calluses from years of holding pruning-hooks, the faint stippled texture of skin that has handled resin and sap until the substances became part of the epidermis. Their fingerprints are worn smooth in places from decades of gripping the same holds, the same tools, the same familiar stalk-surfaces. Touch a wall in Ashiin and you will feel it differently than they do. They feel the pulse of it. The direction of growth. The places where it wants to go.

Their hair grows thick and straight, weighted by constant moisture into sleek sheets that cling to skulls and necks. Most wear it bound—braids weighted with carved wooden beads that serve dual purpose as decoration and as anchors for the thin lines they sometimes use to lower themselves through gaps in the canopy. The beads click against each other when they move, a percussion that cuts through the environmental noise, and you learn to recognize individuals by the particular music of their descent.

Their eyes have adapted to the filtered light. Large pupils, irises in shades that seem to shift with the canopy—browns that edge toward green, blacks that hold a faint amber ring around the center. They see differently too. Color distinction heightened in the green-blue spectrum, movement perception acute, the ability to read the health of a plant in the subtle shift of a leaf's angle, the early signs of blight in the slight curl of a growing tip. A visitor looks at a stalk and sees a stalk. An Ashiini looks at a stalk and sees history, health, age, growth-pattern, the trajectory of its future branching and the places where rooms might one day be carved.

Children are born into this knowledge. By the time a child in Ashiin can walk, they can already identify a dozen edible fungi by smell, know which vines will hold weight and which will crumble, understand intuitively that every surface is alive and must be treated accordingly. They are not taught this in formal lessons. They absorb it. They climb before they can speak clearly, their small bodies learning the architecture of growth the way lowland children learn the rules of a house—through bumping into things, through small falls, through the gradual accumulation of experience written into muscle and reflex.

## The Names They Carry

Names in Ashiin are not given. They are grown.

A child enters the world inside a stalk, in one of the birthing hollows that families maintain—small spaces carved with particular attention to air flow, moisture level, the presence of certain plants whose respiration eases the labor. The midwife waits. The family waits. And eventually, the child emerges into the wet light.

But they do not name the child then.

The name comes later, after observation. Days, sometimes weeks. The family watches. They notice what the child reaches for, what sounds catch their attention, how they respond to the constant noise of the canopy. They notice which stalk the child seems drawn to, which bridge-crossing makes them laugh, which corner of which room calms them when they cry. And from these observations, the name emerges.

Not a word, exactly. More like a description compressed into sound. A name in Ashiin might translate roughly to "the one who prefers the eastern bridge at dawn" or "she who hums with the evening chorus" or "he who always finds the dry stone." The name is a shorthand for the child's emerging relationship with the place. It grows as they grow, adding complexity and detail, occasionally shifting entirely if the child's nature changes.

This means that names in Ashiin are long. Not in syllables—they compress efficiently—but in meaning. When you know someone's full name, you know their history, their preferences, their place in the settlement, their family lineage, the particular stalk they were born in, and the trajectory of their life so far. Introductions between strangers take time. They involve gesture and reference, pointing to places, mentioning events, gradually building a picture of who each person is.

Outsiders find this exhausting. They want simple names, handles, things to call people that don't require understanding their entire existence. The Ashiini find this incomprehensible. How do you know someone without knowing these things? How do you interact with a person if you don't understand their relationship to the bridges they cross daily, the stalk they sleep within, the water-channel they drink from?

The names are also, necessarily, temporary. A person is not static. They change as they age, as their relationships shift, as they move between dwellings or take on new roles. The name changes too, gradually, the sounds and meanings stretching and compressing to accommodate growth. The name you have at ten is not the name you have at thirty. Your name at thirty is not your name at sixty. The elders have names as dense as forests, as layered as the growth around them, and they will spend an entire afternoon explaining those names to you if you ask, because they are not just telling you sounds. They are telling you who they have been.

## The Daily Rhythm

Dawn in Ashiin arrives with sound.

The cloud-warblers begin their looping calls before light penetrates the canopy, their songs filtering down through layers of leaf-mass, mixing with the clicking insects and the amphibian bellows until the noise becomes physical, a pressure in the chest. For visitors, this is overwhelming. For the Ashiini, this is an alarm clock. They wake to the chorus, bodies rising in the half-light, minds already attuning to the day.

The morning pruning begins immediately. Before full light, before the heat rises, while the stalks are still turgid with the night's moisture absorption, the residents move through the settlement with their hooks and saws, trimming back the growth that accumulated overnight. It must be done daily. In this environment, a neglected corridor closes within a week. A bridge left unpruned becomes impassable within a month. Every adult spends at least an hour each morning in maintenance—the work that keeps the settlement livable.

Children learn early. A child of five can handle a small pruning-hook, can identify the new growth that must be cut versus the mature branches that will form next year's structure. By ten, they are responsible for a section—a window to maintain, a corridor to keep clear. By fifteen, they are helping to train new bridges, to redirect vines, to plan the slow expansion of their family's dwelling. The work is constant, and it is communal, and it is the foundation of all other activity.

After pruning, breakfast. The damp bread, the roasted beetles, the paste made from ground larvae and fermented seeds. The food in Ashiin is deliberately bland to outsiders—the flavors muted, the textures uniform. But the residents taste differently. Their palates have adapted to subtle variations, to the mineral notes of different spring-waters, to the sweetness that certain fungi develop at specific stages of decay. They eat without ceremony, quickly, because the day's work waits.

The middle hours belong to craft and trade. The market amphitheater fills with residents exchanging goods and services—fiber-cords woven from trained vines, containers sealed with resin, tools carved from the dense inner wood of stalks that have reached the end of their lives and been respectfully harvested. No money. No currency. The Ashiini trade in time, in labor, in promises of future assistance. A length of cord buys a morning's help with pruning. A carved bowl buys attendance at a birth. The economy is personal, immediate, impossible to abstract.

During the hottest hours, retreat. The canopy provides shade, but the humidity intensifies, and the residents withdraw into the cooler hollows of their stalks. This is when the elders tell stories, when the children practice the rhythmic speech-patterns that allow communication through noise, when families sleep or work on small crafts. A kind of siesta, but not silent—the noise continues, and the residents rest *within* it, their bodies accepting the wall of sound as background, the way lowland folk accept the sound of wind or rain.

Evening brings the second chorus. The cloud-warblers sing again as light fades, different songs from the dawn, more complex, more variable. The insects intensify. The amphibians begin their territorial declarations. The noise rises to its daily peak, and the residents emerge from their hollows for the social hours—the time when bridges fill with people, when conversations happen through proximity and gesture and shouted fragments, when children chase each other through corridors that will change by tomorrow but are fun today.

Night is not quiet, but it is quieter. The noise drops to a background level, the persistent clicking and croaking of the nocturnal species, the drip of water from leaf to leaf. The bioluminescent fungi in the mulch-layer below glow faintly, their light rising through gaps in the architecture, casting strange shadows. The residents sleep in family groups, in hollows carved into stalks that have sheltered generations, in rooms that breathe with them, walls that expand and contract slightly with the day's temperature shifts.

The stalks are warm inside. Living wood, still conducting fluids, still growing, generates a small amount of heat. The hollows hold this warmth through the night, the way a body holds warmth under blankets. Residents sleep wrapped in thin fiber-cloths, their skin touching skin, their breath entering the same air, their dreams mixing with the sounds that penetrate from outside.

## The Intimacy They Inhabit

There is no solitude in Ashiin. Not really.

You can be alone in a room, but the room is alive. You can be separated from others by distance, but the distance is small, the walls are thin, the noise carries. Every resident lives within hearing of at least a dozen others, within touching distance during any shared passage, within the constant awareness that they are surrounded.

This has shaped them.

The Ashiini do not have the same relationship with privacy that outsiders do. They cannot. The concept of a private thought is different here—thoughts happen in context, in proximity, in the knowledge that anything spoken might be heard by anyone. They have developed instead a kind of collective privacy, a shared understanding that some things are not discussed outside certain circles, that some knowledge stays within families, that the failure to speak about something can itself be a form of protection.

But this does not mean they are secretive. It means they are discerning. They know each other intimately, and they know each other's boundaries, and they navigate the constant closeness with a grace that visitors struggle to match. The way you learn not to look at someone when they are having a private conversation in a public space. The way you learn to be present without being intrusive, to share space without claiming attention, to touch without demanding.

Touch is constant here. A hand on a shoulder to move past. Fingers brushing as tools are exchanged. The press of bodies in narrow corridors, the necessity of squeezing through gaps that require you to press against the person already there. Residents do not flinch from contact. They have learned to read it—the tension or relaxation in a shoulder, the quality of a touch, the way someone breathes when you're close to them. They know each other's bodies in ways that would seem invasive to outsiders but are simply functional here, part of the shared vocabulary of existence.

Arguments happen, of course. Disagreements, conflicts, the frictions of any community. But they resolve differently. You cannot storm away from someone when there is nowhere to go. You cannot avoid a person when you share a bridge, a water-channel, a morning pruning route. You must work it out. You must find accommodation. And so the Ashiini have developed a culture of patience, of persistent engagement, of the understanding that resolution is not optional—it is required by the architecture of their lives.

This extends to the dead. They do not bury their dead in the ground, because the ground here is alive, because the mulch-layer is necessary, because the cycle of growth and decay that sustains the settlement cannot be interrupted. They have a different practice. When someone dies, their body is wrapped in fiber-cloth and lowered into the mulch-layer at specific sites—places where the decay is most active, where the transformation back into soil will happen fastest. The family tends the site for a season, ensuring proper decomposition, and then they harvest from it. Not the body—they do not eat their dead. But the enriched soil. The mushrooms that grow there. The nutrients that have cycled through their loved one and become available to the living stalks.

The mushrooms from a grave-site are not eaten. They are planted. They are used to seed new growth, to enrich new hollows, to feed the stalks that will house future generations. The dead become the architecture. They become the home. It is not ghoulish to the Ashiini. It is the opposite. It is the deepest form of continuity—knowing that your ancestors are literally part of the walls around you, that they are still sheltering you, still present, just in a different form.

## The Knowledge They Carry

Writing does not work in Ashiin.

Paper molds. Ink runs. Bark-paper disintegrates in the humidity. They have tried other methods—carving into wood, etching into stone, weaving patterns into fiber—but none of these allows for the storage and transmission of complex information in the quantities needed. And so the Ashiini have developed alternatives.

Their oral tradition is dense, rhythmic, designed to survive noise. It is not quite song—the pitch does not vary in conventional musical ways—but it has a cadence, a pattern of stresses and pauses that allows it to cut through the environmental chorus. They speak in a way that visitors find strange: emphatic, percussive, with repetitions and variations that seem redundant until you hear them emerge from the wall of sound around you. Then you understand. The pattern creates a path through the noise. The rhythm allows recognition.

Knowledge passes through bodies as well as words. A child learns to identify edible fungi not by being told but by being taken, by handling, by watching their elders find and prepare. A young bridge-tender learns their craft not from instructions but from years of assisting, of watching where the cuts are made, of feeling the response of the plant over seasons. The knowledge is encoded in muscle and reflex, in the subtle adjustments of hand and eye that no written description could capture.

But some knowledge must be shared more broadly. The history of the settlement. The agreements between families. The location of resources, the patterns of migration, the timing of seasonal shifts that affect everything from food availability to predator behavior. For this, they have developed a system of notations carved into the inner walls of certain stalks—shelters, not homes, marked with symbols that indicate shared information. The carvings are maintained by a council of elders, and they use a system of pictographs and patterns that any resident can read, even if they cannot reproduce it.

The system is necessarily limited. It cannot capture nuance. It cannot tell stories. It can only mark: water-source here, danger there, harvest-ready in this section, avoid during the following season. The real knowledge, the deep knowledge, lives in the bodies and voices of the people, passed down through proximity and practice.

This makes the elders irreplaceable. They are the repositories of knowledge that cannot be written, cannot be easily shared, exists only in their memory and their bodies. An elder's death is a crisis not just because of loss but because of the information that dies with them—if they have not fully transmitted their knowledge, if they have not spent enough years teaching, the settlement loses something it cannot recover. And so the elders are protected, cherished, listened to. Their teaching time is protected. Their presence is valued beyond their practical contributions, because they *are* the library, the archive, the living record of everything the settlement has learned.

## The Outsiders They Encounter

Outsiders come, occasionally.

Traders, mostly, from the lower slopes, bringing materials that cannot be grown or crafted in the highland bowl—metal tools, carefully wrapped in waterproof cloth; certain spices that do not grow at this elevation; fabrics woven from fibers that resist mold. They come, they trade, they leave. They do not stay long. The noise, the damp, the constant contact—it is too much for people accustomed to different architectures, different densities, different relationships with space.

The Ashiini are patient with visitors. They recognize that outsiders need time to adapt, need space that cannot be easily provided, need a form of privacy that the settlement cannot offer without disrupting itself. They host visitors in specific dwellings set aside for this purpose—slightly larger, slightly more separated, still part of the whole but positioned to give the illusion of solitude. They speak slower, accommodate the communication difficulties, provide guides who understand that outsiders cannot yet hear the meaning in the noise.

Some visitors adapt. Over weeks, over months, they begin to learn. Their skin stops pruning. Their ears start to distinguish individual voices from the chorus. They develop the calluses, the reflexive knowledge, the comfort with contact. They become less visitors and more residents, and the settlement shifts to accommodate them—new names, new roles, new spaces in the architecture of relationships.

Some visitors do not adapt. They leave, and they carry stories of a strange place where people live inside plants, where the noise never stops, where you can never be dry, where the dead are fed to mushrooms. The Ashiini do not resent this. They understand that their way of life is not universal, that it requires a kind of surrender that not everyone can make, that it produces a kind of person that not everyone wants to become.

They do not try to convince. They do not proselytize. They do not believe their way is better—only that it is theirs, that it works for this place, that the place has made them and they have made themselves to fit the place. Outsiders who fit will stay. Outsiders who do not will leave. The settlement continues either way.

## What They Become

If you stay—if you come to Ashiin and remain, through the initial discomfort, through the adaptation, through the years and decades of living in the bowl—you change.

Your body changes. Your feet widen, your toes spread. Your skin becomes porous, accepting, capable of holding moisture without pruning, of drying without cracking. Your eyes adjust to the filtered light, and you begin to see the colors that were always there but invisible to you—the health of a leaf, the disease in a stalk, the readiness of a fungus for harvest. Your hearing becomes selective, capable of pulling meaning from noise, of tracking individual voices through the chorus, of knowing who is approaching by their footfall on a bridge.

Your mind changes. You stop expecting permanence. You learn that corridors close and open, that bridges grow and die, that the architecture of your life is always in negotiation with the living things that contain it. You learn patience—not the patience of waiting, but the patience of *working alongside time*. You prune knowing that the results will not be visible for months. You train a vine knowing that it will not bear your weight for years. You plant knowing that your grandchildren will harvest. You think in generations, because the settlement thinks in generations, because the stalks live longer than people do, because the growth you tend will outlive you.

Your relationships change. You lose the expectation of isolation, the sense that you need time alone, the belief that solitude is necessary for peace. You find peace instead in the presence of others, in the shared rhythm of pruning and cooking and sleeping, in the constant hum of lives being lived in proximity. You touch without thinking. You speak in the rhythmic patterns that cut through noise. You know your neighbors the way you know the stalks—intimately, functionally, as part of yourself.

You become, eventually, Ashiini. Not just a resident, but the kind of person this place produces. Soft-skinned, broad-footed, noise-adapted, touch-comfortable. Someone who wakes to the dawn chorus and knows immediately which birds are singing, which insects are hatching, which part of the settlement needs pruning today. Someone who navigates the bridges without conscious thought, whose body knows the paths, whose hands know the plants. Someone whose name contains a history, whose knowledge lives in muscle, whose presence in the settlement is part of the settlement's presence in the world.

This is who lives in Ashiin. Not a people who conquered the land, not a people who built over it, but a people who grew *with* it. Who became part of the canopy, part of the cycle, part of the constant living growth that holds the moisture, catches the clouds, breathes the air, and shelters the small human lives within its stalks. They are not separate. They never were. They are simply one more species in the dense, wet, loud, overwhelming ecosystem of the highland bowl. The difference is that they know it. They chose it. They made themselves to fit.

And in making themselves to fit, they made a way of being that works. Not forever—nothing here is forever. But for now. For this time. For as long as the stalks keep growing and the bridges hold and the chorus keeps singing.

They are the people of Ashiin. And they are home.
