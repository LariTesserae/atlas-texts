# What Lives on the High Everplain

---

## The First Ones

The insects were here before the trees. Before the moss. Before the first vine wrapped the first rotting trunk and began the long project of turning geology into biology. They were here when the plateau was bare stone, scraping lichen from rock with mouthparts evolved for exactly that purpose, and they are here now, unchanged in everything but number.

They do not live *on* the plateau. They are the plateau's nervous system.

---

The beetles come first, always, in any accounting. Not because they are the most numerous—_that distinction belongs to the gnats, those impossible clouds that hang in the still air above every water-collecting depression, dense enough to obscure the faces of people walking through them, so thick that inhaling through the nose means inhaling dozens of bodies, and the people who pass through here learn to breathe through cloth, through woven fiber masks that they hold against their faces, the gnat-bodies collecting in the weave like black sand. No. The beetles come first because they are the most *visible*, the largest, the ones that announce themselves with weight and presence.

There are dozens of species. The description could spend pages on them and not exhaust the variety, and even then, new species would be found in the next valley, the next depression, the next square mile of this endless green. But some recur. Some are everywhere.

The thumbnail beetles—dark-shelled, metallic in certain light, green and purple and copper depending on angle—they move through the ground-cover in steady columns, never deviating, never accelerating, following pheromone trails laid down by scouts that are themselves following moisture gradients. They eat dead plant matter. They are the first stage of decomposition, breaking down what falls, softening it for the fungi and the bacteria that do the real work. If you sit still in the vegetation and watch the ground, you will see them, hundreds of them, moving with purpose, their legs finding purchase on the spongy substrate, their bodies catching the filtered light and reflecting it back as small flashes of color. They are beautiful in the way that functional things are beautiful—no excess, no ornamentation, every surface serving a purpose. Their shells are armor. Their legs are traction. Their antennae are sensory arrays that map the world in chemical gradients rather than visual images. They experience the plateau as a landscape of smells—moisture here, decay there, the pheromone signatures of their own species threading through the air like highways.

The click beetles are larger, jointed, and they fly poorly, which is a problem on a plateau where flight is the most efficient mode of travel. They compensate with a mechanism: when threatened, when overturned, when they need to right themselves or escape, they snap a hinge in their thorax and launch themselves into the air with an audible click—the sound gives them their name—and the launch is violent enough to startle predators, to buy a second of confusion in which the beetle can reorient and try, once more, to fly. They are not elegant. They are effective. On the ground, they hunt smaller insects, moving through the leaf-litter with a clumsiness that seems at odds with the precision of their escape mechanism. They are the plateau's awkward children, tolerated by the ecosystem not for their grace but for their function, which is to eat and be eaten in roughly equal proportion.

The longhorns are the specialists. Their antennae extend twice the length of their bodies, sweeping the air ahead of them, reading it for the chemical signatures of dead wood—which is everywhere, on a plateau where the ground itself is composed of layers of dead wood, but which the longhorns must find in the right stage of decay, not too fresh, not too far gone, the narrow window when the lignin has softened enough for their larvae to bore through it and consume it from within. They find a fallen branch, a dying trunk, a section of the ancient mat exposed by erosion, and they lay their eggs in the cracks, and the larvae that emerge spend months—sometimes years—tunneling through the wood, eating as they go, their galleries becoming the architecture of decomposition, creating channels for water and fungi and bacteria to enter and accelerate the breakdown. The adult longhorns are slow, deliberate, their flight a laborious buzzing from one dead thing to the next. They mate on the surface of their chosen nursery, the male mounting the female while she tests the wood with her ovipositor, both of them vibrating with a low hum that is not quite audible but almost, felt more than heard, and then she deposits her eggs and they part and neither looks back. There is no parenting on the plateau. There is no need. The larvae emerge into a world made of food, and the world is generous.

The weevils are the gardeners. They tend the fungi—or at least, their behavior has the effect of tending it, whether intention is involved being a question that the people who study such things have not resolved. They carry spores on their bodies from one fruiting body to the next, from one fallen log to the next, from one patch of decomposition to the next, and where they walk, fungi follow. Orange shelf-fungi fruiting in clusters along the vines—the weevils put them there. Or they didn't, and the correlation is coincidence, the weevils and the fungi sharing habitat preferences rather than a relationship. The argument persists. The weevils continue their work regardless.

And the dragonflies—these are the hunters. They own the airspaces between branches, the corridors of open space that exist fleetingly in the canopy, the gaps where one great tree's leaves haven't quite met another's. They are large. Four inches, five, their bodies iridescent, catching the green-gold light and refracting it into blues and purples that have no other source on the plateau. Their wings are transparent, veined, moving so fast that they blur, and they hover, and hover, and hover, and then they move, and the movement is instantaneous, a teleportation that covers six feet in less time than the eye can follow, and when they land, there is a smaller insect in their legs, still struggling, still alive, being consumed methodically, the dragonfly's mouthparts working with a precision that is horrifying if you are the prey and irrelevant if you are not. They mate in the air, the pairs linked, flying in tandem, the male clasping the female behind the head with abdominal appendages evolved for exactly this purpose, and they lay their eggs in the temporary pools that form in the depressions, the eggs sinking to the bottom, hatching into aquatic nymphs that will live in the water for months or years before climbing out of the pool on a stem of vegetation, splitting their skin, and emerging as adults. The pools are temporary. The dragonflies time their emergence to the pools' existence, and if the pool dries before the nymph is ready, the nymph dies, and this is not a tragedy on the plateau—this is information. The dragonflies that survive to adulthood are the ones whose timing was correct, and they pass that timing to their offspring, and over generations, the population synchronizes with the water cycle, becomes a function of it, another element of the system reading the plateau's signals.

The mantises are the assassins. They are not insects that fly. They are insects that wait. They sit on branches, on stems, on the upper surfaces of leaves, their forelegs folded in the posture that gives them their name, and they are still, and they are patient, and they are invisible—not because they hide but because their bodies mimic the plant surfaces they sit on, the green of their exoskeletons matching the green of the leaves, the brown of their joints matching the brown of the stems, the texture of their wings matching the texture of the bark. They are the same color as the world around them, and they do not move, and so they do not exist, and then something small lands within range, and the forelegs unfold with a speed that defies the patience that preceded it, and the prey is caught, and the mantis begins to eat, and the eating is slow, methodical, starting at the head and working backward, the prey alive throughout most of the process, its struggles diminishing as the mantis consumes it section by section. The mantises are not cruel. They are not kind. They are hungry, and the plateau provides.

---

The cicadas are the voice. Not the whole voice—the soundscape is a choir, not a solo—but the cicadas are the dominant register, the baseline drone that everything else sits atop, and without them the silence would be so sudden and so total that the people who have lived here would not recognize the plateau without it.

They live underground. For years. Five, seven, some species thirteen, some seventeen, the nymphs buried in the plant-mud, the living substrate, feeding on root exudates, on the fluids that the vegetation exudes as it grows and dies and grows again, and they are patient in a way that nothing aboveground can match, waiting in darkness for the signal that tells them it is time.

The signal is temperature. The substrate reaches a particular warmth, a threshold that varies by species but always correlates with the plateau's seasonal rhythm, and the nymphs emerge, climbing out of the ground in numbers that defy counting, climbing the nearest vertical surface—tree trunk, vine, the wall of a woven structure—and they split their skins, and the adults emerge, pale and soft at first, drying in the filtered sunlight, their wings expanding, their exoskeletons hardening, and within hours they are calling.

The call is produced by a mechanism unique to cicadas—tymbals, ribbed membranes on the abdomen that buckle inward and outward, producing clicks at frequencies that, when produced by thousands of individuals simultaneously, merge into a sustained tone that fills the air from ground to canopy. The tone varies by species—some produce a high, sustained whine, others a pulsing throb, others a rising-and-falling wail that has the quality of an air raid siren, and each species occupies a frequency band that does not overlap with the others, partitioning the soundscape the way the vegetation partitions the light. They do not compete acoustically. They coexist.

The chorus peaks at midday, when the temperature is highest, and drops to near-silence at night, when the cold-blooded cicadas cannot generate the muscle contractions required to drive the tymbals. The silence they leave is not silence—it is the absence of one layer, the exposure of the layers beneath, the insects that call at night, the frogs, the mammals, the creak of the vegetation itself. But it is felt as silence by anyone who has spent a day in the chorus, a sudden lightness in the air, a sensation of pressure releasing.

They mate. They lay eggs in the stems of plants, the ovipositors slicing into the living tissue, depositing rows of eggs that will hatch into nymphs that will drop to the ground and burrow and disappear and wait. And the adults die. Within weeks of emerging, they are gone, their bodies littering the ground-cover, their wings catching in the vines, their exoskeletons still clinging to the bark where they emerged, empty husks that the rain will soften and the beetles will carry away and the fungi will colonize and the system will absorb. They were here. They called. They reproduced. They died. The plateau continues.

## The Watchers

The birds are the plateau's awareness. If the insects are the nervous system, the birds are the eyes—distributed, mobile, attending to everything, missing nothing, their attention shifting across the landscape in patterns that an outside observer could, with enough time, learn to read as a real-time map of events on the plateau.

They are not one species. They are hundreds, and the description cannot do justice to hundreds, but patterns emerge.

---

The canopy flocks are the most visible. They move in groups of dozens, sometimes hundreds, species mixing freely, the flock a shifting, dissociating, recombining mass that rolls through the emergent trees like a single organism, each bird responding to its neighbors' movements with a synchronization that has no visible signal, no apparent leadership, just a collective response to stimuli that propagates through the flock at speeds that seem to exceed the speed of individual reaction. They are foraging. The canopy is a feeding ground—insects in the bark, fruits on the branches, nectar in the flowers—and the flock moves through it systematically, each species occupying its niche, the short-billed insectivores probing bark, the long-billed nectar-drinkers reaching into the deep-throated flowers that bloom from the trunks of the great trees, the fruit-eaters grasping berries in their feet and tearing them apart with decisive bites. They are not competing. They are partitioning, each species so precisely adapted to its food source that overlap is minimal, the evolutionary pressure of a saturated ecosystem having pushed each lineage into ever-narrower specializations.

The colors are impossible. Or they should be, by the logic of camouflage—why stand out in a world of green and brown and more green? But the plateau's light is filtered, green-gold, and in that light, the blues and oranges and reds that the birds display are not conspicuous but *legible*, signals that read clearly to conspecifics but dissolve into the background for predators hunting by shape and movement rather than color. The birds know what they look like to each other and what they look to everything else, and the difference is the point. They are speaking a visual language that only their own species can parse, hiding in plain sight, and the brilliance of their plumage is not vanity but cryptography.

The watchers—the ones that land on branches near people and tilt their heads and examine with unsettling intelligence—are the generalists. They are not locked into a single food source, a single niche. They eat insects and fruits and seeds and small vertebrates, and because they are not specialized, they are curious, because curiosity is how generalists survive—they must learn what is edible, what is dangerous, what is new, what has changed. They watch because watching is their primary survival strategy, and they approach because they have learned, over generations, that the people who pass through are not predators—or are not *usually* predators—and the risk of proximity is outweighed by the opportunity of observation, the chance to learn something about a new thing in their environment that might, eventually, matter.

When one calls and others respond, it is not alarm. It is announcement. *There is a new thing here. Come look.* And they come, dozens of them, settling on surrounding branches, tilting their heads in that characteristic gesture that is not curiosity performed for an audience but genuine investigation, their eyes—large, dark, reflective, adapted to the filtered light—focusing and refocusing, their brains processing, categorizing, filing. They are learning. They will remember. If another person comes through this area, weeks or months later, the birds will respond differently—they will be less alarmed, less attentive, having already categorized *this type of thing* as *not threatening*, and their calls will change, the announcement becoming briefer, less urgent, until eventually, if the passage becomes regular, the birds will ignore it entirely, absorbing the new element into their baseline model of the world.

---

The ground-birds are different. They are heavier, built for walking, their wings shortened and their legs lengthened, their bodies leaning forward in a posture that suggests perpetual motion, and they move through the ground-cover in small groups, scratching at the substrate, flipping leaves, probing the soft plant-mud with beaks designed for the work—short, thick, powerful. They eat insects, mostly. The beetles, the larvae, the eggs laid in the vegetation by species that never see the ground. They are the plateau's janitors, cleaning the surfaces, turning the material, and their scratching is a sound—dry, papery, rhythmic—that sits in the lower registers of the soundscape, a percussion beneath the melody of the canopy birds and the drone of the insects.

They are wary. They do not approach. They maintain distance, and when a person moves toward them, they move away, not flying—flight is a last resort, an emergency measure that expends energy they cannot easily replace—but walking, accelerating smoothly from a scratch to a stroll to a brisk trot to a run that carries them into the vegetation and out of sight within seconds. They will fly if pressed. The flight is explosive, noisy, a burst of wing-beats that startles everything within earshot, and the bird rises from the ground-cover and crashes through the vegetation to a low branch, where it sits and watches with an intensity that communicates its displeasure, its crest raised, its posture rigid. It will come down when the threat has passed. It will resume its scratching. It will pretend the interruption never occurred.

---

The raptors are present but invisible. You know them by their effect—the absence of small birds in a particular area, the warning calls that cascade through the canopy, the way the insects seem to pull back, to go quiet, as if the air itself has tightened. They hunt from above, from the top of the canopy, from the spaces between the emergent trees where the sky is visible, and they drop through the foliage in controlled falls, their wings tucked, their bodies streamlined, striking prey on branches or in midair with a precision that suggests they can see through leaves, which they cannot, but they can see movement, and movement is all they need. They take birds, mostly. Sometimes the small mammals that live in the canopy. Sometimes insects, the large ones, the mantises and the cicadas, snatched from their perches and carried aloft to be dismembered on a branch in the emergent layer.

They are solitary. They are territorial. They do not tolerate others of their kind within their hunting range, and their hunting range is large—square miles, not acres—and the boundaries are marked not by sight lines or physical features but by sound, a high, piercing cry that carries across the canopy at dawn and dusk, announcing ownership, declaring presence, saying *I am here and this is mine* in a language that every other raptor understands and respects. They mate for life, or for as long as the partnership is functional, and they nest in the emergent trees, building structures of woven branches that are the avian equivalent of the people's dwellings—temporary, requiring maintenance, subject to the vegetation's constant efforts to absorb them. They raise one chick at a time, sometimes two, and the chick grows slowly, dependent on the parents for months, learning to fly, learning to hunt, learning to read the plateau's signals, and then it leaves, and the parents are alone again, and the cycle continues.

---

The night-birds are a separate category, a separate world, species that evolved for the darkness that falls when the sun drops below the horizon and the bioluminescence begins. They are silent. They hunt by sound, their ears asymmetric—placed at different heights on their heads, which allows them to triangulate the source of a noise with enough precision to strike prey they cannot see. They eat the night insects, the moths and the beetles that fly after dark, and they eat the small mammals that forage in the ground-cover at night, and they sit on branches in the canopy and wait, and the waiting is so complete, so total, that you can look directly at a night-bird and not see it, your eyes passing over it without registering, the bird's silence so profound that it renders itself absent. You hear them only at dusk and dawn, when they give their territorial calls—low, resonant, the kind of sound that seems to come from the ground rather than the air, that vibrates in the chest rather than the ears, and that the people who live here have learned to associate with the transition between light and dark, the boundary between the day-world and the night-world, the moment when one set of creatures goes to sleep and another wakes.

## The Callers

They are not quite primates. They are something older, something that diverged from the primate lineage before the plateau existed, before the mountains wore down, before the biomass accumulated to the point where the ground became flesh. They have been here longer than anything else with a backbone. They remember this place in a way that memory, as humans understand it, cannot encompass—not as images or narratives but as patterns, rhythms, the seasonal shifts in temperature and moisture that their bodies track without conscious effort, the circannual clock that tells them when to move, when to stay, when to mate, when to rest.

They are the callers. The howler-equivalents, the source of the guttural roars that carry miles across the plateau, threading through the insect drone and the bird calls, a sound that is not music and not noise but communication, pure and functional, stripped of everything except the need to be heard.

---

They live in the emergent layer. The great trees are their homes, the canopy their world, the ground a place they visit only when necessary and leave as quickly as possible. They are large—the males a hundred pounds, the females seventy—and they are built for the arboreal life, their arms longer than their legs, their hands and feet prehensile, their tails thick and strong and capable of supporting their full weight, a fifth limb that frees the other four for climbing, reaching, grasping, manipulating. Their fur is dark—brown-black, almost invisible in the shadowed canopy, and they do not groom it for appearance. They groom for health, for parasite removal, for the maintenance of social bonds, and the grooming is a practice so embedded in their social structure that an individual that is not groomed is an individual that has been excluded, shunned, pushed to the edge of the group, and this is a signal too, read by every other individual in the group and acted upon—no one mates with the un-groomed, no one shares food with the un-groomed, no one comes to the defense of the un-groomed, and the un-groomed individual either reintegrates or leaves, and leaving is usually death.

The calls are the focus. They begin in the morning, before the light has fully reached the canopy, the males inflating their throat sacs—specialized structures, hypertrophied hyoid bones that create a resonating chamber in the throat, amplifying the call, directing it, giving it the carrying power that allows it to travel miles through dense vegetation. The sound begins as a low rumble, almost subsonic, felt before it is heard, a vibration in the chest, and it builds, crescendoing into a roar that seems to come from everywhere at once, the trees themselves amplifying it, the canopy reflecting it, the air carrying it in directions that defy the expectation of line-of-sight transmission. The call says: *I am here. This is my territory. My group is here. We are strong. Do not approach.*

The females call differently—shorter, higher, more conversational, the calls used for intra-group communication, for coordinating movement, for alerting the group to food sources, for warning of predators. The male calls are broadcast. The female calls are narrowband, directional, intended for a specific audience—the group, not the world.

They live in groups. A dominant male, several females, their offspring, sometimes a subordinate male tolerated because he is useful—for predator detection, for infant care, for the occasional genetic contribution that keeps the group's diversity from collapsing. The subordinate male knows his place. He does not call the territorial roar. He does not mate unless the dominant male is distracted or absent. He grooms the dominant male more than the dominant male grooms him. He is, in every visible way, subordinate, and he will remain so until he challenges, or until the dominant male dies, or until he leaves and tries to join another group as a dominant male himself, a risky strategy that fails more often than it succeeds.

Their diet is the plateau's diet—leaves, fruits, flowers, the occasional insect consumed accidentally with the plant matter, not sought but not rejected. They eat what the trees offer, and the trees offer abundantly, the biomass so total that almost every surface is edible, almost every branch carries fruit or flowers or young leaves tender enough to strip and chew. They do not need to travel far. A single emergent tree can feed a group for weeks, and they move when the food in one tree is exhausted, shifting to the next, their movement slow, deliberate, the mothers carrying infants on their bellies, the juveniles racing ahead, testing their skills on branches they will one day own.

They are aware of the people who pass through. They watch from the canopy, silent, their dark forms invisible in the shadowed foliage, and they do not call—they do not need to, the people are not competitors, not predators, not relevant—and they do not approach, and they do not flee. They simply watch, and when the people have passed, they resume their lives, their calls beginning again, the roar building in the morning, the conversation threading through the day, the slow rhythm of a group that has lived here for longer than the people have existed and will live here for longer than the people will.

## The Herds

They are the plateau's largest creatures, and they are the only ones that walk the ground by choice.

Not by preference—the ground is not their habitat, the canopy is, but the canopy cannot support them. They are too heavy. A thousand pounds, fifteen hundred, the males larger, bulkier, their shoulders hunched with muscle built for pushing through vegetation that resists passage with the accumulated stubbornness of a living mass that does not want to be moved. They are the architects of the plateau's only temporary paths, the trails that appear and disappear with their passage, the trampled ground that heals within days as the vines and ground-cover reclaim what was pushed aside.

They are not cattle. They are not deer. They are not any creature that has a name in any language except the ones spoken on the plateau, and those names are descriptive rather than taxonomic: *the large ones that walk, the brown ones, the ones that make the ground shake*. They are something that evolved here, on the plateau, in isolation, their lineage diverging from everything else so long ago that comparison is meaningless. They are ungulate-adjacent. They have hooves—or structures that serve the function of hooves, thick keratinous pads on the toes that grip the spongy substrate without sinking too deeply. They have legs built for endurance rather than speed, the muscles dense and slow-twitch, the joints broad and stable, the gait a walk that can be maintained for hours without rest. They do not run. Running is not an option at their size, in this vegetation—the density of the undergrowth would stop them, the vines tangling their legs, the branches blocking their path. They push. They lean. They move.

The herds number in the hundreds. They are not always together—the herd fragments, subgroups splitting off, rejoining, the composition shifting constantly—but they are connected, the subgroups communicating through the low moans that travel through the ground as much as the air, felt in the feet before heard in the ears, a seismic signal that carries information about location, about direction, about the presence of food or water or danger.

They follow the water. The temporary pools, the depressions that collect condensation and rain—these are their destinations, their waypoints, the landmarks in a landscape that has no landmarks. They move from pool to pool, drinking deeply, the water filtering through their massive digestive systems, emerging as urine and feces that the vegetation absorbs immediately, the nutrients returned to the system, the cycle unbroken. They are mobile composters, their bodies processing the plateau's biomass, breaking it down, returning it in forms that the plants can use, and the plants do use it, the vegetation around the herd's resting places greener, denser, more vigorous than the surrounding growth.

The males carry horns—or structures that serve the function of horns, bony cores covered in keratin sheaths that grow from the skull in spirals or curves or straight projections that serve no purpose except display and combat. They fight. The males fight, in the mating season, the herds pausing while two males face each other, lowering their heads, their horns interlocking, and pushing, the contest one of strength and leverage, not violence, the loser giving way, retreating, accepting a position in the herd's hierarchy that he will hold until the next mating season, when he will try again. The fights are quiet. No roaring, no dramatic displays. Just the creak of horn against horn, the scrape of hooves on the plant-mud, the heavy breathing of two large bodies straining against each other, and then it is over, and the herd moves on.

The young are born able to walk. They emerge from their mothers already large, already mobile, already capable of keeping up with the herd, because the herd does not wait. Within hours of birth, the calf is walking, stumbling, finding its legs, and within a day it is keeping pace, its mother staying close, the other females forming a loose protective ring, and the predators watch from the middle levels of the canopy and calculate the odds and usually decide that a calf surrounded by a hundred fifteen-hundred-pound adults is not worth the risk.

Usually.

## The Predators

They are the plateau's editors.

Not destroyers. Not villains. Editors—selecting for fitness, removing the weak, the sick, the slow, the old, the unlucky, shaping the populations they hunt with a pressure that is as formative as any environmental factor. Without them, the herds would grow until they overgrazed their range. The canopy flocks would expand until they stripped the trees of insects and fruit. The callers would multiply until their groups fragmented into units too small to defend themselves. The predators are the limit, the check, the boundary that keeps life on the plateau from consuming itself.

---

The first kind is the ambusher. It lives in the middle levels of the vegetation—neither canopy nor ground, the zone between, where branches are thick enough to support weight but light enough to flex, where the filtered light creates a permanent twilight, where the angles of vision are confused by the density of growth. It is large—the size of a large dog, but lower, longer, built for concealment and sudden acceleration rather than sustained pursuit. Its fur is patterned in a way that breaks up its outline—mottled, irregular, the greens and browns and blacks merging into a visual texture that matches the vegetation so precisely that it disappears when motionless. And it is motionless. For hours. It finds a position—a branch overlooking a game trail, a gap in the vegetation near a water source, a place where the ground-cover is thin enough that prey will pass through—and it waits, and the waiting is so complete that its heartbeat slows, its respiration drops, its body temperature adjusts to match the ambient air, and it becomes, for all practical purposes, a part of the vegetation.

It hunts by patience. The prey comes to it—the herds moving through, the small mammals foraging, the ground-birds scratching—and it watches, and it calculates, and it selects. Not the strongest. Not the fastest. The weakest. The one that limps. The one that pauses too long. The one that is distracted by food or water or social interaction. The one that, in the calculus of survival, has the lowest probability of escaping. This is not cruelty. This is efficiency. The predator cannot afford a failed hunt—the energy expenditure is too high, the recovery time too long, the risk of injury too significant. It must succeed, and it succeeds by selecting prey that cannot escape, and in doing so, it removes from the population the individuals that are least fit, and the population that remains is stronger.

The strike is fast. Not fast in the way of the mantis—no explosive, impossible acceleration. Fast in the way of a released spring—stored energy discharged in a single motion, the predator launching from its position, crossing the gap between branch and prey in a leap that covers the distance in less time than the prey needs to react, and the impact is the end, the weight of the predator driving the prey to the ground, the teeth finding the throat, and the prey dies quickly, not slowly, the predator's efficiency extending even to the kill—prolonged death is wasted time, wasted energy, an opportunity for the prey to injure the predator, for other predators to intervene, for the commotion to attract attention that the predator does not want.

It eats. It eats quickly, tearing the carcass apart, consuming the high-value portions first—the organs, the muscle, the fat—leaving the rest for the scavengers, the insects, the decomposers that will reduce the remains to nutrients that the vegetation will absorb. It does not gorge. It eats what it needs, and it leaves, and it finds another position, and it waits again, and the cycle continues. It hunts every few days, sometimes less frequently, the intervals determined by the success of the last hunt and the availability of prey, and it is always hungry, and it is always patient, and the two conditions coexist without contradiction, the hunger driving the patience rather than undermining it.

---

The second kind is the tracker. It hunts on the ground, and it is larger, and it is built for endurance rather than ambush—a walker, a follower, a creature that finds its prey and stays with it, wearing it down, matching its pace, appearing behind it, beside it, ahead of it, a constant presence that the prey cannot escape because the tracker does not stop. It does not sprint. It does not charge. It walks, and it follows, and it follows, and the prey runs, and the prey stops, and the tracker appears, and the prey runs again, and the prey stops, and the tracker appears, and this continues until the prey cannot run, and then the tracker closes, and it is over.

They are social, the trackers. They hunt in pairs, sometimes in small groups, and their coordination is silent—not the howling of wolves, not the dramatic pack behavior that dominates stories of cooperative hunting. They communicate with glances, with body language, with subtle shifts in posture and direction that each individual reads and responds to without conscious thought. They have done this before. They have done this their entire lives. The hunt is not planned; it is performed, a practiced sequence of behaviors that each individual knows and executes in the appropriate order, and the coordination emerges from shared knowledge rather than active communication.

They are territorial. They patrol their range—the same range, day after day, season after season, the boundaries marked by scent, by sight, by the accumulated evidence of their presence: claw marks on bark, feces deposited at prominent locations, the remains of kills that serve as advertisements. *I am here. I hunt here. This is mine.* Other trackers respect the boundaries. Conflicts are rare and, when they occur, are resolved through display rather than violence—two groups facing each other across a clearing, posturing, vocalizing, the tension building until one group gives way, not because they are weaker but because the cost of conflict exceeds the value of the contested territory, and this calculation is not conscious but evolutionary, the survivors being the ones who knew when to fight and when to withdraw, passing that knowledge to their offspring in the form of instinct rather than instruction.

They fear nothing on the plateau except the plateau itself—the vegetation that grows over their scent marks, obscuring them; the rain that washes away their tracks, erasing the evidence of their passage; the slow, patient growth that absorbs everything, including the dens they dig in the softer substrate, the vegetation colonizing the walls, the roots threading through the roof, the den becoming less a den and more a plant structure that happens to contain a predator. They maintain their dens, pushing back the growth, reinforcing the walls, and the maintenance is constant, ongoing, a tax imposed by the plateau on anything that attempts permanence.

The third kind is the hunter of the canopy. Smaller, agile, living in the emergent trees, hunting the callers and the canopy birds and anything else that moves through the upper layers. It is the predator that the warning calls describe, the stuttered alarm that spreads through the flock, the sudden silence that falls over a section of canopy. It is quick. It is silent. It moves through the branches with a fluidity that seems impossible for its size, its weight distributed across four feet, its tail providing balance, its eyes—forward-facing, binocular, the mark of a predator—tracking prey with a focus that excludes everything else.

It strikes from above. It positions itself above its prey, on a higher branch, a different tree, and it drops, and the drop is controlled, the tail steering, the limbs adjusting, and the impact is precise—the forelimbs seizing the prey, the hindlimbs anchoring on the branch, the teeth dispatching the prey before it has time to struggle.
