# What Lives in the Hollowing Works

The carvers come first because the carvers were here first. Or rather, the carvers and the stone arrived together, and it has never been entirely clear which of them is shaping the other.

There are perhaps forty of them who hold the title—forty who are trusted to read the grain of limestone and know, before the first strike, whether the rock will open graciously or resist. They are not all men. They are not all old, though the oldest among them are the most respected, and their hands have a particular look: the skin across the knuckles is pale and tight from perpetual damp, the fingertips are broad and calloused in patterns that mirror the texture of the stone they work. They hold their ch—their picks, their chisels, their wedges—with a grip that looks loose to anyone who has done manual labor above ground. The carvers do not fight the stone. They lean into it. The tools are extensions of attention, not force.

The senior carver is a woman named Achet. She is perhaps fifty—age is difficult to track here, where the light does not mark the years the way sun does—and she has been reading stone since before the descent. Her mother read stone on the surface, in a quarry that no longer exists, in a place she does not name. Achet does not speak much. When she does, it is usually to say *no*. No, do not cut there. No, that vein goes deeper than it appears. No, the chamber is not ready to be widened. The other carvers defer to her not through hierarchy but through a recognition that her *no* has been correct more often than anyone else's *yes*. She has a scar on her left forearm where a fracture ran the wrong way two decades ago and the stone opened like a mouth and took a piece of her with it. She does not cover the scar. She does not explain it. The younger carvers touch their own arms when they pass her in the corridors, an unconscious gesture, a kind of sympathetic magic.

---

Around the carvers, the settlement arranges itself like tissue around bone.

The clearers come next in importance, though they would not describe themselves that way. These are the workers—men and women both, and some who are neither, because the settlement has lost interest in categories that the surface once found meaningful—who follow behind the carvers and move the rubble. The raw stone comes out of the working face in chunks and dust and slabs, and it must go somewhere. The clearers load it into baskets, into slings, into the rough sleds that slide along grooves cut into the passage floors specifically for this purpose. They haul it to the upper levels, where it is sorted: usable stone for construction and tool-making in one pile, waste material to be carried to the surface and dumped downslope in another, finer dust saved for the packing of floors and the sealing of cracks. The clearers are the strongest people in the settlement, and they are also the most sociable. The work permits conversation in a way that carving does not. The clearers know everyone's business. They are the settlement's informal news network, carrying information along with the rubble.

Then there are the channelers—fewer of them, perhaps a dozen—who maintain the drainage system. This is detail work, unglamorous, ceaseless. The channels cut into the floors are shallow, no deeper than a finger, and they must be kept clear or the condensation will find other paths. The channelers move through the settlement on their knees, scraping mineral buildup from the channel grooves with flat tools, checking the gradients, looking for places where the water has begun to pool or divert. They smell like wet stone. Their knees are permanently calloused. They are, without knowing it, the settlement's most essential workers, because if the drainage fails, the settlement fails. The damp is not an inconvenience here. It is a structural feature. If it is not managed, it will unmake everything.

---

The lamp-tenders are mostly children.

This is not exploitation in the way the surface would understand it. It is practical. The oil lamps are set into niches carved into passage walls at varying heights, and many of them are most easily reached by someone small. The children who tend the lamps move through the settlement in pairs—never alone, because the rule is old and absolute—and they carry small clay flasks of oil and trim the wicks with their fingers, pinching off the charred ends, and they know every passage, every turn, every inhabited and abandoned hollow. They know the settlement by its light. When they describe a place, they describe it by the quality of its illumination: *the corner where the lamp burns yellow, the passage where the wick always smokes, the junction where three lights are visible at once and the air smells like the upper shafts.* They are the settlement's cartographers, though they would not use that word. They do not make maps. They do not need to. The lamps are the map, and the children carry it in their heads.

They are not paid for this work. They are fed for it—extra portions, the best of what is available, because a hungry child will rush, and rushing near open flame in a stone passage is how people die. The settlement learned this in its first generation, when a lamp-tender dropped an oil flask in a deep passage and the fire took three chambers before it burned itself out. No one died, but the smoke lingered for weeks, and the chambers were uninhabitable for months after. Now the lamp-tenders are well-fed and patient. They learn patience as they learn the passages: by repetition, by getting lost, by finding their way back.

---

The families live where they can.

A family in the Hollowing Works is not always a matter of blood. The term has expanded to include those who share a hollow, who share a cooking fire, who share the cost of a lamp. A hollow might hold a woman and her three children and the woman's brother and the brother's partner and the partner's mother. It might hold two couples who were strangers before the descent and who now sleep separated by a curtain of woven fiber that smells like damp and the bitter fruit. It might hold an elder who outlived her family and was taken in by a younger household because the settlement does not leave its people alone—alone, in the deep, is dangerous. Alone is where the dark finds its way in.

The children who are born here—third generation now, small and pale and quick—do not fear the dark the way their grandparents did. They do not know what the dark *is*, because they have never been in a place where darkness was not punctuated by lamplight or the distant gray of a surface shaft. They play in the passages when the foot traffic is light, chasing each other through routes that the adults lose track of, hiding in crevices that the clearers have not yet sealed, climbing the rough walls where tool marks make natural handholds. They are the settlement's scouts. They find things. A child named Peret, who is perhaps six, discovered the narrow fissure behind the third-level grain storage that opens, if you wriggle through it on your stomach, into a natural chamber no one had mapped. The chamber was empty. It was also warm—warmer than any carved space, as if the stone itself was holding heat from something deeper. Peret told the other children. The children told no one else for three days, because they wanted it as a hiding place. Then Peret told her mother. Her mother told Achet. Achet went to look at the chamber. She sealed it herself, with her own hands, with stone dust and water and a paste that hardens over days into something almost like the surrounding rock. She did not explain. The children asked. She said *not this one.* They did not ask again, but they remember. Peret, who is six, will remember the warmth of that chamber for the rest of her life, though she will not be able to describe it, though she will not be certain, as she grows, whether it was warm because the stone was warm or because the small dark space was warm with her own body's heat and the bodies of the children who pressed in beside her.

---

The elders are fewer than you would expect.

The descent was three generations ago, but three generations is not three lifetimes. The first-comers—the ones who swung the first picks, who chose the site, who remembered why—most of them are dead. The ones who remain are in their seventies or beyond, which is old for this place. The cold and the damp and the limited diet take something from the body that the surface did not take. The elders' joints ache in ways that the settlement's healers attribute to the stone itself, as if the limestone were slowly claiming them back, pulling their calcium into its own structure, making them part of the geology. The healers are not certain about this. They are certain about the pain.

There is a man named Sabit who was among the first to descend. He is not a carver; he was a farmer, before. He planted the first herbs near the first light shaft, and the cultivated courtyard three levels down was his idea, and the twisted trees that bear the bitter fruit were his seedlings, carried down from the surface in a pouch against his chest. He talks to the trees. He talks to the soil that he has built, handful by handful, from stone dust and waste and the slow accumulation of organic matter. He does not talk to people much. When he does, he talks about the surface—the color of the sky before rain, the way certain plants smell when the sun is on them, the sound of wind in grass—and the younger people listen with a curiosity that is also a kind of pity, because they cannot imagine what he is describing and they suspect he may be embellishing, or that memory has made the surface more beautiful than it was. They are wrong about this. If anything, Sabit's memory has made the surface less beautiful than it was, because he does not trust beauty that cannot be touched, and he does not want to make the young people want something they cannot have.

There are eleven elders. They live together in a hollow near the central chamber, close to the light, close to the warmth of other bodies. They do not need to be told that they are the settlement's memory. They know that when they die, the surface dies a little more in the collective imagination. They are careful about what they carry. They are careful about what they let go.

---

The healers are four women and one man who learned what they know from a woman who is now dead, who learned it from her mother, who learned it from a surface tradition that no longer exists in any complete form. They work with what is available: the herbs from the light shafts, the fungi that grow in the abandoned passages (some of which are medicinal, some of which are deadly, and the healers know which is which by taste), the mineral deposits that the channelers scrape from the drainage grooves, which contain a compound that reduces inflammation when applied as a paste. They are not doctors. They are something older. They are people who have looked at what the environment offers and have learned, by trial and by desperation, what it can do for the human body.

They know the lung-sickness that comes from the deep levels, where the air is thick and the oil smoke collects. They know the skin conditions that come from the perpetual damp. They know the bone-ache that the elders complain of. They do not have cures for all of these things. They have management. They have comfort. They have the knowledge that some conditions are simply the cost of living here, and that the body will adapt to most of them if given time and patience and enough of the bitter fruit, which contains something—no one is sure what—that the body seems to need.

The healers do not speak about what they cannot cure. They speak about what they can manage. This is not denial. It is strategy. Despair is a contagion that the settlement cannot afford.

---

There is a settlement-enforcer, though that is not his title. His name is Kebi, and he is large—not fat, but dense, built like a column of stone—and he speaks in a voice that carries through the carved corridors with a resonance that sounds geological, as if the stone is amplifying him. He does not enforce the rules through violence. He enforces them through presence. When Kebi arrives at a dispute, the dispute tends to quiet. When Kebi stands in a doorway, the doorway is full. When Kebi says *this is the decision,* the decision has usually already been made by others, but his saying it makes it real, gives it weight, anchors it in the stone.

He was not chosen for this role. He grew into it, the way certain formations grow in limestone—by accretion, by the slow accumulation of trust. He is not a carver, not a clearer, not a channeler, not a healer. He is the person who is there when the settlement needs someone to be there. He sleeps little. He eats when food is brought to him, which is often, because the settlement understands that Kebi is doing work that no one else wants to do and that the doing of it costs him something. He has a family—a partner, two children—but they see less of him than they would like. His partner, a woman named Tesfi, is a clearer, and she understands. She does not like it, but she understands.

When someone breaks a rule—digs without approval, blocks a channel, extinguishes a lamp—it is Kebi who comes. He does not punish. He explains. He explains what the rule is for, what the consequences of breaking it are, what the settlement has decided about people who put others at risk. The explanation is usually enough. The settlement is small enough that reputation is currency, and being the person Kebi had to come speak to is a cost that most people will not pay twice.

When the explanation is not enough, the settlement gathers in the central chamber, and the matter is decided collectively. This has happened three times in the settlement's history. The decisions were: exclusion from the deeper levels, temporary. Reassignment to the channeling crews, indefinite. And once, for a carver who broke through into a sealed space and would not say what she saw, removal to the upper levels and a prohibition against carving. The carver, a woman named Denit, still lives in the upper levels. She tends the herb gardens near the light shafts. She does not speak. She does not need to. Everyone knows what happened, or they know enough, and the silence around her is a kind of monument to the rule she broke.

---

The settlement has its children, its lovers, its old grudges, its petty thefts and its generous gestures. It has people who sing while they work—a low, rhythmic chanting that matches the strike of the tools, that helps the carvers time their blows. It has people who do not sing and are irritated by those who do. It has people who are very good at carving and people who are very bad at it, and the settlement has found other roles for the bad ones, because there is no surplus of labor here, and every pair of hands is needed.

It has a woman named Iuni who makes the oil. The oil comes from the rendered fat of the small animals that are caught in the lower passages—the four-legged hunters that came from no one knows where—and from the seeds of the bitter fruit and from something else, something Iuni will not discuss, that she prepares in a sealed chamber that no one else enters. The oil she produces is consistent in quality and burns with a clean, low flame. She is the settlement's most private person. She lives alone, which is permitted because her work requires isolation, and because no one wants to share a hollow with the smell. She is not unhappy. She has a cat—not a cat exactly, but one of the small four-legged things that has been domesticated over two generations, that kills the insects and sits on her shoulder while she works, that purrs with a sound like stone settling.

---

The settlement has its myths. The goat that became a beast. The fox that knew too much. The sealed spaces in the deep, which may or may not contain formations too regular to be natural, which may or may not breathe with a current of air that tastes different from the air above, older, mineral, as if it has been circulating through spaces that have been closed since before the limestone was limestone. The carvers do not speak of these things to outsiders, because there are no outsiders, but among themselves they speak of the patient waiting, and they mean by this something that they cannot quite articulate: the sense that the stone is not indifferent to them. The sense that the hollowing is a conversation, and that the other party in the conversation is listening.

Achet says, when asked directly, that the stone does not think. She says this with a flatness that is not conviction but a kind of protective wall, a sealed chamber around an opinion she will not share. The other carvers watch her when she says it. They note what she does not say. They do not press, because pressing Achet is like pressing stone: you will learn nothing, and the effort will cost you.

---

And below everything, below the carvers and the clearers and the channelers and the lamp-tenders and the families and the elders and the healers and the enforcer and the oil-maker and her not-quite-cat, below the central chamber where decisions are made, below the deepest inhabited hollow, below the drainage channels and the abandoned passages where the pale fungi spread their velvet colonies, below the places where the carvers have stopped—below all of this, there is whatever there is.

No one who lives in the Hollowing Works can say what it is. They know only that the carvers sealed it, and that the carvers do not seal things lightly, and that the rule against speaking of it is newer than the other rules, which means it was made in response to something, which means something happened, which means something is there. The settlement lives above it the way a person sleeps in a house with a basement they have never visited. The floor is solid. The walls are sound. But the knowledge of the space beneath is always present, a kind of undertone, a low note in the settlement's music that most people have learned not to hear.

The children hear it. The children who play in the deepest passages, who find crevices and fissures and chambers that the adults have not mapped, who press their ears to the stone and listen—they hear it. They do not know what it is. They know only that the stone makes sounds that are not the sounds of tools, not the sounds of settling, not the sounds of water. Sounds that are regular. Sounds that have a pattern. Sounds that might be nothing, that are probably nothing, that the carvers say are nothing, that Achet says are nothing with the flatness of a sealed wall.

The children are not afraid. They have never learned to be afraid of the stone. The stone is their world. But they are curious, and curiosity in the deep is a thing with consequences, and the settlement knows this, and Kebi watches the children, and the lamp-tenders watch the children, and the carvers watch the stone, and the stone—

The stone is warm.
