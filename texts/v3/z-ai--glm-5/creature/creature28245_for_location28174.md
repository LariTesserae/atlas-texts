# What Lives Here

The first answer is the obvious one: the moss. The moss lives here the way weather lives somewhere—it is not an inhabitant but a condition. Every surface that holds moisture holds moss, and every surface holds moisture, so moss is everywhere, and to describe it as living here is like describing the air as living here. Technically accurate. Missing the point.

But it is where you start, because it is what you see first, and because it is what sees you first, in whatever sense moss sees. It has been watching since before you arrived. It will watch after you leave. Its rhizoids are threaded through the steel at a depth that would surprise you if you could see it—not merely coating the surface but laced *into* the corroded grain of the metal, a secondary root structure that has become load-bearing in places where the original steel has thinned past usefulness. The moss is holding up parts of the Works. Not many. But some. Enough that if you pulled all the moss out, something would shift. The moss does not know this. It is not the kind of organism that knows things. It grows where it grows because the growing conditions are there, and the growing conditions are everywhere, and so it grows everywhere, and some of that everywhere happens to be structurally critical, and the moss does not care, and the Works does not care, and the arrangement holds.

The vines are the same, in a larger key. Cable-thick. Woody. Leaves the size and approximate shape of an open hand, reaching. The vines are the closest thing this place has to architecture—not the original architecture, which was steel and function, but the current architecture, which is organic and accidental. They bridge gaps where catwalks have fallen. They brace struts that have buckled. A vine that has anchored itself to a vertical pipe and climbed thirty feet to the next terrace is now, functionally, a structural member, and it will remain a structural member until the pipe it is anchored to corrodes through, at which point the vine will sag, find a new anchor, and resume its work. This happens constantly. The vines are always re-routing. They grow toward light, and the light in the Works is distributed by the gaps in the structure, and the gaps in the structure are always changing as floors sag and walls shift, so the vines are always following a moving target, always redirecting, always in the process of abandoning one path and committing to another. From a distance, they look still. From close up—close enough to see the tendrils at the growing tip—they are visibly, relentlessly searching.

The ferns are quieter. Ferns do not search. Ferns *arrive*. A spore lands in a crack where water pools, and the crack is deep enough and the water is consistent enough, and a gametophyte grows, and a sporophyte grows from the gametophyte, and now there is a fern where there was not a fern, and the fern will be there until the water changes or the light changes or the crack closes, and then the fern will not be there, and this is not a tragedy for the fern. The fern has released ten million spores. Some of them, statistically, will land in other cracks. The ferns do not experience loss. They experience distribution. In the Deep Terraces, where the tree ferns grow twenty feet tall from the accumulated muck of former pump stations, the ferns have been distributing themselves for so long that they have formed a canopy—a fern canopy, a spore-and-frond canopy, filtering the graphite light into something even dimmer and greener, a light that is specifically *their* light, a light that exists because of them and for them and that nothing else uses because nothing else is down there to use it. The Deep Terraces are the ferns' country. You can walk through it, but you are a tourist. The ferns know this. The ferns do not mind.

---

The fungi are less visible and more important.

You notice them first as smell—that green-bread, that overripe, that damp-flour scent that sits under everything in the Works, the baseline olfactory note of the place. It is the smell of decomposition, which is to say it is the smell of conversion, which is to say it is the smell of the process that turns dead moss into soil and dead fern fronds into humus and dead vines into the dark fibrous muck that builds up in the lower terraces and becomes ground. The fungi do this. All of this. The fungi are the metabolic engine of the Works, the thing that keeps the nutrients cycling, the thing that prevents the whole system from strangling on its own accumulated dead matter. They are everywhere, in the soil that is not yet soil, in the moss that is dying, in the wood of the vines, in the corroded steel itself—because some of the fungi here have evolved to metabolize iron, or rather to metabolize the organic compounds that form in the corrosion layer, the interface between rust and biology, and they are accelerating the conversion of metal into earth in a way that no one designed and no one intended but that the system absolutely requires, because without new soil the system stagnates, and the fungi are the thing that prevents stagnation, and they do this by eating everything that has stopped moving.

There is a slime mold that lives in the lower Works. Or there are many slime molds. It is hard to tell. They merge and separate and merge again, and the individual organism is a concept that does not map well onto something that can be a single creature or a million creatures depending on conditions. The slime mold lives in the anaerobic ooze of the marsh-terraces, the places where water has sat long enough to become proper wetland, and it moves through the ooze at a speed that is not visible in real time but that is visible over days—a yellowish network that extends and retracts, exploring, feeding, mapping the nutrient gradients of its environment. If you put a camera on time-lapse and pointed it at a marsh-terrace for a week, you would see the slime mold pulsing, flowing, retracing, advancing—a slow yellow thought moving through black mud. No one has done this. No one will. But the slime mold does it anyway, because the slime mold is not performing for an audience. It is solving a problem that is its own existence, and it is solving it at a tempo that the Works does not share and does not need to share, and this temporal independence—this slowness relative to the plants, this quickness relative to the corrosion—is part of what makes the fungi and slime molds the hidden inhabitants of the Works, the ones who live here most fully because they live here most invisibly, because their lives are conducted at scales and speeds that make them functionally invisible to anything walking past on two legs.

---

The wingless moths live in the moss.

They are the only insects that have achieved any real density here, and they have done it by abandoning everything that moths are supposed to be. No wings. No light to navigate by. No flowers to feed from. They live their entire life cycle in the moss—the eggs laid in the rhizoid layer, the larvae feeding on dead plant matter and the organic film that forms on damp metal, the adults crawling through the moss like tiny pale beetles, mating, dying, being consumed by the fungi that will also consume the moss they lived in. Their entire world is a few cubic centimeters of moss. They will never see the sky. They have no reason to. The sky, in the Works, is just another surface, and the surfaces that matter to the moths are the surfaces they can reach by crawling, and those surfaces are all moss, and the moss is all they need.

They are, in a way, the most adapted creature here. Everything else—the plants, the fungi, the rare birds—is using the Works, is making a life in a place that was not designed for them. The moths were *made* by the Works. Or they made themselves, over generations, by the slow pressure of living in a place with no light and no flowers and no reason to fly. They are the Works' own creature, the thing it produced from itself, the way it produced soil from steel and marsh from ductwork. They are small and pale and unremarkable and they are the only animal here that truly belongs.

---

The rail bird is a problem.

There is one. Maybe there are more. In three days of walking the terraces, you might see one—a dark-feathered, leggy thing, picking its way through a shallow pool with the tentativeness of something that knows it is alone. It is a species of rail, almost certainly derived from something that lived in wetlands before the Works existed, something that found its way up here through the valley—when there was a valley—and adapted to the marshes that the Works created, and stayed, and became this.

The problem is the aloneness. A single bird—or a handful of birds spread across kilometers of terraces—is not a population. It is a relic. The rail is here because it was here, and it is still here because it has not yet not been here, and the deep structural question of whether it is surviving or merely persisting is a question that the Works does not answer because the Works does not deal in questions. The bird is the kind of living thing that makes you aware that this ecosystem, for all its lushness, for all its balance, for all its self-perpetuating closed-loop botanical magnificence, is *incomplete*. Something is missing. Many things are missing. The herbivores that would eat the grasses. The insects that would pollinate the ferns (though the ferns don't need pollination, spores being different). The amphibians that would live in the marshes. The food web that would make this a full ecology and not a botanical monoculture with a few marginal animals hanging on at the edges.

The rail does not seem to suffer from this awareness. The rail has its own awareness, which is the awareness of a bird that is looking for invertebrates in shallow water, and this awareness is sufficient for the rail, and the rail does not require the Works to be anything other than the place where the shallow water is, and the invertebrates are mostly not there, but sometimes they are, and the rail finds them, and this is enough. The rail's relationship to the Works is unmediated by concept. The Works is where the rail is. The rail is where the water is. The water is where the pipes are. The pipes are where the Works is. It is a small closed loop, the rail's world, and it does not intersect with most of the rest of the system, and the rest of the system does not notice.

---

There are people.

Not many. Not enough to count as a population in any meaningful sense. A handful, maybe. Living in the upper terraces, where the air is slightly less damp and the light is slightly more present, in spaces that the Works has accidentally made habitable—a former control room with a door that still closes, a storage bay with a roof that has not yet rusted through, a section of catwalk where someone has laid down additional grating and built a rough shelter from sheet metal and vine. The shelters are not buildings. They are negotiations with the space—claims staked on a few square meters of surface that the Works is not actively using, and the claims are temporary in the way that everything here is temporary, and the people who make them know this, and they make them anyway, because you have to live somewhere, and somewhere is here.

They are thin. Not starving—the terraces produce enough in the way of edible plants (certain ferns, certain grasses, some mushrooms that are not poisonous) and the water is clean enough to drink from the pipes that carry the aquifer water, the cold water, the water that has never touched the outside air—but thin in the way that people who live at altitude and eat a marginal diet and expend significant energy simply navigating their environment are thin. They are not unhealthy. They are *efficient*. They have the economy of the place—the economy of a system that does not waste anything, that does not accumulate reserves, that runs at exactly the rate it runs at and no faster.

There are not enough of them to have a culture in the way the word is usually used. There are not enough of them to have a language, even—they speak whatever they spoke when they arrived, or they don't speak, or they speak in the shorthand that people who have lived in the same small space for too long develop, a system of gesture and sound that is not a language but a protocol, a way of coordinating movement through a tight environment without wasting energy on communication. If there are three of them in the upper terraces, they know each other the way beams in a structure know each other—they are load-bearing for each other, they hold each other up, and this holding is not an emotional bond or a social contract but a structural fact, a physics of dependence, and if one of them were removed the others would sag, would find a new equilibrium, would hold, because the system is redundant and the system does not collapse from single failures. But they would notice the gap. They would sag and then they would adjust and then the sagging would become the new shape, and this is what it is to live in the Works.

The people who live here—if they live here, if you can call it living here—are not caretakers. That is the important thing to understand. They are not maintaining the Works. They are not operating the pumps. They are not pruning the vines or clearing the drains or repairing the grating. The Works does not require their labor. They are here the way the rail is here: because they are here, because they have not yet not been here, because the Works is large enough and complex enough and generous enough (in the sense that a landscape can be generous, in the sense that any environment with enough niche space is generous to whatever can fit in a niche) to support a few humans at the margins without noticing the cost.

They know the Works the way you know weather. Not as a system understood but as a pattern felt. They know which terraces flood after a heavy condensation day. They know which pipes carry cold aquifer water and which carry surface runoff. They know where the moss is thick enough to walk on without the grating giving way beneath it. They know which vines will hold their weight and which will pull free. They know the sound the Works makes when it is settling—specifically, the particular groan that means a platform three levels down has shifted, and the particular silence that means a pump has stopped, and the particular hum that means everything is still running, and the difference between these sounds is the difference between safety and danger, and they hear it the way you hear a car horn: automatically, without translating, the signal directly into the response, no thought required.

They do not think of the Works as alive. They do not think of it as dead. They do not think of it as a machine or an ecosystem or a ruin. They think of it as *the place*, and the place is where they are, and the place does what it does, and they do what they do inside of it, and these two activities do not intersect very often because the people are very small and the Works is very large and the Works is busy with its own processes and the people are busy with theirs and the overlap is minimal—a few square meters of grating here, a section of catwalk there, a path through the terraces that the water has not yet claimed and the vines have not yet covered—and they live in the overlap and they do not live anywhere else.

One of them—we will not say which, because it does not matter, because they are all the same kind of thing in the way that all moss in the same crack is the same kind of thing—has a habit. When the light brightens at midday, when the overhead glow intensifies to something almost amber, this person stops what they are doing and stands in the brightest available patch of light for the duration of its peak—maybe an hour, maybe less—and this standing in the light is the closest thing to worship that exists in the Works. Not worship of the sun, which the person may or may not know exists as a concept. Worship of the light. Worship of the only variable, the only thing that changes in a place where everything else is constant—the light, which is sometimes more and sometimes less and which at its most is almost warm, almost gold, almost something other than the graphite constant, and the person stands in it because it is the moment when the Works is most itself and also most other-than-itself, and this is worth standing still for, and this person has learned that without being taught it, and it is the one thing about the Works that cannot be explained to someone who has not stood in the light.

---

But the deepest inhabitant is the water.

Not the pipes. Not the pumps. The water itself. The water that moves through everything, that is in everything, that falls from every surface and rises through every crack and pools in every depression and feeds every root and corrodes every steel and carries every spore and fills every lung and touches every skin. The water is the oldest thing here. It was here before the Works. It will be here after. The Works was built for it and by it and because of it, and now the Works is made of it, in the way that a body is made of blood—in the way that a body is mostly water and the water is mostly the body and the distinction is academic.

The water lives here the way the moss lives here: as a condition, not an inhabitant. But the water is more than a condition. The water *moves*. The water *chooses*. Not consciously—water does not choose—but in the way that water always chooses, which is the way of finding the path of least resistance, which is the way of finding the crack, the gap, the seam, the joint, the place where the structure has weakened enough to let it through, and this finding is the engine of the Works, the thing that drives the constant rebuilding, the constant rearranging, the constant slow landslide of water and vegetation and corrosion. The water decides what the Works looks like tomorrow. The water decides which terrace floods and which drains and which fern colony dies and which moss colony thrives and which vine redirects and which strut buckles. The water is the author. Everything else is the text.

And the water is patient. The water has been working on the Works since the day it was built, and it has not finished, and it will not finish, because finishing is not what water does. Water dissolves and deposits and dissolves again, carries minerals from one place and leaves them in another, eats steel here and builds soil there, and the cycle is not a cycle but a spiral, a helical process that does not return to where it started but moves sideways through time, always creating, always destroying, always making the Works into a different Works, and the Works of today is not the Works of yesterday and the Works of tomorrow will not be the Works of today, and the water is the reason, and the water does not know it is the reason, and the water does not need to know, and the water runs, and the Works goes on.

---

What lives here?

The moss. The vines. The ferns. The fungi. The slime molds. The wingless moths. The single rail, or the few rails. The water. The pumps, in their mechanical not-alive way, cycling in the deep guts because no one has told them to stop. The lights, in their sealed rooms, glowing for no one. The people, at the margins, in the overlap, standing in the midday light because the light is the only thing that changes.

And something else. Not a creature. Not a presence. Something more like a *disposition*. The Works itself, in the way that a forest is a thing and not just a collection of trees. The Works as a single entity, a single process, a single living thing made of steel and water and moss and time. It does not think. It does not feel. It does not want. But it *persists*, and it *adjusts*, and it *responds* to perturbation by finding new equilibria, and these are the characteristics of life, or close enough to life that the distinction is the kind of distinction that matters more to philosophers than to the thing being described.

The Works lives here. It is what lives here. Everything else—the plants, the people, the water, the rust—is part of it, the way your cells are part of you, the way your blood is part of you, the way your breath is part of you. The Works is a body. It is not a metaphor. It is a body in the only sense that matters: it is a system that maintains itself, that processes energy, that grows and decays and grows again, that has a boundary (the cloud line, the terraced walls, the last platform before the air thins) and an interior (the Deep Terraces, the fern wells, the marshes, the dripping corridors) and a metabolism (the water cycle, the corrosion cycle, the plant-fungus-soil cycle) and a pulse (the pumps, the midday light, the slow groan of settling steel) and it is alive in the way that a river is alive, in the way that a forest is alive, in the way that a coral reef is alive—not because it has a central nervous system but because it is a self-organizing, self-maintaining, self-transforming system that has found a way to continue, and continues, and will continue, long after the people at the margins have gone and the rail has gone and the light in the sealed rooms has finally flickered out, and the only thing left will be the water and the moss and the iron turning slowly into earth, and even that is not an ending, because the earth will grow the plants and the plants will hold the water and the water will find the cracks and the cracks will become the structure and the structure will become the Works and the Works will go on being itself, and this is what lives here, and this is what has always lived here, and this is what will always live here: the process, the continuing, the *going on*.
