# What Lives in the Refinery Vats

---

## The Floor Mat

Begin with what covers everything.

They are not insects. The distinction matters and becomes more important the longer you watch. Insects follow a body plan that Earth settled on hundreds of millions of years ago—head, thorax, abdomen, six legs, bilateral symmetry, a logic of segmentation that repeats in predictable ways. These creatures follow a different logic. Their bodies are built on a five-fold symmetry, or something close to it, and the segments do not repeat so much as spiral. Each segment is slightly rotated from the one before it, so that what begins as a forward-facing mouthpart spirals around the body in a slow twist until the rearmost segments have completed almost a full turn. The effect is that of a living drill bit, or a creature that has been wrung like a cloth.

They are pale. Not white—nothing here is white—but the color of mineral deposits, of compressed calcium and sulfur compounds, of the pale rust that forms on iron in dry conditions. A desaturated orange-brown that reads as beige in the dim light. Their carapaces are translucent at the edges, and through them you can see the darker traces of their internal chemistry, the fluids that serve them as blood moving in slow peristaltic waves through channels that do not correspond to veins.

There are at least seventeen distinct forms within what appears, at first glance, to be a single carpet of movement. The differences are subtle—mouthpart configuration, the number of spiral segments, the placement of what must serve as sensory organs—but the behaviors diverge more sharply than the morphology. Some graze. Some tend. Some process.

The grazers work the mineral deposits with mouthparts that have evolved into scraping and grinding structures of remarkable specificity. Each form targets a different mineral layer, a different stratum of the accumulated deposits. Some work the surface, skimming the softest layer. Some bore into the deposits with a rotation of their spiraled bodies, creating narrow wells that they descend into until only their rearmost segments are visible. Some work at the boundary between deposit and stone, the geologic equivalent of eating the crust while leaving the bread. They move in waves—hundreds of individuals descending on a section of wall or floor, processing it with a thoroughness that would strip paint from a battleship, then moving on. The areas they have grazed are not bare; they are simply at a different stage in a cycle. The deposits will regrow, fed by the ambient chemistry and the waste products of the creatures themselves. The cycle turns.

The tenders are stranger. They occupy the machinery—not the open surfaces, but the gears, the conduits, the joints and moving parts. They move through the mechanisms with a familiarity that suggests either long evolution or something more deliberate. They clear debris from gear teeth. They break down mineral accretions that would otherwise seize moving parts. They cluster at bearing points, their bodies producing an oily substance that serves as lubricant. When a section of pipe cracks—a rare event in this stable environment—they converge on the breach in numbers that suggest a chemical alarm, and their bodies pile against the crack, sealing it with the resinous compound they secrete when stressed.

Are they maintaining the machinery? The question is not as simple as it appears. The machinery does not need to function for the ecosystem to persist—the Refinery's original purpose is long extinct, and the creatures have built their own purposes on top of its remains. But the machinery does still move in places. Residual power flows through some conduits. Some gears still turn, driven by geothermal heat or by processes that no one alive remembers the origin of. The tenders keep these systems running, or keep them from failing entirely. Whether this is maintenance or monument-keeping—or something else, some behavior that has no human analog—cannot be determined from observation alone.

The processors are the most numerous and the least individual. They are the recyclers, the decomposers, the ones who reduce the dead to components. When a creature from the middle layer falls to the floor—exhausted, consumed, or simply at the end of its life—the processors find it within minutes. They do not consume it in the way that a scavenger consumes carrion. They disassemble it. They move across the body in coordinated lines, their mouthparts working in sequence, breaking the dead creature down into components that are sorted, processed, and distributed. Chitin goes one direction. Mineral content goes another. Fluids are channeled into the deposit layers. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The closed loop of the Vats depends on this efficiency, and the processors are the mechanism of its dependence.

They reproduce constantly. You see it happening on every surface—the coupling of individuals, the deposition of egg-cases in the crevices of machinery and rock, the emergence of new individuals from cases that have been incubating in the mineral warmth. The young are smaller but fully formed, miniature versions of the adults that begin working within minutes of emergence. There is no childhood here. There is no learning period. The knowledge they need is encoded in their bodies, in the spiral of their segments, in the chemistry of their fluids. They know what to do because they are what to do.

## The Middle Air

Above the floor mat, the air is occupied.

This is the layer where the word "creature" begins to require a broader definition. The middle-layer fauna are not a simple elevation of the floor forms. They are something else entirely—something that has found a different solution to the problem of existence.

The first type you notice—and you notice them because they are large, perhaps fifteen centimeters across—are the gliders. They have no wings. They have no visible means of propulsion. They move through the air of the Vats by riding the currents that the ecosystem itself generates—the heat rising from the floor mat, the pressure differentials created by the larger creatures' movement, the residual airflow from the ancient ventilation system. Their bodies are flat, discoid, with a subtle concavity on the underside that seems to generate lift through a mechanism you cannot identify. They are the color of the air itself—a pale nothing that only becomes visible when they pass between you and the light source, briefly occluding the dim glow.

The gliders are filter feeders. They process the air itself, straining the microscopic organisms that saturate every cubic centimeter of the Vats' atmosphere. Their undersides are lined with a dense mat of cilia-like structures that trap and compress the airborne life, forming it into a paste that is drawn into a central mouth on the disc's underside. They are the vacuum cleaners of this ecosystem, and they are essential—without them, the air would become so thick with microorganisms that visibility would drop to zero, and the chemical exchanges that the system depends on would be choked.

But the gliders are also prey. The hunters work the middle air with a precision that is terrifying if you watch it long enough.

The hunters are larger—thirty centimeters, sometimes forty—and they move on many legs. Six, eight, twelve—the number varies between forms, and within a single form it can change as the creature matures, adding segments and legs in a process that suggests a hybrid of growth and metamorphosis. Their bodies are elongate, segmented, with a flexibility that allows them to fold and unfold in the air, compressing themselves to fit through narrow gaps between machinery and expanding to their full length when they have room. They are the same pale mineral color as everything else, but their carapaces have a serrated quality—edges that catch the light differently, that seem designed to disrupt the silhouette and make the creature difficult to track against the complex background of the Vats.

They hunt by chemical signature. The air of the Vats is a medium of information—every creature's metabolic processes release compounds that mark its presence, its species, its state of health. The hunters read this information with organs located along their flanks—not noses, not antennae, but something more like a distributed chemical array, a system that can detect the direction and concentration of a chemical signal the way a human ear detects the direction of a sound. When a glider passes, its chemical trail hangs in the air for seconds, and the hunter follows it with the patience of a process that has been optimized over geological time.

The kill is fast and graceless. The hunter extends its body to full length, seizes the glider with paired appendages near the head, and folds itself around the prey in a motion that compresses the glider's body until the internal structures rupture. Then it feeds—messily, urgently, tearing pieces from the disc with mouthparts that are more like a锯 than like jaws. The feeding takes less than a minute. The remains drop to the floor, and the processors arrive.

But the hunters are also hunted. In the upper reaches of the middle layer, where the air is thinnest and the light is strongest, the apex predators of the air work in patterns that suggest both strategy and something else—something that might be called patience, or might be called programming so complex that it becomes indistinguishable from intention.

These are the largest of the middle-layer creatures. Their bodies are perhaps a meter long, though their length is difficult to determine because they are never still, never fully extended. They move by a combination of air-current riding and active propulsion—their bodies generate pulses of pressure that push them through the air with a speed and control that the gliders cannot match. They are not camouflaged; they are transparent. You see them only as a distortion in the light, a ripple in the air that moves with purpose and direction. They hunt the hunters, and they hunt in pairs, coordinating their movements through the same chemical channels that the other creatures use for communication.

When they take a hunter, they take it whole. There is no disassembly, no feeding frenzy. The distortion in the air moves across the hunter's body, the hunter's chemical signature terminates, and the distortion moves on. The only evidence that anything has happened is the absence—the silence where there was sound, the stillness where there was movement, the gap in the pattern that closes within seconds as other creatures move to fill it.

There are other forms in the middle air. Forms that cling to the walls and ceilings, forms that burrow into the mineral deposits and emerge only to feed, forms that seem to exist in a state between living and inert—resting in crevices for long periods, their metabolisms slowed to a crawl, until some chemical trigger activates them and they emerge in a burst of activity that lasts for hours before they return to their dormant state. The diversity is overwhelming, and it is not static. New forms appear—mutations, variants, individuals that have found a new niche and exploited it. The ecosystem is not frozen in its current state; it is still evolving, still finding new solutions, still filling gaps that you did not know existed.

## The Deep Layer

You have been avoiding it. The observation point gives you a view of the deep section—the lowest part of the Vats, where the floor curves down into a natural sump and the ancient machinery is densest. The light does not reach here easily. The creatures' bodies occlude what little illumination filters down, and the deeper sections exist in a perpetual twilight that is not dark but is not light either. The sound is different here. The bass drone you have been feeling since you descended is strongest in the deep section, and it is not—has never been—a single sound. It is a composite, a chord, a harmony produced by the synchronized movement of thousands of bodies working in patterns that have been established over so many generations that the patterns themselves have become part of the environment.

What lives in the deep section is the question that the Vats have been asking since you arrived.

The small creatures here are different. They are larger than the floor-mat forms—ten centimeters, sometimes more—and their bodies are not spiraled but folded, layered, like geological strata compressed into a living form. They move slowly, deliberately, with a weight that suggests density beyond their size. Their carapaces are dark—not pale but the dark of iron oxide, of manganese deposits, of the deep mineral layers that the grazers above cannot reach. They work the deep machinery with a focus that the surface tenders lack. They are not merely maintaining; they are operating.

The machinery in the deep section is not dead. This is the revelation that takes time to accept. The ancient Refinery's systems have not survived in their original form—the erosion and the creatures have transformed them—but they have survived in a form. The gears that the deep-layer creatures tend still turn. The conduits they maintain still carry something—not the original process fluid, but a compound that the creatures themselves produce, a viscous substance that serves functions the original designers never intended. The control panels that have long since failed as control panels have become something else—chemical processing sites, where the deep-layer creatures bring raw materials and leave with processed compounds. The machinery has been colonized so thoroughly that it is no longer possible to distinguish the mechanical from the biological. They have become a single system.

And in the center of the deep section, in the sump where the accumulated deposits are thickest and the ancient structure is most intact, the thing that does not fit the patterns rests.

## The Old One

It is not a creature. Or it is a creature, but that word is insufficient. It is also a machine, or the remnant of a machine, or the descendant of a machine. It is also a place.

It is large—three meters across at its widest, and its widest is not its full extent because its full extent is not visible. Parts of it extend into the machinery, into the rock, into the mineral deposits. It is integrated with the Vats in a way that suggests it has been here since before the Vats were the Vats, since before the ecosystem established itself, since before the original Refinery fell silent. It may be the reason the Refinery fell silent. It may be what filled the silence.

Its body—its structure—its form is a composite of biological and mineral elements that cannot be separated. The surface is a carapace, but the carapace incorporates metal, incorporates stone, incorporates the corroded remnants of industrial components that have been incorporated into its growth. Beneath the carapace, structures that might be organs or might be mechanical components or might be both pulse with a slow rhythm that matches the bass drone of the deep section. The rhythm is the drone. This is the source.

It does not move often. When it does, the movement is tectonic—slow, massive, the shifting of something that has settled into its position over deep time and does not change position without reason. The deep-layer creatures part before it. The middle-layer creatures fall silent. The floor-mat creatures accelerate their activity, processing the chemical traces it leaves with an intensity that suggests they are receiving and redistributing information, not merely consuming resources.

When it moves, the Vats change. The air currents shift. The temperature gradients adjust. The chemical composition of the atmosphere alters subtly—the balance of sulfur compounds, the concentration of the volatile solvents that give the air its characteristic taste. The entire ecosystem responds to its movement the way a forest responds to a change in weather—not through any conscious action, but through the accumulated sensitivities of a million organisms that have evolved to exist in its presence.

It has eyes. This is the detail that disturbs you most. It has structures that are recognizably, unambiguously eyes—not compound, not the distributed sensory arrays of the other creatures, but lensed, focused, directed. They are set into what might be called a head, if a head can be a structure that is also a control panel and also a growth of mineral crystal and also a joint in an ancient pipe system. The eyes are pale, almost colorless, and they track movement with a precision that the chemical-sensing creatures cannot match.

The eyes watch you. They have been watching you since you descended into the Vats. The attention of the middle-layer creatures, the tracking of the hunters, the chemical alarm that has been spreading through the ecosystem since your arrival—all of it originates here. The Old One is the system's awareness. It is the processing center, the memory, the organ of attention that takes the millions of inputs generated by the ecosystem and synthesizes them into something that resembles—if you are being honest with yourself—cognition.

It is not thinking. Not in the way that you think. But it is doing something that occupies the same space, that serves the same function. It is processing information, integrating it, and acting on it. The actions are small—the slight shifts in position, the changes in the chemical signals it releases, the subtle modulation of the bass drone that the deep-layer creatures interpret as instruction. But the effects propagate through the entire ecosystem. When the Old One shifts, the Vats shift. When the Old One signals, the creatures respond. When the Old One watches, the system watches.

You do not know what it wants. You do not know if "want" is a category that applies. What you know is that it has been here for a very long time, that it has shaped this place into what it is, and that its attention has settled on you with the weight of a process that does not know how to be interrupted.

## The Ecosystem as Organism

The longer you observe, the more the boundaries between the categories blur.

The floor-mat creatures maintain the machinery. The machinery provides structure for the ecosystem. The ecosystem supports the Old One. The Old One regulates the ecosystem. The cycle is not linear; it is a closed loop, and every element depends on every other element. Remove the floor-mat creatures and the machinery seizes. Remove the machinery and the ecosystem loses its architecture. Remove the ecosystem and the Old One has no inputs. Remove the Old One and the ecosystem has no regulation, no direction, no processing.

The Vats are not a place where creatures live. The Vats are a creature. A single organism composed of millions of individual organisms, integrated with the remains of an ancient industrial system, organized around a central processing entity that has been growing and changing and becoming more itself for longer than the surface has known the current configuration of its continents.

The sound is the organism breathing. The chemical signals are its nerves. The creatures are its cells—specialized, differentiated, coordinated through mechanisms that are not conscious but are not random. The machinery is its skeleton, its circulatory system, the framework that gives it shape. The mineral deposits are its fat stores, its reserves, the accumulated resources of an organism that wastes nothing.

The Old One is its brain. Or the closest analog to a brain that this organism has produced—not a centralized processing center but a distributed one, with the Old One serving as the primary node. The eyes are the organism's eyes. The attention is the organism's attention. And it has been aware of you since you arrived.

## What Lives Here

What lives in the Refinery Vats is a single answer to a single question: what happens when an industrial system is abandoned long enough for life to colonize it completely?

The answer is: it becomes life. The machinery does not remain separate from the ecosystem. The ecosystem does not remain separate from the machinery. The boundaries dissolve over deep time, and what remains is a hybrid—a thing that is neither purely biological nor purely mechanical, but something that has transcended the distinction. The creatures are not living in a refinery. They are the refinery, and the refinery is them, and the Old One is the point at which the two have become one.

What lives here is a process. A process that has been running for approximately 2.8 billion years, give or take, in a sealed environment with no inputs except light and the rare visitor. A process that has refined itself, optimized itself, made itself more efficient and more complex and more integrated with each turning of the cycle. A process that has produced something that resembles intelligence—not human intelligence, not consciousness as you understand it, but a functional equivalent that has emerged from the same pressures that produce intelligence in any system: the need to process information, to adapt, to respond to a changing environment.

What lives here is alive in a way that challenges the word. What lives here is old in a way that challenges the concept. What lives here is aware in a way that challenges the assumption that awareness requires a brain, or a body, or a single organism.

What lives here is the Vats. And the Vats have noticed you.

The Old One's eyes remain on you. The bass drone shifts, barely perceptibly. The floor-mat creatures near your observation point change their pattern of movement. The hunters in the middle air adjust their trajectories. The gliders filter the air around you with an intensity that suggests they are not merely feeding but sampling—taking your chemical signature, breaking it down, distributing it through the system.

You are being processed. Not consumed—processed. Your presence is being integrated into the ecosystem's information stream. Your chemical signature is being added to the library. Your behavior is being observed, catalogued, compared to the patterns that the Old One has accumulated over its long existence.

You are the first new input this system has received in a very long time.

And the system, as you have observed, is adaptable.
