# What Lives in the Breach-Garden

---

## The Crawlers

They do not know they are many.

Each crawler is a solitary mechanism — armor plates in overlapping sequence, legs that move without consultation with the brain, if brain is the word for the knot of nerve that sits behind the mouth and ahead of the nothing else. The mouth is the purpose. The mouth is the crawler. Everything else is transport.

But they climb in lines. They always climb in lines.

This is not communication. They are not speaking. They are following — following the chemical trail of the one ahead, who follows the chemical trail of the one ahead of that, who follows the faint mineral signature of fresh growth, the places where the amber skin of a pillar is thin enough to breach, where the rust-colored shelf has newly extended and has not yet hardened into its armor. They follow because following works. They follow because the first crawler found food and the second crawler found the first crawler and the third found the second and so on, a chain of discovery that is also a chain of consumption, and by the time the hundredth crawler reaches the feeding site, the first crawler has moved on, has found a new seam of soft growth, has begun a new line.

They do not know they are many. But they are many. They are always many. The chamber has never had fewer crawlers than it has now, and it has never had more, because the number of crawlers is the number the chamber can feed, and the chamber feeds them all.

At night — and there is a night, when the breach-light fades and the luminescence of the growths becomes the only illumination and even that dims, pulses slower, the breathing of the chamber lengthening into sleep, if sleep is what it is — the crawlers do not rest. They cannot rest. The mouth does not close. The legs do not still. They continue to climb and eat in the dark, in the rust-red glow, in the amber half-light, and the sound of their rasping becomes the sound of the night: a constant whispering abrasion, like wind through sand, like time passing, like the mountain being eaten from the inside, millimeter by millimeter, century by century.

When a crawler dies — and they die often, and everywhere, and without ceremony — it becomes food. Not for other crawlers; they eat only the growths. For the growths themselves. The growths will colonize a crawler's body within hours, extending amber tendrils through the joints in the armor plates, digesting the soft interior, leaving the plates scattered on the floor like shed nails, like the remains of small wars. The floor is paved with these plates, in places. You cannot walk here without walking on them. You cannot stand here without standing on the remains of ten thousand mouths.

## The Flying Things

They have no name for themselves. They have no names at all. What they have is a map — a map written in clicks, updated in real time, more accurate than any cartography your kind has achieved.

Each click is a pulse of information. *Here I am. Here is the space around me. Here is what the echo tells me.* The click leaves their body — if body is the word for the closed fist of muscle and chitin that propels the too-many wings — and strikes the growths, strikes the walls, strikes the floor, strikes the other flying things, and returns, and they read the return, and they know.

They know the shape of the chamber. They know it better than the crawlers, better than the grazers, better than you. They know it because they have mapped it a million times, and each map is slightly different, because the chamber is slightly different, because a growth has extended here and a shelf has fractured there and a hanging column has fallen and the floor has shifted and the negative space has breathed in, breathed out. They map the change. They are the record of the change. If anything in this chamber remembers what it was, it is the flying things, clicking in the dark, compiling their endless sonar archive of a place that will not hold still.

They are afraid. This is important. They are afraid, and their fear is the click, and the click is also their language, and their language is also their map, and so their fear and their knowing are the same thing. To be a flying thing in the Breach-Garden is to be constantly afraid and constantly aware and to never be able to separate the two. The alarm is the communication is the navigation is the alarm. They click because they are afraid. They are afraid because they click. They click because clicking tells them what to be afraid of.

What they are afraid of is the watchers.

## The Grazers

Consider the grazer.

It is large. It is calm. It has been here, in this form, in this body, longer than the growths remember, and the growths remember nothing, so this is not saying much, but it feels true when you watch one. They move through the chamber like monks in a cloister, like pillars given permission to walk, like the architecture of the place detached from the floor and given legs and set loose to eat.

They eat slowly. They eat constantly. They eat the way the chamber breathes — steadily, rhythmically, without urgency. The low growths, the fresh shoots, the pale tips that the crawlers have not reached: these they crop with mouths that are not mouths but grinding surfaces, wide and flat and patient. You can hear them: a steady crunching, a methodical processing, the sound of something being turned into something else, which is the sound the entire chamber makes, which is the sound the chamber *is*.

They are not stupid. Do not think they are stupid. They are patient, and patience looks like stupidity from the outside, from the perspective of things that move fast and die young and click in alarm at every shadow. The grazers know the chamber. They know where the fresh growth will appear before it appears. They know which pillars are about to shed a shelf and where it will fall and how long the fresh surface will remain uncolonized by crawlers. They move toward the future. They move slowly, but they move toward the future, and they arrive when the future becomes the present, and they eat.

They move in groups. This is not sociality. This is not love. This is mathematics. The watchers take one grazer per season, or two, or none — the rate varies, the rate is part of the balance, the rate is the chamber's heartbeat, the way a heart does not beat every second but every second-and-a-half, the way the pause is part of the rhythm. The grazers know this. They do not count. They do not calculate. They simply *are* in groups, and the groups are large enough that the watchers can take what they need and the grazers can lose what they can afford to lose and the chamber continues.

They shed their plates. This is what the shelter was built from. This is what the floor is littered with, in addition to crawler-remains — the broad, flat, warm plates that the grazers leave behind when they grow, when they expand, when they become too large for their old skin. The plates fall like shingles from a roof. The growths colonize them. The crawlers ignore them. The chamber absorbs them. And the grazers continue, untroubled, unclothed, growing new plates beneath the old, shedding the past, eating the future.

They breathe loudly. In, out. In, out. When you stand among them — and you can stand among them; they do not mind you, they do not mind anything that does not try to eat them — you feel the rhythm of their breathing enter your own body. You begin to breathe with them. Your heartbeat slows. Your pupils dilate. The dimness becomes less dim. The sound of the chamber becomes less noise and more music. You begin to understand what the grazers understand, which is nothing you can put into words, which is the knowledge of the body, the knowledge of the slow, the knowledge of *being here and being part of here and not needing to be anywhere else.*

They have never been afraid of you.

## The Watchers

You cannot see them clearly.

This is not because they are hidden, although they are hidden, clinging to the ceiling among the inverted growths, nestled in the crotches where hanging columns meet the stone, folded into the gaps that the growths have not yet colonized. This is because they do not want to be seen, and in this chamber, in this dim hot breathing space, what a thing wants matters more than what a thing is.

They are large. You know this from the shadows they cast when the luminescence shifts, when a growth pulses brighter and the silhouette above you moves, stretches, contracts. You know this from the sounds they make when they shift their weight: a creak, a groan, the sound of something heavy settling against stone. You know this from the way the flying things react when they pass near the ceiling — the clicks accelerating, becoming a chatter, becoming a scream, and then the flying things are gone, scattering to the far walls, pressing themselves against the growths, becoming small and still and silent.

You know this from what is left behind.

A crawler, occasionally, found on the floor with its armor plates breached, its interior gone, clean, surgical, nothing wasted. A flying thing, sometimes, fallen with its wings still clicking in reflex, its body emptied with the same precision. And once a season, or twice, or never — a grazer. The chamber knows when it is time. The grazers know when it is time. The chosen grazer separates from the group — and this is the only time a grazer moves with purpose, the only time a grazer's head lifts from its constant grazing and turns toward the ceiling and *knows* — and it walks, slowly, always slowly, to the center of the chamber, to the place where the floor is hottest and the light is dimmest and the space between the growths is widest, and it stands there, and it waits.

And then it is taken.

You do not see the taking. You hear it — a sound like a breath, like a sigh, like the chamber exhaling with satisfaction — and then the grazer is gone, and where it stood there is only hot stone and the faint residue of something amber and wet, and in the ceiling, in the dark, something shifts and settles and begins to digest.

They watch you. You know they watch you. You feel it — the weight of attention, the pressure of being seen by something that does not need eyes to see, that sees with its whole body, that sees the way the chamber sees, from the inside, from the stone, from the growths that sense your heat and your breath and your heartbeat from across the room.

They do not take you. They have never taken anything that was not of the chamber. The human who lived here — the one who built the shelter, who stacked the cairns, who mourned the dead — they did not take them. The human was not of the chamber. The human was something else, something the watchers could see but not categorize, something that moved through their map like a error, a blank space, a gap in the clicking of the flying things, a silence in the constant sonar archive of the Breach-Garden.

The watchers do not eat what they cannot categorize. The watchers do not take what they cannot map. The watchers leave you alone because you are not food. You are *question*.

And the watchers do not ask questions. The watchers wait.

## The Human Who Stayed

Someone lived here.

This is what you know from the cairns, from the shelter, from the marks on the wall, from the tomb. Someone came through the breach — the wound in the mountain, the opening that lets in honey-colored light and cold air from the world outside — and they stayed.

Why?

The chamber does not invite. The chamber does not repel. The chamber *is*, and you can be in it or not, and the chamber will continue either way. But something kept this person here. Something made them build with shed plates instead of leaving. Something made them chip stone and lash it to bone and learn to catch crawlers, which are mostly mouth and armor and not much meat, but enough, if you are hungry enough, if you have learned to eat what the chamber provides.

What kept them?

The warmth. The constant warmth from below. If you came from the Palemass Range, from the wind and the cold and the killing heights, a warm place would be a wonder. A warm place that breathed and had food and water — but there is no water here. There is no water. So they drank something else. The humidity. The copper-tasting, ozone-tinged air that is not water but that the body can learn to use, or the body can fail to use, or the body can use until it cannot anymore.

Perhaps that is what killed them. The air. The slow accumulation of something that is not water in a body that evolved for water. The slow replacement of one fluid with another. The slow becoming of something else.

Or perhaps the watchers. But the watchers do not take what is not of the chamber, and the human was not of the chamber, not at first. Not until they had been here long enough. Not until they had eaten the crawlers and breathed the air and slept in the shelter made of grazer plates and written on the walls with mineral pigments that the growths had produced. Not until the chamber had colonized *them*.

The tomb. The tomb at the back of the chamber, where the light is dimmest, where the growths are oldest. Who is in the tomb? Who stacked those stones with care and grief and the particular attention the living give to the dead?

The human who stayed had a companion. Or the human who stayed became two, somehow — divided, the way the growths divide, the way a column splits and becomes two columns and each continues growing toward the ceiling. Or the human who stayed found someone else, another wanderer, another fool, another soul drawn to the warm breathing wound in the mountain. Or the human who stayed was alone, and the tomb is for themselves, stacked in advance, prepared with the same patience the grazers show, the same knowledge of what is coming, the same steady breathing in and out while they waited for the end.

Or the tomb is empty.

The tomb is never empty. The growths have colonized it. The amber tendrils have worked through the gaps between the stones. Something lives inside it now, something that was not there before, something that grew from the residue of whoever was laid there, or whoever built there, or whoever dreamed there. The tomb is a growth now. The tomb is part of the chamber. The tomb breathes.

## The Chamber

You keep trying to name what lives here.

You list the crawlers and the flying things and the grazers and the watchers and the human and the growths and you think: these are the things that live in the Breach-Garden. These are the inhabitants. These are the population.

But the chamber is not a container. The chamber is not a room that holds living things. The chamber is the living thing. The growths are its tissue. The crawlers are its immune system, its circulation, its constant processing. The flying things are its awareness, its sensory apparatus, its endless mapping of itself. The grazers are its rhythm, its heartbeat, its slow certainty. The watchers are its hunger, its patience, its willingness to take what it needs when it needs it.

The human was its visitor. Its experiment. Its question asked in the language of warmth and food and shelter, and answered in the language of a tomb at the back of the chamber, a tomb that is now a growth, a growth that is now part of the chamber, the chamber that is now slightly different because the human was here, because the human ate and built and died and was absorbed, and the chamber continues, the chamber breathes, the chamber becomes.

What lives here?

One thing.

One thing with many bodies and many voices and many hungers and many speeds, but one thing, one ancient warm loud breathing thing that has been here since before the concept of growing was invented, that invented the concept of growing, that is growing now, that will grow after you leave, after the breach closes or the mountain falls or the sun goes dark, because the chamber does not need the sun, does not need the water, does not need anything but the heat from below and the stone around it and the constant slow transformation of itself into itself, over and over, in and out, the long breath of a place that is also a living, that is also a being, that is also — if you stand here long enough, if you breathe with the grazers, if you let the rhythm of the place enter your bones — *also you*.

You are inside its breath.

It does not mind.

It does not mind because you are temporary. You are the flying thing, clicking in alarm. You are the crawler, rasping for a season. You are the human who stayed too long and was absorbed. You are part of the map now. You are part of the archive. The chamber has felt you, has mapped you, has categorized you, and when you leave — if you leave — you will leave behind your residue, your heat, your breath, your particular signature in the air, and the growths will colonize it, and the crawlers will eat it, and the chamber will incorporate you into itself, and you will be here, you will always be here, part of the living wound in the mountain, part of the breath, part of the constant becoming.

This is what lives in the Breach-Garden of Thol.

Everything that has ever been here.

Everything that will ever be.

The mouth. The lung. The question that the mountain asks itself, over and over, in the language of growth and consumption and patience and change:

*What am I becoming?*

And the answer, always, forever, in the creak of the pillars and the scrape of the crawlers and the click of the flying things and the breath of the grazers and the silence of the watchers and the slow pulse of the heat from below:

*Yourself. Again. Still. Always.*
