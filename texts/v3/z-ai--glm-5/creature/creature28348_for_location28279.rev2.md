# A Census of the High Everplain

Ask who lives **on** the High Everplain, and the question breaks at once on its little word. Nothing lives *on* it. There is no on. What would be the surface elsewhere is, up here, the resident—feet of it, warm, working—and every creature that has ever stood on this plateau has stood *inside* something. A census, then, taken from within the body. And it should begin where the plateau itself begins.

Begin with the dead, because the plateau does. They are the majority. They outnumber every living thing here by weight, by volume, by tenure, and they are not idle. The ground gives underfoot because it is occupied: beneath the living mat lies the deep layer, dark and warm and feet thick, and the deep layer is them—the accumulated centuries, everything that ever grew here and lay down. The great trees rise from the hollowed cores of their ancestors. Up here, the dead are not merely present. They are load-bearing.

The fungi keep the estate. Orange shelves fruiting in clusters along the vines—undertakers with offices in the lobby, accountants of return, converting what fell into credit the living can spend. They taste of nut and spore, which is to say they taste like their work. At night, some of them light it. The second shift is worked too.

---

Above the dead: the plants. And here the census has to stop and argue with itself, because the plants are not the setting in which the residents live. They are residents of the first order—tenants with tenure, landlords, staff, and next of kin, all in the same bodies. Vines climb vines that climb trees. Ferns lease the bark of strangers. Moss takes the damp underside of every branch as its inheritance. And every morning, across unbroken miles, the pale blossoms open from the trunks of the great trees in near-unison, synchronized by a signal no one has ever caught traveling. Something moves through the mass. The flowers obey it. Whatever you decide to call that—chemistry, network, a nervous system with nobody home—the behavior stands: the vegetation coordinates. Keep that fact. It matters later.

Note, too, the arrangement, because it is the arrangement of everything else: the plants are also the architecture, so listing the residents means listing the rooms. The plateau is stacked like a house with no outside. At the bottom, the ancient mat. Above it, the hand-leaved ground-cover catching the light. Above that, the shrub-lock, where small trees grow interlocked in a competitive embrace tight enough to shoulder through. Then the dim middle levels, where things hold office unseen. Then the crowns, a hundred feet up, where the light turns green-gold—and everywhere, at every level, clouds drifting through at eye level, the only neighbors here who never learned to knock.

---

The living majority, then: the insects. Do not count them; a count of the insects is dishonest within the minute. They are less a population than a medium, and the drone they maintain is a census that never closes—different species warming up and winding down in overlapping shifts, so the chord is never the same twice and never different enough to be foreign: a language in which the speakers rotate but the grammar holds. When one lands on the back of your hand, it is not investigating you. It is the plateau, tasting itself through one of its ten thousand tongues. The tick of legs on skin, the whine at the ear, the scatter of small bodies out of a sunlit shaft as you push through—this is the texture of being sampled. Let yourself be sampled. It is the warmest welcome on offer.

The birds live out loud. Tens of thousands, flocks that darken the canopy when they pass, calls layered into a wall of shriek and song, some pitched below hearing to vibrate the sternum rather than the ear. They are between pet and eagle in size, colored in ways that shouldn't work—blue and orange in patterns that ache the eye—crests rising and falling with their voices like punctuation. Some of them mimic, and they do not merely mimic each other. They collect the place the way other species hoard seeds: the creak of wood under morning heat, the moan of a distant herd, the wet sound of a boot pulled from plant-mud—filed, and played back days later from a direction you did not come from, so that sooner or later you are ambushed by your own footsteps. They are the plateau's memory of sounds. When one lands nearby and tilts its head at you, others answer its call, and then there are dozens, a gallery of eyes, assessing you with an accuracy you will find unflattering. If the insects are the plateau tasting, the birds are the plateau paying attention.

From the crowns of the emergent trees comes the bass note. The howlers—though they are not quite primates, and whatever else they are is older than the word for it—were built for this air, thin and carrying and damp. A single roar crosses miles, threading the drone, answered from farther crowns, and for a few seconds the plateau has a pulse you could hear from anywhere on it. You will see one eventually: a barrel of dark fur in the crotch of a great limb, hands like roots, a face that studies you back with an expression you will spend the evening failing to parse. It will not come down. There is no need. Everything the plateau has, it sends up.

---

The herds are the weather. You hear them in your feet before the air bothers to deliver the news—the moan travels through the mat, up the bones of your ankles, and by the time it reaches your ears it has already arrived everywhere else in you. Massive, low-headed, shoulders built for shouldering, trailing musk and the smell of digestion, they move on a seasonal logic of rainfall and new growth that no map has ever captured, because it is a map in time. When they pass, the plateau holds its breath—the insects pause, the birds pause, even the howlers—and for the length of the herd's passing there is a silence here, the only one, and it is the herd's shadow. Then the flank of them goes by, the sound closes over like water over a dropped stone, and the plateau resumes. Their roads are forgiven within the week.

You will not be shown a predator. Nobody is. The predators of the High Everplain are visible only as an absence in the choir: insects going quiet in one particular quarter, the birds giving their stuttered alarm, a stillness spreading through the middle levels like cold through water. The hole moves. You track it. You walk away from it at the speed of boredom—and that is not cowardice, it is the local etiquette, refined over longer than anyone has been counting. The predators are not evil. They are *employed*: solitary, territorial, patient, very good at their work, and their work is the maintenance of the chord—ending the slow, turning weakness back into warmth for the mat. They hold office between the canopy and the ground, where nothing above can see down and nothing below can see up. Everything that lives here long enough learns to flinch at the birds' stutter. That flinch is the closest anyone comes to seeing one whole.

---

And the people. **The people do not live here. They return here.**

A settlement on the High Everplain is not a place; it is an interval—a ring of clearings visited in rotation, years between visits, each left alone long enough to half-forget its tenants, each returned to before it forgets them entirely. A house of bent saplings and woven vine is not built so much as negotiated: an agreement with an expiration date, renewed by hand, season by season. A house they still weave is a house they still remember. A house they stop weaving is not lost; it is incorporated—and you can find the old ones as green ghosts, denser patches in the mass, almost-structures more plant than building now. They consider this the correct outcome for a house. If pressed, they will admit it is also their plan for themselves.

Their own name for themselves translates, with some loss, as *guests in good standing*, and the standing is maintained the way everything here is maintained: by hand, at the speed of the place. They build near the water-holding depressions and never in the lowest of them. They take the morning sun and cede the afternoon glare. Their tools are wood and bone and such stone as the plateau can be persuaded to give—shaped for cutting, weaving, grinding—and they make nothing that requires heat beyond a cooking fire. When the herds pass, the people move, traveling the herd-roads while the roads last, which is about a week; they have learned to want nothing that lasts longer than the plateau's forgiveness. There are not many of them. A herd is a more common sight than a person, and they do not consider that a grievance.

Their great skill is the soundscape. An elder can stand motionless in the drone for a minute and tell you what is wrong three miles east: water standing where water shouldn't, a herd turning, a hole in the choir moving upwind. Their children learn the birds' stuttered alarm before they learn their own words for danger. It is possible their words for danger came second. It is possible most of their words came from birds—and the birds, for their part, know the people's individual voices and spend them back across the canopy, which is the closest thing to mail either species has ever arranged.

Their highest social achievement is to be uninteresting. They walk slowly. They stop often. They practice boredom as a discipline and a devotion, because attention here is a many-eyed thing and they would rather not be its subject.

They do not bury their dead. They *return* them—the same verb, in their speech, as a borrowed tool handed back—laying them into the warm mat where the orange shelves can do the arithmetic. Ask what happens after, and the answer is a gesture that takes in the whole visible horizon: the trees, the blossoms, the vines, the ground that gives underfoot. It is not a comforting answer. It is not meant to comfort. It is meant to be accurate.

They are not here now. But the smoke-stains on a certain fire-pit are fresh, and the stems where the growth was pushed back are still pale at the cut, and somewhere out on the plateau—recently; this season at least—someone is walking at the speed of the place, tasting the air, reading the hour off the angle of the leaves, being unremarkable with tremendous skill.

---

Which leaves the largest resident: the place itself.

This is not a metaphor, or it is one only in the way large true things begin as metaphors. The plateau lives here more completely than anything in it. The trees are its cells, if cells can stand a hundred feet. The vines are its capillaries. The insect chord, its constant hum of sensation. The birds, its speech. The herds, its slow turning of attention. The predators, its sense of its own limits. The dead, its memory, in the only format memory takes up here—warm, deep, load-bearing. And you, walking through, are a sensation in it: a taste of something new on a tongue too large to notice that it is tasting.

I won't tell you it thinks. The evidence never gets better than it is: flowers opening in unison across miles, paths healing behind whoever makes them, the chord reorganizing itself ahead of weather that hasn't arrived, every living thing behaving as though informed. Perhaps *informed* is only a word for *tuned*. The plateau has been tuning itself for longer than recorded history, and the end state of perfect tuning looks, from inside, exactly like intention. Call it chemistry if it helps you sleep. The plateau has no use for the verdict either way.

---

Evening comes, and the census changes hands. The flowers close in unison. The birds give their last calls; the howlers stop mid-note, like bells racked for the night; the herds settle somewhere out of sight. Every surface begins to bead—leaf, bark, skin—the damp of a place exhaling its day.

The night population clocks in. Moths. Frogs in the wetter depressions. Wing-buzzers in the dark. Fungi lighting their shelves. Flowers that glow on purpose, for an audience of moths. By night the medium is light instead of sound, blue-green and low, and the conversation continues in a different register.

And underneath the changeover, the chord never stops, because the census never closes, and the census was never a list. It is a conversation—life talking to life about life, in shifts, in layers, in each other, at a volume you can feel in your sternum. By morning the fog will lie at ankle height, the blossoms will open across the miles, and the count will begin again, the same count that has been beginning for longer than anyone has been counting.

That is who lives here: everyone, all at once, inside everyone else. The conversation has no silence in it, and no audience, and no need of one.
