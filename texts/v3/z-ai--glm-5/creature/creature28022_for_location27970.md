# What Lives Here

## The People

They are not a tall people. Generations of protein drawn primarily from the blind fish-kin and the processed fungal mats have produced a population that tends toward compactness, dense bone, thick joints. Not stunted—nothing about them suggests deprivation—but converged upon, as if the environment selected for efficiency of form. Carrying less mass means losing less heat to the damp, and the damp is always there, always pulling warmth from the body.

Their skin tells the story of the place. A resident of the central districts has a complexion that outsiders have described, somewhat unkindly, as oxidized. Not dark, not pale, but somewhere between—a tone like bronze left in salt air, like the copper tubing in the condensers, like the mineral-streaked concrete. It is not a suntan; the sun rarely asserts itself strongly enough to burn. It is something subtler, a chemical response to a lifetime of exposure to air that carries trace compounds from the factories, from the processing vents, from the water itself. Children born here show it within their first year. Visitors develop a faint version of it within months. Those who leave lose it slowly, over years, their skin fading to whatever their genetic inheritance would produce in a drier climate.

Their hair adapts differently. It curls. Not uniformly, not in the way that suggests a single genetic origin, but universally. Straight-haired immigrants find their hair kinking within weeks, the constant humidity restructuring the keratin bonds day after day until the original texture is a memory. The locals wear it cropped close or bound back, practical choices for an environment where loose hair accumulates moisture, accumates the calcium-rich dust from the sky-vermin droppings, accumulates the fine particulate that the factories exhale. Vanity here is a function of maintenance, not display. You keep your hair managed because unmanaged hair becomes a problem.

Their lungs are the thing that truly distinguishes them. A lifetime of breathing air that carries machine oil, ionized minerals, the faint sulfur taint, the aerosolized byproducts of condensation processing—it builds something. Not resistance, exactly. Adaptation. The bronchial tissue of a lifelong resident has a characteristic mottling that a trained eye can identify, a pattern of mineral deposition that would alarm a lowland physician but functions, here, as a kind of filtration system. The lungs learn to process what the air brings. They have to.

Newcomers cough for the first three months. It is expected. It is called the settling, and it is treated with a syrup made from rendered fish-kin oil and a distilled compound drawn from the fungal processors. The syrup tastes terrible and works adequately. Those whose lungs cannot settle leave or develop chronic conditions that the local medical infrastructure has been managing for so long that the treatments are routine.

---

They move differently. You see it in the streets: a lateral, slightly forward-leaning gait, weight kept low, feet placed with a deliberateness that seems overly cautious until you feel the pavement yourself—slick with condensation, with the mineral residue the crawlers leave in their wake, with the seepage from the buildings. The plateau's residents walk as if the ground might betray them, because it sometimes does. A street that was safe yesterday may have developed a film overnight. A crawler swarm can leave surfaces treacherous for days. The walk becomes habitual, becomes identity. Even on dry ground—in the deep archives, in the sealed chambers—a lifelong resident maintains that careful, grounded stride.

They do not run in the streets. Not because they cannot, but because running on wet pavement among buildings that weep is an invitation to injury. The young learn early that speed is a liability. The closest thing to haste in the Condensation Cities is a purposeful acceleration, still controlled, still grounded. Urgency expresses itself through efficiency of route and certainty of direction, not through speed. A messenger moves quickly by knowing exactly where she is going and taking the optimal path, not by sprinting.

---

They have a relationship with temperature that outsiders find unsettling. The plateau's altitude means the air is cool—cold, by some standards, rarely above fifteen degrees even in the warmest seasons. But the dampness changes the perception. The moisture conducts heat away from the body with an efficiency that dry cold cannot match. Fifteen degrees in the Condensation Cities feels colder than five degrees in a dry climate.

And so they dress in layers, always. Not the dramatic layering of a lowland winter—no heavy coats, no thick insulation—but a continuous, modulated system. Underlayers of treated fabric that wicks moisture from the skin. Middle layers that trap what body heat they can. Outer layers that shed the condensation, treated with the same compounds used to waterproof the electrical systems. The aesthetic is one of function that has, over generations, become its own kind of style: the drape of a properly treated condensation cloak, the set of a worker's collar, the way the seams are sealed. You can identify a resident's district by the cut of their outerwear, the specific treatment of their fabric, the pattern of their waterproofing.

The clothes never fully dry. This is a fact of life, not a complaint. A resident's wardrobe includes a rotation—garments worn while others hang in the warm exhaust vents of the factories, drying slowly, never completely, taking on the faint smell of machine oil and processed air that is, to a local, the smell of home. Visitors who remark on the smell are reminded, gently or not, that every place has its scent.

## The Workers

The city's economy is not complex, but it is vast. The condensation infrastructure alone employs a significant portion of the population. Condenser maintenance is a trade passed through families, through apprenticeship, through the kind of bodily knowledge that develops when you have spent your life among the skeletal towers of rusted steel and copper tubing. A condenser technician knows by sound whether a tower is drawing properly, by the color of the oxidation whether a section of tubing is approaching failure, by the taste of the output whether the filtration is functioning. These are not skills that can be written down and transferred. They are held in the hands and the ears and the tongue.

The cistern networks beneath the city require their own workforce. The cistern tenders descend into the limestone channels, into the dark where the blind fish-kin swim, and they maintain the systems that channel the city's water. It is wet work, and it is claustrophobic work, and it attracts a particular kind of person: not reckless, not adventurous, but comfortable with enclosure, with darkness, with the presence of creatures that move in the water beside them. The cistern tenders develop, over years, an ability to navigate by sound and by the feel of the stone under their hands, the way the water moves, the temperature gradients that indicate flow patterns. Some of them, it is said, can sense the fish-kin moving in the dark, can feel the displacement of water against their skin. Whether this is true or whether it is a professional mythology, the tenders themselves believe it, and their competence is undeniable.

The factories employ the largest single segment of the population. The processing infrastructure—the synthesizers, the digesters, the breakdown tanks, the refineries—runs on human labor as much as on mechanical power. Workers feed the systems, monitor the outputs, clear the blockages, perform the maintenance that keeps the centuries-old machinery in operation. The work is not skilled in the way that condenser maintenance is skilled; it is skilled in the way that endurance is skilled. A factory shift in the Industrial Quarter lasts ten hours, and the atmosphere is thick with steam and chemical output, and the noise is a physical pressure against the body. Workers develop a particular kind of deafness—not total, but selective, their hearing narrowing to the frequencies that indicate malfunction, the frequencies that signal danger. Conversation in the factories is conducted in a sign language that has evolved over generations, a system of gestures specific to each facility, incomprehensible to outsiders, efficient beyond what speech could achieve in that environment.

The harvesters work the margins. The fish-kin populations in the cisterns and condensation channels are managed, culled, processed for protein and oil. The sky-vermin are not harvested—their populations are too volatile, their flesh too permeated with the compounds they absorb from their diet of aerial plankton and cable insulation—but their eggs are collected from the roosts, a dangerous and unpopular task that falls to those who need the work. The crawlers are harvested for their shells, which provide a chitin-like material used in construction and in the manufacture of the waterproofing treatments. The shaggy grazers at the margins are processed for meat and for their pelts, which are used in the few luxury goods the city produces. Harvesting is not a prestigious occupation, but it is an essential one, and the harvesters know the animal populations with an intimacy that the city's administrators rely on but rarely acknowledge.

The archivists are a separate case. They work in the deep chambers, the oldest districts, where the stone is dry and the air is still. They maintain the records, the maps, the accumulated knowledge of centuries. They are the closest thing the city has to a leisure class—not wealthy, but insulated from the industrial atmosphere, from the noise, from the constant dampness. They tend toward thinness, toward pallor, toward a certain fragility that the surface-dwelling population regards with a mixture of respect and mild contempt. The archivists can read the old scripts, can navigate the deep tunnels, can identify the mineral composition of a stone sample by sight. They also, it is widely believed, know things about the city's history that are not written in the archives, knowledge that has been passed orally through their institution in a parallel tradition that predates the written records. Whether this is true, the archivists do not say.

## The Children

They grow up with the noise. This is the first and most fundamental fact of childhood in the Condensation Cities. A newborn here has been hearing the city's soundscape for months before birth—the bass thrum of the generators, transmitted through the stone and through the mother's body, the distant shrieking of the sky-vermin, the mechanical rhythm of the factories. The silence of the deep archives, which distresses adult visitors, is something a child of the plateau has never experienced and, if their parents can help it, will never experience. It is considered unhealthy. There are stories—not written, not official, but told—that children raised in silence grow strange. The stories are specific and consistent enough to suggest a historical basis, but no archivist will confirm this.

Children learn the streets before they learn to read. The grid is their first text: which routes are safe, which are wet, which accumulate crawlers at dusk, which are roosting sites for sky-vermin and should be avoided at dawn. A child of six can navigate their district with a precision that a newcomer to the city cannot match with a map. This knowledge is not taught formally; it is absorbed through movement, through the daily patterns of family life, through the simple necessity of getting from one place to another.

They are given responsibilities early. By eight, a child in the Industrial Quarter is expected to help with domestic tasks that include checking the family's condensation catchment, clearing the drainage channels around their dwelling, and monitoring the waterproofing seals on the building's exterior. By twelve, they are working—part-time, alongside schooling that is practical rather than academic, learning the trades their families practice. Childhood here is not a separate, sheltered phase of life in the way that some cultures construct it. It is a gradient, a progressive integration into the systems that keep the city alive.

They play, but their play is specific. They race along the streets—carefully, on the safer surfaces, in games that test their ability to move fast without falling. They hunt crawlers, collecting them in jars, trading them, competing for the largest specimens. They dare each other to approach the roosting sites, to touch the condenser towers, to venture into the shallower tunnels. They are not cruel children, but they are not sentimental. The animals are not pets; they are co-inhabitants, sometimes resources, sometimes nuisances, never romanticized.

## The Old

To grow old in the Condensation Cities is to become something specific. The accumulated exposure—the dampness, the mineral-laden air, the noise, the industrial compounds—manifests in the body in ways that the population accepts and that outsiders find alarming. The skin thickens. Not uniformly, but in patches, in the places of greatest exposure—the hands, the face, the neck. The mineral deposition that gives the young their bronze tone becomes, in the old, a kind of calcification, a roughening that produces a texture like the stone of the plateau itself. The old residents of the Condensation Cities look, in their faces and their hands, like the buildings they inhabit: stained, weathered, marked by centuries of water and air.

Their hearing narrows. The selective deafness that begins in the factories and the loud districts becomes, with age, a general attenuation. But it is not a simple loss. The old residents hear the city differently—they feel it, it is said, more than they hear it. The vibrations of the generators, the subsonic frequencies of the condensers, the tremor of crawler swarms in the streets—these register in the body, in the bones, in a way that goes beyond hearing. An elder sitting in a doorway, eyes closed, can tell you what time it is, what the factories are producing, whether the wind has shifted, whether the crawlers are swarming. They feel it in the stone beneath them, in the seat beneath them, in the air against their skin.

They are respected, but not revered. The culture of the Condensation Cities is practical, and the old are practical people. They continue to work as long as they can contribute. When they cannot work, they contribute knowledge—the routes, the timing, the judgments that only decades of living in the city can produce. An elder's knowledge of the water systems, of the crawler cycles, of the safe paths through the tunnels, is a resource that the community draws on. It is not stored in books. It is stored in the bodies and the memories of those who have lived longest in the city's damp.

When they die, they are returned to the water. The fungal processors, which break down the city's organic waste, accept the human dead. The practice is ancient, practical, and— Outsiders who learn of it are often disturbed. The locals find the disturbance puzzling. The fungal mats process, the synthesizers cycle, the nutrients return to the system that sustains the living. What else would you do with a body in a city without soil?

## The Animals, Again

The sky-vermin have a relationship with the human population that is closer to commensalism than parasitism, though the distinction is sometimes hard to maintain. They roost in the city's infrastructure because the infrastructure provides warmth—residual heat from the machines, from the electrical systems, from the buildings' own condensation processes. In return, they provide nothing intentionally, but their droppings, which cause such annoyance on the horizontal surfaces, are rich in calcium and nitrogen compounds that the municipal collection services process into fertilizer for the synthesizers. The city has tried, over the centuries, to control the sky-vermin populations. The attempts have uniformly failed. The populations recover within a season. The current policy is management, not elimination: the roosting sites are monitored, the droppings are collected, the electrical cables are reinforced with materials the vermin will not eat. An equilibrium has been reached, if an uneasy one.

The crawlers are more troubling. Their consumption of concrete is not merely aesthetic. A crawler swarm can compromise structural integrity, their acidic digestive processes pitting and weakening the aggregate until a foundation requires reinforcement or a wall begins to lean. The city has developed countermeasures—surface treatments that repel crawlers, physical barriers at the base of vulnerable structures, culling programs in the seasons when populations peak—but the crawlers adapt. A treatment that repels them for a decade becomes, in the eleventh year, ineffective. The crawlers are, in their own way, as much a part of the city as the stone.

The fish-kin in the cisterns are perhaps the most genuinely domesticated of the plateau's fauna. The cistern tenders who manage the aquatic colonies know their populations by feel, by the movement of the water, by the patterns of breeding that indicate health or stress. The fish-kin are not fed; they feed on the organic matter that enters the cisterns through the condensation channels, the waste that the city's systems produce. In a sense, the fish-kin are a processing system—converting the city's dissolved organic output into protein that the city can consume. The harvesters take what the population can sustain, and the population, in its dark, wet tunnels, breeds and breeds and breeds, as everything does here.

The grazers at the margins are the least integrated. They exist in a zone where the city meets the plateau's stone-y wastes, and they move between the two worlds with an indifference that the human population cannot share. A grazer will stand in the condensation runoff at the edge of an outer district, drinking from the gutters, its shaggy coat dark with moisture, its six stubby legs planted in the wet pavement, and it will look at the buildings and the people and the machines with an expression that is not blankness but something more infuriating: indifference. The grazers do not need the city. The city needs the grazers, for their meat, their pelts, their waste. This asymmetry is felt, if not articulated, in the way the marginal-district residents treat the animals: with a rough care, a practical respect, the kind of attention you give to something that could leave but doesn't.

## The Fungal Layer

It is there. It is always there. Beneath the city, in the water channels, in the waste processing systems, in the subterranean spaces that the cistern tenders navigate, the fungal mats spread and process and break down. They are the city's digestive system, the thing that makes the cycle work—waste to nutrient, old to new, death to the substrate that supports life.

The fungal cultivators are a specialized caste, separate from the factory workers, from the cistern tenders, from the harvesters. They work in the deepest processing levels, where the smell is strongest and the air is thickest with the compounds the fungi produce as they metabolize. They are marked by their work: their skin carries a distinct pallor, not the healthy pallor of the archivists but something else, a leaching of color that comes from prolonged exposure to the fungal atmosphere. They are not shunned, exactly, but they are separate. Their work is essential. Their work is invisible. They are spoken of the way the fungal layer itself is spoken of: acknowledged when necessary, otherwise ignored, never celebrated.

The fungi themselves are not a single species. They are a colony, a consortium of organisms that have been cultivated and refined over centuries. Some strains break down organic waste. Others process mineral compounds. Others produce the compounds used in the waterproofing treatments, in the medicines, in the chemical feedstocks the factories require. The fungal layer is not a natural system; it is a managed one, as artificial as the condenser towers, as designed as the street grid. But it has been managed for so long that the distinction between natural and artificial has lost its meaning. The fungi are the city, as much as the stone.

## The Way They Live

A day begins early. The transitional hour, when the night shift ends and the day shift begins, is the city's morning, regardless of what the sky is doing. Which is usually nothing—nothing visible, nothing distinct, just the diffuse brightening of the haze as the sun climbs behind the moisture and the city's own illumination dims in response. People rise. They check their condensation catchments. They eat—a meal of processed fish-kin, or fungal cakes, or the nutrient-dense paste that the synthesizers produce, flavored with the mineral salts that the refineries extract from the water. They dress in their layers. They go to work.

The streets are full. The population density of the Condensation Cities is high, higher than the low buildings and the horizontal sprawl would suggest, because the city extends downward as well as outward. The subterranean districts, the older chambers cut into the stone, house significant populations. The surface districts are dense with low, closely-spaced buildings. The streets are narrow, the passages tight. You are always near other people. Privacy in the Condensation Cities is not a function of space but of convention: you do not look too closely at your neighbor, you do not listen too carefully to the sounds through the wall, you do not remark on the condensation that pools on the shared surfaces. The city's density has produced a culture of studied inattention, a way of being constantly in the presence of others while maintaining a kind of social solitude.

The evenings bring the crawlers. Every surface that is not treated acquires a thin crust of hard-shelled bodies, clicking and scraping, foraging for minerals. The residents step over them, around them, through them. The crawlers are not a crisis. They are a condition. You sweep your stoop. You check your shoes. You go about your business.

Night is not dark. The condensation mist catches the gaslight and the electric light and the glow of the factories, and the city exists in a perpetual amber-and-blue twilight that is neither day nor night but something specific to this place, this altitude, this atmosphere. The noise continues. The machines hum. The sky-vermin murmur. The water runs in its channels.

People sleep. They sleep in the noise, in the damp, in the light. They have always slept this way. They do not know another way to sleep.

## What They Believe

This is harder to describe, because belief in the Condensation Cities is not a matter of creed or doctrine. It is a matter of practice. The city's cosmology, if it can be called that, is materialist in the most literal sense: the world is what you can touch, what you can feel, what you can taste. The stone is real. The water is real. The machines are real. The animals are real. What lies beyond the plateau—the lowland seas, the places where rain falls and plants grow—is known but not emphasized, a place of origin for some, a destination for the few who leave, but not a place that organizes daily life.

The city's oldest belief, the one that persists in the deep archives and in the unspoken assumptions of the people, is that the plateau itself is alive. Not metaphorically. The limestone, porous and saturated, yielding water from its depths, giving slightly under pressure like a soaked sponge—the stone is understood as a participant in the city's life, not merely a substrate. The cistern tenders speak of the stone the way a farmer might speak of soil: as something that needs to be understood, worked with, not against. You do not cut a tunnel where the stone is not ready to be cut. You do not build where the water flows strongest. You listen to the plateau, and the plateau tells you what it will support.

The machines occupy a similar position. They are not worshipped—the concept would be foreign—but they are respected in a way that goes beyond their function. A condenser tower that has been operating for two centuries is not just equipment; it is a presence, a participant in the city's identity. When a tower is decommissioned, it is not simply dismantled. Its components are distributed, its copper tubing repurposed, its steel recycled, but the event is marked. The workers who maintained it remember it. The tower's specific sound, its specific vibration, is absent from the soundscape, and the absence is noted.

The animals are believed to have been here first. This is not a matter of faith but of evidence—the archives confirm it, and the animal populations' integration with the city's systems suggests a co-evolution that predates human settlement. The sky-vermin roost in the city's structures because the structures replaced the natural formations they once roosted in. The crawlers consume the city's concrete because the city's concrete replaced the mineral surfaces they once consumed. The fish-kin swim in the cisterns because the cisterns replaced the natural water channels. The city did not displace the ecosystem. It became the ecosystem.

This belief, if it can be called that, manifests as a particular attitude toward the animals: they are not pests to be eliminated, not wildlife to be preserved, but co-residents to be managed. The distinction is subtle but important. You manage the sky-vermin because they are part of the city. You manage the crawlers because they are part of the city. You do not seek their removal. You seek an equilibrium in which the city's systems and the animal populations coexist with minimal friction.

Whether this equilibrium has been achieved is a matter of perspective. The city is damp and loud and crowded and old. It functions. It has functioned for a long time. It will, in all probability, continue to function. The condensation continues to form. The animals continue to breed. The machines continue to turn.

The people continue to live.
