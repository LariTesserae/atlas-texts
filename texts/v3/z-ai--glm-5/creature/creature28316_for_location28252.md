# What Lives in Chen

---

## The People, In General

They are short-winded. Not from illness but from residence. Generations at this elevation have selected for deep chests and efficient lungs, for bodies that treat thin air as a birthright rather than a hardship. The Chen-folk are not a tall people. They are dense, compact, built close to the ground in the way that things which endure are built close to the ground. Their frames carry a particular economy—nothing wasted, nothing extraneous, every body a negotiation between the resources available and the work required. You see them moving through the vertical streets and they have a gait that belongs only here: unhurried but never idle, each step placed with the unconscious precision of people who have walked the same stone since childhood, who know where the moss is slippery when wet and where the drip-channels overflow on heavy condensation mornings.

Their skin tells you about the light. It runs to darker tones—not from sun, which rarely penetrates directly, but from the cumulative effect of living in diffuse ultraviolet that never quite blocks and never quite burns. A perpetual low exposure, generation after generation. Their skin is also wet fairly often. They don't notice this. A visitor notices this. You shake hands with someone in Chen and their skin is faintly damp, always, from the condensation that touches everything, and they don't think to apologize for it because to them dry skin would be the strange thing, the thing requiring explanation.

Their eyes have adapted too, but differently than you'd expect. Not larger, not more sensitive in the way of nocturnal animals. Rather, their eyes have become better at distinguishing gradients. In a place where light is always diffuse and distance is always ambiguous, the ability to read subtle variations in the gray-white murk becomes survival. They can tell the difference between a wall and a doorway at fifty meters in fog that would reduce a lowlander to blind fumbling. They can read the weather in the clouds not because they study meteorology but because their retinas have learned, over generations, to parse distinctions that other eyes collapse into uniformity. A person from Chen looking at an overcast sky sees texture, variation, indication. A visitor sees a flat white ceiling.

They dress in layers, always. The temperature doesn't swing dramatically, but the difference between morning condensation chill and midday's weak warmth is enough to matter, and the layering system they've developed is less about protection from extreme cold and more about fine adjustment—a garment added, a garment removed, a shawl pulled closer or loosened, the continuous small calibrations of a people living in a climate that never commits to one state. The fabrics are their own: plant fibers processed in the factory spaces, sometimes woven with thread salvaged from older garments that have been taken apart and reconstituted. Nothing is simply discarded. Everything returns to the system. A worn shirt becomes rags becomes fiber becomes board becomes wall becomes substrate for moss becomes compost for garden becomes food becomes person becomes shirt. They understand this cycle not as abstraction but as daily practice.

## The Children

Children in Chen grow up touching everything. This is not permissiveness; it is education. The city is a continuous text and the children learn to read it by running their hands along the walls, the moss, the metal fixtures, the drip-channels. A child who doesn't learn to distinguish by touch between healthy moss and moss that's harboring rot, between a warm pipe (carrying steam) and a cool pipe (carrying condensation), between a stone that's settled and a stone that's about to shift—such a child doesn't thrive here. The hands come first. Then the eyes. Then, eventually, the ears, which learn to parse the city's continuous low hum into its components: which machine is running well, which is straining, which has stopped.

The children are quieter than lowland children. This is noticeable. Not subdued—there is laughter, there is play, there is the universal chaos of young humans—but the volume is different, the energy is different. They grow up in a place where shouting accomplishes nothing because the dense air and the soft surfaces absorb and flatten sound, where the industrial hum provides a continuous background that makes loudness feel like intrusion rather than expression. They learn early that the way to be heard in Chen is to speak clearly at a moderate volume, to wait for the right pause in the ambient noise, to use the architecture—the courtyards, the stairwells, the channels carved into stone—as acoustic tools that carry voice where it needs to go.

They are given small responsibilities early. A child of six can be trusted to check a section of drip-channel for blockages. A child of eight is already learning which plants need tending at which point in the condensation cycle. By ten, they've spent time in the factory spaces, not as workers but as observers, absorbing the rhythms of production. By twelve, they've begun to specialize—not rigidly, not in a way that locks them into a single path, but in the way that their particular affinities have become clear. This one is good with the moisture systems. That one has a feel for the kiln temperatures. Another has the patience for the medicinal plants, the ones that require intimate knowledge of leaf and phase and timing.

The adolescence of Chen is not dramatic. There are no rebellions against the system because the system is not perceived as a system; it is perceived as the way things are, the way water flows downhill, the way condensation forms on cool surfaces. A teenager might question the allocation of resources or the hierarchy of the work-crews, might argue for a different approach to maintaining the upper-level gardens or challenge the foreman's decisions about which machines to repair and which to retire. But these are practical arguments, grounded in the shared context of keeping the settlement alive. They are not existential. The teenagers of Chen do not stare at the fog and wonder if there's something more, because the fog is not a barrier to something else—it is the medium of their existence, and they can read it like a page.

## The Elders and the Knowledge-Holders

The elders of Chen are not necessarily old. Authority here is not chronological but accumulative—it belongs to those who have absorbed the most information about the city's systems, who can diagnose a problem in the moisture network by listening to the sound of water moving through stone, who know which building is going to need structural attention before the cracks become visible, who remember which crop varieties failed in the drier years and which survived. Some of these people are old. Some are in their thirties, having specialized early and deeply. What they share is a quality of attention that is hard to describe but easy to recognize: they listen to the city the way a doctor listens to a body, with a trained ear that picks up abnormalities invisible to the untrained.

The priests of the moisture shrines are a separate category, or perhaps not separate—perhaps a specialized extension of the elder function. They tend the oldest parts of the hydrological infrastructure, the collection basins carved into bedrock that predate every standing structure. Their work is half practical (maintaining the channels, clearing sediment, ensuring the flow) and half something else—ritual, perhaps, or memory, or a form of attention so focused and so prolonged that it becomes indistinguishable from devotion. They know things about the plateau that geologists don't. They know which whalebacks produce the most condensation and why. They know the taste of water from different collection points and can tell you which minerals it has picked up from which stone. They know the rhythms of the cloud-sea below the plateau's edge, the patterns that determine whether a day will be wet or dry, the subtle indicators that precede a heavy rainfall or a drought.

Whether what they do is religion depends on your definition. They perform rituals at the collection points—quiet, private, involving water and stone and the laying on of hands in a literal sense, palms pressed flat against the bedrock. But the rituals have practical outcomes: the moisture-channels they maintain are cleaner, the collection more efficient, the water sweeter. Whether the rituals cause this or whether the rituals are simply the form that maintenance takes when it's been practiced long enough to become habitual, nobody asks. The priests don't ask. The people don't ask. The plateau doesn't ask.

The factory foremen are another kind of knowledge-holder. They run the industrial processes that keep the city alive—not with the mechanical precision of engineers but with the intuitive understanding of people who have grown up around machines and learned their personalities. A foreman knows that the pump in building seven runs better in the afternoon because the warmer air helps the condensation cycle. A foreman knows that the kiln in the old moisture-channel needs its bricks replaced on a schedule that isn't written down anywhere but is remembered in the foreman's body, in the way a musician remembers a piece of music. The machines of Chen are not abstractions. They are presences—stubborn, idiosyncratic, requiring a particular kind of attention that is half technical and half relational. The foremen don't fix machines. They work with them.

## The Specialists

Beyond the generalists—the people who maintain, who build, who harvest, who repair—there are those who have gone deep into particular niches the city provides.

The gardeners are the most visible. They tend the vertical growth that covers every surface, the trained vines and rooftop trees and moss carpets and herb pockets that make Chen look less like a settlement and more like an inhabited cliff-face. They work at heights, climbing the trellises and scaffolding with an ease that suggests they were doing this before they could walk, and some of them were. The gardeners know the plants the way the foremen know the machines—as individuals, as personalities, as living things with preferences and aversions and bad days. A gardener can look at a vine and tell you whether it's getting enough moisture, enough light, enough nutrients from the substrate it's rooted in. A gardener can look at a rooftop garden and see the whole system at once—the way the trees shade the herbs, the way the moss retains water for the flowers, the way the insects move between them carrying pollen and information.

The herbalists are a more specialized subset, the ones who work with the medicinal plants. Their knowledge is precise and largely oral, passed from practitioner to apprentice in a chain that goes back further than anyone can trace. They know which leaves to pick at dawn condensation and which at the evening cycle. They know which roots to harvest during the dry periods and which to leave alone. They know how to prepare compounds that treat the ailments common to Chen—respiratory issues from the damp air, joint complaints from the cold, the occasional infections that spread through the dense living conditions. Their workshops smell of green things and damp earth and the faint mineral tang of the water they use in their preparations. They are quiet people, the herbalists. They tend to speak in the lower registers that the city's acoustics favor, and they have a way of looking at you that suggests they're reading your body the way they read their plants—checking for signs of health and deficiency, noting what needs tending.

The metalworkers occupy a different space. Their workshops are warmer than the rest of the city, heated by the forges and furnaces they maintain, and they work with materials that are scarcer than stone or plant fiber—salvaged metal, repurposed equipment, the occasional vein of ore that someone has found in the bedrock and mined with patient effort. They produce the structural supports that hold buildings together, the fittings for the moisture systems, the tools that every other trade relies on. They are among the most respected specialists in Chen, not because their work is more important than anyone else's but because their work enables everyone else's. Without the metalworkers, the pumps fail. Without the pumps, the water stops. Without the water, the plants die. Without the plants, the city dies. The metalworkers know this. They carry the knowledge with a particular kind of gravity that is not pride but responsibility.

The moisture engineers—if that's the right term, and it might not be, because their work doesn't have a single name in the settlement—occupy the space between the priests and the plumbers. They maintain the distribution network that moves water from the collection basins to every floor of every building, through channels carved in stone and pipes fashioned from fired clay and salvaged metal. Their work is invisible when it's going well, which is the goal. A perfectly functioning moisture system is one that nobody notices, one that delivers water where it's needed without sound or spectacle. The engineers know this and accept it. They are the sort of people who take satisfaction in the absence of evidence of their work, which is a particular kind of personality, one that values function over recognition. You can spot them by their hands, which are always slightly damp and slightly stained with the mineral deposits left by the water they work with. Their hands are maps of the distribution network, the calluses and stains corresponding to the valves and junctions they maintain.

## The Amphibious Things

Something lives in the moisture-channels. Everyone knows this. Nobody has fully described it.

The moisture-channels are the oldest infrastructure in Chen, carved into the bedrock by hands that may not have been human, or may have been human but different, or may have been human and exactly the same but working with knowledge that has since been lost. The channels are shallow grooves, some only a hand's-width wide, some broad enough to walk in if you crouch, and they direct the night's condensation from the collection surfaces to the storage basins. They work by gravity and by capillary action and by principles that the moisture engineers understand in practice but not in theory.

The things that live in the channels are small. This is known because the channels are small, and nothing larger than a hand could move through them comfortably. They are dark-colored—dark gray, dark green, reports vary—and they are wet, obviously, because they live in water channels. They keep the channels clear of sediment. They keep the water moving. They eat whatever accumulates in the slow flow—mineral deposits, organic debris, the occasional insect that falls in and can't get out. Their presence is inferred from their effects: channels with thriving populations of these creatures run clear and clean. Channels without them clog and fail.

The leading theory is that they are amphibians of some kind, descended from a species introduced by the original settlers, adapted over generations to the specific conditions of the moisture network. The alternative theory is that they evolved in place, in the channels themselves, from something that was already there when the first humans arrived. The priests have a third theory that involves the moisture-channels being alive in a way that has nothing to do with conventional biology, the creatures being manifestations of the channels' own maintenance function. Nobody argues about this. The theories coexist because the creature's actual nature is less important than their actual function, and their function is clear: they keep the water flowing.

Children love them. Spotting one in a channel is a childhood milestone, a moment of delight and accomplishment. You have to be patient and still and quiet, crouching by a channel access point at the right time of day—early morning is best, when the condensation flow is strongest and the creatures are most active—and you have to be lucky. They are fast and they are shy and they are gone before you can focus your adapted eyes. But sometimes, sometimes, you see a dark shape moving through the water, a sinuous ripple that could be current but isn't, and you know.

## The Birds

The birds of Chen are small and brown and unremarkable in every way except the way that matters: they have chosen to be here. At this elevation, with this thin air and this limited food supply, most birds don't bother. These ones have. They've adapted to the city's microclimates, nesting in the warm spaces near factory exhausts, feeding on the insects that the vegetation supports, drinking from the collection basins and the drip-channels. They are not numerous. A careful count might find a few hundred individuals across the entire settlement. But they are present, and their presence is a sign of something—the city producing enough life to sustain creatures that have a choice about where to live.

They ride the thermals that rise from the city's heat sources, spiraling upward in the dim light, and from below they look like flecks of ash caught in an updraft, except that ash doesn't turn and dive and settle on a railing and look at you with one bright eye before flying off to investigate something more interesting. They are curious birds. This is perhaps their defining trait, beyond the smallness and the brownness: they are interested in things. They watch the people work. They investigate new structures. They land on machines that have just been shut down and peer into the cooling mechanisms. Whether this is intelligence or simply the behavioral flexibility that comes from living in a complex environment, nobody has determined. The children name them. The adults tolerate them. The gardeners appreciate them because they eat the insects that would otherwise damage the crops.

## The Moths

The moths are a problem. This is acknowledged.

They are large—larger than a hand, sometimes larger than two hands, if you're measuring wingspan. They are gray-white, the color of the clouds, and they are attracted to light in a way that goes beyond attraction into something more like compulsion. The electrical lights that the city maintains for nighttime visibility—necessary for the crews that work the dark hours, for the safety of people moving through the vertical streets—draw the moths in vast numbers, and the moths, being large and not particularly coordinated, clog intake vents and settle on warm machinery and generally interfere with systems that were not designed to accommodate them.

The moths have their season. During the warmer periods, when the temperature rises enough to trigger their breeding cycle, they emerge from wherever they hide—crevices in the stone, folds in the building fabric, the spaces between walls that nobody has inspected in decades—and they come to the lights. The city has adapted. Intake vents are screened. Machines that run hot are shielded. The night crews carry nets and brushes and a kind of resigned patience that comes from knowing that the moths are not going away and that the solution is management, not elimination. The moths are part of the ecosystem. Something eats them. Something depends on them. Removing them would create a gap that something else would fill, and the something else might be worse.

The children catch them, sometimes. Not to keep—to hold briefly, to feel the impossible softness of the wings and the frantic vibration of the body, and then to release, because the moths are fragile and the children know this. There is a game that involves guessing where a released moth will go, which light it will find, and the children are better at this game than the adults because their eyes read the dim light better and they understand the moth's trajectory in a way that requires no calculation, only intuition.

## The Plants, As Presences

To call the plants of Chen "vegetation" is to understate their role in the settlement's life. They are not decoration. They are not landscaping. They are inhabitants.

The trees—the things that are like trees, the woody vines that have learned to stand upright, the large shrubs that have committed to height—they are the most visible. They occupy the rooftops and courtyards and their roots push through the stone in ways that would be destructive if the stone weren't already so worn, so soft, so ready to accept intrusion. The trees are old, some of them. Older than the buildings they grow beside, in some cases. Their growth rings, if anyone were to cut them—which no one would, because a tree in Chen is an investment of decades of care and training—would tell the history of the settlement's moisture cycles, wet years and dry years encoded in the width of the wood.

The moss is the most ubiquitous and the most easily overlooked. It covers everything. Every horizontal surface that isn't actively maintained, every vertical surface that isn't regularly scraped. It is the city's skin, in a way—a living layer that mediates between the stone and the air, absorbing moisture, providing substrate for other plants, softening the visual harshness of the built environment. The moss is always damp. The moss is always growing. The moss is always dying and being replaced, the lower layers decomposing into the substrate that the higher plants need. It is a slow, continuous cycle that happens at a timescale invisible to a casual observer but visible to anyone who sits still long enough to watch.

The food plants are the most managed. They grow in designated spaces—balcony railings, rooftop beds, vertical gardens trained on south-facing walls where the thin light is most likely to reach. They are adapted to the cool temperatures, the intermittent moisture, the compressed growing cycles that the plateau's climate imposes. They are not the food plants of lower altitudes—no tomatoes, no corn, nothing that requires sustained heat. Leafy greens. Root vegetables that tolerate shallow soil. Legumes that fix their own nitrogen because the soil here is thin and must be helped. The gardeners select for traits that the lowland world doesn't value: cold tolerance, mist tolerance, the ability to grow in substrate that is more moss and compost than dirt. The resulting crops are nutrient-dense and unremarkable. They taste like Chen: slightly mineral, slightly green, substantial in a way that doesn't announce itself.

The bioluminescent varieties are the strangest, the aspect of Chen's vegetation that visitors most often remark on. Someone, at some point, introduced plants that glow. Or engineered them. Or discovered them. The origin is unclear. But they are here now, integrated into the urban fabric, planted in deliberate patterns along walkways and stairwells and the edges of courtyards. At night, when the condensation cycle begins and the temperature drops, they emit a soft light—not bright enough to read by, not bright enough to replace the electrical lights, but bright enough to see, to navigate, to find your way through the city's vertical maze when the clouds are thick and the darkness is close. The light is blue-green, the color of deep water, and it gives the nighttime city an appearance that is hard to describe and hard to forget: a living architecture, glowing from within, the walls and floors and railings marked with the soft light of organisms that have found their niche and filled it.

The people of Chen have incorporated this into their daily life. The bioluminescent plants are maintained like any other—not revered, not ritualized, just tended. The children learn which ones are brightest at which phases of the condensation cycle. The gardeners prune them to maximize light output along the paths that need it most. The elderly sometimes sit beside them in the evening, watching the glow intensify as the temperature drops, and this is not a ritual or a practice but simply a thing that people do because the light is soft and the evening is cool and the plants are beautiful in a way that requires no explanation.

## The Way They Live Together

The density rating says 2.4—many presences per volume—and this is the truth of Chen. The people live close. Not just physically close, though they are that—walls shared, floors overlapping, the breath of your neighbor audible through the stone—but close in the way that people are close when they have depended on each other for generations. Everyone knows everyone. Not in the way that small-town gossip suggests, with its intrusions and judgments, but in the way that a body knows itself: the knowledge of parts that function together, each one aware of the others, each one adjusting continuously to maintain the whole.

Privacy exists in Chen, but it is a different kind of privacy. It is not the privacy of distance and walls but the privacy of respect and restraint. You can hear your neighbor through the wall. You do not listen. You can see into the courtyard below from your balcony. You do not stare. The social contract is not enforced by law but by the deep, practiced understanding that in a place this dense, the only way to have privacy is to give it. Everyone retreats into themselves not by building barriers but by practicing a kind of attention that knows where to stop, where to look away, where to let the other person be alone even when they are physically three meters away.

They eat together, often. Not in formal communal settings—the settlement is too large for that now—but in the informal clusters that the architecture creates: the shared kitchens that serve several families, the rooftop gardens where people gather at midday, the workshop floors where workers eat lunch among their machines. The food is shared, the work is shared, the space is shared, and the sharing is not generosity but structure. This is how the city works. This is how it has always worked. The people of Chen would not understand the concept of self-sufficiency as a virtue, because in Chen, sufficiency is always collective. No single person can maintain a moisture-channel, operate a factory floor, tend a vertical garden, raise a child, and repair a roof. But a building can. A neighborhood can. The settlement can.

They argue. They disagree. There are tensions—between the gardeners and the builders, who compete for the same surfaces. Between the factory foremen and the moisture engineers, who compete for the same water. Between the old families who have lived in the lower levels for generations and the newer arrivals who have built upward into the fresher air. These tensions are not suppressed. They are worked through, in the slow, patient way that everything in Chen happens, through meetings and conversations and the gradual adjustment of systems to accommodate competing needs. Sometimes the tensions resolve. Sometimes they persist, becoming part of the city's texture, another layer of complexity in a place that has never been simple.

## The Visitors, Who Are Also Inhabitants, Briefly

Visitors come to Chen. Not many, but some. They come up the long gentle slope of the plateau, following paths that have been walked smooth by a billion years of foot traffic—animal, human, wind. They come because Chen produces things that the lower world wants: the plant-fiber fabrics that are uniquely fine because the plants grow slowly in the cool air. The metalwork that has a particular quality because the salvaged materials and the patient forging produce something that fresh metal doesn't. The herbal compounds that the lower world values for their effectiveness against ailments that the lower world's medicines don't treat as well.

The visitors stay in the lower levels, usually, in the spaces designated for trade and guest-occupancy. They find Chen disorienting. The dim light, the dense air, the verticality, the proximity of other people, the continuous low hum of machines, the omnipresent damp, the moss and the vines and the bioluminescent glow at night—it's a lot. The visitors tend to speak too loudly at first, then too softly as they overcompensate. They get lost. The streets don't follow a logic that makes sense to someone who learned to navigate in a grid or a radial pattern. The streets follow the moisture-channels, which follow the bedrock, which follows the terrain of a plateau so old that its terrain is more memory than geology.

But the visitors adjust. Given a few days, the light begins to resolve. The sounds begin to separate. The verticality becomes navigable rather than threatening. The damp becomes pleasant—or if not pleasant, then tolerable, a reminder that this is a place where water is not wasted but collected, where moisture is the medium of life rather than an inconvenience to be wiped away.

And then they leave. They go down the long slope with their trade goods and their stories about the city in the clouds, the place where the plants glow and the people speak softly and the machines hum in the basements and something small and dark lives in the water channels and nobody knows exactly what it is. They tell these stories and the stories become part of Chen's existence in the wider world—a place that is known but not visited, described but not understood, a rumor of a city at the crown of the worn-down world, where everything has been happening for long enough that the happening has become the place itself.

## The City, As A Living Thing

What lives in Chen, ultimately, is Chen.

Not the people, though the people live there. Not the plants, though the plants grow there. Not the machines, though the machines run there. Not the creatures in the channels or the birds on the thermals or the moths at the lights. All of these are inhabitants, presences, parts. But the thing that lives in Chen—the thing that has developed emergent properties that nobody designed, the thing that operates as a single integrated system of stone and water and fiber and metal and chlorophyll and breath—that thing is the city itself.

It has the quality of an organism. Not in the metaphorical sense, or not only in the metaphorical sense. The city takes in resources and processes them and produces outputs and maintains itself and grows and adapts and responds to damage and evolves over time. The people are its nervous system, sensing and responding. The plants are its respiratory function, exchanging gases and managing moisture. The machines are its organs, performing specific tasks within the whole. The moisture-channels are its circulation. The stone is its skeleton, and the moss is its skin, and the bioluminescent plants are its eyes in the dark.

This is not how the people of Chen think about it. They think about their work and their families and their neighbors and their food and their lives. They think about the moisture cycle and the growing season and whether the pump in building seven needs its gaskets replaced. They do not stand back and perceive the city as an organism, because they are inside the organism, the way a neuron doesn't perceive the brain.

But visitors perceive it. Standing in a courtyard at dusk, watching the bioluminescent glow begin as the temperature drops, hearing the hum of the machines shift to their nighttime register, feeling the condensation begin to form on their skin, they perceive it. And they are not wrong. The perception is accurate. Chen is alive in a way that goes beyond the sum of its inhabitants, alive in the way that a coral reef is alive or a forest is alive, alive in the way that only becomes visible when you see the whole system at once, which is only possible from outside.

From inside, from the perspective of someone who has lived in Chen their whole life, the city is not an organism. It is home. It is the place where you wake to the sound of dripping water and eat the food that grew on your balcony and walk to work through streets that your ancestors walked and tend the machines that your predecessor tended and go to sleep in the cool quiet while the city hums around you, alive, alive, alive, in a way that doesn't require your awareness to be real.
