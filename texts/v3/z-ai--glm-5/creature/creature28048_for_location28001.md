# What Lives Here

---

The first living thing you would miss.

It is not hidden — it is simply so much a part of the landscape that your eyes pass over it the way they pass over the salt itself. The fog-towers do not announce themselves. They stand, and the plateau stands with them, and everything is the same mineral white under the same black-blue sky, and if you do not look carefully you might believe you have come to a place where nothing lives at all.

But they live.

---

## The Towers

Begin with them, because they are the architects of everything else.

A fog-tower is not a plant in the way you might think of plants. It does not grow toward light. It does not bend toward warmth. It grows toward *wet* — reaching upward into the altitude where fog forms, its branching spines extended like hands cupped to catch rain that will never fall as rain. Each spine is a filament of structural tissue sheathed in a living skin no thicker than parchment, and the skin is coated with an excretion that has the texture of dried honey: tacky, faintly sweet-smelling if you bring your nose close enough, which you would not want to do because the air at that height is not generous to lungs.

The excretion is the key. It is hygroscopic beyond anything the lowland world has produced — it pulls water vapor from the air with an efficiency that borders on violence. When a cloud passes through a stand of towers, the moisture in it condenses against the spines in droplets so fine they are almost invisible, and these droplets run down micro-channels etched into the spine surfaces by their own slow passage, gathering into threads, then rivulets, then a slow drip into the core of the plant. The core is a reservoir — a hollow chamber in the lattice skeleton, lined with a waxy tissue that holds water against the vacuum of the air. A mature tower can hold hundreds of liters in its core, and it dispenses this water in measures so stingy, so carefully rationed, that a single fill can sustain a tower through months of drought.

They are old. A fog-tower that stands forty meters tall may be older than the pyramids. Their growth is measured in the accumulation of dry seasons — each year, if the fog was good, adds a new layer of spines at the crown, and the previous year's growth hardens into scaffold. If the fog was poor, nothing is added, and the tower simply persists, waiting. You can read their histories in their geometry: thick bands of dense branching mark good decades; thin gaps, where the skeleton jumps from one level to the next without intermediate growth, mark years when the plant barely survived. Some towers have dark streaks in their lower skeletons — scars from some ancient event, a period of dust storm or extreme drought, when the living skin died back to near-nothing and the plant existed as little more than a post of dried tissue, waiting for better times.

They do not reproduce in any way you can observe. No seeds fall from them. No spores drift from their crowns. And yet there are young towers — small, waist-high frameworks of a dozen spines, standing in the lee of older individuals. They appear in the shelter of the mats, where the ground is slightly less hostile, and they grow with a slowness that makes coral seem hasty. How they arrive is one of the plateau's unspoken mysteries. Perhaps the wind carries something — a fragment of tissue, a dormant nodule, small enough to be invisible — and it settles where the conditions are marginally right, and it begins. Or perhaps the mats produce them, in some botanical exchange too slow and too quiet for human observation to catch.

What is certain is that they die. The dead towers stand for a long time — their skeletons are mineral-dense, hardened by decades of growth, and the dry air preserves them. But the wind abrades them, grain by grain, and over centuries, they erode into streamlined shapes, then stumps, then low ridges of crystalline dust that merge with the salt-plain. In some parts of the plateau, you can find stands of dead towers still standing, their lattice-structures white as bone, hollow, the wind singing through them in tones that rise and fall with the weather. These are not graveyards. The towers do not commemorate their dead. They simply stand beside them, living and dead together, in the same relation they had in life — spaced apart, each in its own territory of fog, each reaching upward into the same clouds.

## The Mats

At the base of perhaps a third of the towers, the ground changes. The bare salt gives way to something that is not quite soil and not quite plant — a spreading circle of reddish-brown matter, low and soft, covering the white surface in patches that range from a meter to ten meters across. These are the mats.

The mats are not single organisms. Each one is a colony — or perhaps a community — of small, disc-like growths that multiply at their edges, expanding outward in a slow tide. Each disc is the size of a coin, flat, covered in fine hairs that are not hairs at all but elongated cells adapted to trap moisture. The hairs are hygroscopic, like the tower spines, but they work differently: they absorb water directly through their cell walls, swelling visibly when wet and shrinking to near-invisibility when dry. In the fog-hours, a mat becomes damp, dark, almost muddy to the touch. In the clear-hours, it dries to a crispness that crumbles underfoot if you are careless.

The mats derive something from the towers in their proximity. What, exactly, is unclear. They cluster on the lee side of the towers, where the wind is slightly less fierce and where the tower's fog-harvest leaves a residue of moisture in the air — a mist the towers have passed through but not fully consumed. Perhaps they are parasites, drawing from the towers' catchment. Perhaps they are commensals, taking what the towers do not want. Perhaps the relationship is something else — the mats break down mineral substrates in the ground, releasing trace elements that wash into the tower's root-analogues during the rare rain, and the towers shelter the mats in return. If this is true, it is a partnership so slow that no human lifetime could observe a full cycle of exchange.

In the center of older mats, there are nodules. They are dense, dark, the size of a fist or smaller, and they are warm to the touch — warmer than the surrounding air by several degrees. This warmth is faint but real, and it suggests metabolic activity: the nodules are doing something, processing something, producing some kind of energy. It is tempting to call them storage organs, or seeds, or even primitive flowers, but none of these terms fits. They are simply themselves — growths that appear in mature mats, that persist for decades, that do not visibly change, that are warm. If there is a purpose to the mats, it lives in these nodules, and the nodules do not explain themselves.

The insect-like things are drawn to the nodules. Whether they feed on them, or tend them, or simply shelter in their warmth, is unclear.

## The Insects

They are not insects. They have six legs and segmented bodies and they are small, and so the word suggests itself, but they are not insects in any taxonomic sense. They are something else — a form of life that arrived at a similar body plan through a different evolutionary path, or perhaps through no evolutionary path at all, if this place has its own history of life separate from the lowland world.

They are pale. Translucent, even. In the harsh sunlight, you can see the shadows of their own organs through their exoskeletons — dark threads of gut, small pulsing nodes that might be hearts. They are the size of a thumbnail, flat-bodied, and they move with a slowness that seems deliberate, each leg placed with care, as if they are navigating terrain far more complex than the smooth bark they cling to.

They live in the crevices of the fog-towers, in the gaps between the lattice-structures where the living skin meets the dead scaffold. There, in those narrow spaces, the air is slightly less thin, slightly more humid — the tower's own respiration creates a microclimate, a thin envelope of moisture and warmth that the insects never leave. They live their entire lives in a corridor no wider than your finger, crawling from one end to the other, feeding on what, no one knows. Perhaps they eat the tower's outer tissue. Perhaps they eat the hygroscopic excretion. Perhaps they eat each other. There are never many of them — a dozen, perhaps, on a large tower — and their populations seem stable over long periods, neither growing nor shrinking, as if they have reached some equilibrium with their enormous hosts and see no reason to change.

In the fog-hours, they become more active. The moisture that runs down the tower's spines reaches their crevices, and they cluster around the drops, drinking, or absorbing, or simply sitting in the wetness. Their translucent bodies darken when wet, as if the water fills spaces in their tissues that were empty before. Then the fog lifts, and they retreat into the deepest crevices, and they wait.

They do not fly. They do not sing. They do not seem to reproduce in any visible way — no eggs, no larvae, no molting. And yet they persist, generation after unobserved generation, in the same narrow corridors of the same enormous towers, living at a scale so small and a pace so slow that their lives are, to a human observer, indistinguishable from stillness.

## The Fox

There is one animal here that you might see, if you are patient and lucky and if the light is right.

It is not a fox. It has the shape of a fox — the pointed face, the narrow body, the tail carried low — but it is larger than any fox you have known, and its limbs are too long, and its chest is too deep, and its lungs, if you could see them, would be enormous, filling the bulk of its thorax with surfaces for extracting oxygen from air that has almost none. Its fur is pale, not white but silver, the same silver as the salt-plain, and it is dense — so dense that the wind, which moves everything else on the plateau, does not move the fur at all. It lies flat and smooth, and the creature seems carved from the landscape itself, a living piece of the plateau that has detached itself and begun to walk.

It walks. That is what you see it do. It walks between the fog-towers at a pace that is neither fast nor slow, covering ground with a stride so efficient it appears almost lazy. It does not run. It does not stop. It moves in a straight line, or nearly straight, from one tower to the next, and at each tower it pauses — sometimes for a moment, sometimes for longer — and it does something at the base that you cannot see from a distance. It lowers its head. It seems to press its muzzle into the mat, or into the ground, or into some feature too small to observe. Then it lifts its head and walks on.

You see it once. It appears at the edge of visibility, a pale shape moving against the white ground, and it passes across your field of view in a silence so complete that you doubt its reality. Then it is gone, behind a tower or over a ridge, and the plateau is empty again, and you are left with the impression of something that does not belong to you — a life that has its own purposes, its own routes, its own relationship to this place, and that relationship does not include you.

There is only one. Or if there are more, they are spread so thinly across the plateau that they never meet, or if they meet, they meet in ways that leave no evidence. No tracks. No dens. No remains. The fox-creature passes through this place like weather — present, then absent, leaving nothing behind but the memory of its passing.

What does it eat? It is not clear. It is not emaciated — the one you see, or think you see, seems well-fed, its fur thick, its movement unhurried. Something on this plateau sustains it. Perhaps the nodules in the mats. Perhaps the insects in the towers. Perhaps something in the towers themselves — the sweet-smelling excretion, or the water in the core reservoirs, or the living skin, which might be nutritious in ways that are not obvious. Or perhaps it descends. Perhaps the plateau is not its whole world but only its refuge — the place it returns to when the lowlands are too hot, too wet, too full of competitors, and it rides the fog up the continental shelf and walks the white plain in the cool hours and descends again before dawn to feed in places you cannot follow. If this is true, it crosses an ecological boundary every day of its life, and the plateau is not its home but its way station, and its true life is lived somewhere else, somewhere lower and warmer and more hospitable, and the plateau knows it only as a visitor.

But this is speculation. The fox-creature does not explain itself.

## The Wind

There is one more thing that lives here, if living is the word.

The wind is not alive. It has no cells, no metabolism, no intent. But it is the dominant presence on the plateau, the force that shapes everything, and to live here is to live in relation to the wind. It is a thing with character: it is never still, never random, never gentle. It blows from the west-southwest, always, and it has blown from that direction for longer than the fog-towers have stood. It is the reason the towers lean. It is the reason the salt-plain is ridged. It is the reason the dead towers erode into streamlined shapes. It is the reason the insects live in crevices and not on surfaces, the reason the mats grow on the lee side of towers and not the windward, the reason the fox-creature's fur lies flat.

The wind carries dust. Fine crystals of salt, mineral grains, fragments of eroded fog-tower skeletons — all of this moves in the wind, at a height between ankle and knee, in a layer of abrasive suspension that is too low to breathe but exactly high enough to scour everything it touches. Walk through it and your boots are sandblasted. Stand still and the ground around your feet is swept clean in a circle. The wind is the plateau's cleaning crew, its sculptor, its undertaker, its gossip — carrying news of distant weather in the shifting weight of its dust load, its temperature, its moisture content.

The fog-towers have learned the wind. Their spines grow in patterns that channel it, redirecting gusts around their crowns, minimizing drag. Their skeletons are streamlined without being symmetric — they are thicker on the windward side, where the stress is greatest, and thinner on the lee, where the shelter allows. The dead towers, stripped of their living skin, reveal this architecture clearly: the windward face is dense, almost solid, while the lee face is open lattice, almost hollow. The wind built these structures as much as the plants did.

And the wind makes the only sound that is not silence. When it passes through a stand of dead towers, it produces tones — low, sustained, harmonics that shift as the wind shifts. The sound is not music. It has no melody, no rhythm, no intention. But it has form. It rises and falls, and it carries across the white plain in waves that come and go, and if you stand in the right place at the right time, you can hear it — a chord that has been sounding for thousands of years, unchanged, unbroken, the voice of the wind in the bones of the dead.

## The Everything

These are the inhabitants of the plateau: the towers that harvest fog, the mats that cluster at their feet, the insects that live in their crevices, the fox-creature that walks between them, the wind that shapes them all. And beneath and around and through them, the thing that is not an inhabitant but the condition of all inhabitation: the salt itself, the fossil sea, the mineral plain that is the ground and the medium and the constraint.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Nothing here is competing in any way you can observe. The towers do not crowd each other. The mats do not overrun their neighbors. The insects do not swarm. The fox-creature does not hunt. The wind does not vary. Everything is in a balance so finely tuned and so slowly oscillating that it appears static, and it is not static — it is simply moving at a pace that the human eye, the human lifespan, the human sense of time, cannot register.

To live here is to live in a conversation that has been going on for a million years and will go on for a million more. The towers speak to the fog. The mats speak to the towers. The insects speak to the towers and to each other. The fox-creature speaks to the mats, or to the towers, or to something else. The wind speaks to everything. And the salt listens, and the salt remembers, and the salt will be here when the conversation is over.

These are the lives on the cloud-fed plateau. They do not need you. They do not know you. They do not care. They are going on without us.
