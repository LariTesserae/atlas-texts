# What Lives Here

Nothing lives here in the way you mean the word. But that distinction becomes less useful the longer you stay.

---

The bird-things come first because they are the most visible, if *visible* is a word you can apply to something that exists primarily in the negative space of sound. There are more than one—you understand this after several encounters, though you never see more than a single dark shape at any given moment. They differ in the gaps they occupy. One rides the higher harmonics, the shimmering overtones, and when it passes you feel a brief cold spot near the crown of your skull, a place where the sound thins enough for something to slip through. Another works the lower registers, the bass tones that live in the stone, and this one you feel in your gut when it moves—a momentary unsettled lurch, as if the ground has forgotten its own density.

They are not birds. You call them that because the shape implies wings, implies a body tapered for flight, but the implication is all you receive. They don't have feathers. You're fairly certain of this. What they have is a relationship with the sound—a syntax, a way of reading the plateau's constant output and finding purchase within it. They eat the silence between tones. They nest in the places where the frequency wavers, those momentary instabilities that shouldn't exist in a sound this steady but do, because even perpetuity has its stutters.

When you think about it—lying on the stone at what might be night, the violet-dark pressing down and the staring stars fixed above—you realize the bird-things might be the sound's way of knowing itself. Its own immune system, perhaps. Its own white blood cells, moving through the waveform, testing for integrity. You are an anomaly. They pass you without aggression but with an attention that feels diagnostic. They are determining whether you belong here, and they are patient about delivering a verdict because patience is the only currency this place trades in.

---

The goat-thing is not alone. You learn this on what you decide to call the fourth day, though the numbering is meaningless—a private anchor against the thickened time. You find another in a different shallow cave, and then a third in a depression that seems carved specifically for it, a body-shaped hollow in the granite that fits the creature so precisely it might have been grown rather than cut.

They are not goats. The resemblance is a convenience, or a concession, or a kind of protective camouflage aimed at something that no longer visits. The flesh gives like bread dough because it is not flesh as you understand it. It is something more like memory given mass, or sound given a body, or the plateau's own substance experimenting with the shape of something warm-blooded. They breathe because breathing is a pattern the plateau has observed and is trying on. The breath is real—the air moves, the chest expands—but the slowness of it, the minute-long exhale, suggests a respiratory system built to a different timescale. Not slow because they are lethargic. Slow because they exist at a rhythm where a minute is a heartbeat and a heartbeat is a breath and a breath is a season.

They don't eat. You find no evidence of grazing, no scat, no signs of consumption. The condensation beads on their fur in the mornings—the same moisture that pools on the stone, the same water that waits—and by afternoon their coats have dried to the same pale, neutral color as the tufted grasses. You think, at first, that they drink the condensation through their skin, but the truth is stranger and simpler: they *are* the condensation, partly. The moisture cycle includes them. They are one of its stages, the way a cloud is a stage of water. The water beads on them and leaves them and returns to them and this is not a thing that happens to them but a thing that they *are*.

There are more than you initially count. In the dim light, against the pale granite, they are nearly invisible. You develop an eye for them eventually—a slight wrongness in the texture of a rock face, a curve too smooth for erosion, a patch of color that sits one shade too warm for the surrounding stone. They choose caves and depressions and hollows, but some of them simply sit in the open, tucked against boulders, and you walk past them for hours before your mind registers what your eyes have been showing it. They are everywhere. Dozens. Perhaps more. They do not react to you because you are not relevant to them in the way that weather is not relevant to stone. You pass through their awareness the way a brief change in humidity passes through yours—noted, perhaps, but not actionable.

You wonder, sometimes, if they were once something else. If the plateau's patience has been working on them the way it works on the granite—wearing away the sharp distinctions, eroding the edges of whatever they used to be until they fit so perfectly into this place that they have become part of its furniture. They might be the end result of staying too long. The final form of someone who broke the second rule—*do not stay still for too long*—and was slowly, gently, irreversibly absorbed.

You think about the mold your body left in the stone after a day's rest. You think about how deep it might have gone if you'd stayed a week. A month. A year.

You think about the breathing that takes a full minute and you do not think about it too long.

---

The vegetation is not vegetation. Or it is, but only technically—a taxonomic truth that obscures a functional lie. The grasses that cling to the fissures, the bone-colored tufts, the translucent succulents swollen with condensation—they photosynthesize, or seem to, or go through the motions of something that once was photosynthesis and now is a habit too ingrained to abandon. They are organic in the sense that they contain carbon. They are alive in the sense that they persist.

But they don't grow. Not in any observable way. The colonies you find on your first day are the same size, the same shape, the same degree of shrivel-and-swell on your last. They exist in a state of completion, having arrived at whatever they were becoming and stopped there. They are not waiting to grow. They are not waiting for anything. They are finished in a way that living things rarely are, and this is what makes them frightening in a way the goat-things are not. The goat-things at least breathe. The plants have achieved a stillness that goes beyond stillness into something like defiance. *I am this,* they say without saying. *I will not be more. I will not be less. The cycle of moisture is the only change I require.*

The way they seem to track you is the way a compass needle seems to track north. It is not attention. It is orientation. They have aligned themselves to something—some aspect of the sound, some frequency or harmonic that gives them their direction—and you, moving through the sound, disturbing it with your counterpoint pulse and your breathing, create a disturbance they reorient toward. Not watching. Adjusting. The way a gyroscope adjusts to remain upright. You are a perturbation in their field, and they correct for you.

If you stayed long enough, you think, they would grow around you. Not as a vine grows over a wall, actively, hungrily, but as water fills a container—passively, inevitably, because you would be there and they would be there and the space between you would eventually become occupied. It would not be aggressive. It would not even be intentional. It would just be the nature of things that have finished becoming, filling available space.

You step around them carefully.

---

The stone that thinks is the most difficult to describe because description requires separation—the observer and the observed, the subject and the object—and the thinking stone does not recognize this separation. It is the ground you walk on. It is the boulders you rest against. It is the mountainside and the plateau and the bedrock beneath both, extending down into the earth in a continuous body that has no surface because it is all interior. It does not think the way you think, with sequences and symbols and the bright flicker of association. It thinks the way a mountain thinks, which is to say it *is* a thought, and has been thinking for longer than most thoughts have words for.

You feel it underfoot. That quality—the *thinking* quality—is not a metaphor. The ground has a presence that is different from mere solidity. It knows you are there. Not in a cognitive sense—the stone does not have cognition, does not have a self to cognize with—but in a material sense. The way a riverbed knows the river. The way a mold knows the hand that pressed it. The stone receives your weight and your warmth and the vibration of your pulse, and it incorporates these into its own slow awareness without urgency or interest.

The pale blue warm stone in the hearth-ruin is different. It is separate—not continuous with the bedrock, not part of the thinking mass. It has been placed. It holds heat with a stubbornness that defies the plateau's uniform cold, and when you touched it, you felt something you have not yet been able to name. Not warmth, though warmth was part of it. Not thought, though thought was adjacent. Something like *recognition.* The stone knew your hand. Not your hand specifically, but the category of your hand—the warmth, the pressure, the oils, the particular frequency of a living mammal's touch. It had felt this before. Many times. Over a span of time that made your time here feel like a blink.

Someone placed it there. Someone who knew the plateau's rules, who understood the sound and the moisture cycle and the goat-things and the patience-erosion, and who placed a single warm stone at the center of a structure that might have been a home. A deliberate act of warmth in a place that has none. A small defiance. A hearth that gives nothing but presence.

You did not touch it again because the recognition was mutual. Something in the stone reached for something in you. Not hungry. Not malicious. The way a hand reaches for another hand in the dark. And you were not ready to be held by something that has been waiting that long.

---

The clicking is something you do not describe because description makes things real and you do not want this to be real.

But you will say this: it is beneath the sound. Not beneath in volume—beneath in *layer*. The sound is the mountain singing. The clicking is what the mountain is singing about. And the mountain is singing about counting. And the counting is not metaphorical. Something here is keeping track. Of what, you don't know. You don't want to know. The rules said not to follow the clicking and you obeyed and you will continue to obey because the plateau has borders where reality thins and beyond those borders the sound has no structure and you understand, with a certainty that bypasses your rational mind and arrives fully formed in your gut, that the clicking comes from beyond those borders.

From the place where the structure dissolves.

From the place where the counting has no need for the things it counts.

---

And then there is whoever lived here before.

The artifacts tell you about them, but the artifacts lie, because they are the only things left and they are worn smooth by the same patience that rounds the granite. The bowl carved from blue stone—held condensation, held warmth, held something someone needed. The fitted walls surrounding nothing—enclosed a space, protected a space, the way you build a wall to say *this is mine, this is not the plateau's, I live here and not you.* The marks in the rock—language, perhaps, or art, or the desperate record of someone who needed to prove they existed in a place that does not trade in proof.

They learned the rules. You know this because the artifacts show adaptation—the warm stone placed deliberately, the walls positioned to catch what little shelter the terrain offers, the marks carved deep enough to survive the erosion that has worn them illegible. They were not casual visitors. They stayed. They made accommodations. They carved out a small space of human warmth in a place that has none and they held it for a time.

And then they stopped. Or left. Or became something else.

The plateau does not tell you which. The plateau does not tell you anything. It simply continues, the sound filling the space their absence created, the condensation beading on the blue stone bowl that no one drinks from, the goat-things breathing their minute-long breaths in their body-shaped hollows, the bird-things threading the gaps in the tone, the thinking stone knowing all of it without caring about any of it.

You wonder, as you walk, if they left something of themselves in the warm stone. If the recognition you felt was not the stone's own memory but theirs—a human warmth absorbed into the mineral matrix, preserved by the plateau's strange relationship with time, waiting to be felt by the next warm hand.

You wonder if you will leave something too.

You wonder if you already have.

---

What lives here: the sound, which is oldest. The stone, which is thinking. The moisture, which cycles. The plants, which have finished. The goat-things, which are becoming. The bird-things, which are the sound's way of testing itself. The clicking, which is beneath all of it, which counts without ceasing, which you will not think about.

And you, briefly. A pulse in the waveform. A counterpoint that the sound tolerates because tolerating counterpoint is what makes a tone a tone rather than a single undifferentiated note.

You are what lives here for now. For now is all the plateau offers. And when you leave, you will carry it with you, and it will carry you—pressed into the stone, a shallow mold in the granite, slowly filling, slowly fading, slowly becoming part of the sound.
