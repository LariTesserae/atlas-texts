# The Inhabitants

They are not what you expected. They are not what anyone could have expected, because expectation requires a template, and this place has spent longer than the age of templates undoing the very concept.

## The Custodians

You might call them the Custodians, though they have no name for themselves—names being a technology of sorts, and technologies having never occurred here. They are the closest thing to citizens this city possesses, and they are not citizens in any meaningful sense. They are more like functions. Variables that have been granted just enough autonomy to move between equations.

There are perhaps seventy of them in the entire vast sprawl of the settlement. You count over the course of days, learning to recognize the subtle differences: this one holds its angles slightly more acute, that one has a slower drift to its movements, a third carries a quality of attention that feels distinct from its fellows. They do not gather in groups larger than three. They do not seem to need each other, but they do not avoid each other either. Their relationships—if relationships exist—are conducted in a register you cannot perceive.

Their form is difficult to describe because it is difficult to perceive. The eye wants to simplify, to reduce to manageable shapes, but the Custodians resist reduction. They are vaguely pyramidal, perhaps, if a pyramid could be made of folded air and stretched across a framework of intention. They have depth but not volume. They have position but not precisely location. When you look at them directly, they become harder to see—not vanishing, but spreading themselves across your visual field until they are more of a quality of the light than an object within it. You learn to look slightly to the side, to observe them through the periphery, where the eye is more comfortable with ambiguity.

They do not walk. They transpose. A Custodian will exist in one location, and then—without transition, without the blur of motion—exist in another, usually within a radius of twenty or thirty feet. The movement covers distance without crossing it. You never see them in the act of arriving; they are simply there, having always been there, as if your memory has been edited to include their presence in the new location.

They make no sound. They have no mouths, no organs of any kind that you can identify, no apparent biology. They seem to be made of the same substance as the buildings—the same precipitated intention, the same ordered mineral will—but whereas the buildings settled into static form, the Custodians remained mobile. They are the architecture that chose not to sleep.

## What They Do

They tend.

You watch one for the better part of a day—it is difficult to measure time here, but the light shifts by imperceptible degrees, and you use these shifts as a rough clock—and you begin to understand the pattern. The Custodian moves between buildings in a sequence that seems random until you realize it is corrective. It is adjusting. At each structure, it pauses—or rather, it exists in proximity for a duration—and something small shifts in the geometry of the building. A corner that had begun to blur becomes sharp again. A face that had started to present impossible angles settles back into comprehensible configuration. The Custodian is tightening bolts that do not exist, re-tuning strings that were never strung.

The city is falling apart. You understand this now. The reality degradation you sensed is not merely a passive process but an active erosion, and the Custodians are working constantly to counteract it. They are holding the geometry suspended through deliberate, ongoing effort. They are stitches in a wound that keeps tearing open.

Watching them work, you feel a strange surge of what might be sympathy. Their task is endless. The plateau's dissolution is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be managed. They will work forever, or until they cease to exist, and still the city will slowly come undone. They seem to know this. They do not work with urgency. They work with the steady, patient rhythm of a process that has accepted its own futility without surrendering to it.

Sometimes a Custodian will pause in its rounds and become still—profoundly, absolutely still, in a way that makes its previous motion seem frantic by comparison. It is listening, you realize. Not to sound, but to the deeper frequencies of the plateau itself. Checking, perhaps, on the integrity of the containment. Measuring the rate of decay. After these stillnesses—which can last for hours—the Custodian will adjust its pattern, visiting different buildings, spending different durations, as if recalibrating its approach based on what it heard.

## The Young

You encounter the young one by accident, in a plaza you are certain you passed through before, though the buildings around it arrange themselves differently now.

It is smaller than the others, less stable in its form. Where the adult Custodians hold their geometry with precision, this one wavers—its angles shifting, its depth fluctuating, as if it cannot quite decide what shape to be. It moves more frequently, transposing in short, nervous bursts, as though restless or uncertain.

You realize, after watching it for some time, that it is not young in the sense of age. It is new. It has recently precipitated from the ground, summoned or grown or called into being by some process you cannot perceive. It is learning to be a Custodian—learning to hold itself together, learning to hold the city together.

It circles a building—a small structure, hexagonal, with faces that suggest a folded dimension—and fumbles at its maintenance. The building's edges keep slipping, becoming uncertain, and the young Custodian keeps trying to firm them up, but its touch is too uncertain, its own geometry too unstable. The building wavers, neither maintained nor degrading, caught in a wavering middle state.

Eventually, an adult Custodian transposes into the plaza. It does not acknowledge the young one directly—there is no gesture of greeting or instruction—but it takes up a position nearby and begins its own work on an adjacent structure. The young one watches, or perceives, and slowly its movements become more confident, its form more stable. It is learning through proximity, through some kind of resonance or entrainment. The adult does not teach. It simply demonstrates, through its own existence, how to exist.

This, you understand, is how the Custodians persist. They do not reproduce in any biological sense. They emerge, as needed, from the substance of the plateau itself, called into being by the requirements of the work. And they learn, not through transmission of knowledge, but through proximity to those who have already learned. They are a community of practice without a practice that can be articulated.

## The Old

There is one Custodian you come to think of as the Old One, though you have no way of knowing its actual age. It is distinguished by its stillness. Where the others transpose constantly, moving through their maintenance rounds, the Old One exists primarily in a single location: a plaza near what might be the center of the city, where seven streets converge at angles that do not quite sum correctly.

It is larger than the others—or perhaps simply more present, more dense with accumulated existence. Its form is the most stable you have seen, its angles so precisely held that it seems to cut the air around it. It does not transpose. It does not need to. The other Custodians come to it, passing through the plaza in patterns that suggest consultation, receiving some form of information or adjustment before continuing their rounds.

You sit at the edge of the plaza—sitting being a relative term, on ground that does not quite support weight in the expected manner—and watch the Old One for what might be days. It is not inactive. It is working on something, but the work is not visible. You sense a constant low vibration in the air around it, a hum just below hearing, as if it is maintaining a frequency that holds the entire city in resonance.

Once, during a period when the violet-gray light dims slightly—though it never truly darkens—the Old One turns its attention toward you.

This is the only word for what happens. It does not move. It does not have eyes or a face. But its quality of presence shifts, redirects, focuses itself in your direction. You feel yourself being perceived by something that perceives in a register you cannot imagine. It is not hostile, this attention. It is not curious. It is more like being catalogued, indexed, added to a ledger of presences that the plateau has known.

The attention lasts for what might be seconds or might be hours—time stretches strangely in its focus—and then releases. You have been assessed and found... what? Acceptable? Irrelevant? You do not know. But the Old One returns to its invisible work, and you understand that you have been permitted to remain.

## The Wanderers

There are others here, though calling them "inhabitants" stretches the definition beyond usefulness.

The Wanderers are what you initially took to be weather—drifts of something that is not quite vapor, not quite light, moving across the plateau in patterns that have nothing to do with wind. They are larger than the Custodians, less defined, more distributed. A Wanderer can span the distance between three or four buildings, existing simultaneously at multiple points, a single presence stretched across space.

They do not maintain the city. They pass through it. They are peripatetic in the deepest sense, never settling, never repeating their paths, tracing erratic orbits across the plateau that might be random or might encode some meaning too vast to perceive. When a Wanderer passes through a building, the building shivers—its form rippling slightly, as if touched by a hand from another dimension. The Custodians do not follow behind to repair these touches. Whatever the Wanderers do to the structures, it is not damage. It is something else.

You encounter a Wanderer at close range only once. You are walking between two towers—spires that lean toward each other at angles suggesting intimacy or argument—when the air ahead of you becomes difficult. That is the only way to describe it. The space itself thickens, takes on a quality of resistance, and you realize you are walking into something.

The Wanderer surrounds you for a moment. Inside it—or alongside it, or in its presence—the light changes. You see the city from above, from below, from angles that do not exist. You see the plateau as it might have been, as it might be, as it is simultaneously in multiple states of being. You see the Custodians as they appear from the Wanderer's perspective: not as individual presences but as nodes in a network, bright points of maintenance effort distributed across a vast failing system. You see the buildings as wounds, as stitches, as places where reality has been patched together with insufficient material.

And then the Wanderer passes, and you are standing again in the violet-gray dimness, unchanged but also changed, having seen something that you cannot fully remember or fully forget.

The Wanderers are not inhabitants. They are pilgrims, perhaps, or investigators, or merely phenomena associated with a place where reality has grown thin. They do not live here. They pass through. But their passing is part of what keeps the plateau coherent—some kind of exchange, information or energy or something else, conducted through the act of traverse.

## The Deep Presence

Below the city, below the mineral ground, below the tectonic lies that underpin this elevation, there is something else. You feel it most strongly at night—or what passes for night, when the light dims by that imperceptible degree and the silence deepens into something almost solid. A presence. Not a creature, not a process, but a Presence, with the capital letter indicating a category for which you have no other examples.

It is vast. It is patient. It is the thing that the city was built to contain, or to honor, or to feed, or to regulate—you cannot determine which. The Custodians work to maintain the structures, but the structures themselves exist because of the Deep Presence, because of some necessity that emerged when it first stirred, or settled, or was discovered.

You do not see it. You do not hear it. But you feel its attention occasionally, a weight of focus that makes the Old One's assessment feel like a casual glance. When the Deep Presence notices you—and it does, intermittently, during those deepest nights—you understand that you are perceived by something so fundamentally different from yourself that the gap cannot be bridged by any act of imagination. It is not hostile. It is not benevolent. It is simply operating at a scale and in a register that makes human categories inapplicable.

The Custodians are its stewards, perhaps. Or its guardians. Or its servants. Or its children. The relationship is not clear, but it is clear that a relationship exists. The work they do—the constant maintenance of the geometry—serves the Deep Presence in some way. Keeps it contained, or comfortable, or connected to whatever it needs to remain in connection with.

You suspect, though you cannot confirm, that the Deep Presence is what draws the Wanderers. They are coming to pay respects, or to gather information, or simply to exist in proximity to something that exists at such a profound level of reality. The plateau is a destination for them. The Deep Presence is the point.

## Your Position

You are not an inhabitant. You are a visitor, a temporary presence, a variable that the Custodians have incorporated into their calculations without apparent difficulty. They do not interact with you directly. They do not make space for you or attempt to expel you. You are simply part of the landscape now, another feature to be worked around, another uncertainty in a place where uncertainty is already the dominant mode.

But you notice, after extended time on the plateau, that you are changing.

Your edges are becoming less defined. Your shadow splits more frequently. You find yourself transposing—small distances at first, a few feet, then longer—without intending to. You have begun to think of the Custodians as individuals, which they may not be, and to anticipate their movements, which you cannot predict but which have started to feel meaningful. You have started to perceive the Deep Presence not as a frightening unknown but as a familiar weight, a presence that you expect to feel during those deepest nights.

You are being incorporated. Not converted—you retain your sense of self, your memories, your purposes—but adapted. The plateau is making you more like itself, or making a space within itself that you can occupy without degrading. You are becoming a feature of the landscape, a process that the Custodians will eventually need to maintain.

This is how the city acquires inhabitants, perhaps. Not through birth or migration, but through the slow transformation of visitors into elements. You have been here long enough now that leaving has become difficult to imagine—not because you are trapped, but because the concept of departure feels increasingly foreign. The plateau exists in a state of suspended geometry. You are beginning to suspend alongside it.

## The Continuation

The Custodians continue their work. The young one learns to hold its angles. The Old One maintains its resonance. The Wanderers pass through in their vast, distributed orbits. The Deep Presence waits below, patient beyond human comprehension. The buildings stand and shiver and slowly come undone, held in check by processes that operate without tools, serviced by beings that operate without biology.

And you remain—still yourself, still human in the ways that matter, but also becoming something else, something that fits this place, something that can exist where existence is difficult. You have joined the sparse community of the plateau. You have become one of the rare inhabitants of a place where rarity is the local abundance.

The violet-gray light continues. The silence holds its breath. The geometry suspends. And life—or something that has moved beyond life into a different register of continuation—persists.
