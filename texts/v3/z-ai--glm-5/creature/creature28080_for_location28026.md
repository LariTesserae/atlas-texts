# What Lives on the Gjalvarr Expanse

---

## The Grazers, More Closely

You have to lie down to really see them.

From standing height, they're texture—the stone moving the way still water moves when something beneath it shifts. You might think your eyes are failing, that the heat shimmer and the pale light are conspiring to make the ground look alive. But lie down. Press your cheek to the stone, feel its surprising warmth, and look along the surface at an angle so shallow that the Grazers rise into view like hills on a horizon.

There are more than you thought. Hundreds in a square meter. Thousands. Each one moving in its own slow arc, its own deliberate path, and the paths cross and overlap until the stone beneath them is a palimpsest of routes worn so deep they've become grooves. The Grazers follow these grooves. Or they made these grooves. Or both—the way a river both follows and carves its channel, and you can't say which came first, the water or the path.

Up close, a Grazer is not what you'd call beautiful. It's functional. A low oval body, flattened, the shell divided into segments that overlap like shingles on a roof. The legs—six of them, you think, though it's hard to count because they move in a coordinated ripple that blurs them together—are short and thick and end in something that isn't quite a claw. More like a scraper. A built-in tool, evolved for one purpose: removing whatever thin film of biological matter grows on the stone's surface.

But they eat the stone too. You've watched them do it. They press their mouthparts to a crack where moisture has collected, and they stay there for a long time—not drinking, or not only drinking. They're ingesting mineral. You can see, when one lifts its head, a faint discoloration around the spot where it was feeding. A slightly deeper tone in the pale stone. Over years—over centuries—this feeding might shape the rock as much as wind does. Maybe more. The Grazers are erosion with legs.

They have predators. You learn this on the fourth day when you see a Glider descend in its usual slow drift, fold itself, and then—not eat, exactly. The Glider moves toward a Grazer with a deliberate, unhurried stride and extends something from beneath its shell that you can't quite resolve. A proboscis, maybe. A tube. It touches the Grazer, and the Grazer goes still. Not dead, not immediately. Just still, the way a creature goes still when something has interrupted whatever signal makes it move. The Glider stays attached for several minutes. Then it folds away whatever it extended and moves on, and the Grazer resumes its own movement after a pause—diminished, you think, though you can't say how. Smaller, maybe. Or simply lighter, as if something has been taken from it that it will need to rebuild.

This isn't violence. It's something else. Something so embedded in the rhythm of the Expanse that both creatures seem to accept it as weather, as condition. The Gliders take what they need and the Grazers give what they can afford to lose, and life continues.

## The Gliders' Logic

The Gliders, you come to understand, are not one thing.

There are the ones you first noticed—the hand-sized, broad-shelled drifters that ride the morning and evening thermals. But as you watch longer, you realize there are smaller Gliders too, no bigger than the Grazers, and their shells are narrower, less capable of sustained flight. They make short hops on the thermal currents, rising a meter or two, drifting a few meters laterally, then settling again. And there are larger ones—rare, seen only at the highest thermal peaks in late afternoon—that are the size of your spread hand and have shells so broad and thin that they seem translucent at the edges, catching the light like mica.

You think they might be life stages. The small ones are young, the mid-sized ones are mature, the large translucent ones are old—or final, or reproductive, or something for which you don't have a framework. The large ones don't land often. They stay aloft for hours, riding the thermals in wide spirals, and when they finally do descend, they go to the structures. The low circular mounds. They land in the shallow central depressions and fold their shells flat and stay there, sometimes for a full day, sometimes longer. When they leave, the depression is empty. Nothing visible has changed. But something has happened there—a deposition, maybe, or a communication, or a death that doesn't leave a body.

The Gliders that aren't in the structures live among the Grazers, and the relationship is more complex than predator and prey. You've seen a Glider use its scraper-legs to clear lichen from a crack, making the mineral beneath more accessible, and then move away without feeding while a Grazer settles into the cleared space. You've seen Grazers cluster around a landed Glider and remain there, motionless, for long periods, as if sheltering. The Glider's broad shell does provide shade, and in a landscape where the midday sun turns the pale stone into a reflector, shade might be as valuable as food.

You stop thinking of them as separate species. You start thinking of them as parts of the same system—different organs of the same body, each performing a function that the others can't. The Grazers process the surface. The Gliders move biomass across distances the Grazers can't cover on their own. The Burrowers process what lies beneath. And something—some organizing principle you can't see—connects them.

## What the Burrowers Are

You finally see one on the seventh day.

You'd been watching a hole—a fresh one, the edges still sharp, perhaps two centimeters across—and you noticed that the Grazers nearby were behaving differently. They were moving toward the hole, not away from it, and clustering around its rim in a way that looked less like caution and more like attention. You lay down and waited.

The Burrower emerged slowly. It was longer than the Grazers, tubular, with segments that contracted and expanded in a way that reminded you of an earthworm, except that it had legs—short, thick, numerous— that gripped the edges of the hole and pulled it upward. Its head, if that's what it was, was blunt and pale and had no visible eyes. Its body was the same bone-clay color as everything else, but darker along the back, almost gray, as if it carried a residue from whatever it moved through below.

It emerged about three centimeters of body, paused, and then turned its head—slowly, deliberately—in a scanning motion. Not looking. Feeling, maybe. Sensing vibration, or chemistry, or some quality of the air that you can't perceive. Then it descended again, and the Grazers went back to their feeding.

But you noticed that one of the Grazers followed the Burrower down. It walked to the edge of the hole, paused, and then continued into it, its pale shell catching the last light before it dropped below the rim. It didn't come back up. Not that day. Not the next.

The holes go somewhere. Of course they go somewhere—you knew that. But now you have to think about what "somewhere" means in the context of the Gjalvarr Expanse. The stone is warm from below. Geothermal heat rises through cracks and fissures, meeting the cool air and producing the condensation cycle that sustains the surface life. But the Burrowers aren't just using existing cracks. They're making holes. They're opening passages into something, and the other creatures are using those passages.

Below the plateau, there is space. You don't know how much. But the Burrowers live there, or travel there, or both, and they bring the surface world with them—or they take the surface world down, piece by piece, one Grazer at a time, and whatever happens below happens in a place you can't see and can only guess at.

## The Hoppers and the Spiral-Shells

The Hoppers are the Expanse's only gesture toward urgency.

Everything else here is slow. The Grazers move with bovine patience. The Gliders drift. The Burrowers emerge and descend with the unhurried rhythm of tides. But the Hoppers move in sudden, arcing leaps that span meters, and when they land, they land hard—a small sharp sound in the quiet, a click that's louder than anything the Grazers make. They're leaner than the others, their bodies angled upward, their back legs thick with muscle or whatever serves as muscle in creatures this size. Their shells are smoother, more aerodynamic, and they're slightly darker—tinged with the iron-rust color of the darker stone patches.

You don't know what they eat. They don't pause long enough for you to observe feeding. They land, they stay still for a moment—you can see their bodies tensing, coiled for the next jump—and then they're gone again, another leap, another arc. They move in patterns that look random until you follow them long enough to realize they're not. A Hoppper's path covers ground in a rough spiral, widening outward from a center point, and if you mark that center point and go to it, you usually find a crack in the stone. A deep one. Sometimes a Burrower hole.

The Hoppers are checking the openings. You think. They're monitoring the connection between surface and subsurface, moving in patterns that ensure every entry point is visited, and maybe—maybe—what they find there determines where they go next. They're the Expanse's scouts, its perimeter-walkers, and their speed isn't urgency. It's coverage. They need to be fast because they need to be everywhere.

The Spiral-Shells are the opposite. They are so slow that you have been watching one for three days and it has moved perhaps a meter and a half. Its shell is a flat spiral—two centimeters across, coiled in a way that follows a mathematical progression you almost recognize—and it leaves a trail in the surface film that's deeper and more deliberate than a Grazer's trail. Where a Grazer seems to wander, a Spiral-Shell goes straight. Or straight-ish. Its path is a curve so gentle that it looks straight over short distances, but over the full length of its movement, you can see that it's arcing toward something.

You find what it's arcing toward on the ninth day. A structure. One of the low circular mounds. The Spiral-Shell reaches the wall of the structure, climbs it—slowly, so slowly, its single muscular foot gripping the fitted stone—and settles into the central depression. And there it stays. For two days. When it leaves, its trail leads away in a new direction, a new curve, toward something you can't identify.

The Spiral-Shells are the oldest things here. You feel this without being able to prove it. Their shells are thicker than the Grazers', worn smooth in a way that suggests not just age but *duration*—they have been this shape, doing this thing, for longer than the other creatures have existed. They predate the structures, or they co-created them, or they remember whatever built them, and their slow spirals are not wandering but pilgrimage.

## The Pale Shape at Dawn

You see it again on the eleventh day, and this time it's closer.

It's moving across a low swell in the plateau, silhouetted against the pre-dawn sky, and the first thing you register is its gait. It doesn't walk like an insect. It moves with a fluid, muscular motion—low to the ground, legs or leg-equivalents working in a coordinated ripple that reminds you of nothing so much as a salamander. Its body is long, perhaps half a meter, and segmented, but the segments are soft, fleshy, visible only as subtle ridges beneath skin or shell. Its color is the pale of the stone, but warmer. Not bone-clay. More like milk left to stand. There's a faint luminescence to it—or no, not luminescence. A reflectiveness. It catches the first light and holds it differently from the rock.

It doesn't notice you, or it doesn't care. It moves with the same unhurried pace as everything else on the Expanse, and it's heading for a structure—one you haven't seen before, lower than the others, its walls almost completely eroded. The pale shape reaches the structure and flows into it, and for a moment you see its body compress, flattening to enter a space barely wide enough to accommodate it, and then it's inside, and the structure looks empty again.

You wait. An hour. Two. The sun rises. The Grazers begin their morning feeding. The Gliders catch the first thermals. The pale shape doesn't emerge.

You go to the structure. You kneel beside it and look into the depression, and there's nothing there. Nothing visible. But the stone is warm—warmer than the surrounding surface, warmer than the geothermal gradient alone would explain. And there's a smell. Faint, mineral, organic. The smell of something living that has passed through, or passed away, or transformed into something you don't have senses to perceive.

The large pale shape is part of the Burrower network. Or it *is* the Burrower network—a surface manifestation of whatever exists below, the way a mushroom is the visible fruit of a mycelium that extends for meters in every direction. The Burrowers are its roots. The structures are its flowers. And the Grazers and Gliders and Hoppers and Spiral-Shells live in its garden, sustained by its processes, shaped by its ancient patience.

You can't prove this. But standing at the structure's edge, feeling the warmth rise from its center, you can't think of another explanation that fits.

## The Lichen and the Moss

You almost forget the plants, because they're so easy to overlook.

The lichen grows in the deeper cracks, the ones where moisture lingers longest, and it's not one lichen but several—gray-green mats, a paler almost-white crust that forms along crack edges, and a dark reddish-brown variety that appears only where the darker mineral patches intersect with the condensation channels. They grow slowly. You mark a patch with a scratch in the stone and check it every day, and after twelve days you can't see any change at all. But change is happening—you just can't perceive it at human speed.

The moss—or the thing you call moss, which may not be a moss but fills the same ecological niche—grows in the depressions where water pools. It's almost succulent, thick and slightly rubbery to the touch, and when you press it, water beads out. The Grazers eat it. They eat it preferentially, in fact, seeking out the moss patches before they turn to the stone-scraping that makes up most of their diet. The moss is rich. The moss is what makes this ecosystem work—the bridge between the mineral world below and the biological world above, turning water and heat and stone into something that can be eaten.

And the lichen may be older than the structures. There's a crack running through one of the mounds, and the lichen in that crack has grown in a pattern that doesn't follow the crack's shape—it follows the shape of the wall, curving where the wall curves, thickening where the wall's joints are. It's growing on something that was put there when the structure was built, some organic mortar or bonding agent, and it has been growing on it so long that it has mapped the structure itself in its body. The lichen is an archive. It remembers what the structures were.

## What Lives Here, Ultimately

The Gjalvarr Expanse is not empty. It has never been empty. It is, in fact, so full that the fullness has become indistinguishable from the landscape itself—the creatures the same color as the stone, the stone shaped by the creatures, the plants growing in the stone's cracks, the whole system so tightly integrated that "life" and "geology" are words that don't quite apply here. Better to say: this is a place where the distinction between living and non-living has worn away, just like everything else. Worn down by time, smoothed by patience, reduced to its essential architecture.

What lives here is the Expanse itself.

The Grazers are its mouth, tasting the stone. The Gliders are its breath, rising and falling on thermal currents. The Burrowers are its roots, probing the depths. The Hoppers are its eyes, scanning the surface in widening spirals. The Spiral-Shells are its memory, tracing slow arcs toward the structures that mark something it once knew. The pale shape is its heart, or its mind, or the thing it is when all the parts come together—and it sleeps below, mostly, but surfaces sometimes at dawn, and its body is warm, and it is very old.

The structures are its bones. The lichen is its skin. The moss is the blood that it has learned to make from water and heat and the slow patience of stone.

And you—lying on your belly on the pale surface, your cheek against the warm rock, watching thumbnail-sized creatures scrape their slow calligraphy into a landscape older than your species—you are the only thing here that doesn't belong. The only thing that still makes a distinction between itself and its surroundings. The only thing that still thinks in terms of *here* and *there*, *self* and *other*, *living* and *dead*.

The Expanse doesn't think in those terms. The Expanse has outlived that kind of thinking. It simply is, and it simply continues, and when you leave—if you leave—it will go on without noticing your absence, the way a river doesn't notice when a single drop of water evaporates from its surface.

It will go on. It will breathe. It will graze itself, slowly, for another million years, until even the stone is gone and there is nothing left but the warmth from below and the pale sky pressing down like a hand on a closed book.
