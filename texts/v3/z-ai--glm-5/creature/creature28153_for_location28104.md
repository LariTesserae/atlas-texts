# The Dwellers in Yil

## The Callers

They are the voice of the basin. The large amphibians that gather in the warm center pool, the ones whose thrumming calls fill the dark with a soundscape so dense it has texture. The researchers who study Yil — those careful few who descend with sensors and patience — have counted at least seven distinct species sharing the central waters, but in the dark, you experience them as one presence: the Callers.

The largest among them are the size of a human torso, perhaps larger — size is difficult to judge when you cannot see. You know them by the depth of their voices, the way their calls resonate in your chest cavity, finding the space between your ribs and turning it into a resonance chamber. They call in frequencies that blur the line between sound and vibration. When they speak to each other across the warm pool, you feel their conversation in your bones before you hear it.

Their bodies are adapted to the dark in ways that the preserved specimens — the few that have been brought to the surface — cannot fully capture. In death, they are flattened things, eyeless and pale, their skin translucent enough to show the organs working beneath. But in life, in the warm water, they are presence. You feel them moving before you hear them. The water displaces around their bulk. They are not aggressive. They are not interested in you. But they are large, and they move with a confidence that comes from owning their environment completely.

The Callers have a language. This is not metaphor. The researchers have documented at least forty distinct vocalizations, combined in patterns that repeat and vary in ways suggesting grammar, or something like grammar. They are not talking to you. They are talking to each other, across the warm water, across the dark, calling and responding in sequences that take hours to unfold. Their conversations are slow. They do not rush. A single exchange can last for minutes, each caller waiting for the resonance of the other's voice to fade before answering.

They are communicating about something. The researchers believe they are tracking the thermal flows of the basin, sharing information about where the warm water rises strongest, where the mineral content shifts, where the vegetation has changed. They may be coordinating the care of their young — the eggs they lay in the warm pool, the tadpole-things that swim in the mineral-rich soup and grow slowly in the dark. They may be telling each other about you, the strange warm presence that has entered their territory and stands making foreign sounds.

You will never know what they say. But you can feel that it matters. You can feel the intention in their calls, the pattern and purpose. They are not making noise. They are speaking, in a language that predates your species and will outlast it.

## The Walkers

The larger things that move between the stalks, the ones whose footfalls you hear in the dark, heavy and wet — the researchers have named them Walkers, though the name is not quite right. They do not walk so much as they move, their locomotion a combination of stepping and sliding and something almost like swimming through the dense vegetation.

There are several species. The basin supports a diversity of large-bodied animals because the ecosystem is old, because it has had time to specialize, because the thermotrophic base provides enough energy to sustain predators and grazers and the complex food web that connects them. You hear them moving but you rarely encounter them directly. They keep their distance. They have learned that strange presences in the basin are not to be approached.

The most common are the grazers. They move through the thermotrophic thickets, stripping leaves and stems with mouths that have evolved to process the mineral-rich, heat-generating vegetation. They are slow, their metabolism matching the pace of their food source. They eat for hours, methodically working through a patch of growth, then rest for hours more, digesting. Their bodies generate heat through fermentation — long digestive chambers where bacteria break down the tough plant material and release warmth that the creature uses to maintain its own temperature. They are, in a sense, eating the basin's energy and becoming it.

You might encounter one in the dark if you are careless. You would know it by its smell first — a mineral musk, like warm stone and growing things. Then by its sound — the steady methodical tearing of vegetation, the wet crunch of stems being processed. Then by its presence — a large body moving away from you, not in fear but in the calm repositioning of an animal that has all the time in the world. It would not run. It would simply not be where you are, gradually, without urgency.

The predators are less visible, but you feel them. The basin has its hunters — things that track the grazers and the Callers and each other through the dark, navigating by sound and heat and the electromagnetic signatures of living bodies. They are not large compared to surface-world predators, but they are large enough. They have evolved for stealth in an environment where stealth means silence, where the slightest sound can reveal your position to prey that hears as well as you do, where the best strategy is stillness and patience and the sudden strike.

You would not know if a predator was tracking you. You would not hear it or see it or feel it until it had decided you were not prey, until it had moved on to something it understood. The predators of Yil are not interested in you. You are too strange, too temporary, too unpredictable. They hunt the things they know, the animals they have evolved alongside for millions of years. You are an anomaly that will pass.

But if you stood very still in the dark, if you quieted your breathing and your heartbeat, if you became as patient as the basin itself — you might hear them. The careful placement of feet. The small sounds of hunting. The sudden wet cry of something caught and killed. The basin has its violences, its cycles of predation and death. They happen slowly, and they happen in the dark, and you are not meant to witness them.

## The Skimmers

The air above the vegetation is not empty. It is filled with the silent passage of things that fly. The researchers call them Skimmers, though this name also fails to capture what they are. They are not birds. They are not bats. They are something else entirely, evolved from different stock, filling the ecological niche of aerial insectivore in a world without light.

They navigate by heat and by sound. Their bodies are adapted for a dark existence in ways that seem impossible until you encounter them. They have no eyes, or rather, they have eyes that have become something else — organs for sensing temperature gradients, for detecting the faint electromagnetic fields generated by living tissue, for parsing the complex acoustic landscape of the basin. They fly in perfect darkness and they never collide with anything. They know exactly where they are.

You feel them as much as you hear them. The displacement of air. The faint warmth of bodies passing near. Occasionally, one will land on you, and you will feel the thin-leg probe of something testing your surface, tasting your heat, determining whether you are food or perch or neither. They are light, much lighter than they should be for their size. Their bones are hollow, their bodies optimized for a life spent mostly in the air. They land only to rest, to digest, to lay their eggs in the warm crevices of the basin walls.

The Skimmers are hunting the smaller flying things — the true insects of Yil, or the things that occupy that ecological role. There is a whole layer of life in the air above the vegetation, a complex ecosystem of predator and prey that you cannot see but can sometimes hear: the high-pitched clicks of navigation, the wing-beats of tiny bodies, the brief struggles of things caught and consumed in mid-air. The Skimmers are at the top of this aerial food web, but even they are hunted, occasionally, by things that climb the thermotrophic stalks and wait for them to pass.

## The Swimmers

The water beneath the vegetative mat is not empty either. There are things that live in the warm mineral soup, things that never surface, things that have evolved for an existence entirely beneath the floating floor of Yil.

The researchers know about them because of the samples they have taken — nets lowered through the vegetation into the water below, pulled up dripping and examined in the light at the rim. The Swimmers are diverse. Some are tiny, barely visible, the planktonic base of the basin's food web. Some are larger, the size of a hand or a forearm, elongated and pale and eyeless like the Callers but more fully aquatic, never surfacing, never leaving the warm dark of the water.

They feed on each other and on the detritus that falls through the vegetative mat from above. They process the basin's waste, turning death into life in cycles that have been running for longer than human civilization has existed. They are the recyclers, the clean-up crew, the things that ensure the basin does not choke on its own excess.

You do not encounter them directly. They are beneath you, always beneath you, living in the warm water that moves slowly through the basin's deepest channels. But you can feel their presence in the way the mat sometimes shifts, in the subtle vibrations that rise through the vegetation from the water below. The basin has depth that you cannot access. There is life down there, in the dark and warmth, living its own existence entirely removed from yours.

## The Moss

There is one more dweller in Yil, and it is everywhere.

The researchers call it moss, but this is a convenience, a word applied to something that does not quite fit. It is a single organism, or it is many organisms working together, or it is something between those two states, a colonial life-form that covers every surface of Yil in a thin layer of living material.

It is on the walls. It is on the stones. It is on the thermotrophic stalks and the built structures and the remains of the technological platforms. It is beneath your feet, woven into the vegetative mat, adding its own contribution to the complex medium that you walk on. It is on the surfaces you cannot see, growing in the dark, processing the air and the water and the minerals it extracts from stone.

The moss is the base of the basin's ecosystem in ways the thermotrophs are not. It does not generate energy from heat; it processes the byproducts of other life, the waste material and the dead tissue, turning it into something usable, something that feeds the bacteria and the small crawling things and the roots of the larger plants. It is a recycler and a substrate and a home for the microscopic life that forms the foundation of the basin's food web.

It is also, the researchers believe, a communicator. Or a medium of communication. The moss responds to touch, to vibration, to the chemical signals passed through the water. When something moves across it, it responds — not visibly, not in ways you could detect, but chemically, electrically. It signals to itself. A footprint in one place causes a reaction in another, a change in the way the moss processes its environment, a shift in its metabolism.

This means that the basin knows where you are. The moss under your feet is telling the moss under other feet that something is moving, something warm and strange and temporary. The information travels slowly, at the speed of chemical diffusion, but it travels. The whole basin is a single sensing surface, touched and touching, responding to every presence within it.

The researchers have documented this. They have shown that the moss's electrical activity changes in waves that spread outward from disturbances, and that the animals of Yil — the grazers and the predators and the Callers — have evolved to sense these changes, to read the moss as you might read a map. They know where you are. They always know. The moss has told them.

## The Absence

There is one thing that does not live in Yil, and that is whatever built the structures.

The technological remains that still hum and generate warmth, the platforms and pillars and foundations — they were placed here by something. But that something is gone. The basin holds no trace of the builders, no bones, no artifacts beyond the structures themselves. Whatever they were, they left long ago, long enough that their technology has been absorbed by the ecosystem, has become part of the thermal landscape that the life of Yil has evolved to exploit.

The researchers have theories. Perhaps the builders were human, from some civilization lost to time, seeding the basin with life and technology for purposes now forgotten. Perhaps they were not human, were something else entirely, something that came and went before the current era. Perhaps they are still here, in some form, their presence folded into the technology that still functions, their minds integrated with the systems that maintain the basin's stability.

But if you stand in the dark and listen, you do not feel them. You feel the Callers thrumming in the warm pool. You feel the Walkers moving between the stalks. You feel the Skimmers passing overhead. You feel the moss beneath your feet, sensing you, telling the basin about your presence.

You do not feel anything that built.

The basin is full of life. It is loud with life, dense with life, ancient with life that has evolved and specialized and found its place in the dark. But the builders are not part of it. They are absence, memory, the suggestion of presence in the technology they left behind. They are the question that the basin cannot answer, the origin that the ecosystem has forgotten.

What lives in Yil is what has always lived in Yil, or what has lived here long enough to belong. The Callers and the Walkers and the Skimmers and the Swimmers and the moss. The complex web of heat-eating life that fills the dark with sound and presence. The slow metabolism of an ecosystem that measures time in centuries and does not hurry.

You are the anomaly. You are the brief presence, the temporary warmth, the strange footprint that the moss will remember and then forget. What lives in Yil will continue to live in Yil long after you have climbed back to the rim, long after you have returned to whatever life you came from, long after your species has risen and fallen and been forgotten.

The Callers will continue to call. The Walkers will continue to walk. The basin will continue to hold its dark and its warmth and its slow, patient life.
