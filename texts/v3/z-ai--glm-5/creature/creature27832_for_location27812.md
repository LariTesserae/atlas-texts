# Inhabitants

The first things you actually meet are the drifters.

They come into view as soft interruptions in the light — not shapes exactly, but a kind of texture in the water, a graininess that resolves, as you watch, into hundreds of bodies. They are small. Most are no larger than a thumbnail, though some reach the size of a closed hand, and they are almost entirely transparent. You see their organs before you see their bodies: a pale thread of gut, a faint cluster of something neural, the slow rhythmic pulse of what serves them as a heart. They move by contraction — a wave that passes from one end of the body to the other, compressing and releasing, compressing and releasing — and the motion carries them forward only in the loosest sense. Mostly they are carried. The currents own them. They go where the water goes.

They have been here longer than anything else alive in the Forebay. You know this not because anyone told you but because it is obvious in the way it is obvious that a stone has been somewhere longer than a tree. They fit the place too well. Their transparency is not camouflage — there is nothing here they need to hide from — but efficiency. They do not invest in pigment or armor or eyes because they have no use for any of it. They are little more than mouth and gut and gonad suspended in a gelatinous envelope, and they have been this way for long enough that the envelope itself has become architecture: thin, tough, barely denser than the water around it. When you touch one — and you will, because they are everywhere, because the water is full of them — it feels like nothing. A slight resistance, a coolness, a passing.

They colonize the heated pipes in layers so thick that the automated scrapers cannot keep up. The scrapers run on their cycles, indifferent and dutiful, and the colonies reform within hours. This is the central transaction of the Forebay's ecology: the machinery provides heat, and the drifters convert that heat into bodies, and the bodies become food for everything above them. They are the grass of this place. Everything else eats them or eats something that ate them.

---

The fish are the next thing you learn.

They are not one species. You can tell this even without training in anything like biology, because the schools separate by behavior. One kind stays tight, moving in coherent masses that turn and wheel as if guided by a single intelligence — these are the grazers, the ones that move along the heated pipes and crop the drifter colonies and the microbial film. They are pale. Not white, but the color of milk thinned with water, a faint opalescence that catches the light from the conduits and gives them a look of something only half-materialized. Their eyes are large and dark and not quite round — slightly flattened, as if pressed by the pressure of the water column, though the pressure here is not extreme. They see in the blue-green haze that the Forebay's lights produce, and their vision has adapted to it: they can distinguish shapes at distances you would find impossible in this light, and they use this ability to maintain their schools and to watch for the predators below.

The grazers move between the warm zones and the cool water in patterns that seem rhythmic but are not — or not in any way you can decode. They descend to feed. They ascend to rest. The descent takes them down past the light conduits into the mid-depths where the warm plumes rise, and there they spread out along the pipes, and their mouths work constantly against the substrate. The ascension brings them up into the cooler water, where they drift in loose aggregations, barely moving, conserving energy. They do not sleep, or if they do, they do it with one eye — metaphorically — on the water below.

A second kind of fish is solitary and larger and does not school. These are the ones that live in the margins, in the space between the lit region and the unlit edges, where the light conduits dim and the water darkens and the walls are still far off. They are darker-bodied, a slate gray that approaches black, and their fins are longer and more elaborate, trailing behind them like banners. They are territorial, but their territories are enormous by any normal standard — each one claims a volume of water that would encompass several city blocks, and they patrol it ceaselessly, swimming slow circuits that take them past the same heat-exchange structures, the same light conduits, the same grouted seams where the threadlike growth persists. They call to each other: a sound that is not the predators' bark but something lower and more resonant, a sustained tone that drops in pitch as it fades, like a bell struck underwater. When two of them encounter each other at the edges of their ranges, they do not fight. They circle. They call. They display — fanning those long fins, turning broadside to make themselves look larger. Then one leaves, and the other stays, and the boundary holds for another season.

There may be other kinds. You have seen shapes at the edge of visibility that do not match any of these — something fast, something dark, something that moves in a way that suggests a body plan you cannot quite parse. It may be a predator. It may be something else. The Forebay is large enough that it keeps its own secrets.

---

The predators are what you hear most.

Their call is the sound of the Forebay, more than the pumps, more than the hum of the turbines. Two notes, sometimes three, in a descending sequence, and the sound carries because it is designed to carry — not by any engineer's intention, but by the predators' own evolution. They have been calling across this water for long enough that the call itself has been shaped by the acoustic properties of the place. The notes fall in the frequency range that travels farthest in water of this temperature and this salinity. The intervals between them match the reverberation time of the space — by the time the echo of the first note returns from the nearest wall, the second note is sounding, and the two overlap in a way that reinforces rather than cancels. It is not music. It is optimization. But it sounds like music if you listen long enough.

They are dark-bodied and quick, as the description said, but the word "dark" undersells them. They are the color of the water at the unlit edges — a blue-black that seems to absorb light rather than merely lack it. Their bodies are elongated, muscular, built for acceleration rather than endurance, and they hunt by ambush: positioning themselves at the boundaries between thermal layers, where the warmer water rising from below meets the cooler water above, where the currents are unpredictable and the visibility is reduced by the mixing of different temperatures. They hang in the water and they wait. When a school of grazers moves through the boundary zone, the predator accelerates — not a sprint but a single sustained burst — and strikes one fish out of the school, and the school scatters, and the predator turns and takes its catch to a quiet depth to eat.

The stuttering that follows a kill is communication. You have listened to it enough times now to hear the structure in it: a rapid series of short pulses, four or five per second, that carries for kilometers, and you have watched what happens when it goes out. Other predators arrive. They come from their own hunting positions, drawn by the sound, and they converge on the kill site, and for a few minutes the water is chaotic — bodies moving in every direction, the stuttering overlapping and intensifying, the remains of the killed fish disappearing in a frenzy that looks like competition but is not. It is distribution. The predators share the kill. You have seen this enough times to be sure of it. The largest predator — the one that made the kill — takes the largest portion, but the others eat too, and when it is over, they disperse to their own ranges, and the stuttering stops, and the barking resumes its territorial rhythm.

They have been here for a long time. Their bodies carry the evidence: scars from encounters with machinery, with each other, with the structures at the edges of the space. One predator you have seen several times — you know it by the notch in its caudal fin and the pale scar that runs the length of its left side — has a territory that encompasses three of the light conduits and a section of the floor where the substrate is newly poured. It is old. It is large. It calls less frequently than the others, and when it does call, the note is lower, longer, more like a groan than a bark. The younger predators give it space. When it kills, they arrive to share, but they approach slowly, and they wait at a distance until it has finished before they move in.

---

There are things on the floor.

You do not see them often because the floor is far down and the light is thin there, and because they do not want to be seen. But the sonar picks them up — the fish-finder, or whatever the equipment is, the equipment that no one is operating anymore but that keeps running — shows shapes moving across the bottom, low and slow, hugging the substrate. They are not fish. Their bodies are flat and wide, like something adapted to a surface rather than open water, and they move by means you cannot clearly identify — perhaps undulation, perhaps a kind of creeping motion that involves their entire body. They feed on what settles out of the water column above: the organic debris from the drifter colonies, the fragments of the grazers' waste, the occasional remains of a kill that has sunk from the hunting grounds above. They are the Forebay's undertakers. They process what falls.

They do not make sound. They do not respond to sound. When the seventeen-minute pump cycle shudders through the water column, the fish above startle — a brief, electric tightening of the school, a momentary quickening — but the things on the floor do not react. They are below the sound, or beneath it, or indifferent to it. They have been here since before the pumps. You feel this about them, though you cannot prove it. They have the quality of something that was always present, that emerged from the first organic matter that found its way into the reservoir and colonized the floor and stayed.

There are not many of them. The sonar shows fewer than fifty across the entire scanned area, which is only a fraction of the floor. They may be territorial at a scale you cannot measure, or they may simply be rare because the food supply is limited. Whatever the reason, they are the loneliest things in the Forebay. They live in the dark, at the bottom of the water column, on the newest surface in the place, and they move across it with a patience that makes the machinery above them seem frantic by comparison.

---

There are things in the walls.

This is speculation. You have not seen them. But you have heard them — a scratching, a rasping, a sound that is not mechanical, that does not match any of the known operations of the Forebay's systems, and that comes from the direction of the walls, which are too far away to see but not too far away to hear. The sound is small and irregular and organic. It is the sound of something moving in a space that is not the reservoir but is connected to it — perhaps the intake pipes, perhaps the spaces between the structural panels, perhaps the network of maintenance corridors that must exist behind the walls, though you have never seen an access point. Something lives there. Something has found a way into the interstitial spaces of the construction and has made a home in them, and it moves through them, and the sound of its moving reaches the Forebay as a faint scratching that you notice only when the pumps are between cycles and the predators are quiet and the water is as still as it ever gets.

You imagine them — and this is imagination, not observation — as small things, warm-blooded perhaps, adapted to the narrow spaces, moving through the structure the way rodents move through the walls of a house. They may have come from wherever the water came from, carried in as larvae or eggs and maturing in the dark interstices of the construction. They may have been here since the beginning. They may be the original inhabitants, the ones who were present before the reservoir was filled, who survived the inundation by finding the high ground inside the walls and adapting to a life in the spaces between.

You do not know. You will not know. The walls are too far away and the spaces within them are sealed and the things that live there are beyond your reach. But you hear them, and the hearing is enough. It tells you that the Forebay is not a closed system. It tells you that life has found every corner of this place, every gap, every margin, every place where the construction does not quite meet itself and the world outside leaks in.

---

There are things you have only seen once.

In the upper water column, near the ceiling, in the place where the water presses against the manufactured substrate and the condensation forms and the light from below is faintest, you saw something you cannot describe clearly and cannot forget. It was large — larger than the predators, larger than the solitary fish, larger than anything you have seen in the Forebay. It moved slowly, with a deliberateness that suggested it did not need to move quickly because nothing here threatened it. Its body was dark, or it was light, or it was something in between that changed as it moved through the faint gradients of illumination near the surface. It had a shape that you could not hold in your mind — not because it was complex, but because it was simple in a way that your visual system did not seem prepared to process. It was there. It was real. It displaced water as it moved. And then it was not there, and the water filled the space it had occupied, and the sound it had made — if it had made a sound — was gone.

You have not seen it again. You do not expect to. The Forebay is large enough for things that are rarely encountered, and this thing — whatever it is — may be the largest animal in the reservoir, and it may be the rarest, and it may have been here since the water was first poured, and it may be something that the other animals know and avoid or follow or ignore. You do not know. You know only that the upper water column, which seemed empty and featureless and dull, is not empty, and that the ceiling of the Forebay, which is a flat and impermeable manufactured surface, is also a place where something lives.

---

And there are the things that live in the sound itself.

This is not literal. Or it may be literal. You are not sure anymore.

The pumps cycle every seventeen minutes. The turbines hum. The water moves through the pipes. The predators call. The solitary fish call. The drifters pulse. The things on the floor creep. The things in the walls scratch. And all of these sounds layer and mix and reflect and re-reflect until the water is not silent but is not noisy either — it is full, the way a room is full when every surface is occupied but no one is speaking loudly. The sound has a texture. It has a presence. It is the Forebay's way of being alive, the way the place expresses itself through everything that lives in it.

The animals are part of this. Their calls are not separate from the machinery; they are woven into it, shaped by it, adapted to it. The predators' two-note bark fits between the pump cycles the way a melody fits between the beats of a drum. The solitary fish's sustained tone harmonizes with the turbine hum — not by accident, but by selection, by the long slow process of animals that survive better when their calls can be heard over the background and whose calls therefore drift, over generations, toward frequencies that complement rather than compete with the mechanical baseline. The drifters pulse at a rate that matches — almost exactly, within a fraction of a percent — the thermal cycle of the heat exchangers. They may be responding to the temperature changes. They may be responding to the pressure changes that accompany the pumping. They may be keeping time with the Forebay itself.

Everything here lives in the sound. Everything here is part of the sound. The machinery and the animals and the water and the walls are not separate systems running in parallel; they are one system, one organism, one thing that has been running long enough that its components have begun to synchronize, to adapt to each other, to fit together in ways that no one designed and no one intended and no one needs to maintain. The Forebay is alive. Not metaphorically. Functionally. It is a living system that includes its machinery, the way a body includes its bones.

The things that live here do not know this. They do not need to. They swim and they call and they eat and they die and their young swim and call and eat and die, and the pumps cycle, and the lights hold, and the water moves, and the sound never stops, and the Forebay continues, and the things that live here continue in it, and that is enough. That is all it has ever needed to be.
