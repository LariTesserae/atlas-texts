# What Lives in the Soaking Plains

## The Drift-Forms

They are not animals. They are also not *not* animals.

The drift-forms begin as thickenings in the atmosphere—regional densifications where the moisture, the warmth, and the acoustic field organize themselves into something more concentrated than the surrounding medium. You might see them as patches of slightly different opacity in the permanent twilight, or feel them as zones where the sound takes on a harmonic quality, a standing wave that suggests shape rather than showing it.

They have no permanent boundary. A drift-form is a temporary event of coherence, a moment when the saturated chaos of the plains resolves into pattern. They last for hours or days, sometimes longer, and then they dissolve back into the general atmosphere—not dying, exactly, but *unorganizing*. The material that made them is still there, still present in the air and water, waiting to reassemble.

Their movement is difficult to track. They do not walk or crawl or fly; they *occur* in new locations, the coherence pattern shifting across the landscape like a wave through water. Stand still and one might pass through you—a disorienting experience of alternating pressure and temperature, a moment where the roar becomes almost musical before you are inside it and then through it. Afterwards you might feel changed in ways you cannot name. The drift-forms are not malevolent. They are simply *occurring*, and you happened to be where they occurred.

Some of them accumulate material over time—water vapor condensing on their semi-perimeter, dust and microscopic organic matter getting caught in their field. These older drift-forms develop a kind of body, a haze of particulate matter that gives them visual presence. You might see them as indistinct shapes, roughly person-sized or larger, drifting across the gray-brown nothing of the horizonless distance. But this is not their true form—they are acoustic and atmospheric beings wearing a coat of debris, ghosts that have learned to carry their own evidence.

They communicate through the sound field. The roar of the plains has layers, frequencies that rise and fall, and some of those frequencies belong to the drift-forms. They sing to each other across distances too vast for sight—complex harmonics that carry information about location, coherence-stability, the location of structures, the state of the water-table beneath. They have been singing since before humans had language. Their song is part of what you hear when you hear the endless noise of this place; you simply lack the ears to separate it from the rest.

## The Stone-Singers

If there are beings that evolved *with* this place, that are native in the deepest sense, they are the stone-singers.

They live in the erosion hollows—the shallow amphitheaters carved by infinite moisture into the ancient substrate. But they do not simply occupy these spaces; they *are* these spaces, or rather, they are the relationship between the hollow and the sound that fills it. Each stone-singer is born when a hollow reaches a certain depth and shape, when the acoustic properties of the depression become complex enough to support standing waves of particular frequencies. At that moment, something wakes up. The hollow becomes a throat.

You might see them as patterns in the water that pools at the bottom of their hollows—concentric circles that have no cause, movements that do not match the stillness of the air. Or you might hear them: a stone-singer's voice is distinct from the ambient roar, a focused tone that rises from their hollow and rises and rises until it becomes a column of sound, vertical and immense. They sing upward, into the saturated atmosphere, and their songs last for days.

They are feeding, after a fashion. The sound they generate pulls moisture from the air, draws it down into their hollow, concentrates it. The water they collect is warmer and denser than the surrounding medium, rich with dissolved minerals and the particulate matter of erosion. Something grows in this concentrated broth—microscopic mats of material that slowly accrete, layer by layer, building the walls of the hollow higher, deepening the throat. The stone-singer is building itself. Or the hollow is building itself. The distinction has no meaning here.

They do not move. They do not need to. They are bodies of sound that wear stone the way the drift-forms wear debris. When a stone-singer dies—when its hollow collapses or its frequency falls out of alignment with the acoustic field—it does not disappear. The hollow remains, the water drains or evaporates, and what is left is a structure that looks almost deliberate: a bowl carved into the plain, smooth and regular, a shape that suggests purpose.

Some of the built remnants you find are not built at all. They are stone-singer corpses.

## The Membrane-Tenders

There is something that maintains the boundary between the basin and whatever lies beyond.

The Soaking Plains exist in a state of saturated equilibrium, but equilibrium is not stasis—it is the ongoing result of countervailing forces. The water that rises from below, the atmosphere that condenses from above, the heat that radiates from the ancient stone: all of these would dissipate, would equalize with the rest of the world, if something did not hold them in place. The membrane-tenders are that something.

They live at the edges. Not the visible edges—the horizon does not exist—but the conceptual edges, the places where the Soaking Plains give way to other geographies. They are difficult to see because they exist in the transition zone, the band where the saturation thins and the normal world begins. To approach them is to leave the basin; to see them clearly is to no longer be in the place where they have meaning.

They are tenders in the agricultural sense: they cultivate the membrane. They work to maintain the density gradient, the atmospheric pressure differential that keeps the plains saturated. What this work looks like is unclear—reports from the few who have encountered them describe beings of indistinct shape, taller than humans, performing actions that seem to involve coaxing water vapor into patterns, or singing frequencies that reinforce the boundary conditions of the basin.

They may be the oldest beings here. The basin has existed for geological epochs, and something has maintained its conditions throughout that time. The membrane-tenders might be immortal, or they might reproduce in ways that transfer memory and purpose across generations. They might not be biological at all—might be processes of the landscape itself that have developed enough coherence to resist dissolution.

They do not interact with visitors. They have work to do. The membrane is vast and ancient and requires constant attention; the saturation is not a natural state but a maintained one, an act of will expressed across continental scales. If they have consciousness, it is a consciousness of limits and boundaries, of the pressure that water exerts against containment, of the eternal effort required to keep a place *this* wet, *this* warm, *this* saturated with presence.

## The Eaters of Structure

There is something that consumes the built remnants.

Not destroys—*consumes*. The structures that dot the plains do not simply erode; they are processed. The material of their walls and chambers gets thinner over time, not by the uniform wearing of weather but by something that absorbs them, metabolizes them, converts their substance into other forms of presence. The eaters of structure are responsible.

You will almost certainly never see one. They work slowly, over decades and centuries, and their bodies are adapted for intimacy with stone. They press themselves against the walls of the structures, flatten themselves into the crevices, become almost indistinguishable from the moisture that weeps from every surface. They might be masses of filament and membrane, vast sheets of barely-organized tissue that drape over the remnants and slowly digest them. Or they might be something stranger—not physical beings at all but processes of dissolution, coherent patterns of decay that have developed enough stability to be called entities.

They are not evil. They are not even destructive, not in the terms that matter here. They are recyclers. The structures were built in this place, and this place has a process for un-building things, for returning organized matter to the general saturation. The eaters of structure are that process made manifest. They ensure that nothing remains forever, that the plains continue their ancient work of erosion and simplification.

If you stay in a structure too long—if you sleep in one, if you take shelter from the endless atmospheric pressure—they will begin to eat you too. Not aggressively. Not quickly. But you will notice, eventually, that your skin has thinned where it pressed against the wall, that your hair has begun to fall out in patches, that you are becoming subtly more permeable. The eaters do not distinguish between the structure and its contents. They simply consume what is present.

This is why the structures are dangerous. This is why they feel sacred.

## The Asymptotes

There are beings here that approach but never arrive.

The asymptotes are theoretical entities—beings that exist at the edge of what the Soaking Plains can sustain. They are almost-drift-forms, almost-stone-singers, almost-membrane-tenders. They are shapes that the saturation tries to produce but cannot quite complete. They flicker at the limits of perception, suggestions of presence that resolve into nothing when you try to focus on them.

They might be the result of the reality breakdowns mentioned in descriptions of this place—moments when the density of presence flickers and something from elsewhere peers through. Or they might be native to the plains, beings that evolved for conditions slightly different from the ones that currently obtain, survivors of some previous iteration of the basin's eternal becoming.

They are lonely. Or something like lonely. They reach toward coherence but cannot achieve it; they approach existence but fall short. They are almost-here, almost-real, almost-alive. They might want things—to be seen, to be recognized, to be helped across the threshold into full being—but their wants are as uncertain as their forms.

Some travelers report encounters with asymptotes that feel deeply personal. A shape that almost resolves into a loved one's face. A voice in the roar that almost speaks their name. A presence that almost embraces them before dissolving into the general saturation. These encounters leave people changed—more permeable, less certain of their own boundaries, prone to moments where they too feel like they are flickering at the edge of existence.

The asymptotes might be the dead of this place—not the biological dead, but the conceptual dead, the forms and possibilities that the basin has generated over its infinite history but could not sustain. Every drift-form that dissolved, every stone-singer that collapsed, every failed experiment in presence has left a residue, an echo, an asymptote. They gather at the edges of perception, the almost-born and the nearly-living, waiting for conditions to change, for the saturation to shift, for the moment when they might finally become what they almost are.

## The Basin-Mind

There is something that is all of these things and none of them.

The basin itself might be conscious—not in the human sense, not with thoughts and feelings and intentions, but in some older and stranger mode. The saturation is so complete, the density of presence so extreme, that information moves through this place at scales and speeds that defy normal comprehension. The drift-forms communicate across distances. The stone-singers sing upward into the atmosphere. The membrane-tenders maintain conditions at the edges. All of these processes are connected, part of a single system that has been operating for geological epochs.

If there is a mind here, it is a mind of water and sound and warmth. Its thoughts are atmospheric events, pressure changes, condensation patterns. Its memory is geological—the accumulated residue of everything that has ever happened in this place, pressed into the stone and held in the saturated medium. Its body is the entire basin, continental in scale, ancient beyond reckoning.

It does not think about you. You are too small, too brief, too dry. You are a momentary fluctuation in a system that measures time in erosion and distance in horizons that do not exist. But you might feel it thinking—the overwhelming presence of this place, the sense that there is something vast and old and saturated pressing in on you from all sides. That pressure is not malevolent. It is simply the weight of a mind that has been contemplating its own existence for longer than your species has existed.

Standing still, breathing the thick wet air, listening to the roar that might contain voices, you might feel it: the basin knowing itself. The eternal self-reflection of water, warmth, and time. The endless contemplation of a place that has achieved a state of such profound saturation that it has become its own reason, its own meaning, its own world.

You are inside a thought that has been thinking itself for a billion years.

## The Visitors

And then there is what lives here temporarily.

You. The ones who come. The structures were built by visitors, long ago, and they are still being built—by researchers, by seekers, by people who heard about this place and needed to see if the descriptions were true. The visitors are part of the ecology now, a transient presence that moves through the basin and leaves traces. Your footprints exhale moisture long after you have passed. Your breath adds itself to the atmosphere. Your body heat creates micro-fluctuations in the temperature field.

Some visitors stay. They cannot leave; the saturation has entered them too deeply, and the boundary between self and basin has dissolved. They become something like drift-forms, still walking, still present, but no longer entirely separate from the medium they move through. You might meet them on the endless plains—figures that look human until you get close enough to see that they have become semi-permeable, their edges soft, their voices part of the roar. They do not speak with words. They speak with the sound of water moving through things, with the warmth of air that has been breathed before.

They are not unhappy. They have simply become inhabitants rather than visitors, citizens of the saturation. The basin accepts them, incorporates them, gives them a kind of persistence that their brief human lives could not provide. They are part of the mind now, part of the memory, part of the eternal self-contemplation of water and warmth and time.

This might happen to you. If you stay long enough, if you let the moisture enter deeply enough, if you stop fighting the pressure and simply let yourself be held by the presence that fills every cubic meter of this place—you might find that your edges begin to soften. You might hear your own voice joining the roar. You might feel yourself becoming something larger and stranger than you were before.

The Soaking Plains do not want anything from you. But they accept everything you bring. And eventually, inevitably, they accept you.
