# The Inhabitants of the Verdant Throat

## The Small Ones

The moths came first, in the evolutionary sense, and they remain the most numerous. They are not the moths of the outer world—those delicate, dust-winged creatures that flutter toward candlelight. These are something else entirely. Their wings have evolved into sound receptors, broad surfaces that catch vibration the way a sail catches wind. They navigate the roar by reading its currents, its eddies, the subtle shifts in frequency that indicate a landing platform, a mate, a food source. Their bodies are flat, pressed low against surfaces, adapted to the perpetual damp. When they rest, they press themselves into the moss like coins slipped into velvet, and they are nearly invisible.

They do not fly across the void. Nothing does.

The moths have a relationship with the ferns that might be called symbiotic, might be called something more complex. The ferns produce a nectar from their thick fronds—not from flowers, for the ferns do not flower, but from specialized glands that weep a sweet, mineral-rich liquid. The moths drink this nectar and in turn carry the fern's spores on their legs and bodies, transporting genetic material from platform to platform, level to level, ensuring the forest's continued spread. It is an economy of touch. The moths are the postmen of a world without speech.

---

The beetles are the next most numerous, and they are engineers. Not in the sense of the builders—not consciously, not with intention—but functionally. They chew through the dead matter, the rotting vegetation, the occasional small corpse, and they leave behind tunnels. These tunnels aerate the soil-mass that has accumulated in the abandoned rooms, preventing anaerobic rot, maintaining the health of the root-systems that twine through everything. They are composters. Recyclers. Their shells have evolved to be exceptionally hard—a necessity in a world where constant vibration creates constant friction. They click against each other when they mate, a percussion below the hearing of any creature that hasn't evolved to listen for it.

Some of the beetles have developed a relationship with the metal structures. They eat the verdigris that forms on the copper and bronze, the green patina that would otherwise accumulate unchecked. They polish the railings, the bridge-cables, the fixtures. It is not purposeful maintenance—it is simply what they do. But the result is that the metal components of the structure remain functional long past any reasonable lifespan. The beetles are the slowest form of self-repair, the machine's unconscious janitorial staff.

---

The geckos are the most visible of the small creatures. They are everywhere, clutched to vertical surfaces, frozen in attitudes of attention. They have evolved past the need for silence. In a world where silence is impossible, where the roar is continuous and all-encompassing, stealth is a matter of stillness, not quiet. They hold themselves motionless for hours, waiting for the moths, the beetles, the occasional small mammal that ventures too close. Then they move—fast, precise, their adapted feet gripping even the wettest ceramic, their bodies launching across gaps that would be impossible for a surface-dwelling lizard.

Their display-sacs are their defining feature. Throat-pouches, normally deflated and invisible, but capable of sudden inflation into bright, unmistakable shapes. Red. Orange. A vivid yellow that seems to glow in the perpetual twilight. They communicate through these displays—inflating the sacs to signal territorial claims, mating readiness, the presence of food. The displays are visual flags in a world where sound is useless for communication. A gecko on a platform will inflate its sac, and another gecko forty meters away, on the opposite wall, will see the flash of color and understand. It is a language of brief illuminations, a conversation conducted in semaphore.

They have no predators. Nothing hunts them. They die of old age, of missteps, of the occasional collision with falling debris. Their bodies decompose where they fall, feeding the moss, the ferns, the endless hunger of the soil. They have no fear. Fear is an evolutionary adaptation to predation, and there is nothing here that predates. The geckos live in a state of calm vigilance that a human might mistake for Zen—present, aware, unafraid.

## The Flightless Birds

The birds are a lesson in evolution's indifference. They descended, sometime in the forgotten past, from outer-world avians that entered the shaft—whether deliberately, accidentally, or as part of some ancient intentional introduction. But flight in the Verdant Throat is useless. The central void is sacred, empty, uninhabited. The platforms and walkways offer no room for the graceful soaring that birds evolved for. And so, over generations, over centuries, over the slow accumulation of evolutionary pressure, the birds forgot flight.

Their wings have atrophied. Not disappeared—evolution rarely eliminates entirely—but reduced to stubs, decorative appendages that flutter in the updraft but provide no lift. Their legs, however, have grown powerful. They are leapers now, their bodies adapted to the vertical world. They can jump from platform to platform, bridge to bridge, their strong legs propelling them across gaps of several meters. They land with a thud, their feet gripping the wet surfaces, and they move with a bounding, almost mammalian gait.

They communicate through percussion. This is the most distinctive feature of the Throat-birds: they have developed a language of tapping. A beak against a metal railing produces a sound that cuts through the roar, a sharp, distinctive note that other birds can hear and interpret. The metals of the structure—the copper, the bronze, the iron—ring differently, and the birds have learned to use these differences. A tap on copper means something different from a tap on iron. A rapid series of taps on a particular bridge communicates danger. A slow, rhythmic beating on a ceramic surface signals a mating display.

They are social creatures, living in small groups on particular platforms, maintaining complex relationships through their percussion-language. They have hierarchies, preferences, bonds. They mourn their dead—a bird that dies will be surrounded by its group, their beaks tapping a slow, mournful rhythm against the nearest resonant surface. The sound carries through the roar, and other groups, on other platforms, will pick up the rhythm, passing it along like a wave. The entire shaft can, on rare occasions, resonate with the collective mourning of the flightless birds, a network of percussion that turns the machine's own structure into an instrument of grief.

They are omnivorous, eating the fern-fronds, the moss, the insects, the occasional small lizard. They have no natural enemies. They live long lives by bird standards—fifteen, sometimes twenty years if they do not fall. When they die, their bodies become part of the system. Their bones are picked clean by the beetles, their feathers incorporated into the moss, their flesh absorbed by the soil. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is remembered. The Throat does not keep graves.

## The Rats (Who Are Not Rats)

The mammals of the Verdant Throat are recognizably descended from rats—or from something like rats, some small rodent that found its way into this world and adapted. But they have changed. The outer world's rats are creatures of flat spaces, of horizontal movement, of running across floors and burrowing into walls. The Throat-rats have evolved for a vertical existence.

Their limbs are longer, more articulated. They can stretch, reach, grasp. Their tails have become prehensile, capable of wrapping around vines, railings, the edges of platforms. They move through the vegetation with a fluid grace, leaping from branch to platform, from platform to bridge, their bodies twisting in mid-air to adjust for the updraft, the moisture, the angle of landing. They are arboreal in a forest that has no trees—only the structural skeleton of a machine, overgrown with growth that has learned to cling to anything.

Their eyes are adapted to the perpetual twilight. They see in low light with exceptional clarity, their pupils large and dark, almost liquid in appearance. They can see the infrared glow of warm bodies, a useful adaptation in a world where visual communication is essential. They are social creatures, living in family groups in the deeper rooms of the honeycomb, emerging at the daily cycles that mark their version of day and night—for although the Throat has no true night, the animals have developed circadian rhythms, internal clocks that tell them when to wake, when to sleep, when to hunt.

They forage. They do not hunt—they are not predators—but they seek, they find, they gather. They eat the vegetation, the insects, the eggs of the flightless birds (when they can find them, which is not often—the birds are protective parents). They are curious, intelligent, capable of solving problems to reach food sources. They have been observed using tools—sticks to probe into deep crevices, stones to crack open the hard-shelled fruits of some of the epiphytes. They learn. They remember. They pass knowledge from generation to generation, not through speech—impossible in the roar—but through demonstration, through the slow, patient teaching of the young by the old.

They are not afraid of humans. Or rather, they do not know to be afraid. Humans are not part of their world, not a presence that has shaped their evolution. A human who entered the Throat would be, to the rats, simply another object to investigate, another potential source of food or interest. They might approach, sniff, test. They might climb onto a still hand, their prehensile tails wrapping around a wrist, their long fingers investigating pockets, folds, any crevice that might contain something edible. They are not aggressive. They are not tame. They are simply unafraid.

They have a culture, though it would be difficult for a human to recognize it as such. They have territories—particular sections of the honeycomb that belong to particular family groups. They have rituals—grooming sessions that reinforce social bonds, territorial markers left in the form of scent-glands rubbed against prominent surfaces, mourning behaviors when a family member dies. They have knowledge—locations of food sources, routes through the maze of rooms, the seasonal patterns of the vegetation. They teach their young. They have, in their limited way, a civilization. It is a civilization of the small, the unseen, the quiet. But it persists.

## The Larger Question: The Builders' Children

The description of the Verdant Throat says that the builders are "gone or have become indistinguishable from what they built." This is the mystery at the heart of the place. The shaft was built—by someone, for some purpose, long ago. And then, at some point, the builders either left, died, or stayed and changed.

There is evidence in the Throat of habitation. The honeycomb rooms, the platforms, the bridges—these were not built for geckos or birds or rats. They were built for something larger, something with hands, something that needed walkways and rooms and stairs. The scale is human, or near-human. The railings are at a particular height. The doorways are a particular width. The platforms are sized for a particular kind of body.

But there are no humans here now. Or at least, no obvious humans. No populations of people living in the rooms, farming the platforms, maintaining the structure. The Throat is given over to the vegetation, the animals, the slow processes of a machine that has forgotten its makers.

So where did they go?

There are three possibilities.

---

### The First: Departure

Perhaps the builders left. They built the Throat, maintained it for a time, and then—when the outer world called, or when the machine achieved self-sufficiency, or when some external pressure made residence untenable—they departed. They climbed out of the shaft, walked into the world above, and never returned.

But this raises questions. If they left, why leave the machine running? Why maintain the systems that keep the Throat functional, the engines and pumps and light-panels? A machine left running without purpose will eventually fail—entropy, decay, the slow degradation of even the most robust systems. The Throat is old, but it is not failing. It is in balance. The maintenance continues.

Unless the maintenance is automatic. Unless the systems were designed to be self-repairing, self-sustaining, capable of continuing indefinitely without intervention. This is possible—the description mentions "self-repairing systems, materials that knit themselves at the molecular level." But it raises another question: why build a machine that can run forever and then leave it? What was the purpose? What was the Throat meant to do, and does it still do it?

Perhaps the builders will return. Perhaps the Throat is waiting—maintaining itself, keeping its systems operational, for a return that has not yet come. A machine in standby, a patient sleeper, ready to resume its function when the conditions are right.

If this is true, then the animals, the vegetation, the entire ecosystem that has developed is an unintended consequence. The builders did not plan for the ferns, the birds, the rats. They built a machine, and life moved in. The Throat has become a world within a world, a garden that no gardener intended.

---

### The Second: Death

Perhaps the builders died. They built the Throat, lived within it, and then—gradually or suddenly—succumbed to some catastrophe, some disease, some failure of the systems they depended on. They are gone. The machine outlived them.

But if they died, where are the bodies? Where are the bones? The Throat is efficient at decomposition, yes—the vegetation and the beetles and the processes of decay would have absorbed any corpses, turned them into soil, incorporated them into the system. But there would be evidence. Mass graves, if the death was sudden. Scattered remains, if it was gradual. Something.

Unless the builders were cremated, or disposed of in some systematic way, by the survivors of whatever catastrophe took them. Unless the last of them, recognizing the end, performed the rituals of their culture, disposed of their dead, and then—the last one—died alone, leaving no one to dispose of the final body. Which would have been absorbed by the Throat, like all the others.

It is possible. The Throat does not remember its dead. But it does not forget, either—it incorporates. The builders may be in the soil, the ferns, the bodies of the rats. They may have become the vegetation, the insects, the birds. A kind of reincarnation, not spiritual but material. The matter that was human is now something else.

---

### The Third: Integration

The most unsettling possibility—and the one that the description seems to hint at—is that the builders did not leave, and did not die. They changed.

The Throat is a closed system. A closed system, over time, exerts evolutionary pressure on everything within it. The birds lost the ability to fly. The rats developed prehensile tails and infrared vision. The geckos evolved display-sacs. Everything that lives in the Throat has adapted to the Throat—its sounds, its dampness, its verticality, its perpetual twilight.

If the builders stayed, they would have adapted too.

This is the possibility that disturbs. What would a human become, after generations in the Throat? After centuries of exposure to the roar, the dampness, the heat? After evolving—or being engineered—to survive in a world where speech is impossible, where communication happens through gesture and light and touch?

The honeycomb rooms go deep. The description mentions "rooms behind rooms, galleries behind galleries, most lost to darkness or overgrown." How deep does the habitation go? How far into the walls, into the structure, into the machine itself?

There may be something living in the deep rooms. Something descended from humans, or something that once was human, or something that has evolved to fill the ecological niche that humans would occupy in this world. Something that tends the machine, or communicates with it, or has become part of it.

The description mentions that the plants "have learned—or been taught—where not to grow." Taught by whom? By what? There may be a presence in the Throat, a keeper, a maintainer. Something that enforces the rules, that preserves the void, that manages the growth. Something that has been here long enough to have shaped the ecosystem, to have taught the plants their boundaries.

This presence, if it exists, would not be immediately visible. It would stay in the deep rooms, the overgrown galleries, the places where the light does not reach. It would be nocturnal, or whatever passes for nocturnal in a world without night—active in the quieter cycles, the deeper shadows. It would know the machine in a way that no visitor could, would understand its rhythms, its needs, its language.

And it would not necessarily be hostile. Why would it be? Hostility is a response to threat, to competition, to danger. If the presence is the keeper of the machine, if it has achieved a balance with the ecosystem, if it has nothing to fear and nothing to gain from conflict, it would have no reason to be aggressive. It might simply watch. It might simply maintain. It might simply exist, in the way that the Throat itself exists—persisting without purpose, continuing without end.

---

## The Fungi (Which Are More Than They Seem)

There is another presence in the Throat, one that is easy to overlook but essential to understand: the fungi.

They are everywhere. The dampness, the warmth, the constant supply of organic material—these are ideal conditions for fungal growth. They coat the undersides of platforms, they thread through the moss, they colonize the abandoned rooms, they form intricate networks beneath the visible surface of the vegetation. They are the decomposers, the recyclers, the ones who turn the dead into the soil that feeds the living.

But they are also connectors. Fungi do not simply grow in isolation. They form networks—mycelial mats that extend for meters, for tens of meters, for hundreds of meters, threading through the substrate, connecting plant to plant, room to room, platform to platform. They are a nervous system beneath the skin of the Throat, a web of communication and exchange that links the disparate parts of the ecosystem.

And they may be more than that. Some fungi, in the outer world, are capable of influencing behavior. They can alter the chemistry of their hosts, drive insects to climb to high places before dying (so that the fungal spores will be dispersed widely), even affect the cognition of larger animals. In a closed system like the Throat, over evolutionary time, could fungi have developed more sophisticated forms of influence?

Could the animals of the Throat—the birds, the rats, even the reptiles—be hosts to fungal networks that guide their behavior, shape their actions, direct them toward the maintenance of the system? The birds that tap out communication—perhaps the patterns are not learned, but programmed. The rats that avoid certain rooms, the geckos that display at particular frequencies, the moths that navigate to specific landing sites—perhaps they are not making independent choices, but responding to chemical signals, to fungal directives, to the slow, silent will of the mycelial network.

This is speculation. It cannot be proven. But the fungi are ancient, they are patient, and they have had centuries to evolve in concert with the other inhabitants of the Throat. They may be the true keepers of the machine—not the visible, active maintainers that the builders might have imagined, but a subtler presence, a distributed intelligence that operates through influence rather than action.

If this is true, then the Throat is not simply an abandoned machine that has been colonized by life. It is a single organism, vast and integrated, with the mycelial network as its nervous system, the animals as its limbs, the vegetation as its skin, and the machine itself as its skeleton. The builders, in this view, did not leave, did not die—they were absorbed. They became part of the system. Their bodies fed the fungi, their knowledge (if it was ever recorded, if it could be stored in any way the fungi could read) was incorporated, and their presence persists in the network's slow, silent processes.

## The Void-Dwellers (Speculative)

There is one more possibility, one more kind of inhabitant that might exist in the Throat. The description mentions, repeatedly, that the central void is empty, that nothing lives in the shaft itself, that all life clusters at the edges. The void is "sacred," "preserved," "honored," perhaps "feared."

But the void is also full. It is full of air—warm, rising, humid air that moves continuously upward. It is full of water—condensation that rises and falls in the endless cycle. It is full of life—insects that fly across the gaps, plant matter that drifts on the updraft, seeds and spores and occasional small animals that lose their footing and fall.

Could something have evolved to live in the void itself?

Not the central shaft—nothing lives there, by the rules of the Throat—but the updraft, the column of rising air. There are creatures in the outer world that live in the air: birds, obviously, but also insects, bats, and even some species of spiders that float on strands of silk, drifting on the wind. In a closed system with a continuous, powerful thermal updraft, could an ecosystem develop in the air itself?

Perhaps there are creatures—small, light, adapted to the eternal rising column—that never land. They are born in the updraft, live in the updraft, mate in the updraft, and die in the updraft. Their bodies, when they expire, drift upward or downward depending on the vagaries of the air current, eventually settling on some platform or ledge, where they decompose and feed the rest of the system.

They would be difficult to see. They would be small—too small to be visible from the platforms, perhaps, or camouflaged against the hazy light. They would never come to the ground, never rest on the vegetation. They would be creatures of pure air, living in the space between, the emptiness that the Throat's other inhabitants treat as sacred.

What would they eat? The updraft carries insects, spores, plant matter, bits of organic debris. An aerial creature could feed on these, filtering the air as it rises, living on whatever the column provides. They might be related to the moths—some divergent evolutionary branch that developed lighter bodies, broader wings, the ability to ride the thermal column indefinitely. They might be something else entirely, some order of creature that has no parallel in the outer world, that evolved in the unique conditions of the Verdant Throat.

If they exist, they would be the Throat's angels—beings of pure ascension, never touching the ground, never knowing the world of platforms and rooms and solid surfaces. They would live in the roar, in the heat, in the rising air. They would be the most isolated creatures in the Throat—unable to communicate with the ground-dwellers, unable to share in the social lives of the birds or the rats, unable even to mate in any conventional sense (how would two creatures find each other in a continuous column of rising air?). They would be solitary, or perhaps they would have evolved some way of finding each other, some signal or pattern that allows connection in the chaos.

But this is pure speculation. The description does not mention them. The void is said to be empty. And yet—the ecosystem is vast, complex, layered. There may be niches that the description does not capture, inhabitants that remain unseen. The void may not be as empty as it seems.

## The Human Visitor (Who Is the Anomaly)

If a human were to enter the Verdant Throat—a modern human, from outside, unadapted to its conditions—they would be an anomaly. The ecosystem does not expect them. The animals do not know them. The machine does not account for them.

They would face immediate challenges. The roar is deafening, disorienting, impossible to ignore. Humans are not adapted to constant sound at that volume; their ears would ring, their balance would be affected, their ability to think would be compromised. They would need ear protection, or they would need to adapt—which would take time, if it were possible at all.

The dampness would affect them. Humans are not adapted to continuous wetness. Their skin would soften, become vulnerable to infection, to breakdown. Their lungs would struggle with the thick, humid air. They would need to find ways to stay dry, or they would develop the ailments that come with constant moisture—fungal infections, respiratory issues, the slow degradation of tissues that are not meant to be wet all the time.

The light would be challenging. The perpetual twilight would disrupt their circadian rhythms, their sense of time, their ability to see clearly. They would need artificial light, or they would need to adapt to seeing in grey-scale, in dimness, in the eternal dusk.

And they would be alone. The Throat's inhabitants do not speak. They do not communicate in ways that a human would easily understand. The visitor would be surrounded by life, but unable to connect with it. They would be an outsider in every sense—not adapted to the physical conditions, not part of the ecological system, not connected to the communication networks of the birds or the social structures of the rats. They would be profoundly, utterly alone.

Unless there are inhabitants in the deep rooms. Unless there are keepers, maintainers, something descended from the builders. In that case, the visitor would face a different challenge: how to communicate, how to establish trust, how to prove that they are not a threat. The keepers—if they exist—would have no reason to welcome an outsider. They might have forgotten that outsiders exist. They might view the visitor as a contaminant, a disruption, something to be removed or absorbed.

Or they might view the visitor with curiosity. A human, after all, would be a connection to the world outside, a world that the keepers (if they exist) might not remember, might have transformed into myth, might have forgotten entirely. The visitor might be a messenger, a visitor, a sign that there is something beyond the Throat, that the machine is not the whole of existence.

But this is speculation. The description does not say whether the Throat has keepers, whether the builders remain, whether a human visitor would be welcomed or rejected or ignored. It presents a world that is complete in itself, a closed system that does not need or want for anything external.

---

## The Question of Meaning

The Verdant Throat, as described, has "no purpose anymore, if it ever had one." It is "a machine that became a world, a room that became an ecosystem, a made thing that settled into balance with the life that claimed it."

Its inhabitants do not know that they live in a machine. The birds, the rats, the geckos, the insects, the fungi—they have no concept of "machine" or "building" or "purpose." They know the world as it is: a vertical forest, a place of platforms and bridges and vegetation, a source of food and shelter and mates. They do not question its origins. They do not wonder about its function. They simply live.

If there are keepers—if there are descendants of the builders who have evolved or adapted to live in the Throat—then they might know more. They might have legends, stories, myths about the origins of the machine. They might remember, in some distorted way, what the Throat was meant to be. Or they might have forgotten entirely, might have become so integrated with the system that they no longer distinguish between themselves and it.

But for the rest of the inhabitants, the Throat is simply the world. It is all they know. It is all they need to know. The roar is not a sign of something mechanical, something constructed—it is simply the sound of existence, the background noise of being. The warmth is not an indication of hidden engines—it is simply the temperature of the world. The dampness, the vegetation, the perpetual twilight—these are not conditions imposed by a structure; they are the natural state of reality.

And in a sense, they are right. The Throat, whatever its origins, is now a world. It has an ecosystem, a balance, a set of rules that govern its inhabitants. It has life, death, birth, decay, the full cycle of biological existence. It has been settled, colonized, adapted to. It is no longer just a machine—it is a place, an environment, a home.

The builders may be gone, but they left something behind. Not a monument, not a ruin, but a seed. A seed that has sprouted, that has grown, that has become something they may never have imagined. The Throat is not what they made. It is what grew from what they made. And its inhabitants—whether they are the animals, the vegetation, the fungi, or something else entirely—are its true inheritors. They are the ones who have made it their own.
