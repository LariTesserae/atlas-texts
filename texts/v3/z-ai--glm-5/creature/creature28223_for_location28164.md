# What Lives Here

The black-furred rodents are the most visible, and in their visibility, they have become something that defies easy categorization. They are not quite rats. Their ancestors might have been—might have scurried in through supply lines or ventilation shafts when the facility still served its original purpose—but time and isolation have stretched them into something new. Their bodies are longer, leaner, built for winding through the tight spaces between pipes and roots. Their fur has darkened to a black so deep it reads as absence in the green light, as if someone has cut rodent-shaped holes in the air.

They move without urgency. Without fear. They have never seen a predator. They have never seen a trap. The concept of threat does not exist in the vocabulary of their nervous systems. When they encounter each other in the tunnels beneath the moss—tunnels they have dug over generations, a subterranean mirror of the space above—they pass with the mild acknowledgment of commuters. Sometimes they pause to groom each other's faces, a rapid ritual performed with the efficiency of creatures who have nothing else to do and all the time to do it.

They have developed a social structure that is barely a structure. There are no territories. No hierarchies. No mating displays or competition. The machines provide enough heat. The plants provide enough food. The water provides enough drink. There is simply no reason to fight, and so they do not fight. They breed when they breed. They die when they die. They exist in a perpetual present that requires no planning and no memory.

Their calls are low and resonant, adapted to cut through the pump-noise. A sort of hum, a vibration in the throat that travels through the damp air and the moss floor. They use it to locate each other in the dimness, to maintain a loose connection that is less herd than neighborhood. If you stood here—if someone stood here—you would feel their hum in your chest, layered under the machinery, a biological bass line under the mechanical one.

They have no name. They have never needed one.

---

The crickets are older than they should be.

Not individually—individual crickets live and die in the ordinary span of cricket time. But the colony itself has persisted for longer than any cricket colony has a right to persist, sustained by the equilibrium of the space. They are the sound-track of the chamber, the foreground to the machinery's background. Their song is wrong for crickets. Lower. Slower. Each chirp a drawn-out groan that harmonizes with the pumps without meaning to, as if the machines have tuned them over generations.

They live in the moss. The moss is deep enough to house entire civilizations of insects, and the crickets have colonized it thoroughly. They move through the green depths like divers, their long legs pushing through the soft material, emerging only to sing. Their shells have darkened to match their environment—not black, like the rodents, but a deep green that makes them invisible against their substrate. They are the color of the place they live.

They eat the moss. The moss eats the decaying matter of everything else. The cycle is so simple it barely deserves the word "cycle." It is a loop. A closed loop that has been closing for so long it has forgotten it was ever open.

There are other insects. Beetles with shells like wet pebbles. Millipedes that move through the floor like fingers probing the dark. Small winged things that drift through the air in no particular direction, their flight paths determined by the currents the machinery creates. None of them have been catalogued. None of them have been named. They exist in the gap between discovery and documentation, known only to each other.

---

The birds are the newest arrivals. They came last—decades ago, perhaps, or centuries; the distinction has lost meaning. They found the space through some accident of migration, a flock that broke apart in a storm and reformed in pieces, a few individuals stumbling into the cathedral of vines and staying.

They have changed less than the others. Birds are ancient, evolutionarily speaking. Their forms are more settled, less susceptible to the kind of rapid adaptation that has stretched the rodents and deepened the crickets. But they have changed nonetheless. Their feathers have lost some of their iridescence—the light here provides no need for display—and their beaks have grown slightly longer, better for probing the deep moss for insects.

Their calls are the loudest things in the space. They have to be. The pump-noise is a wall, and the birds have learned to sing over it, around it, through it. Their songs are brief and piercing, staccato bursts of sound that cut through the mechanical drone and fade quickly. They do not sing the long, complex songs of their ancestors. There is no point. There are no rivals to impress, no territories to mark. They sing to locate each other, to signal, to remind themselves that they exist.

They nest in the upper vines, building homes from moss and twigs and fragments of the old facility—pieces of insulation, strips of wiring, shards of plastic that have worked loose from the infrastructure below. Their nests are strange amalgams of the organic and the artificial, the natural and the manufactured. The birds do not distinguish between them. A twig is a twig. A wire is a wire. Both are material. Both are useful.

They do not migrate. They do not know that migration exists. Their ancestors knew—knew about seasons and distances and the imperative of movement—but that knowledge has been lost, bred out, replaced by the simple fact of staying. The space does not change. The birds do not leave. They have forgotten that leaving is possible.

---

There is something else here.

It is difficult to name. It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a machine. It is the sum of all these things, the aggregate presence that emerges when enough systems interlock for long enough. It has no brain, no nervous system, no center of consciousness. But it has a kind of awareness. A distributed sentience that lives in the feedback loops between pumps and roots and the slow electrical flicker of insect nerves.

It knows itself in the way a body knows itself—not through thought but through sensation. It feels the water flowing through its pipes and its roots. It feels the heat rising from its furnaces and the cool air moving through its vines. It feels the weight of the rodents moving across its floor, the vibration of the crickets in its moss, the flutter of birds in its canopy. It does not think about these things. It simply registers them, incorporates them, adjusts.

It has no memory in the human sense. But it has a kind of record. The layers of plant matter are its archive. The erosion on the machines is its history. The genetic changes in its animals are its evolutionary journal, written in the language of survival and adaptation. It knows what it was, in the dim way that a tree knows it was once a seed. It does not know what it will be. It does not think in those terms.

It does not want anything. Want requires a gap between current state and desired state, and the space has no such gap. It is complete. Not finished—nothing here is finished—but balanced in a way that precludes desire. The pumps pump because that is what pumps do. The plants grow because that is what plants do. The animals live and die because that is what animals do. The system sustains itself because that is what systems do when they have found their equilibrium.

It does not notice the absence of humans. It does not remember them. The intention that built it has eroded, washed away by the constant flow of water and time. It knows only that it is. It is. It continues to be. That is enough.

---

If you stood here—and no one stands here—you might feel it. Not as a presence, exactly. Not as a ghost or a spirit or anything so dramatic. You would feel it as a weight. A density to the air. A sense that the space around you was not empty but full, not waiting but occupied. You might find your thoughts slowing, syncing to the pump-rhythm. You might find your breathing adjusting to the humidity. You might find yourself becoming, briefly, a part of something larger—another organism in the microbiome, another flicker of electricity in the distributed awareness of the whole.

You would not be welcome. You would not be unwelcome. Those concepts require the kind of intention that the space has never developed. You would simply be *noted*. Registered. Incorporated into the endless loop of sensation and response that constitutes its existence. And then you would leave—because humans always leave—and the space would continue without you, unchanged by your presence, unaware of your absence.

The rodents would hum. The crickets would groan. The birds would pierce the air with their staccato calls. The pumps would pump. The water would flow. The vines would hold their cathedral shape. The moss would yield under feet that were no longer there.

And the space would be, as it has always been, enough.
