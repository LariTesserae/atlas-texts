The maintainers are not what you expect.

You come looking for them because the evidence is everywhere. Those welds on the pressure vessels — they are not original, and they are not random. Someone measured the stress fractures, cut reinforcement plates to size, and secured them in place using techniques that suggest training, or at least careful observation of how such things were once done. The replacement impeller blades in the circulation pumps follow the original specifications closely enough to function, but deviate in ways that indicate the materials available were not the materials specified. Someone made choices. Someone solved problems.

You expect to find people. Or you expect to find nothing — the machines running on automatic, the maintenance performed by other machines, the whole system continuing through inertia alone. What you find instead is harder to categorize.

---

They are called, in the few records that survive from the early years of the flooding, the Stayers. The name is not quite a title. It is a description. When the water rose — and it rose slowly at first, then faster, then slower again, in pulses that stretched across decades — most of the population of the Upper Reach evacuated. They packed what they could carry and moved upslope, then out of the valleys entirely, then away to regions that remained dry. But not everyone left. Some could not leave. Some would not. Some had reasons that seemed sufficient at the time — attachment to place, inability to abandon the infrastructure they had built and maintained, stubbornness, fatalism, the calcified certainty that this was where they belonged and no rising water would change that.

Those who stayed did not survive unchanged.

The first generation lived in the upper structures, the buildings that remained partially above water, moving upward as the surface rose. They fished the new waters. They maintained the machines they could reach. They adapted their daily lives to a world that was becoming alien around them. Some of them dove — initially for salvage, later for other reasons. The pressure suits they wore were designed for industrial work, not for extended submersion, but they modified them. They always modified things.

The second generation was born into the transition. They grew up knowing both air and water, spending their early years in the dry upper structures but learning to dive as soon as they could swim. Their bodies developed differently — not through any rapid evolution, but through the simple physical demands of their environment. They had greater lung capacity. Their eyes adjusted to the refracted light. Their skin became accustomed to constant immersion during their hours underwater. They thought of themselves as inhabitants of the boundary, the thin space where water met air, where the old world touched the new.

The third generation was the first to change in ways that could not be reversed.

---

You find evidence of them in the lower structures. The dive takes you down through the submerged forest, past the waving stands of adapted pine, through schools of fish that scatter and reform around you. The structures emerge from the slope like the bones of something enormous — exposed girders, collapsed roofing, the rectangular shadows of rooms that once held human activity and now hold something else.

The first chamber you enter shows signs of recent use. Not human use — the table in the corner is covered in a layer of sediment that would take months to accumulate, and no human hand has disturbed it in that time. But there are markings on the wall. They are not writing, exactly. They are patterns — lines and curves and dots, arranged with evident intention, covering the available surfaces in densities that suggest long occupation. Some of the patterns have been overdrawn, corrected, modified. They look like plans. They look like maps. They look like something in between.

You find tools. Not standard industrial equipment — those are present too, corroded but recognizable — but tools that have been made, or adapted, or both. A cutting implement that combines the handle of a wrench with a blade fashioned from sheet metal, the edge maintained with surprising sharpness. A pry bar with an additional gripping surface added, designed for hands that differ from the standard human shape. A collection of wire and cable organized by size and function, stored in containers that were once food tins and are now bent and repurposed for this new use.

Someone has been here. Someone works here. The evidence is inescapable.

---

You find them in the deeper chambers, where the light from above does not reach and the only illumination comes from bioluminescent organisms and the faint glow of functioning industrial displays. There are three of them. They are working on a pump assembly that has been disassembled across the floor of what was once a maintenance bay, its components laid out in patterns that echo the markings on the walls upstairs.

They do not look up when you enter. They do not, at first, seem to register your presence at all. Their attention is fixed on the machinery, on the problem at hand, on the work.

When you see them clearly — when your eyes adjust, when you process what you are seeing — you understand why your expectations were wrong.

They are human-descended, clearly. The shape of them, the proportions, the way they move — these are recognizably people, or were, once. But they have been here for generations. They have been shaped by pressure, by cold, by the constant presence of water, by a diet of fish and adapted forest products and whatever else this drowned country provides. Their skin is pale in a way that goes beyond complexion — it has the translucence of organisms that have not seen direct sunlight in evolutionary time. Their eyes are larger than they should be, adapted to the perpetual twilight of the depths, the irises a pale color that catches and holds whatever light is available. Their fingers are long, the webbing between them visible but not excessive — enough to aid in swimming, not enough to prevent the fine manipulation that their work clearly requires.

They are not wearing dive suits. They have not worn dive suits for a very long time. Their skin, their lungs, their entire physiology has adapted to this environment through a combination of natural selection, epigenetic activation, and — you realize this later, after you have seen more — deliberate modification. They have become what they needed to become.

---

The oldest of the three is the one who finally acknowledges you.

She has been here the longest — that much is evident in the way she moves, in the depth of the adaptations visible in her body, in the calm with she regards you. Her hair floats around her head like a halo of pale thread, moved by the slight currents her own breathing creates. Her eyes settle on you with an expression that you cannot immediately read. Curiosity, perhaps. Wariness. Something else that has no clear above-water equivalent.

She does not speak. The water would distort sound beyond recognition at this depth, and perhaps she does not remember how, or perhaps she never learned. Instead, she makes a gesture — a motion of her hands that seems to carry meaning. Come. Look. See what we are doing.

You approach. The other two — younger, their adaptations less pronounced — watch you but continue their work. They are cleaning components, inspecting seals, replacing worn surfaces with materials that they have clearly gathered from somewhere else in this drowned complex. The work is patient, methodical, practiced.

The older woman shows you the pump. She points to the problem area — a seal that has degraded, allowing water infiltration where water should not go. She shows you the replacement they have prepared, fashioned from a combination of original-specification rubber and some new material that has a organic sheen to it, something harvested from the surrounding ecosystem and processed into a functional substitute.

She looks at you. The question is clear: do you understand?

You understand enough. You understand that this work matters to them. You understand that they have been doing this work, and work like it, for longer than anyone up above has been paying attention. You understand that the machines keep running because they keep them running, and they keep them running because keeping things running is what they do, is what they have always done, is what they have become.

---

They are not the only inhabitants. They are not even, strictly speaking, the most numerous.

The Upper Reach teems with life that has nothing to do with the human-derived maintainers. The fish have their hierarchies, their territories, their patterns of migration and predation. The crustaceans click and scuttle across every available surface, establishing colonies in the sheltered places, processing the constant rain of organic debris that falls from the forest above. The larger predators cruise the open water, their movements tracking the movements of prey, their presence a constant ambient fact that shapes the behavior of everything else.

But the maintainers occupy a unique niche. They are not at the top of any food chain. They are not particularly numerous — you learn later that there may be fewer than fifty of them in the entire Upper Reach, scattered across the flooded structures in small groups like this one. They do not appear to reproduce quickly, or often, or perhaps at all; the youngest you see are clearly adolescents, but there are no infants, no children, and the age distribution seems weighted toward the older end of the spectrum.

What they are is necessary. Not in a mystical sense — the machines would eventually stop without them, but the machines stopping would not end the world. The water would continue to circulate through other means. The ecosystem would adjust. Life would persist. But the maintainers have inserted themselves into the function of this place in a way that goes beyond utility. They have become part of its rhythm.

They know things that no one else knows. They know which pumps are failing and which have been failing for decades without ever quite stopping. They know which structures are stable and which are one stress fracture away from collapse. They know the migration patterns of the fish, the growth cycles of the submerged forest, the locations where the light falls most strongly and the depths where it never reaches. They have maps in their heads that were never written down, because they were never needed in any form other than memory.

---

The younger maintainers are curious about you. They approach when the older woman is not watching, and they make sounds — clicks, whistles, tones that might be words in a language that has never been spoken above water. They touch your equipment with careful fingers, examining the materials, the construction, the differences from what they know. They are learning from you, or trying to, in the same way that they learn from everything they encounter.

You realize, gradually, that this is how they have survived. Not through strength, not through numbers, but through attention. They notice things. They remember what they notice. They incorporate what they remember into their work. Every adapted tool, every modified structure, every compromise solution that has kept the machines running — all of it came from watching, from learning, from the patient accumulation of knowledge that comes from staying in one place and paying attention to how it changes.

One of the younger ones finds a tear in your suit, a small breach near the shoulder that you had not noticed. She shows it to you with a gesture that clearly means something like "this is wrong" or "this is a problem." Then she produces, from somewhere, a small kit of repair materials — a strip of adhesive polymer, a patch of reinforced fabric. She shows you how to apply it, her movements precise and practiced, and when she is finished, she looks at you with something that might be satisfaction.

They take care of things. It is what they do.

---

You do not learn their names. You are not sure they have names, at least not in the way that you understand names. They seem to recognize each other through some combination of appearance, movement, and the specific patterns they make in the water — the clicks and whistles that serve as communication in a medium where speech is impractical. They may have names for themselves, or for each other, but if they do, they do not share them with you.

You do learn, over the days you spend among them, that they have a relationship with the place that goes beyond mere occupation. They do not just live here. They tend it. They maintain the machines because the machines need maintaining, but they also clear debris from the submerged channels, they guide the larger fish away from vulnerable areas, they harvest materials from the forest in ways that do not damage the trees' ability to regenerate. They act, collectively, as gardeners of an entire ecosystem.

It is not stewardship in the moral sense. They do not seem to see themselves as protectors or guardians. They simply understand, at a level that precedes language, that the health of the system and their own survival are intertwined. The forest provides materials they need. The fish provide food. The machines provide the currents and flows that shape the distribution of nutrients. Everything connects. They stay at the center, watching the connections, maintaining the nodes where maintenance is required.

---

The older woman — you think of her as the elder, though you have no way of knowing if she holds any formal position — spends time with you in ways that seem deliberate. She shows you things. Not just the machines, but the history of the place. She leads you through flooded corridors, past chambers that were clearly living quarters once, past workshops and storage areas and spaces whose original purpose is no longer clear. She points to markings on the walls, dates and initials carved into stone before the water came, remnants of the people who once worked here in air and light.

She shows you where they have continued the practice. In a side chamber, on a wall that was once bare, there is a new set of markings — not carved, but applied in some substance that hardens underwater. Dates, perhaps. Or names. Or some other system of notation that you do not understand. But it is clearly a continuation, a bridge between the people who were here before and the people who are here now.

She looks at you when she shows you this. Her expression is unreadable, but there is something in it that might be pride. Or might be grief. Or might be both, intertwined in a way that does not require separation.

This is who they are. They are the ones who stayed. They are the ones who became what they needed to become. They are the ones who remember the before-time, not directly — she is old, but not old enough for that — but through the chain of transmission that reaches back to the original Stayers, the ones who refused to leave, who learned to breathe water, who chose this place over everything they might have had above.

---

On your final day, they give you something. It is a small object, fashioned from metal and polymer and some organic material that you do not immediately recognize. It takes you a moment to realize what it is: a depth gauge, of sorts, calibrated for this specific environment, designed to be read underwater. It is not a gift in the ceremonial sense. It is a tool. They have given you a tool because tools are what they value, because a tool is something you can use, because they want you to leave with something that matters.

You accept it. You try to convey gratitude, though you are not sure how to do so in a language they will understand. You make the gesture you have seen them use — hands open, palms up, a motion of receiving and acknowledging. The elder mirrors the gesture. The younger ones watch.

You do not know if you will return. You do not know if anyone will return, or if this contact will lead to anything beyond itself. The maintainers do not ask you to come back. They do not ask you to bring others. They have existed without contact from above for longer than you have been alive, and they will continue to exist, or they will not, regardless of what you do next.

But as you ascend through the submerged forest, through the schools of fish and the drifting clouds of sediment, through the transitional zone where the light strengthens and the water warms, you carry with you the knowledge of what lives in the Drowned Works. Not just the fish, the crustaceans, the adapted trees, the larger predators. But the ones who stayed. The ones who learned. The ones who became something new in order to remain something old.

They are still here. They are still working. They are still tending the machines and the waters and the forest and the memory of what this place once was, because it is what they do, because it is what they have always done, because the mountains are still here, and so are they.
