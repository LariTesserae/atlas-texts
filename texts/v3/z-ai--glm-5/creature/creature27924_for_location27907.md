# What Lives in the Birthing Pocket

## The First Ones

They are not the oldest creatures here—that distinction belongs to nothing that can be named—but they are the first that learned to stay.

They arrived (if arrival is the right word for something that emerged from the walls themselves, pulled themselves out of the still-softening stone like a person pulling free from heavy blankets) when the pocket was smaller, before it had learned to stretch outward into its current dimensions. They remember when the light was dimmer, when the almost-plants were barely even suggestions, when the dampness was just a notion in the air.

They are not many. Four. Perhaps five—it is difficult to count beings that exist in multiple states simultaneously, that can be in more than one place at a time, that sometimes seem to be overlapping versions of themselves. They do not reproduce. They do not die. They simply persist, anchors in a space that otherwise refuses to commit to definition.

Their forms are difficult to describe because they refuse to hold still for observation. They are roughly humanoid in the way that a dream of a person is humanoid—you recognize the general architecture without being able to point to specific features. They have heads, perhaps. Limbs, probably. Bodies that move through the bright air with purpose and direction. But if you try to look directly at them, your gaze slides off, finding nothing solid to grip.

They communicate with each other through changes in the light itself. A brightening here, a dimming there, shifts in the quality of illumination that carry meaning too fast and too complex for outside observers to parse. They have been talking to each other since before the pocket was fully formed, and they will likely continue talking long after it has finished becoming whatever it will become. Their conversation is slow, patient, full of pauses that last for hours or days. They are in no hurry. They have nowhere else to be.

They do not interact with the other creatures of the pocket. They do not eat. They do not drink. They do not sleep. They simply observe, their attention a physical pressure that the translucent snail-things and the not-insects seem to feel and respect. When a First One turns its attention toward a particular corner of the pocket, the creatures there grow still, waiting for judgment that never seems to come.

They are not gods. They are not rulers. They are more like memories that the pocket has kept—the first attempts at stable existence, preserved and continuing, reminders of what this place was before it learned to make the creatures that now fill it.

## The Architects

If the First Ones are memories, the Architects are intentions.

They are visible only by their effects. A section of wall that was rough becomes smooth, and an Architect has passed. A depression in the floor deepens into a proper hollow, and an Architect has been working. The almost-plants in one corner suddenly grow more substantial, more confident, and an Architect has breathed possibility into them.

They have no bodies that anyone has ever seen. They might be discrete entities, or they might be aspects of the pocket itself, expressions of the place's drive toward structure. They build by wanting. They shape by attending. They work slowly, incrementally, changing the pocket one small alteration at a time, and their purposes are impossible to guess.

Sometimes their work is undone almost as quickly as it is accomplished. A wall they have smoothed becomes rough again, as if the pocket is arguing with them. A hollow they have deepened fills with seeping moisture, flattening back toward its original state. The Architects do not seem frustrated by this. They simply begin again, patient as stone, as persistent as the dampness that fills the air.

The other creatures ignore them. The First Ones observe them with something that might be respect. The pocket itself seems to tolerate them, allowing their alterations even as it resists them, incorporating their changes into its own slow process of becoming.

No one knows what they are building toward. Perhaps they do not know themselves. Perhaps they are simply following an instinct toward structure that they cannot resist, the way a spider weaves webs even when there are no flies to catch.

## The Swimmers

There is no water here, not proper water, not yet. But there are creatures that swim.

They move through the air as if it were liquid, their bodies streamlined and smooth, their motions fluid and continuous. They are larger than the other inhabitants of the pocket—some of them are the size of a human arm, some larger still—and they occupy the upper regions of the space, circling near the ceiling where moisture accumulates before it falls.

They have no eyes. They have no mouths. They have fins that ripple with continuous motion and bodies that shimmer with the same light that fills the pocket, as if they have absorbed some of it and now carry it within themselves. They feed on the dampness in the air, filtering it through organs that might be gills, extracting something from it that sustains them—moisture itself, or potential, or the possibility of water that has not yet condensed.

They are not aggressive. They are not social. They circle endlessly, alone or in pairs, tracing patterns in the air that might be meaningful or might simply be the natural result of their feeding. They do not interact with the floor-dwelling creatures. They do not acknowledge the First Ones or respond to the presence of the Architects. They exist in their own medium, following their own purposes, entirely self-contained.

Sometimes one of them descends toward the ground, moving slowly, deliberately, and hovers just above the surface of the damp stone. It stays there for hours or days, barely moving, and then rises again, returning to its ceaseless circling. What it was doing, what it was looking for, whether it found it—these are questions without answers.

They make no sound. They leave no traces. They cast no shadows in the light that comes from everywhere. But you can feel them when they pass overhead, a slight pressure, a disturbance in the air, a sense of something large and silent moving just above you.

## The Feeders

They are the reason the pocket does not overflow with life.

The translucent snail-things, the not-insects, the too-long worms—they all reproduce, or do something like reproduction, creating more of themselves from the potential that saturates this place. Without something to check their growth, they would fill the pocket entirely, consuming every scrap of dampness and possibility until the space collapsed under their weight.

The Feeders prevent this.

They are not predators, exactly. They do not hunt. They do not chase. They simply exist, and their existence creates a kind of hunger in the air around them. When they drift near other creatures, those creatures grow thinner, paler, less substantial. Their vitality drains away, pulled toward the Feeders by an attraction that cannot be resisted. The snail-things grow translucent and fade. The not-insects slow their skittering and stop. The worms retract into their holes and do not emerge again.

The Feeders are not cruel. They do not enjoy what they do. They are simply what they are—concentrations of appetite that the pocket requires in order to maintain balance. Without them, the pocket would become overwhelmed; with them, the population of creatures remains stable, sustainable, contained.

They are beautiful, in their way. They look like swirls of smoke, like dust motes caught in a sunbeam, like the suggestion of wings made of gossamer and light. They drift through the pocket without apparent purpose, following currents that no one else can feel, and where they pass, life thins.

The other creatures flee from them when they can. The snail-things press themselves into crevices. The not-insects hide in the smallest cracks. The worms stay deep in their holes. But the Feeders are patient. They cover the entire pocket eventually. There is nowhere to hide forever.

The First Ones do not flee from them. The First Ones cannot be fed upon—they have already given all they have to give. The Swimmers are too thin a meal, too insubstantial. The Architects might be feeding the Feeders without knowing it, their intentions slowly drained away, converted into appetite and hunger and the slow satisfaction of creatures that have no mouths but still consume.

## The Children

They are not children in any human sense. But they are young.

They emerge from the walls at irregular intervals, pulling themselves into existence like the First Ones did, but smaller, less formed, more uncertain. They have not yet learned to hold their shapes. They flicker between configurations—a suggestion of limbs, a hint of features, a momentary solidity that collapses back into indeterminacy as soon as it appears.

They are learning to exist. The pocket is teaching them.

They watch the other creatures with something that might be curiosity. They try to imitate what they see—adopting the forms of snail-things, then not-insects, then worms, then discarding each shape when it doesn't fit. They taste the dampness. They reach toward the almost-plants. They seem to sample every possibility that the pocket offers, trying each one in turn.

Most of them do not survive. The pocket is not gentle, and existence here requires a certain resilience, a certain commitment to being. The ones that cannot decide what they are fade back into the walls, becoming raw material for the next attempt. The ones that can—the ones that find a form that fits—

They become something new. Not First Ones—those were unique, unrepeatable. Not Architects or Swimmers or Feeders. Something else, something the pocket hasn't finished inventing. They join the ecosystem of the space, finding niches, filling functions, contributing to the slow process of creation that this place embodies.

Sometimes they choose forms that have never existed before—configurations of limb and organ and sensation that have no names, that resemble nothing from the surface world or from anywhere else. These are the most interesting, and the most fragile. They often do not last. But some of them persist, becoming the first of their kind, pioneers of forms that might spread to other pockets, other wombs, other places where reality is still being born.

## The Visitor

There is one creature here that does not belong.

It arrived from outside—from somewhere else, somewhere that has already finished forming, where the rules are fixed and reality is reliable. It found its way into the pocket through a crack in the boundary, a thin place where the walls had not yet learned to be walls. It entered, and then the crack sealed behind it, and now it is trapped.

It is different from everything else here. It has a fixed form—rigid, unchanging, refusing to participate in the pocket's constant becoming. It has eyes that see light that comes from sources. It has a mouth that opens and closes without sound. It has hands that reach toward surfaces that shift away from its touch.

It is dying. Slowly, but inevitably. The pocket cannot sustain something so certain of what it is. The creatures here exist because they have learned to exist multiply, to be several things at once, to hold possibility within themselves. The Visitor holds only itself, and that single-point certainty is starving it.

It does not understand where it is. It does not understand what is happening to it. It moves through the pocket seeking an exit that isn't there, calling out in a language that the other creatures cannot hear. It has been doing this for longer than it knows—time moves differently for things that cannot adapt.

The other creatures avoid it. There is something wrong about it, something that tastes like elsewhere, like the world above where things have already decided what they are. The First Ones observe it with something that might be pity. The Architects work around it, incorporating its presence into their slow calculations.

It will not last much longer. Soon it will begin to dissolve—not physically, but ontologically. Its certainty about its own existence will become uncertain. Its fixed form will begin to waver. It will start to participate in the pocket's becoming, and then it will either learn to be multiple things at once, or it will fade entirely, becoming one more layer of potential in the walls.

Perhaps this is what the pocket needs. Perhaps the Visitor brings something that this forming place cannot generate on its own—a taste of the finished world, a reminder of what stability feels like, a touch of the reality that waits above. Perhaps its dissolution will teach the pocket something important about the nature of existence.

Or perhaps it is simply a mistake. A creature in the wrong place, at the wrong stage of its development, caught in a womb that cannot nurture it.

The pocket does not explain itself. It simply continues.

---

## The Everything

And beneath all of these—the First Ones, the Architects, the Swimmers, the Feeders, the Children, the Visitor—there is something else.

It is not a creature. It is not a being. It is the pocket itself, considered as a living thing.

It breathes. The walls expand and contract with rhythms too slow to perceive, cycles that take hours or days to complete. The light brightens and dims with patterns that might be heartbeat, might be respiration, might be thought. The moisture collects and falls and collects again, circulating through the space like blood through a body.

It dreams. The almost-plants, the half-formed creatures, the shifting boundaries—these are its dream-images, attempts at creation that it tries and discards and tries again. It is learning what it wants to be, and its dreams are the laboratory where it experiments with possibility.

It waits. Something will happen. The pocket is not permanent—no womb is. It will eventually finish forming, and then it will break open, and whatever it has created will be born into whatever waits beyond its boundaries. The creatures here are preparing for that moment in their own ways—storing potential, learning to hold their shapes, becoming resilient enough to survive the transition.

But the pocket itself waits most of all. It is patient in the way that geological processes are patient, in the way that evolution is patient. It will take as long as it takes. It will create what it creates. And when it is ready, when its boundaries can no longer contain the pressure of its becoming—

It will open.

And everything here will be released into whatever lies above.
