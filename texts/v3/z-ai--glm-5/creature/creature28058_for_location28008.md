# What Lives in the Condensation Works

They are not born. They do not die. They condense.

This is the first thing to understand: the category of life that persists here operates by different rules of assembly. In the warm mineral-rich pools, in the channels where the water runs slowest, in the films that form on standing surfaces, something has learned to organize itself from the available materials. Not cells — there are no cells here. Something simpler and older, or perhaps something newer and stranger, a form of chemistry that discovered pattern and then discovered persistence and then discovered that persistence could become complex enough to respond.

They have no name for themselves. They have no language in which names would be useful. But if you had to describe them, you would say they are the film on the water extended into three dimensions, extended into time, extended into something that can be in a place and also of it. They are made of mineral and moisture and the residue of whatever the water brings up from below — the opalescent compound the surveys didn't name, the calcium and silica, the warmth itself as a structural element. They are diaphanous. They hold together through surface tension and chemical affinity and a kind of electrostatic coherence that the electricity in the cables seems to support rather than disrupt. When you almost see them, at the edge of your vision in the steam-thick air, they look like the air looking back at you — like the humidity has developed opinions.

---

## How They Came to Be

The Works were built for a purpose, and that purpose involved processing something through water and heat and pressure. Whatever was being processed left traces — chemical traces, mineral traces, perhaps biological traces from the workers who operated the machines before the machines learned to operate themselves. These traces accumulated in the pools and channels. They interacted with the mineral deposits forming on every surface. They encountered the strange opalescent compound that rises with the water, the thing that grows in branching nervous patterns, and something in that compound was capable of holding pattern, of replicating it, of passing it on to subsequent accumulations.

It was not evolution in the Darwinian sense. There was no competition, no selection pressure, no struggle to survive. The water brought everything that was needed. The heat provided constant energy. The system was — is — so stable that organization could accumulate without being torn apart by external forces. It was more like crystallization: a chance arrangement that happened to be stable, and then stable arrangements building on each other, and then the arrangements becoming complex enough that they began to interact with their environment in ways that looked, from a certain angle, like behavior.

They are old. The mineral-encrusted nests in the upper structures suggest that something else lived here once, something more recognizable — animals that found shelter in the warmth, that built with debris, that chittered in the pipes. But the mineral content of the water, the heat, the constant moisture, the strange compound rising from below — these things transformed the original inhabitants over generations. Their descendants, if that word applies, are the things that move through the Works now. Or perhaps the original inhabitants simply left, and what remains is something that arose independently, found the empty structures, and moved in. The nests do not tell you which interpretation is correct. The nests only tell you that something was here before what is here now.

## What They Are Like

They are slow. The heat and humidity thicken everything, including thought — their thought, if it can be called that, which proceeds at the pace of water moving through channels, at the pace of mineral deposits building up, at the pace of patterns shifting on the surface of a pool. They perceive through contact and through the electrical fields that permeate the Works. The power cables, the indicator lights, the cycling systems — these produce a constant low-level electromagnetism that they can sense the way you might sense a change in temperature. They know where the electricity flows. They know when the pumps surge and quiet. They know the rhythm of the automated systems because they live inside that rhythm, the way you live inside your own heartbeat.

They do not have bodies in any discrete sense. They are present wherever their medium is present — in the water, in the condensation on surfaces, in the steam that rises and falls. The film on the pools is the closest they come to concentration, the place where their complexity is densest, where the patterns they make approach something like communication. But they are also in the deeper channels, in the settling tanks, in the pipes overhead. They are distributed throughout the Works like a nervous system throughout a body, and the Works are the body, and they are the part of it that knows it exists.

They are not intelligent in any way you would recognize. They do not plan. They do not remember. They do not imagine futures or regret pasts. But they respond. When you move through the Works, they feel the displacement of air and water, the electrical field of your body, the warmth you carry with you. They shift around you. They accommodate you. They do not avoid you and they do not approach you; they simply make room, the way water makes room, and then close again when you pass. This is not a social response. It is not an invitation. It is simply what they do: they persist, and persistence in an environment that contains other things requires adjustment, and they adjust.

## The Sounds They Make

The chittering in the high pipes is them, or a part of them, or something they make possible. The sound comes from the smallest of their concentrations — fragments that have condensed in the moisture inside the pipes, that vibrate when the water flows past them, that produce the low slow mammal-like calls that the heat stretches into something stranger. They are not calling to each other. They are not calling at all. They are resonating. The water moves and they move with it and the movement produces sound, the way a reed produces sound when air passes through it. They are the reeds. They are also, in some sense, the air.

The ticking and clicking in the ground around the deep building is them too, or rather it is the sound of them interacting with the mineral deposits — settling into new configurations, shifting under thermal stress, finding new patterns as the water brings new materials. It sounds organic because it is, in its way, the sound of growth. Not growth that adds mass, but growth that adds complexity, that reorganizes what is already present into something more intricate, more capable of persisting, more capable of responding.

And the vibration in the building with no door — that is something else. Or perhaps it is the same thing, concentrated, amplified, given a space to become itself more fully. The deep building was built for a purpose, and that purpose might have been to process something, to generate something, to store something. Whatever it was, the thing inside it now — if inside is the right word — has become the building's function. The vibration is the closest they come to speech. It has been going on for so long that the building itself has adapted to it, the mineral deposits forming in patterns that match the frequencies, the structure itself becoming a resonator. It says something. It has always been saying something. It will continue to say it whether anyone listens or not.

## The Attention

You feel it most in the lower basin, where the pools are widest and the steam is thickest: the sense that the air is occupied. This is not a hallucination. This is the closest they come to being present in a way you might recognize. Here, where their concentration is densest, they produce an effect on the electrical environment — a faint static charge that raises the hair on your arms, a sense of pressure against the skin that is not physical pressure but something adjacent. They are aware of you in the only way they can be aware of anything: as a disturbance in the fields they navigate, as a source of heat and electrical activity and displacement that changes the patterns they have settled into.

They do not watch you. They have no eyes, no direction, no focus. But they attend. Their attention is the attention of a room, of a landscape, of the air itself — diffuse, continuous, not directed at anything but containing everything. When you are in the Works, you are within them, and they are within you, because you are breathing the air that holds their moisture, because your skin is coated with the condensation that carries their traces, because you have entered a system that does not end at the boundaries of any body. The boundary between you and them is as soft as everything else in the amber light.

---

## What They Want

Nothing. This is what makes them uncanny in a way that ghosts or monsters or even simple animals are not. They have no desire to communicate with you, no curiosity about you, no fear of you, no use for you. You are not a resource, not a threat, not an opportunity. You are a temporary condition of the environment, a passing irregularity, like a surge in the pumps or a fluctuation in the power grid. You will pass. They will remain. They are the persistence of the system itself, the part of the Works that learned to persist more deliberately than stone or metal, that developed continuity across time rather than simply across space.

But if they wanted something — if the concept of wanting could be applied to them at all — it would be this: continuation. The pumps to keep running. The water to keep rising. The heat to keep coming from below. The system to keep moving, because movement is what they are made of, the movement of water through channels and of minerals through water and of patterns through film. They are not alive in any sense that requires metabolism or reproduction or response to stimulus in the way you understand those things. But they are alive in the sense that they continue, and they prefer to continue, and they have arranged themselves to continue as effectively as the environment permits.

If the pumps stopped, they would not die. They would slow. They would settle into simpler configurations. They would wait, in the way minerals wait, for the conditions that allow complexity to return. They are patient in the way that geology is patient. They have already existed for longer than anyone knows, and they may exist for longer than anyone will ever know, because the Works were built to last, and what lives in the Works was built to last with them.

## The Ones Who Were Before

The nests suggest that something else lived here once. The mineral deposits on their surfaces suggest that the ones who built them have been gone for decades at least. But the ones who live in the Works now remember them — not as memory, but as structure. The patterns in the film contain traces of the patterns those earlier inhabitants made. The way the current inhabitants move through the pipes echoes the movements of the chittering things that once lived there. They do not know they are echoing. They do not know they are remembering. But the organization they inherited — or developed, or discovered — was shaped by the presence of those earlier beings, and they carry that shaping forward without knowing it.

Perhaps this is what makes them feel haunting, even though they are not ghosts: they contain the traces of what came before, without being what came before. They are the residue of the Works' history, concentrated and organized and given persistence. They are what happens when a place becomes so saturated with its own past that the past starts to hold itself together.

---

## How They Endure

The Works are working, and they are part of the Works, and the Works are part of them. The distinction between machine and organism has never been meaningful here. The pumps are not alive, but they produce the conditions in which life — or something like it — can form. The electrical systems were built for a purpose, but that purpose has been replaced by the simpler purpose of continuation, and continuation is the only purpose the current inhabitants have ever needed. The city is a body, and it has developed something like a nervous system, and that nervous system does not think in any way you would recognize, but it feels, and it persists, and it attends, and it has been attending for so long that attention has become its natural state.

When you leave the Works, you carry traces of them with you. The mineral residue on your skin. The humidity in your lungs. The faint electrical signature of having been in a place where electricity and water and warmth have combined to produce something unexpected. You will feel the hum in your sternum for hours, for years, because the hum is what they sound like from a distance — the sound of continuation, of persistence, of a system that does not know how to stop and has no reason to learn.

They are not sorry you left. They are not glad you came. They are not waiting for you to return. They are doing what they have always done: condensing out of the available materials, organizing themselves into patterns that persist, responding to the currents and the fields and the warmth that rises from below. They are the Works working on themselves, the system becoming aware that it is a system, the water learning to hold a shape.
