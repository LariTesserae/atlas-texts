# Who Lives Here

---

## The Veildwellers

They are not a people. They have become a population.

Scattered across distances that would take generations to cross, the human inhabitants of the Veldtmere share no government, no language, no coordinated purpose. What they share is more intimate than any of those things: they share the rhythm of breathing in and breathing out, the mist-cycle that governs all movement here, the slow hydraulic pulse of water moving through root-systems that they have learned to feel through the soles of their feet before their minds can name it.

Their numbers are impossible to determine. A traveler might walk for weeks without encountering another person, then find three living within a day's walk of each other, separated by dense growth that made them invisible to each other for years. Sometimes they find each other. Sometimes they don't. Neither outcome seems to trouble them.

They call themselves nothing. The few outsiders who have encountered them and lived to describe the meeting have supplied various names—Veildwellers, the Spongedeep, the Greenlost, the Breathed—but these are projections, categories applied from outside. When asked what they call themselves, one of them (a woman who had lived on the plateau for what she estimated as forty years, though time here resists such measurement) replied: "We don't. We just are here." She seemed genuinely puzzled by the question, as if someone had asked what she called her heartbeat.

## Adaptation

Their bodies have changed.

Not quickly—not within a single lifetime—but across the generations that have persisted here, the Veildwellers have drifted toward something their ancestors would not recognize. Their skin has taken on a grey-green undertone, not pigmentation from sunlight (there is no strong sunlight to pigment them) but something else, a suffusion of chlorophyll-like compounds that their bodies have learned to synthesize from the plants they eat, the air they breathe, the water that emerges from every surface around them. They do not photosynthesize, not exactly. But they can go longer without food than any human should be able to, drawing some marginal sustenance from the light that reaches them, the moisture that condenses on their skin.

Their eyes have changed most visibly. The pupils have expanded to take in the perpetual dimness, pools of black ringed by thin irises of pale amber. They see in the green gloom as others see in daylight, reading the subtle textures of leaf and shadow, tracking the slight movements that betray animal presence. But in bright light—if they ever encounter it—they are nearly blind, their wide pupils painful slits, their faces turned away like sun-shy creatures emerging from caves.

Their lungs have deepened. The air here is rich with moisture, heavy with oxygen exhaled by the vast photosynthetic engine surrounding them, but it resists inhalation—thick, warm, substantive. Those born on the plateau develop broader chests, more efficient gas exchange, a capacity for sustained exertion that would astonish a lowland athlete. They do not breathe faster when they move; they breathe deeper, pulling the wet air through their bodies like bellows.

Their digestion has become omnivorous in the extreme. They eat the plants—which are everywhere, in bewildering variety, some nourishing, some toxic, many both depending on preparation—and they eat the animals they can catch, and they eat the insects, and they eat the decomposing matter in the lower layers when other food is scarce. They have learned which growths are safe raw, which require soaking, which must be fermented in the pools that collect in canopy-crotches, which need to be pressed and dried in the rare clearings where mist evaporates. Their guts can process cellulose in ways that would be impossible for unadapted humans. Waste is not wasted here; they return what they cannot use to the root-networks, and something in exchange finds its way back to them.

Their skin has grown more sensitive, not to temperature (which is constant) but to pressure, to vibration, to the micro-currents of air that signal movement nearby. A Veildweller can stand perfectly still in the dimness and sense the displacement caused by a large animal passing ten meters away, the subtle shift in air-density, the brush of disturbed vegetation. They feel the plateau the way a swimmer feels the ocean—through the medium itself, the whole surface of their body an instrument for reading the world.

## Dwelling

They do not build.

This is not for lack of ability. The ruins prove that others built here, once. But the Veildwellers have learned what the builders learned too late: that construction is temporary here, and the temporary is wasteful. Everything erected on the plateau is eventually consumed. Stone is split by roots. Wood is overridden. Metal corrodes in the perpetual damp. Even the strange seamless structure—the one remnant that resists consumption—has taught them something: that to be excluded from the cycle is to be dead in a way more final than dying.

Instead, they inhabit.

The plateau offers spaces. Beneath the walking-surface, where the root-layers have grown thick enough to create hollows. Within the gaps between canopy-tiers, where the growth has woven itself into platforms. Against the thicker supports—those trunk-like masses that may be plant or may be compressed remains of something older—where a person can lean into the curve and find shelter from the gentle damp.

They find these spaces rather than making them. A Veildweller moves through the vegetation until they locate a hollow that fits, a gap that welcomes, and they settle there. They do not clear or shape or claim. They simply occupy, and the vegetation adjusts around them—growing to accommodate their warmth, their breath, the moisture they exhale, the waste they return. After a time, the space looks as if it were always meant for them.

A dwelling might be occupied for a season, or for decades. When the inhabitant leaves or dies, the space closes gradually, tendrils creeping inward, leaves filling the absence, until nothing remains but a slight impression in the growth—a memory the plateau keeps for a time and then forgets.

## The Mistsingers

Among the Veildwellers, some develop a practice that has no equivalent elsewhere.

They learn to read the mist.

The mist-cycle—morning and evening, the plateau breathing out and breathing in—is not uniform. It varies by region and season (if season applies here; the plateau knows only subtle shifts, rhythms so slow that a human lifetime might catch only part of one). The mistsingers track these variations. They feel the humidity rising before it becomes visible, sense the pressure-changes that signal a thick cycle or a thin one, predict where the fog will pool and where it will thin.

And they sing to it.

The singing is not performance in any recognizable sense. It is a low humming, produced in the throat and chest simultaneously, a chord that vibrates the sinuses and resonates in the skull. The mist responds—not because the song controls it, but because the sound gives the water droplets something to form around, nuclei for condensation that shape where the fog thickens, where it lingers, where it clears.

A skilled mistsinger can create a corridor of visibility through the soup, enough to travel when others must wait. They can pull a clearing into being around themselves, a dome of relative clarity in which things become visible—useful when hunting, or when meeting another person for the first time and wanting to see them whole.

The singers do not teach this practice. It emerges in some individuals—usually those who have lived longest on the plateau, whose bodies have had time to adapt to its rhythms. A singer cannot explain what they do any more than a sleepwalker can explain how they navigate. They simply open their mouths and the mist listens.

## The Children

Children born on the plateau are different from the start.

Their eyes open already adjusted to the gloom—no squinting, no confusion. Their skin bears the green undertone from birth, faint but unmistakable. They do not cry as other infants cry; they make soft sounds, chirps and hums, that blend with the ambient noise of the vegetation around them, evolution selecting against anything that might attract the wrong kind of attention.

They learn to move before they learn to walk. TheVeildweller infants crawl and then climb, pulling themselves through the dense growth with arms and legs that grip by instinct. They can hang from a vine at six months. They can navigate the canopy-tangles before they can navigate the ground.

And they do not learn language as other children do.

This is the strangest adaptation, the one that most disturbs outsiders who encounter it. Veildweller children speak, yes—but they speak late, and they speak strangely. Their first words emerge around age three or four, long after lowland children would be chattering. When the words come, they come fully formed—not single terms but phrases, sentences, complete thoughts delivered in a tone that sounds inherited rather than learned.

The Veildwellers themselves are not concerned by this. They know what their children are doing in the years before speech: they are listening. The plateau communicates in low vibrations, in chemical signals, in the pulse of water through root-systems. The children hear it—not as language, not as words, but as something prior to language, a meaning that precedes expression. When they finally speak, they are translating something that has always been there.

What do they say, these late-speaking children? They describe the plateau's state. They report on regions they have never visited, conditions they have not witnessed, movements of the great slow grazers in distant basins, the death of a canopy-colony days away, the approach of an unusually thick mist-cycle. They do not know how they know these things. The knowledge has simply always been present, like the moisture in the air, like the green light, like the steady warmth.

Among the Veildwellers, the children are the prophets—not of futures, but of presents. They are the ones who tell the scattered population what is happening beyond the horizon, who warn of changes too subtle for adult senses to detect, who navigate by intuition what others must learn by experience.

And sometimes—rarely, but consistently enough to be known—a child will pause in their play, will grow still as the growths around them, and will say something that makes no sense: *Something is coming up.* Not from below the plateau, not from within it, but from beyond the edge, from the lower world that has forgotten this place exists. The Veildwellers do not know what to do with this knowledge. They have no defenses. They have no walls. They have only the vast living surface that has always been enough.

## The Solitaries

Most Veildwellers live alone.

Not in isolation—they encounter others, trade knowledge and goods, sometimes mate and sometimes part, sometimes stay together for a time. But their dwellings are singular, their routines personal, their relationship with the vegetation individual. They have no villages, no gathering-places, no central spaces. The plateau provides no clearings large enough for assembly, and they have learned not to make them.

But there are those who take solitude further.

Every few generations, a Veildweller withdraws. Not from discomfort or trauma—at least, not in any way they can articulate—but from a pull they describe as the vegetation calling them deeper. They stop moving. They find a place that welcomes them and they simply stop.

The one who knew the mistsinger woman estimated that she had watched three solitaries in her forty years. The first was a man who sat against a support-mass and did not rise again. Over the course of months, the vegetation grew around him—not over him, not consuming him, but surrounding him, incorporating him. Vines draped across his lap. Leaves pressed against his shoulders. Roots threaded beneath his legs. He remained conscious, the woman said. He ate what grew within reach. He drank the condensation that formed on his skin and the surfaces near his mouth. He breathed the slow breath of the plateau.

The second was younger, barely past childhood, who simply lay down one day in a shallow depression and let the growth close over her. The woman visited her for a season. The girl seemed content. Her skin had greened further than any adult's, and fine root-hairs had begun to form where the plants touched her, not piercing her but merging with her, a connection forming at the points of contact.

The third was the woman's own mother.

She did not want to discuss this in detail. She said only: *She is still there. She is still aware. She is something else now.*

When pressed, she admitted that the solitaries do not die, not as far as anyone can tell. They change. Their metabolism slows. Their awareness spreads. They feel, through the root-network, through the chemical signals, through the water that moves through all things here, the entire plateau. And slowly, over time, they become less themselves and more the vegetation—still conscious, still present, but diffuse, distributed, a mind spread across kilometers of living matter.

The Veildwellers do not mourn the solitaries. They visit them, sometimes, when they pass near. They bring news, speaking aloud to what looks like a mass of growth but is also still somehow a person. They believe—and the children confirm this, in their strange knowing way—that the solitaries are the plateau's memory, the means by which the vegetation retains awareness of itself.

The ruins, the woman said, the ancient structures that the plants overgrew—perhaps those builders did not fail. Perhaps they succeeded so completely that they became this.

## The Relationship

The Veildwellers do not worship the plateau.

Worship implies distance, a separation between the sacred and the supplicant. There is no separation here. They live inside the organism. They eat its substance and it absorbs theirs. They breathe its exhalations and it breathes theirs. When they die—if they die without becoming solitaries—their bodies are consumed and incorporated within days, returned to the cycle with an efficiency that should be horrifying but instead feels like home.

They have no rituals of propitiation because there is nothing to propitiate. The plateau does not demand. The plateau provides. The water emerges. The growths offer food. The spaces shelter. The mists clear for those who learn to sing. The children know its mind.

What they have instead of worship is attention.

They pay attention. They notice what the plateau is doing—not as a farmer watches crops, not as a steward monitors conditions, but as a body notices itself. A Veildweller knows when a region is entering a dry cycle (never truly dry here, only less wet). They sense when the canopy is thinning, when a collapse is coming, when a new colony of flowering growths is spreading. They track the slow migration of the great grazers, the seasonal (rhythmic, cyclical, not seasonal) blooming of the pale vines.

And the plateau pays attention to them.

This is the part that outsiders struggle to accept. The vegetation responds to the Veildwellers—not as separate organisms responding to stimuli, but as the larger body they are part of adjusting to their presence. Growth curves toward them. Water pools where they need it. The insects that might otherwise infest their dwellings stay away, deterred by something the plants emit in their vicinity.

The Veildwellers have no explanation for this that satisfies an outside framework. When asked, they look confused, as if asked why the ocean accommodates the fish. *We belong here,* they say, and the statement carries no pride, no entitlement—only the simple recognition of a fact as fundamental as gravity, as breath, as the water that emerges from every surface of the world.

## The Outsiders

They come, sometimes.

Rarely. The plateau is not easy to reach—it sits at the edge of what is traversable, beyond the reasonable limits of expedition. Those who arrive are the obsessive, the accidental, the fleeing, the lost. They come from the lower world, the dead world the Veildwellers have mostly forgotten exists, and they bring with them their frameworks and expectations and fear.

The Veildwellers watch them.

Not openly—they have learned that being seen by outsiders leads to complications, to attempts at rescue, to the violence of removal. They watch from the canopy, from the shadows, from the mists that the singers can hold around them like cloaks. They watch because they must know what enters the plateau, whether it brings change, whether the terminal equilibrium is threatened.

Most outsiders do not survive long. The humidity defeats some—their lungs cannot process the wet air, and they develop infections that the plateau's microbes, unused to alien flesh, consume with unusual enthusiasm. Others are poisoned by growths they took for food, or taken by hunters they mistook for vegetation. Some simply lose direction, wandering in circles until exhaustion claims them.

The ones who survive long enough to encounter a Veildweller react with something between wonder and horror. The Veildwellers have seen this before—the recognition that what they are looking at is human, was human, is no longer quite human, is becoming something the outsider has no category for. They have learned to leave before the horror turns to violence. They melt into the growth, and the outsider is alone again, uncertain whether they saw anything at all.

But a few—very few—do not react with horror.

They are the ones who stay. Who find, despite everything, that the plateau welcomes them. Whose bodies begin, slowly, to adapt. Who learn the mist-cycle, who eat what the growth provides, who stop mourning the separation between themselves and the living surface they walk on.

The Veildwellers accept them without question or ceremony. There is no initiation, no community to welcome them, only the vegetation that adjusts to make space and the rhythm of breathing that includes them now. They simply begin to be there. And in time—generations, lifetimes, long enough for their children's children to have no memory of the lower world—they become what the Veildwellers have become: inhabitants of the living surface atop the dead world, breathing and drinking and growing and decaying in the loop that feels like stillness.

They become what lives here. Which is to say: they become part of what the Veldtmere is. Not owners, not stewards, not parasites. Something more like an organ, a function, a cell in a body too vast to perceive its own shape. The plateau has grown a human component, scattered and sparse, as much plant as person now, and still becoming, always becoming, in the dim green light that never changes.
