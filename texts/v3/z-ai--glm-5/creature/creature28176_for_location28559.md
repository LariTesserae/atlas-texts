# The People of the Tel

## Bodies

They are small-boned and dense, these people. Not dwarfish — proportioned like anyone else — but compressed, as if the tel's own pressure has worked on them across generations. A full-grown adult stands perhaps chest-high to a plains-dweller, and weighs nearly the same. Their bones are thick for their size, heavy with minerals drawn from food grown in the tel's mineral-rich soil.

Their skin runs to shades of brown and grey, often with an ashy undertone that deepens to umber in those who work longest outdoors. The sun they receive is diffuse, filtered through haze and vegetation; they do not bronze. Instead, their skin takes on a weathered quality early in life, fine lines forming around eyes and mouths by the time a person reaches what they consider middle age. Old age is written deeply on the body — faces creased like the bark of the tel's ancient shrubs, hands knotted from decades of careful work.

Their eyes are large and dark, the whites slightly dusky. Pupils dilate readily and retract slowly; in the dimness of their daily lives, they see by gathering what light exists. They do not stare. To look directly at something is to let it fill your vision entirely. Instead, they observe by glancing, by catching details at the edges, by building understanding from accumulated glimpses. Their peripheral vision is acute — a necessity in narrow passages where danger or opportunity might come from any angle.

They do not smile with their teeth. The gesture is too much like an animal's threat display. Instead, warmth shows in the crinkling around the eyes, the slight softening of the jaw, the lean of the body toward another person. They learn to read each other in fragments: the set of a shoulder, the angle of a palm, the quality of stillness.

Their hands are their most expressive feature. Thick-palmed, long-fingered, permanently stained by the work they do. Clay-diggers have hands crusted orange-brown; weavers have hands dyed green from processing fiber; healers have hands striped with the pigments of a hundred plants. They speak with their hands when words would carry too far, touching each other to communicate what the ever-listening air should not hear.

## Names

A person receives three names across a lifetime.

The first comes at birth — a sound-word, chosen by the mother from whatever noise the infant makes most readily. These names are often strange to outside ears: *Hm-hm*, *Ssk*, *Aya-ya*. They are intimate names, used only by those who raised you, who remember you small and helpless. To hear your birth-name spoken by someone other than family is an act of aggression or intimacy beyond permission.

The second name comes with puberty, when a child begins the work that will shape their adult life. It is descriptive: *Clay-Hide*, *Green-Fingers*, *Stone-Shaper*, *Silent-Walker*. These names can change; a young woman might be *Dew-Gatherer* for her skill at collecting moisture in the early hours, then later become *Plant-Knower* when she discovers she has a talent for medicine. These are working names, names that tell people what you can do, names that change as you change.

The third name comes with burial. It is given by the person who closes the grave, who speaks the final words over the packed earth. This name is secret — spoken once, never written, known only to the dead person and the one who named them. It is the name that enters the tel, that becomes part of its substance. What these names mean, what power they might hold, the living do not say. To ask about a dead person's third name is to invite death to notice you.

## Children

Children are everywhere and nowhere. The settlement is too small for separate spaces; children share every space adults share. They are carried in slings, set in corners, brought to work, taught by proximity. A child learns to weave by sitting beside a weaver for years, hands resting on the loom, feeling the rhythm before understanding the pattern.

They are not coddled. The tel does not tolerate fragility. But neither are they harshly disciplined — there is no point punishing a child for what they cannot help, and every moment spent in correction is a moment not spent working. Instead, children are shaped by the tel itself: by the narrow passages they must navigate, by the vegetation they must learn not to touch, by the edges they must learn to fear.

Mothers raise children, but not alone. The settlement's density means that any adult might grab a wandering toddler, might feed a hungry child, might correct a dangerous behavior. Children learn early that authority is diffuse — any adult might discipline them, but any adult might also comfort them. Family lines matter, but lineage is less important than proximity. A child who spends more time with a certain weaver than with their own mother's sister will call that weaver *Aunt* and mean it.

Play exists, but it is quiet play. Noisy games disturb neighbors; wild games risk injury or damage to tools. Children develop intricate finger-games, competitions of balance and stillness, contests of who can move most silently across a surface. They play at being animals: the lizard that flattens itself against stone, the moisture-beetle that retreats into its shell, the bird that freezes when a predator passes. They become the tel's creatures, practicing the movements of their environment.

By ten, a child is contributing to household work. By twelve, they have a working name. By fourteen, they are considered adult enough to marry, though marriages at this age are usually provisional — test-unions that may be dissolved if they do not produce children within a few years. A person is fully adult when they have buried a parent and chosen a third name for them. Until then, they remain somewhat children, regardless of age.

## Marriage and Bond

Marriage on the tel is practical before it is emotional. A household needs workers. A lineage needs continuity. Two people joining their labor makes more than either alone.

Most marriages are arranged — not by force, but by negotiation between households. A young person might have preferences, might express interest in someone they have observed, but the final agreement happens between elders. The tel's density means that everyone knows everyone; there are no strangers to fall in love with, no romantic mysteries. You know your potential partners as well as you know yourself — their habits, their tempers, their skills, their family histories. Marriage is a choice among known quantities, not a leap into the unknown.

But this does not mean marriage is cold. The people of the tel understand attachment deeply. They live in each other's presence constantly; they know that comfort in a partner is the difference between surviving and merely existing. A good marriage is prized, spoken of with quiet approval. Bad marriages are endured, because dissolving a household is difficult and expensive — but they can be dissolved, and sometimes are, when the friction becomes unbearable.

Romantic love, as the plains-dwellers understand it, is not a concept here. Or rather, it is not distinguished from other forms of deep attachment. You love your partner as you love your closest friend, your favorite sibling, the work that gives your life meaning. The tel's people do not romanticize passion. Passion is a kind of weather — it comes, it goes, it changes things while it lasts. What matters is the foundation: can you work beside this person for decades? Can you trust them with your children, your secrets, your body when it fails?

Sex is quiet, private, not discussed except in practical terms. Children are not sheltered from the knowledge of it — there is no privacy large enough for that — but neither is it made into a topic of conversation. Bodies are bodies. They do what bodies do. The question is always: is this action building something, or is it merely spending?

## Work

Everyone works. The tel does not support idleness.

Work is not a separate sphere of life. It is life. A person's identity forms around what they contribute: their skills, their products, their role in the settlement's maintenance. There are no full-time specialists; everyone does some farming, some building, some tool-making, some childcare. But there are people known for particular excellence, and these people are called upon when their skills are needed.

*The Clay-Knowers* understand the tel's substance best. They can read a section of exposed slope and tell you which layers will yield good brick, which are too soft, which hide organic material that will rot and weaken. They quarry with patience, cutting blocks that have been compacting for centuries, freeing them from the matrix that holds them. A clay-knower can look at a brick and tell you roughly when it was made, what materials went into it, how long it will last.

*The Plant-Knowers* are healers and farmers both. They understand the thickets, the courtyard gardens, the plants that grow wild on the slopes. They know which leaves reduce fever, which roots draw poison, which flowers can be eaten and which will kill. A plant-knower tends the medicinal patches that grow in shadowed courtyards, harvests the fiber-plants that provide thread, guards the few edible species from over-harvesting. They are often old — the knowledge takes decades to accumulate.

*The Animal-Tenders* work with the moisture-creatures. They understand the beetles' cycles, know when a beetle is full of water and when it needs to drink; they tend the lizards in their terrariums, learning to read their moods and needs; they keep the milk-giving mammals, feeding them on selected vegetation, learning which plants make the milk sweet or bitter. An animal-tender can calm a frightened creature with touch alone. They are often younger — the work requires patience but not the long study of plants or clay.

*The Weavers* make cloth and rope. They process fiber-plants, spinning thread by hand, working looms that click and whisper in the dimness. Their fingers are permanently stained, their hands strong from years of pulling thread tight. A weaver can feel the quality of thread by touch alone, can identify a fiber's source with a single rub against their palm.

*The Stone-Workers* are rare. Good stone is precious on the tel; most tools are made from the tel's own compressed material, cut and shaped with patience. A stone-worker finds the hardest layers, the ones that have become almost like natural rock, and coaxes them into blades and grinders and pestles. Their hands are permanently scarred — one slip with a stone tool means a wound that may never fully heal.

Work begins at first warmth and continues until the light fails. There is no separate time for leisure. Leisure is time spent not working — time for conversation, for rest, for the small rituals that bind people together. A person who does not work is not criticized; they are simply not fully a person. They will be fed, because the tel does not let people starve, but they will not be consulted, will not be included in the small decisions that shape household life. To work is to belong.

## The Old

Age is respected, but not through sentimentality. An old person is valuable because they know things.

The tel changes slowly, but it changes. A person who has lived sixty years has seen weather patterns shift, has watched plant species spread or retreat, has observed the slow settling of buildings, the gradual erosion of paths, the accretion of new layers. They remember where things were buried, which walls are safe and which threaten collapse, which plants grew where before the current arrangement established itself. Their knowledge is a map of the tel's recent history — the only record that exists.

Old people are not put in separate dwellings. They stay where they have always stayed, surrounded by their accumulated possessions, their graves beneath their floors (parents, grandparents, great-grandparents going down in layers). As they grow too weak for heavy work, they shift to lighter tasks: winding thread, sorting seeds, watching children, offering advice. Their final years are spent in conversation — passing on what they know, answering questions, settling disputes from the perspective of long experience.

When an old person cannot move from their sleeping platform, the household shifts around them. They are fed, cleaned, spoken to as though they were still full participants. To ignore the dying would be to ignore the tel's own process — the slow incorporation of the living into the mound. Death is not a rupture here. It is a continuation.

A person is considered ready to die when they have given their knowledge away. Not in a single transfer, but over years — teaching the young, answering questions, making sure that what they know will survive in other minds. The third name, given at burial, is the final seal on this transfer. The person becomes knowledge, becomes layer, becomes part of the tel's substance.

## The Dead

The dead do not leave the tel.

Burial happens beneath the floor of the household. When someone dies, the family digs down through successive layers — past their parents, past their grandparents, through compressed floors that were walked on generations ago. The body is placed in the space created, wrapped in cloth, positioned curled like a sleeper. Then the hole is filled, packed tight, sealed with a layer of new floor.

This is why buildings grow upward. Each generation adds a new surface, burying the one before. A house that has stood for centuries has accumulated meters of floor, each one sealing a layer of the dead. To walk through a building is to walk across hundreds of bodies, arranged in chronological depth, the oldest at the bottom, the newest near the surface.

The dead are not forgotten. They are present — literally beneath one's feet. Children learn to sleep above their ancestors, to work above their great-grandmothers, to live their lives on surfaces that are also graves. The tel's density is not just architectural; it is corporeal. The mound is made of people.

Ancestors are not worshipped. The people of the tel do not pray to the dead or ask them for intercession. But they speak to them — quietly, in the privacy of their minds. A woman might murmur to her mother while weaving, asking for guidance, receiving no answer but presence. A man might apologize to his grandfather after making a mistake the old man had warned against. The dead are companions, witnesses, weight. They hold the tel together.

There are no markers. The floors are smooth, continuous. A household knows where its dead are buried, but the knowledge does not need to be recorded. It is in the body, in the memory of where one has stood during previous burials, in the slight depressions where settling has occurred, in the texture of the packed earth.

When a house becomes too unstable — when the walls lean too far or the foundation shifts — the household moves. They do not abandon the old house; they build directly above it, using its walls as foundation, its rooms as filler, incorporating the structure into the tel's mass. The burials remain where they are, sealed beneath layers of new construction. In this way, the dead are never disturbed. They simply become deeper.

## Belief

The people of the tel believe in emergence.

Their origin story is simple: the first people came from the tel itself. Not formed by a god, not descended from the sky — called forth from the mound by some force they do not name. They climbed out of the earth and stood on its surface and began to live. The tel made them; the tel sustains them; the tel will receive them when they die.

This belief shapes everything. The tel is not a resource to exploit; it is a body they inhabit. They are its creatures, its expression, its way of knowing itself. To harm the tel would be to harm the source of their own existence.

There is no priesthood, no temple, no ritual calendar. What other cultures call religion is here simply the practice of living on the tel. You do not need to affirm what is obvious. The tel exists. You emerged from it. You will return to it. This is not belief; it is geology.

But there are practices that outsiders might recognize as religious. The giving of third names at burial. The careful sealing of floors after interment. The custom of touching the ground before sleeping — a brief acknowledgment of where one stands, of what lies beneath. The silence maintained at new moon, when the tel is darkest and the boundaries between layers seem thin.

They do not speculate about what happens after burial. The dead are present; that is enough. What they experience, whether they dream, whether they watch — these questions are not asked. The tel keeps its secrets. The living have enough to do without probing mysteries that will reveal themselves in time.

## Strangers

The tel is visited, occasionally, by people from below.

Nomads climb the slopes once or twice in a generation, following paths that crumble beneath their feet. They come to trade: salt from distant dried lakes, rare stones, seeds from plants that do not grow on the tel. In exchange, they receive the tel's products — fine cloth, shaped clay vessels, the concentrated medicines that plant-knowers prepare.

These encounters are tense. The nomads do not understand the tel; they treat it as a place, not a being. They ask inappropriate questions: Where do you come from? (From the tel.) Where do your dead go? (Nowhere.) Why do you not leave? (Leave for where?) The tel's people answer minimally, politely, without trying to explain what cannot be understood.

Some nomads stay for days, setting up temporary camps at the plateau's edge. They are watched constantly. Children are kept away from them. The tel's people do not eat food the nomads offer, do not enter their shelters, do not touch their belongings. This is not hostility but caution. Strangers bring change; change threatens equilibrium; equilibrium is survival.

Occasionally, a young person — usually an adolescent, usually a boy — becomes fascinated by the nomads. They watch the strangers from a distance, follow them when they can, ask questions that their elders refuse to answer. Sometimes these youths leave. They climb down the slopes at night, following the nomads' retreat, and are not seen again.

This is not spoken of. To leave the tel is to become a different kind of being. Whether the youths survive, whether they find lives elsewhere, whether they remember their origin — these are not questions. They are gone. They have become strangers.

Rarely, a stranger asks to stay. This is almost never permitted. The tel cannot support additional mouths; the houses are full; the water economy is precise. But twice in living memory, a nomad has been absorbed — once a woman with knowledge of a plant disease that threatened the tel's gardens, once a man who could work stone unlike anyone living. They were allowed to remain, to marry, to become people of the tel. Their grandchildren are indistinguishable from any other children.

But they are still called *The-Below-Born*, and their families will carry that designation for generations, until it wears away like a sedimentary layer.

## Self

A person of the tel does not think of themselves as an individual in the way plains-dwellers do.

The self is porous here. You are your household, your work, your ancestors beneath the floor, your descendants not yet born. Your identity extends into the mound below and the structure above; your body is a temporary configuration of the tel's substance; your mind holds knowledge that will outlast you because you will pass it on.

Privacy does not exist. A person grows up knowing that every word, every gesture, every expression is observed and interpreted. There is no hidden self to protect; there is only the self that is seen, the self that lives in relation to others. This is not experienced as oppression. It is experienced as connection. To be unseen would be to not exist.

Dreams are the only private space. In dreams, a person might fly, might speak to the dead, might wander places that do not exist. Dreams are not discussed except with the closest intimates, and then only fragments. They are the one territory that belongs to the dreamer alone.

Solitude is rare and precious. A person might seek it by climbing to the roof at night, or walking the perimeter where the plateau drops away, or retreating to a storage room where others rarely enter. In solitude, a person might think thoughts that would be inappropriate to speak, might feel feelings that would burden others if expressed. Solitude is the space where the self temporarily separates from the collective, remembers that it has boundaries, breathes.

But solitude cannot be maintained indefinitely. The tel's density pulls people back together. A solitary person is a person in pieces; a person among others is a person whole.

## End

When a person of the tel reaches the end of their life, they usually know.

There is a recognition: the body has given what it can. The work is done. Knowledge has been transferred. The third name is ready to be given.

Dying people often request specific things: to be carried to a particular courtyard, to hear a particular song, to touch a particular plant. These requests are honored. The tel's people understand that dying is a process, a negotiation between the living self and the mound that waits.

When death comes, it is quiet. The household gathers. The oldest person present — not necessarily the eldest relative, but the eldest person available — speaks the third name into the dead person's ear. No one else hears it. The name enters the body, becomes part of its substance, travels into the tel when burial occurs.

Then the digging begins. The floor is opened. The layers are counted. The body is placed among its ancestors, curled, facing a particular direction — toward the center of the tel, toward the oldest known layer, toward the source.

The floor is packed. The room is cleaned. Life continues.

The dead person has become depth. They are now part of the tel's structure, part of its moisture-holding mass, part of the platform on which their grandchildren will stand. They are not gone. They are simply below.
