# The resonant steppe (who lives here)

## The hearer

There is a creature that lives at the very base of the lattice-towers, where the sound is not sound but vibration through solid matter. It has no name in any language you would recognize. It has no need for one.

It is vast in the way that distance is vast — difficult to perceive all at once. The hearer does not move through the substrate so much as it *is* of the substrate, its body a slow extension of the root-matter itself, differentiated only by the faint pulse of the yellow compound moving through its channels. If you stood beside it, you might not recognize it as alive. It looks like the ground. It looks like a ridge, a fold, a place where the fibrous layers have buckled upward into a low dome perhaps thirty meters across. The surface is the same amber-and-rust strata as everything else. But underneath that surface — and here is where you would have to dig, which nothing does, because nothing here digs — there is organization. There is something that could be called, at the furthest stretch of the word, a body.

The hearer does not eat in any sense that you would recognize. It absorbs. The root-substrate around it carries trace compounds, breakdown products, the particulate exhaust of the lattice-growths above, and the hearer's lower surfaces are permeable to these. It takes what it needs from the continuous rain of mineral dust and organic particulate that settles through the fibers. Its metabolism is so slow that a meal is measured in centuries. It has been digesting the same patch of substrate since before your species emerged.

But this is not what makes the hearer remarkable. What makes it remarkable is what it does with sound.

The lattice-towers produce resonance — everyone knows this. But the hearer *uses* that resonance. Its body contains chambers, hollow structures that run through its bulk like voids in coral, and these chambers are tuned. Each one is shaped to capture a specific frequency from the overwhelming chord that fills the air above. The largest chambers resonate with the lowest tones — the deep hum of the biggest lattice-growths, the ones that produce sound you feel in your bones. Smaller chambers capture higher frequencies, harmonic overtones, the thin whistling of wind through the finest lattice-structures. The hearer's body is an instrument that plays the steppe's music back to itself, but modified. Amplified in some frequencies. Dampened in others. It shapes sound the way a coral shapes water, and the purpose — if purpose can be assigned — seems to be communication. Or perhaps something more like agriculture.

Because the hearer tends things. Above its body, where the lattice-growths rise into the bright air, the vegetation is different. Thicker. More complex in its structure. The lattice-patterns here are denser, the frequencies they produce more layered, more intricate. It is as if the hearer has been cultivating these growths for millennia, selecting for resonance, for structural complexity, for the capacity to produce the sounds that it — if it can be said to want anything — seems to prefer.

Other creatures avoid the hearer's territory. Not from fear. There is nothing frightening about the great slow dome of root-matter, nothing that moves quickly enough to be dangerous. But the sound here is different. It has been processed. It carries information that the hearer has encoded into it, frequencies within frequencies, and the smaller mineral-beings cannot tolerate this. It disrupts their own acoustic navigation. It fills spaces in their perception that they need for their own purposes. So they give the hearer a wide berth, and the hearer tends its lattice-garden in acoustic isolation, shaping and reshaping the song of the steppe in ways that only it can perceive.

It is difficult to know if the hearer is intelligent. The category may not apply. Intelligence implies a certain speed of processing, a certain responsiveness to change, and the hearer operates on timescales that make your own lifetime seem like a blink. But it does respond. It has been observed — if anything here can truly be said to be observed — shifting its position over decades, adjusting its chambers, modifying the acoustic profile of its territory. It solves problems, though the problems are ones you would not recognize: how to capture a frequency that a collapsed lattice-growth no longer produces, how to propagate a desired resonance through changing air currents, how to maintain structural integrity when the substrate itself shifts beneath it. These are not the problems of a sessile organism. These are the problems of an engineer.

There are others like it. Scattered across the resonant steppe, separated by distances too great to traverse in any meaningful timeframe, each hearer sits at the base of its own lattice-cluster, each one tending its own acoustic garden, each one listening to the same chord but hearing something different. They do not communicate directly — they have no means to do so — but they share the same medium. The sound that one produces, the modifications it makes to the resonance around it, travel outward on the wind and reach the others after hours or days, attenuated but still perceptible. It is a conversation that takes generations. A single exchange of information might take centuries. But the hearers persist, and the conversation continues, and somewhere in the vast accumulating layers of the substrate, their slow thoughts are recorded in the compression of fiber and the chemical signature of their passage.

## The glider

Above the hearers, above the substrate where the ground-level creatures crawl and burrow and persist, there is a different kind of life entirely.

The gliders do not land. They have no capacity for it. Their bodies are designed for the turbulence — elongated, flexible, composed of segments that can expand and contract independently, allowing them to change shape in flight. They move through the air the way fish move through water, not fighting the constant motion but using it, riding the currents that spiral around the lattice-towers, turning the steppe's chaos into a medium they can navigate.

A glider in flight is difficult to see. Not because it hides — there is no hiding in this brightness — but because it moves at the edges of perception, a shape that appears and disappears as it catches and releases light. The mineral deposits in its exoskeleton create surfaces that flash silver and gold and copper when the sun strikes them, but the underlying body is translucent, designed to let light pass through with minimal shadow. In a world where visibility is total, the glider's strategy is not to disappear but to become difficult to focus on. It is a smear of motion, a flicker at the corner of the eye, there and gone and there again.

The glider does not hear. It has no ears, no auditory organs of any kind. In a world where sound is a physical force, a pressure that can damage, the glider has opted out entirely. Its skull — such as it is, a flexible capsule of mineralized cartilage — is insulated against vibration. It does not perceive the overwhelming chord that fills the resonant steppe. Instead, it navigates by a sense that you might call thermal vision, though this is not quite accurate. The glider detects differences in temperature: the cool shadows within the lattice-towers, the warm updrafts that rise from the sun-heated substrate, the body-heat of other creatures moving through the spaces between growths. It builds a picture of the world from heat signatures and air currents, and it moves through that picture with a precision that seems impossible until you understand that this is all it has ever known.

What does the glider eat? It grazes on the lattice-growths, but not in any way that damages them. Its mouth — a narrow,extendable tube lined with fine bristles — is designed to slip between the strands of the lattice-structures and extract the mineral-rich compounds that accumulate there. It is a cleaner as much as a consumer. The lattice-growths collect particulate matter from the air, deposits of sulfur and copper and other compounds that build up in their structures over time. The glider removes these deposits, processing them through its own metabolism, excreting the waste in forms that the plants can reabsorb. It is a maintenance system. The lattice-towers that host populations of gliders grow faster, last longer, produce more complex resonance structures than those that do not. The relationship is symbiotic in the deepest sense — not a partnership between separate organisms but an interdependence so complete that it might be more accurate to think of the glider as an organ of the lattice-growth itself, a mobile component that happens to exist outside the main body.

There are different kinds of gliders. The smallest are barely the size of your hand, flitting between the finest lattice-structures, cleaning the upper reaches where the larger creatures cannot reach. The largest are substantial — long as a boat, wide as a bridge when their wing-membranes are fully extended — and these move between the lattice-clusters, traveling the distances that separate one growth-city from another. They are the long-distance communicators of this world, though they do not communicate intentionally. What they carry is not information but biology: the propagules of the lattice-growths, the genetic material that allows the vegetation to spread despite its immobility. A large glider will land — if landing is even the word for settling briefly on a lattice-structure, its body curling around a tower for stability — and it will deposit the seeds it has collected from other growths, seeds that have adhered to its exoskeleton during feeding, and then it will move on, carrying other seeds to other towers. The plants do not need animals for pollination in the traditional sense. They need carriers, and the gliders are their carriers.

But the gliders are also prey.

## The hunter

There is a creature that hunts the gliders. It is one of the few things on the resonant steppe that moves quickly enough to be called violent.

The hunter is built for acceleration. Its body is compact, dense with mineralized muscle, and it does not ride the turbulence — it cuts through it. Where the glider is elongated and flexible, the hunter is short and rigid, a dart of a creature, all of its mass concentrated behind a single purpose: the strike. It has wings, but they are small, used not for sustained flight but for the final adjustment of trajectory, the last-second correction that brings its jaws to bear on the soft body of its prey.

The hunter sees the glider as a thermal signature, a warm shape against the cooler air, and it calculates the interception point with a precision that seems mathematical. It does not chase. It predicts. It positions itself in the turbulence above a lattice-cluster and waits, reading the air currents, sensing the patterns of motion that indicate where prey will pass, and then it falls — not like a stone but like a decision, a body committed to a single point in space and time. The impact is brief. The glider's exoskeleton is designed to be light, not strong. The hunter's jaws are designed to crush. And then the hunter is gone, carried away by the momentum of its strike, spiraling upward on the turbulence to position itself for the next.

It is not cruel. Cruelty requires a category of moral judgment that nothing here possesses. The hunter eats, and in eating, it shapes the population of gliders. It removes the slow, the careless, the sick. It keeps the symbiotic relationship between glider and lattice-growth from becoming too comfortable, too dependent. Without the hunter, the gliders might overwhelm the lattice-structures, might graze faster than the plants could grow. The hunter is a regulating mechanism, a principle of selection made flesh.

But the hunter is also subject to its own selection pressures. It cannot survive the sound.

The resonance that fills the resonant steppe is not merely loud — it is pervasive, and it is most intense near the lattice-growths, where the gliders feed. The hunter, which must hunt near these structures, has no natural insulation against acoustic pressure. It is a creature of action, not endurance. It can dive through the sound for the seconds required to make a kill, but it cannot remain. After each strike, it must retreat to the quiet zones — the spaces between lattice-clusters, where the chord diminishes to something that will not damage its body. It spends most of its existence in these emptier reaches, drifting on thermal currents, conserving energy, waiting for the hunger that will drive it back toward the dangerous zones where prey is abundant and sound is a weapon.

Its lifespan is short. The cumulative damage of the sound, the stress of constant exposure and retreat, breaks its body down quickly. A hunter that survives two seasons is old. They do not reproduce often — they cannot, when so much energy goes toward the hunt — but they do not need to. The population regulates itself through mortality. There are always enough hunters to maintain the pressure on the glider populations, never enough to threaten the overall system.

The other creatures of the resonant steppe have strategies for dealing with the hunter. Some of the larger gliders travel in groups, their thermal signatures merging into a mass that is difficult to target. Some of the substrate-level creatures have developed mineralized armor, deposits thick enough to resist the hunter's jaws if it should ever descend to their level. But the hunter rarely descends. There is nothing for it at ground level. Its world is the space between, the dangerous bright air where sound and light and motion create an environment that only the specialized can endure.

## The climber

If you want to see the climber, you must look up.

Not just to the lattice-towers — to the lattice-towers in their specifics. There is a kind of growth that is older than the others, and its structure is different. The lattice-patterns here are denser, the gaps between fibers narrower, the overall form more solid. These are the ancient towers, the ones that have been reaching upward for longer than the others, and they have developed internal structures that the younger growths lack. Hollow spaces. Chambers. Tunnels running through the vegetable matter at irregular intervals, created by some process of growth and decay that no one has fully explained.

The climber lives in these chambers.

It is a slow creature, deliberate in a way that seems almost geological. Its body is long and segmented, each segment covered in fine bristles that can grip the fibrous walls of the lattice-towers. It does not have eyes — what would it see, in the hollow darkness inside the growths? — but it has a sense that might be better: it perceives electricity. The lattice-growths generate faint electrical fields as they metabolize, the movement of the yellow compound through their structures creating currents that the climber can detect at a distance. The climber knows the shape of its home not through sight but through the presence and absence of these fields. It can sense the boundaries of the chamber it moves through. It can sense other climbers in nearby tunnels. It can sense, most importantly, the places where the lattice-growth is damaged, where the electrical fields are disrupted, where something has breached the walls and exposed the interior to the bright world outside.

The climber repairs these breaches.

This is its role in the ecology of the resonant steppe. The climber does not eat the lattice-growth — it eats the parasites that attempt to enter the lattice-growth. Small creatures, mineral-beings that lay their eggs in the fibrous walls, that burrow into the tissue of the plant and consume it from within. The climber hunts these. It moves through the dark tunnels of the ancient towers, following electrical signatures, finding the places where damage has been done, and it consumes the creatures that have done the damage. Its excretion — a thick paste of processed mineral compounds — seals the breaches, fills the tunnels left by parasites, allows the lattice-growth to continue its upward extension.

It is a symbiote, but not in the gentle way that the glider is a symbiote. The climber is an immune system. It is the lattice-growth's defense against infection, and it is ruthless in its function. Nothing that enters the towers survives the climber's attention unless it is small enough to escape notice, fast enough to flee, or valuable enough to be worth keeping.

And some things are worth keeping.

There are smaller creatures that the climber tolerates — even encourages. Certain mineral-beings that process waste compounds in ways that benefit the lattice-growth. Certain micro-flora that convert the climber's own excretions into forms that the plant can absorb. The climber does not tend these directly — it does not have the capacity for agriculture in any form you would recognize — but it leaves them alone, and in leaving them alone, it allows them to flourish. The inside of an ancient lattice-tower is not a hollow space but a complex ecology of interdependent organisms, all of them living in the dark, all of them processing the compounds that the growth draws up from the substrate, all of them contributing to the structure that houses them.

The climber communicates with others of its kind through the same electrical fields it uses to navigate. A pattern of pulses, transmitted through the conductive tissue of the lattice-growth, that can carry information: the location of a breach, the presence of a particularly damaging parasite, the availability of a food source that multiple climbers might share. It is a slow communication, limited by the conductive properties of the vegetable matter around them, but it is sufficient. The climbers form a network within each ancient tower, a distributed awareness that covers the entire structure, and this network extends — in a limited way — between towers. Where the lattice-growths interlock, where their structures touch and merge at certain heights, the climbers can pass from one to another, carrying information (and parasite-hunting) across the boundaries of individual plants.

They do not have what you would call a society. They have something more like a function. Each climber is a component of a larger system, a cell in an organism that happens to be made of many separate bodies. They do not compete with each other. They do not reproduce individually — the climbers within a given tower are produced by the tower itself, budding from the internal tissue in ways that suggest the lattice-growth has incorporated the climber's genetics into its own structure. The climber is not separate from the plant it inhabits. It is an expression of the plant, a mobile organ that exists because the plant benefits from its existence.

And yet the climber is also an animal. It hunts. It eats. It moves, it perceives, it makes decisions based on the information available to it. It can learn — within limits — which parasites are most dangerous, which breaches are most urgent, which tunnels offer the safest passage. It has preferences, though you might not recognize them as such: a climber will choose one route over another, will linger in one chamber rather than another, will spend more time repairing some areas than others. It is not a simple organism. It is a complex creature that happens to be embedded in a structure that gives it no choice but to serve.

## The assembler

There is something else on the resonant steppe. Something that does not fit into any of the categories that the other creatures have established.

You might not see it at first. It does not move like an animal. It does not grow like a plant. It is a presence, a process, a thing that happens rather than a thing that is.

The assembler works in the spaces between. Between the lattice-clusters, between the zones of intense resonance and the quieter reaches, between the areas where the substrate is stable and the areas where it is actively shifting. It is found in the boundary conditions, the places where the steppe's systems interact and overlap.

To understand the assembler, you must understand that the resonant steppe is not static. It is changing. The lattice-growths rise and fall, the substrate accumulates, the mineral deposits shift and reform, and all of these processes create waste — particulate matter, chemical byproducts, fragments of dead fiber that no longer serve a purpose in the biological systems that produced them. The assembler collects this waste. It gathers it, sorts it, and arranges it into patterns.

The assembler itself is difficult to describe. It has no fixed form. It is a swarm of smaller units — mineralized bodies, each one no larger than your thumb, that move in coordination, that cluster and separate and re-cluster according to some internal logic that no observer has successfully decoded. Each unit is simple. It can grasp, it can carry, it can detect certain chemical signatures, and it can communicate with the other units in its swarm through brief pulses of contact. But together, the swarm is capable of behaviors that no single unit could accomplish.

They build.

Not structures — not in any way that you would recognize as architecture. They build patterns. Arrangements of material across the substrate, designs that cover meters or tens of meters, that seem to have no purpose but which the assembler swarms return to again and again, adding material, adjusting the arrangement, maintaining it against the turbulence. Some of these patterns have been observed for decades. Some may have existed for centuries. The same swarm — or perhaps the descendants of the same swarm — continues to tend them, to modify them, to build them up and tear them down and build them again in new configurations.

What are the patterns for?

It is not clear. They do not seem to serve any biological function. They do not attract mates, they do not trap prey, they do not cultivate growth or process waste or contribute to the swarm's survival in any observable way. The assemblers do not live in them. They do not eat from them. They simply build them, and then they maintain them, and then they change them.

One hypothesis: the patterns are a form of communication. The swarms are not building structures but writing messages, encoding information in the arrangement of mineral particulates and fiber fragments, creating records that other swarms can read. If this is true, the messages are too complex for any observer to decode. The patterns do not repeat in any simple way. They do not follow any obvious grammar. But the swarms seem to understand them — or at least to respond to them. A swarm encountering a pattern left by another swarm will often modify its own behavior, will begin building in a different location or following a different design, as if it has received information and incorporated it into its plans.

Another hypothesis: the patterns are a form of memory. The swarms are not communicating but remembering, externalizing information that they cannot hold within their own simple bodies. Each pattern is a record of something — a location, a resource, a danger, an event — and the swarm that maintains it is maintaining a memory that it would otherwise lose. In this reading, the resonant steppe is covered with the thoughts of assemblers, a vast archive of information written in the language of arranged debris.

A third hypothesis: the patterns are art.

This is not a joke. Or rather, it is a joke, but it is also a serious proposition. The assemblers build because building is what they do. The patterns serve no function because function is not the point. The swarms arrange material into designs because the arrangement itself is meaningful to them in some way that has nothing to do with survival, with reproduction, with any of the biological imperatives that drive the other creatures of this world. They create because creation is possible, because the material is available, because the patterns are there to be made.

This would make the assemblers the only creatures on the resonant steppe that do something beyond mere survival. Or it would make them the only creatures whose survival strategy is so deeply embedded in their nature that it is invisible — they build patterns because building patterns is what keeps them alive, in some way that no one has yet observed.

They do not interact with the other creatures. The gliders ignore them, the hunters do not hunt them, the climbers do not encounter them. The hearers do not seem to perceive them. The assembler swarms exist in their own niche, processing the waste that no one else wants, arranging it in ways that no one else understands, and continuing their work across spans of time that make their patterns seem permanent even as they change.

## What lives here

To live on the resonant steppe is to live in sound. It is to have that sound be a presence so total that it shapes everything — your body, your perception, your way of being in the world. The creatures described here are not separate from the resonance. They are expressions of it. They have evolved to fit into the chord, to use it or avoid it or shape it, but they cannot escape it. The sound is the medium in which they exist, the water in which they swim, the atmosphere that they breathe. It is not background. It is not context. It is the world itself.

And yet each creature experiences the sound differently. The hearer experiences it as information, as something to be shaped and used. The glider does not experience it at all — it has opted out of the acoustic world entirely, finding other ways to perceive. The hunter experiences it as danger, as a force that limits where it can go and how long it can stay. The climber experiences it as distance — it lives inside the sound, in the one place where the resonance is muffled and transformed, and it experiences the chord only as vibration through the walls of its home. The assembler may not experience it at all, or it may experience it in ways that cannot be described, because the assembler is a process as much as a creature, and its relationship to the sound may be something other than perception.

What they share is this: they have all found ways to live in a world that was not made for them. The resonant steppe does not care about them. The lattice-growths rise and fall without reference to the creatures that live within and among them. The sound continues without purpose, without meaning, without any consideration for the lives it shapes. And yet lives are shaped. Beings emerge from the chaos, find niches, establish themselves, reproduce and die and leave behind offspring that do the same. The steppe is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is simply present, and the creatures that live here have learned — or evolved — to be present with it.

This is what it means to live somewhere: to find the shape of your existence within the shape of the world that contains you. The resonant steppe is not your world. You could not survive here. Your body is made for water, for silence, for a kind of life that does not exist in this bright, loud, mineral-soaked place. But the creatures that do live here are not struggling. They are not suffering. They are not wishing for a different world. They are at home, in the only way that anything can be at home — they fit. They belong. They are part of a system so complex and so complete that it has room for them and for everything else, and it continues, and it resonates, and it lives.
