What Lives in the Hollow

## The Colony

They are not bats.

This is the first thing you must understand, though you will not understand it immediately. You will call them bats because the word exists and the shape fits and humans reach for names. But names are not the same as truths.

They are larger than they should be. A meter of wing when spread—more, in the older ones, whose wings have stretched over decades of hanging folded. Their bodies are the size of small dogs, but longer, leaner, built for a kind of hanging patience that no surface animal could sustain. Their fur is not fur at all but something finer, filament-thin, nearly invisible against their pale skin. In the dim light of your torch, they look naked. In the true dark, they do not exist at all.

Their coloration reveals their age. The young are gray, dark gray, the color of slate before it weathers. As they age, they lighten. The oldest among them are nearly translucent—skin like parchment, wings like stained glass, the delicate architecture of their bones visible beneath. You can see where the light would pass through them if there were light. The colony is arranged by age, though you do not yet know this. The darkest bodies cluster at the edges, in the colder zones, in the places where the air moves more freely. The pale ones occupy the deepest thickets, the warmest press of bodies, the center of the mass where the heat of ten thousand living things accumulates into something almost comfortable.

Their faces are wrong for bats. The snouts are longer, more fox-like, with nostrils that open and close deliberately, filtering the air. Their ears are not the enormous satellite dishes of insect-hunting bats but smaller, shaped more like leaves, pressed flat against their skulls when they rest. They do not need to echolocate across vast distances. The space they inhabit is known to them by memory, by touch, by the movement of air across their skin. They navigate by pressing outward, by feeling where their neighbors are, by maintaining a constant awareness of their own position within the mass.

Their eyes are present. Reduced, yes—small and pale and set deep beneath ridges of bone—but present. They can see light and dark. They can see the movement of your torch. When the beam swings across the ceiling, a thousand small heads turn to track it, pupils dilating, the reflective tissue behind them catching the light. Tapetum lucidum, adapted for darkness, making their eyes glow.

They have been watching you since you arrived.

## What They Eat

You find the insect remains first—husks and shells and wings scattered beneath the thickest clusters, accumulated into drifts like autumn leaves. Some you recognize: cave crickets, pale and leggy. Moths that must have wandered down from the surface and never found their way back. Beetles with carapaces the color of wet stone.

But these are not enough. Ten thousand bodies this size cannot sustain themselves on accidental prey.

You find the pools where the air is dampest, and you find what grows there.

Pale worms. Thread-thin, finger-long, moving slowly through the shallow water. They are not earthworms. They are something else— translucent enough that you can see the darker threads of their digestive tracts, the pulse of something like blood through something like veins. They cluster in the organic sediment at the bottom of the pools, in the guano-rich deposits where moisture collects, feeding on matter you cannot identify.

The colony descends to hunt them.

Not often. Not en masse. But you see it happen once, at the edge of your perception—a single body dropping from the mass, wings spreading, a silent descent toward one of the shallow pools. The wings do not flap. They spread and catch the air, steering by tilting, adjusting, falling with intention. The landing is nearly soundless. The creature dips its head into the water, holds the position for thirty seconds, a minute, and rises with something pale and twisting caught in its teeth.

It is the only time you see one of them eat.

The rest of their existence is hunger deferred. They are adapted for scarcity, for long stretches between meals, for survival in a place where food is precious and must be earned through patience. Their metabolisms are slow, slower than any mammal has a right to be. They can go months without feeding, living off reserves stored in thick bands of fat along their bellies, in the dense tissue of their wings. The young eat more often. The elders eat barely at all. You wonder if the oldest among them have simply stopped, if they are waiting to die, if dying is another form of waiting they have not yet learned to do.

## How They Move

They are still, most of the time.

This is what strikes you most deeply after hours in the hollow. They hang, and they hang, and they hang. The colony moves like a single organism breathing—small adjustments, compressions and expansions, bodies shifting to make room for neighbors who have shifted to make room for others. But no one flies. The space above you remains a ceiling of living things, and it remains that way for hours, for days, for however long you stay to watch.

The waiting is not passive. You come to understand this. The waiting is a form of attention.

When you move, the colony tracks you. Not with panic, not with the explosive flight-response of surface bats. They track you with interest. Their heads turn. Their ears adjust. The nearest bodies press backward, creating a small sphere of empty air around you, a buffer zone that moves as you move. They are making room for you. They are making space.

You do not know why.

You see one of them drop, once. A young one, gray-furred, restless. It falls from the mass and spreads its wings and spirals downward through the dark. It does not fly—it glides. The air in the hollow is still, but the space is vast, and the heat of the colony creates thermal columns that rise like invisible pillars. The young one catches one of these columns, rides it upward, circles the perimeter of the hollow once, and returns to the mass. The whole journey takes perhaps two minutes. The colony absorbs the return without acknowledgment. The flight was meaningless. It will be repeated, perhaps, when another young one grows restless. It will not become a pattern. It will not become routine. It will simply happen, as things happen, and then not happen, as things stop happening.

## The Sound

The sound is how they know themselves.

You have been calling it a roar, but that is not quite right. A roar is singular, animal, produced by a throat and directed outward. What fills the hollow is not a roar. It is a tapestry. A thousand threads of sound woven together into something continuous.

The click of their throats—this is the base layer. A low, rhythmic clicking that you feel more than hear, a vibration in the air that resonates in your chest. It is not language. It is presence. Each click says *I am here*. Each click says *I am not moving*. Each click confirms the position of the clicker to all the bodies around it, and those bodies click back, and the sound propagates outward in waves, a living sonar map of the colony's own density.

Above the clicking, the high thin calls. These are more directional, more intentional. A mother calling to her infant—a single pup, pale and blind, clinging to her belly with claws that will one day grip stone. Two adults negotiating space, their voices rising and falling in a pattern that might be argument or might be agreement. A young one testing its voice, squeaking into the dark and receiving no answer, squeaking again.

Above even these, the territorial disputes. When a body shifts too close to another, when a space opens and two claimants reach for it simultaneously, when age and strength must be asserted—the sound becomes sharp, aggressive. Clicks become snaps. Calls become shrieks. The dispute resolves in seconds. The colony settles. The tapestry resumes.

And beneath all of it, beneath even the lowest clicks, something else. A hum. So low that you cannot hear it with your ears, only feel it in your bones, in the marrow of the bones, in the part of you that remembers being small and hidden and safe. The hum is the sound of ten thousand bodies at rest. The hum is the colony itself. It says *we are here*. It says *we are waiting*. It says *we will continue*.

## What They Were

You find the bones in the drifts beneath the oldest clusters.

They are not insect bones. They are not the bones of worms. They are the bones of the colony itself—skeletons of their own kind, scattered and intermingled with the guano, with the husks of prey, with the slow accumulation of centuries.

The bones are white. Whiter than the living creatures, whose translucence still carries the faint pink of blood beneath skin. These bones are clean, picked over by something—worms, perhaps, or the younger members of the colony, or simply time and moisture and the patient work of decay. They are delicate. The wings, when you find them intact, extend farther than the living wings you have seen, as if death has stretched them, released them from the duty of folding.

You do not know how they died. Old age, perhaps. Disease. A fall from which they did not recover. The colony does not mourn them. The colony does not acknowledge them. When a body dies and falls, the living shift to fill the space it left, and the sound continues without interruption.

But you notice that the bones cluster in certain places. Not scattered randomly, but gathered in low mounds, in depressions, in the places where the air moves least and the moisture collects. It is as if the colony has been dropping their dead in the same spots for centuries, as if there is an agreement about where the dead belong.

Or perhaps it is simpler than that. Perhaps the dead simply fall where the air is still, where the living do not go, where the space between bodies is wider and there is room to land.

You do not know. You will not know. The colony does not explain itself.

## What They Mean

They are not bats.

They are something older, something that evolved in spaces like this, in the dark places beneath the world, in the hollows that existed before there were names for hollows. They are a lineage of patience. They are a kingdom of waiting.

They have been here for centuries, and before here, they were elsewhere. In other caves, other fissures, other wounds in the earth where the light could not reach and the air moved slowly and the water pooled in the low places. They have been carried on the skin of the planet like bacteria, like the beneficial flora of the underground, filling the spaces that surface life could not fill.

They do not need the surface. They do not want it. They are complete in themselves, a closed system, a self-contained ecology of sound and patience and deferred hunger. When the limestone split open and the hollow was born, they found their way in—crawling through cracks too small for their bodies, folding their wings and compressing their frames and pushing through stone like water through sand. They made the space their own. They hung from the ceiling and waited.

They are still waiting.

## What They Wait For

You do not know.

You sit with your back against the damp wall, and the sound washes over you, and you watch the colony overhead shift and settle and shift again. You try to understand what they are waiting for. You imagine scenarios: a return of something that left, an arrival of something that has not yet come, a signal that has not yet been sent.

But the waiting, you realize, is not for anything.

The waiting is what they do. The waiting is what they are. They exist in a present so extended that it becomes a kind of eternity, each moment folding into the next without boundary, without distinction. They do not measure time. They do not need to. The sound continues. The colony persists. They hang in the dark and press together for warmth and click their territorial clicks and bear their young and drop their dead and wait.

They will wait after you leave. They waited before you arrived. They will wait until the limestone closes again or the water rises or the slow geology of the planet transforms the hollow into something else entirely. They are patient in a way that humans cannot be patient, because their patience is not a choice. It is their nature.

## You

You are a temporary irregularity. A feature passing through. The colony has no opinion about you. It has adjusted to your presence with the same indifference with which it adjusts to everything—by making space, by tracking your movement, by incorporating you into the continuous sonar map of itself.

But you have opinions about them. You cannot help it. You sit in the dark and you feel the hum in your bones and you understand that you are in the presence of something vast and ancient and patient. You understand that you are small. You understand that your time here is brief, that your life is brief, that all human lives are brief against the slow breathless waiting of the colony overhead.

You rest against the damp wall. The sound continues. After a long while, it stops being noise. It becomes something else—something like weather, something like presence, something like the sound of your own heart beating.

You could stay here. The thought comes to you unbidden. You could stay here and wait with them, and your bones would join the drifts beneath the oldest clusters, and the colony would not notice and would not care and would continue without you.

But you do not stay. You rise. You adjust your torch. You begin the long climb back toward the surface.

Behind you, the colony shifts. A thousand bodies reposition. The sound flickers and steadies.

The hollow continues.
