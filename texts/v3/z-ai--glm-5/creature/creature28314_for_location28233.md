The frogs own this place. That needs to be said first because nothing else here makes sense without understanding that. They are not one species. They are not ten. The terraces have produced forms that exist nowhere else—have been producing them for longer than anyone can track, isolation and altitude and the peculiar stability of this engineered ecosystem doing what islands do, what mountains do, what any closed system does over enough time. Translucent tree frogs whose organs are visible as dark shapes within pale bodies, their skin so thin it seems designed for a world where light barely reaches, as though transparency were a concession to the dark. Bullfrogs in the deeper cisterns that have grown to sizes their lowland cousins never achieve, slow and heavy with the confidence of animals who have no predators large enough to matter. Pipers no larger than a thumbnail whose voices carry disproportionately, each one a needle of sound piercing the general mass. And others. Forms that have no name outside this mountain because they exist nowhere outside this mountain. Frogs that have abandoned water for the wet stone itself, laying eggs in films of moisture on vertical surfaces. Frogs that have developed pads on their feet capable of gripping the particular texture of carved channels, living their entire lives in the water system, never still, always moving downstream at a pace so slow it looks like patience but is simply existence. Frogs whose calls have dropped below the range of human hearing, felt in the jaw rather than heard, their massive vocal sacs pulsing in the deepest pools like heartbeats.

They breed constantly. That's the thing about a place where water is perpetual and temperature never drops. There is no breeding season. Every pool contains eggs at some stage, tadpoles at various states of development, metamorphs with legs too new and tails too long. The life cycle that elsewhere marks time here runs continuously, overlapping itself, generations stacked in the same pools. A cistern might contain great-great-great-grand tadpoles and their simultaneously extant great-great-great-grandparents. The frogs do not track lineage. They track moisture. They track the sound of water, moving toward it, filling every space water creates.

And because there are so many frogs, there are things that eat frogs. Snakes, yes—thin brown things that move through the ferns without disturbing a single frond, their bodies evolved to navigate dense vegetation by fitting between stems rather than pushing through them. They take frogs from the edges of pools, striking from positions they have held for hours, motionless until the moment of contact, then motionless again with the frog already half-swallowed, the process so smooth it looks like the snake is simply breathing the frog in. But snakes are incidental here, a presence rather than a pressure.

The real predators are smaller. Invertebrates that have grown large in the warmth and abundance. Water bugs in the cisterns that take tadpoles, that take small frogs, their grip strength enough to hold prey larger than themselves. Spiders that build across the channels, their webs spanning water with engineering that human bridges cannot match in economy of material, taking the insects that come to water and the frogs that come to insects. Centipedes in the leaf litter that move with mechanical precision, each leg finding its placement independently, processing the dead and the dying into the soil that feeds the vegetation that shelters the pools that hold the frogs. Everything connects to the frogs. The entire system runs on frog biomass—their eggs, their tadpoles, their bodies when they die, their waste, their shed skin. The frogs are the engine. The frogs are the foundation. The frogs are the blood of this mountain.

The people who live here are few. That needs saying too, because the scale of the structure suggests a population that once filled it, and the emptiness is the first thing a visitor notices after the sound. Millions of stone blocks. Thousands of terraces. And how many people? Hard to say. They move through the mountain in family groups, in small bands, rarely more than a dozen together. They keep to their territories—particular terraces, particular levels, particular sections of the water system. They know their ground the way the frogs know their pools, by touch and sound and the memory of paths walked since they could walk.

They are small people. Not stunted, not malnourished—small in the way that populations become small when smallness is useful, when navigating tight passages and dense vegetation is easier with a body that doesn't take up much space. Generations of life in the perpetual dim have affected their eyes. Pupils that stay wide. An ability to detect motion in conditions that would appear featureless to a visitor. They don't see better in absolute terms, they see differently—their eyes having become calibrated to the specific spectrum of green-tinted light that filters through the canopy, to the slight variations in brightness that indicate water, stone, vegetation, open space. Color has become less important. Texture has become everything. They read surfaces by looking at them in ways that outsiders find hard to follow—identifying a safe footing by the way light falls on stone, recognizing a particular wall by the pattern of moss on its surface.

But touch is primary. Their hands are their main interface with the world. They read carved markers with fingertips, following grooves and patterns that indicate paths, levels, water sources, territories. The carvings are old—not as old as the mountain itself, but old. Each generation adds to them, marks new paths, notes new collapses, records shifts in the water system. The stone is a living document, written in a language of texture that has no spoken form, that exists only as a thing hands can read. Children learn it before they learn speech. Their fingers trace the patterns while they are still carried against their parents' bodies, absorbing the text before they can walk.

They don't build. This is important. They live within the construction, they maintain it, they modify it in small ways—clearing channels, opening paths, occasionally blocking a passage to redirect water—but they do not build. They have no tradition of construction. They do not quarry stone. They do not shape it. When a wall falls, they do not rebuild it. They accept the new arrangement, learn to navigate it, find the new paths the collapse creates. The concept of making a new structure from raw material is as foreign to them as flight. They live in the wreckage of something they cannot imagine making, and they do not experience this as loss or diminishment. They experience it as the natural state of things. The mountain was always here. The mountain will always be here. The fact that people made it is a fact they know in the way one knows that the sun exists—acknowledged when relevant, otherwise irrelevant.

---

What they do make is music. Or something adjacent to music that has no adequate name. Their voices have adapted to the soundscape, developing a range and a flexibility that outsiders find almost disturbing. They can pitch their calls to individual frequencies within the frog chorus, isolating a particular species, mimicking it, then shifting to another, then another, weaving their own voices into the existing sound in ways that add rather than subtract. They sing to each other across distances, their voices carrying through the wet air with clarity that speech cannot match. The meaning lives in pitch and rhythm and the way one voice responds to another—not words but patterns, not sentences but structures. A mother sings to a child and the child sings back, their voices meeting in the middle distance, each adjusting to the other's frequency until they find a shared tone.

They sing to the mountain too. Or that's how it looks to an observer. They sing to the frogs, maybe. Or to the water. Or to nothing—to the sound itself, to the act of adding a human voice to the mass of voices that already exist. These songs have no audience. They happen when someone is alone, walking a familiar path, hands trailing along a wall. They happen when someone is falling asleep in the humid dark, their voice joining the chorus as it thins toward rest. They happen when children play, chasing each other through ferns that brush their faces, their voices rising and falling in patterns that seem to mean something but that resist translation, patterns that feel like games but that also feel like practice—training for the moment when a human voice will need to carry through a mile of vegetation and be understood.

The mountain has changed their hearing. They process sound differently than others do. The deafening noise that drives visitors to distraction, that makes thought feel thick and slow—this is their baseline. Their hearing has developed to parse the layers, to find the signals buried in the mass of frog and insect and bird sound, to detect a change in the chorus that indicates a shift in water flow or the arrival of a weather system or the movement of an animal through nearby vegetation. They hear the way a reader sees—to them the soundscape is text, is language, is a continuous flow of information that they can attend to or let fall to background as needed. When a visitor asks them about the noise, they have to think about what the visitor means. The noise. They listen. They hear frogs, insects, water, birds, the movements of their family, the particular silence of an empty terrace versus the particular silence of a terrace with someone standing still on it. They don't experience any of this as noise. They experience it as the world being present, being legible, being there.

Let me talk about what they eat. Because eating here is its own practice, shaped by everything else—the dimness, the moisture, the density of growth, the engineered water system.

They harvest from the terraces. Not farming, not exactly—cultivation implies a degree of control that isn't possible here. They tend. They encourage. They visit particular plants and ensure they have what they need, redirecting small flows of water, clearing competing growth, protecting young shoots from the herbivores that would take them. The plants they tend are descendants of crops planted by the builders, or perhaps volunteers that found this niche and were adopted, or perhaps something in between. The distinction between wild and cultivated has no meaning here. Every plant is both. Every plant exists because the mountain creates conditions for it and because the people who harvest it have, over centuries, created conditions slightly more favorable.

Tubers, mostly. Root crops that grow in the accumulated soil of the terraces, their edible parts buried in the dark earth that has built up between stone blocks over centuries. These are the staple. Dense, starchy, capable of being eaten raw or cooked, depending on the situation and the availability of dry fuel. Fuel is a problem here. Everything is damp. Fire requires preparation—gathering wood that has dried sufficiently, protecting it from the constant moisture, building a hearth on stone that won't crack from heat. Cooking is communal and occasional, a group gathered around a small flame in a sheltered spot, the warmth more valuable than the preparation.

They also eat frogs. Not the dominant species—they've learned which populations can sustain harvest and which cannot—but the abundant ones, the ones that breed so prolifically that taking individuals has no visible impact on the chorus. They take them by hand, by touch, finding them in the pools and channels by feel, by the movement of water displaced by a body, by the slight difference in texture between a stone surface and a living one. They eat them raw, more often than cooked, the preparation too much trouble and the fuel too scarce. The taste is said to be clean, vegetal, derived from the algae and insects the frogs consume.

They eat insects. This surprises visitors but shouldn't. The insects here are enormous in their variety and availability, and the people have developed preferences that are precise and specific. Certain caterpillars, found on particular plants. The larvae of beetles that bore into dead wood. Cicada nymphs, dug from the soil where they wait to emerge. Roasted quickly on a hot stone, eaten with the fingers. Protein is not a problem here. Life is not a problem here. The mountain produces more than enough for the few who live on it.

They eat fruit, when it's available. The trees that grow from the terraces produce, some of them, small fruits suited to the humid dark—fleshy, sweet, often fermented before they fall, the alcohol content enough to notice. The people collect these when they can, eating them immediately, not storing them, because storage requires dryness and nothing here stays dry. They eat mushrooms that grow on dead wood and on the damp surfaces of the terraces. They eat the tender fiddleheads of ferns, the new growth of vines, the leaves of plants that have become food over generations of experimentation that nobody remembers beginning.

They do not eat much. The humidity, the warmth, the low level of physical exertion required for life here—all of these reduce the need. A visitor would find the portions small, the meals infrequent, the general attitude toward food one of casual acquisition rather than pursuit. They eat when they're hungry. They're not often very hungry. The mountain provides.

Let me talk about how they raise children. Because childhood on the mountain is strange.

Children here are born small, grow slowly, and learn by absorption rather than instruction. They are carried for the first years of life, pressed against a parent's body, feeling the warmth and the moisture and the vibration of the parent's voice transmitted through bone and muscle. They hear the mountain before they see it. The frog chorus is their first sound, their first continuous experience. It is the sound of the womb amplified, externalized, made permanent. Some researchers who have visited suggest that the people's ability to process the soundscape is not learned but developed—that the neural pathways for parsing dense sound are laid down before birth, shaped by the vibrations that pass through the mother's body, that the mountain's voice is part of their neural architecture from the beginning.

They learn to navigate by following. A parent walks a path, the child follows, hands on the parent's body or on the wall beside the path. They learn the routes physically, kinesthetically, their bodies memorizing the sequence of movements required to move from one place to another. They don't learn directions, don't learn spatial maps in the abstract sense. They learn sequences of touch—this wall, this turn, this staircase, this channel crossing. A child who knows the path from home to a particular water source knows it as a series of physical actions, not as a route on a map. Ask them to describe the path and they will describe it with their hands, tracing the movements in the air.

They learn the soundscape by immersion. There is no teaching. They simply grow into it, the way a fish grows into water. By the time they can walk, they can identify individual frog species by call. By the time they can climb terraces unassisted, they can detect the shift in the chorus that means rain is approaching from above, or that a particular predator has entered their territory, or that the water flow has changed somewhere in the system. They don't learn these things as information. They learn them the way they learn balance—through falling, through correction, through the body's slow accumulation of experience.

They play in the pools. All children do, everywhere, but here the pools are everywhere too, so play and water are inseparable. They catch frogs, examine them, release them. They swim in the deeper cisterns, their bodies adapted to the water from birth, their movements in the water more fluid than their movements on land. They dare each other to dive to the bottom, to hold their breath longer, to find the channels where water enters and exits and follow them as far as they can. They are comfortable in water in the way that amphibious animals are comfortable, their play a rehearsal for a life that will involve constant contact with moisture.

They are not afraid of the dark. This needs saying because visitors are. The mountain's permanent twilight is oppressive to outsiders, the lack of visual information producing anxiety, the constant sound producing disorientation. Children raised here don't experience this. The dark is not a problem to be solved. It is a condition to be inhabited. They move through it with the ease of fish through water, their hands finding surfaces, their ears parsing the sound, their bodies knowing where they are without needing to see. Darkness, for them, is simply the way the world is, and they don't long for anything else.

Let me talk about what they believe. Though "believe" is the wrong word for something that is so little articulated, so rarely spoken of directly.

They believe the mountain is alive. Not metaphorically. They believe the mountain is alive the way a frog is alive, the way a person is alive, in that it responds to things, changes, behaves with something that could be called intention if intention were broadened to include the kind of slow, vast purpose that a mountain operates on. The water system is the mountain's blood. The vegetation is its skin. The frogs are its voice. The people who live on it are... they have different opinions about what the people are. Some say they are the mountain's dream, a flicker of consciousness that the mountain has generated the way a sleeping brain generates images. Some say they are parasites, living off the mountain's body, tolerated because their presence is too small to matter. Some say they are the mountain's memory, the part of it that can still recall being made, that still carries the knowledge of construction even if it can no longer act on it.

They do not worship the mountain. Worship implies a relationship of supplication, of distance, of the inferior addressing the superior. They don't have that relationship with it. They have the relationship a tick has with a deer, or a vine has with a tree, or an algae has with the stone it grows on. They are part of the mountain's ecology. They belong to it. It does not belong to them. The distinction matters.

They have rituals, but they are not religious rituals in the usual sense. They are maintenance rituals, performed at intervals determined by the water system, by the growth patterns of vegetation, by the population cycles of the frogs. They clear particular channels at particular times, because the channels need clearing and because the act of clearing is important, because doing it together is important, because the work is the prayer, or the work is the belief, or the work is the only form of devotion that the mountain recognizes.

They sing, as I've mentioned. They sing to mark events—to celebrate a birth, to acknowledge a death, to note the clearing of a channel, the finding of a new path. They sing to locate each other, their voices meeting across the mountain like the calls of frogs, each one seeking the other through the mass of sound. They sing to the mountain itself, though this is harder to explain, harder to justify as a practice with a purpose. They sing to it the way you might sing to a sleeping person, not to wake them, not to communicate, but to be present with them, to let your voice exist in the same space as their body, to participate in the simple fact of shared existence.

---

The dead are given to the water. This needs saying carefully because it is not disposal, not abandonment, not the release of something unwanted. The dead are given to the water because the water is the mountain's blood, and to put something into the mountain's blood is to make it part of the mountain. The dead become the soil that feeds the plants that shelter the pools that hold the frogs that feed the living. It is the most literal kind of recycling, the most physical kind of continuation. The people understand this with a clarity that visitors find uncomfortable. They know where the soil comes from. They know what the roots are drawing up. They know, and they are not troubled by it, because the cycle is the thing, the cycle is the point, the cycle is the mountain's answer to the question of what happens to the living when they stop living.

There are no graves. No markers for the dead. The dead don't need marking because they don't go anywhere. They stay here, in the soil, in the water, in the leaves and the frogs and the insects and the people who eat what grows here. The dead are present. They are present in the taste of the tubers, in the clarity of the water, in the particular sound of a frog chorus that contains voices descended from bodies that contained voices descended from bodies that contained voices. The dead don't need memory because the dead are everywhere, are everything, are the medium in which life happens.

The living know this. The living are not distressed by it. The living eat their dead with every meal, drink their dead with every swallow, breathe their dead with every breath of the spore-thick air. They are their own ancestors and their own descendants, cycling through the mountain's body, occupying it the way blood cells occupy a circulatory system—temporarily, functionally, necessarily.

---

I want to describe one more thing. The way they age.

They grow old slowly here. The warmth, the moisture, the low stress, the diet—everything contributes to a longevity that surprises. Bodies don't dry out the way they do elsewhere, skin stays supple, joints stay flexible, the constant physical engagement of navigating the terraces keeps muscles toned. People live a long time, and they live usefully for most of that time, their knowledge of the mountain accumulating with every year, every season, every cycle of clearing and growth and clearing again.

The oldest among them are the navigators, the ones who know the most paths, the most routes, the most ways through the ever-changing vegetation. Their value is not in their strength but in their memory, in the accumulated knowledge of a thousand walks through a thousand configurations of the terraces. They remember paths that no longer exist, routes that have been blocked by growth or collapse, channels that have shifted. When the mountain changes, as it always does, they are the ones who find the new way, because they remember the old ways, and the old ways suggest the new ones.

They die eventually, as everything does. The mountain takes them back. The water carries them into the soil, and the soil feeds the roots, and the roots feed the plants, and the plants feed the living, and the living walk the terraces and sing to the frogs and sleep in the humid dark and wake to the sound of a hundred thousand voices calling, always calling, the endless chorus of the mountain that holds them all.

---

What lives here, then, is an ecology. Not a collection of species, not a set of populations, but a single continuous system that flows through itself, that cycles water and biomass and information through channels carved in stone, through channels carved in living tissue, through channels carved in the neural architecture of the people who read the mountain by touch and sing to it in voices it can't hear. The frogs. The insects. The birds. The snakes. The mammals. The people. The water that connects them all. The stone that holds the water. The builders who are gone. The mountain that remains.

They all live here. They are all the mountain.
