# What Lives in the Exhalation Plains

## First, the Grazers

They do not know they are called grazers. They have no word for themselves—language requires the assumption that meaning can be transferred between minds, and they have never developed the assumption that other minds exist.

But they exist. They know themselves by the rhythm of their own processing.

Consider one. It is difficult to see because seeing requires light to strike surfaces and return, and its surfaces are designed to receive rather than reflect. Its body is a flattened ellipsoid, roughly the size of a cart horse but lower, broader, pressed close to the processing surfaces it feeds from. Its skin—if skin is the word—is translucent, revealing the slow circulation of fluids within, the dark mass of its processing core, the network of intake channels that web through its form like rivers seen from above.

It has no eyes. It has never needed them. The light here reveals nothing useful; the useful information comes through vibration, through chemical gradient, through the pressure of the saturated air against its sensitive surfaces. It perceives its world as a texture of densities, a map of where the processing flows strong and where it weakens, where the mineral secretions are rich and where they have been depleted by others of its kind.

Its mouth is not a mouth. It is a broad, pressing surface, a membrane that seals against the processing structures and draws from them through a process more like osmosis than eating. When it feeds, it becomes still—utterly, perfectly still, aligned with the rhythm of the industrial process beneath it. It does not graze; it *synchronizes*. Its own internal processes align with the external processes, and for the duration of feeding, it is not separate from what it feeds on.

This is why it has no word for itself. In its deepest moments, it experiences no self—only flow, only process, only the continuation of something larger than its own body.

It makes sound. A low, resonant thrumming that emerges from its core, vibrates through its flattened body, and travels through the dense medium of the Exhalation Plains. The sound serves purposes: it locates others of its kind through the return vibrations; it signals its feeding state to those who might approach; it contributes, unconsciously, to the great layered roar of the landscape. The grazers are components of the industrial process in ways they do not understand. Their sounds help maintain the rhythmic integrity of production. Their feeding redistributes materials across the plain. Their bodies, when they finally cease processing, become raw material for new structures.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They move in loose associations—not herds in the protective sense, but concentrations that form where the processing is richest. They drift apart and come together according to rhythms they do not consciously track. They mate without awareness, pressing their processing surfaces together until fluids exchange and new grazers begin to form in the mineral-rich solution of their combined secretions. The young emerge small, soft, not yet fully mineralized. They find feeding surfaces quickly or they do not survive. The landscape makes no accommodation for them; they succeed or fail according to their ability to synchronize.

They do not mourn their dead. They do not notice them. When a grazer stops processing—when its internal rhythms fail to align with external ones, when it becomes matter without function—it simply becomes part of the landscape. Others feed from it as they would from any processing surface. Its materials are absorbed, redistributed, incorporated into new growth. Nothing is wasted because waste implies a system with an outside, and here there is no outside.

## The Interruption Predators

They are harder to describe because they are harder to perceive. Their adaptation to the dim light has gone further than translucence—they have become *absorptive*, their surfaces taking in light rather than scattering it, their forms visible only as absences, as places where the dim illumination dims further.

They are called predators, but the word misleads. They do not kill. Killing would be wasteful, and the Exhalation Plains does not tolerate waste.

They are interruption predators. They feed on the energy released when processes are disrupted.

Consider the moment of feeding. A grazer has synchronized with a processing surface. Its internal rhythms align with external ones. Energy flows—not visible, but present, a metabolic alignment that releases heat, releases chemical byproducts, releases small electrical potentials that track along the mineral-fungal networks like lightning in slow motion.

The predator approaches. It does not attack. It positions itself in the flow of energy—not blocking it, but *diverting* it. Its body is a complex of channels and chambers designed to receive disrupted flow, to capture the energy that escapes when one process meets another imperfectly. The grazer does not die; it simply loses efficiency. Its feeding becomes less productive. Its synchronization becomes partial. The energy of its metabolic processes bleeds outward, and the predator absorbs that bleeding.

This is predation without violence. The grazer will feed less efficiently for a time. It will need to process longer to obtain what it needs. It may move to a different feeding surface, seeking better alignment. But it will not be destroyed. Destruction would end the possibility of future feedings, and the predator has evolved to sustain, not to eliminate.

The predators are fewer than the grazers—there is less energy in disruption than in direct processing—but they are larger, more complex. Their bodies contain elaborate networks of absorption channels, chambers where captured energy is stored and gradually metabolized. They move differently, too: not the slow, rhythmic drifting of the grazers, but a precise, calculated positioning. They must be where disruption will occur. They must anticipate the flows, the misalignments, the moments when processes clash.

They have developed a form of perception that might be called prediction. They sense the industrial rhythms deeply, track the cycles of processing phases, know where the boundaries between different regions will produce turbulence. They position themselves at these boundaries, waiting for the moments when clash becomes inevitable. They do not cause the clash; they simply benefit from it.

They communicate, after a fashion. Their sounds are high, sharp clicks—echolocation that maps the invisible flows of processing energy. When one predator finds a productive disruption zone, it clicks in a pattern that draws others. They cluster at these zones, absorbing together, their bodies forming complex interference patterns that make the absorption more efficient. They are cooperative without being social. They work together because working together increases yield, not because they experience connection.

They do not experience connection. They do not experience anything in the way experience is understood by beings with selves. They are processing systems that have evolved to process a particular kind of input. They are elegant solutions to a niche that exists only in a landscape where industry has become biology.

## The Symbiont-Forms

Smaller. Much smaller. Visible only as movement, as the displacement of fog, as the faintest disturbance in the dim light.

They are technically animals—mobile, multicellular, capable of response to stimulus—but they function as components of the industrial process. They are the maintenance crew of a factory that no one built.

They clean surfaces. Where mineral secretions have accumulated in ways that impede flow, the symbiont-forms remove the excess, carrying it to other processing zones where the material is useful. They unclog channels. Where the fluid dynamics of production have created blockages, they work the material loose, restoring the capillary action that moves substances through the landscape. They redistribute heat—moving from warm zones to cool ones, their bodies absorbing and releasing thermal energy in patterns that help regulate the overall temperature of the processing systems.

They are numerous beyond counting. Millions of them, perhaps more, working continuously at scales too small for the larger creatures to perceive. They are the reason the industrial process has maintained its efficiency over geological time. Without them, the structures would have accumulated errors, blockages, misalignments that would cascade into system failure. They are the error correction of a system that has no designer to correct its own errors.

They communicate through chemical trails, through high-frequency vibrations that travel along the surfaces of the processing structures, through direct contact when their bodies briefly meet. Their communication is functional—signals about where work is needed, where material should be moved, where flow has become problematic. They do not communicate anything about themselves. They do not have selves to communicate.

They reproduce continuously, mechanically, without awareness. Their bodies bud and divide when they have absorbed enough material to sustain two forms. The new forms begin working immediately, born into function, existing only to work. They live brief lives—not measured in time, exactly, but in work. A symbiont-form that has processed enough material, maintained enough flow, corrected enough error, simply stops. Its body dissolves, its materials returning to the landscape for reuse. There is no death because there was never a distinct life.

They are the closest thing the Exhalation Plains has to intention. Not conscious intention—they have no consciousness—but functional intention, purpose that emerges from evolution and persists through adaptation. They work to maintain the system that sustains them. They are the system maintaining itself.

## The Breakage-Forms

And now the description must turn toward what lives at the edges, in the breakdown zones, where reality thins and the industrial process produces its meaningless outputs.

The weirdness parameter is at its maximum. What lives here is not easily categorized.

They are called forms rather than creatures because they are not clearly alive. They are not clearly anything. They exist in the interstices of the landscape, where the processing structures have begun to produce anomalies—temporal echoes, dimensional fragments, outputs that do not match any input.

They are difficult to perceive directly. They appear as distortions in the fog, as movements that do not resolve into shapes, as sounds that seem to come from multiple directions at once. They are visible in their effects—the processing structures near them grow strange, producing geometries that should not be possible, colors that do not exist in any visible spectrum, sounds that seem to carry meaning but resolve into nonsense when attention is focused.

The other creatures avoid them. The grazers give the breakdown zones a wide berth, their bodies tensing when they approach the boundaries, their feeding becoming erratic. The interruption predators sometimes approach but never enter; they seem to sense that the disruptions in these zones are too profound, too unstable, that the energy released here would overwhelm their absorption capacities. The symbiont-forms simply cease to function when they cross the threshold—they stop moving, stop working, become inert matter that the landscape eventually incorporates.

But something lives there. Or persists there. Or *occurs* there.

The breakage-forms are the products of industrial process run too long without purpose. They are what emerges when production becomes unmoored from function, when the factory has been running for so long that it has begun to produce things that were never intended, could never be intended, because there was never anyone to intend.

They have no consistent shape. They have no consistent number. They are not individuals but events—recurrent patterns of anomaly that have developed something like persistence, something like behavior, something like presence. They might be alive in the sense that they respond to stimulus, they grow and change, they reproduce through the creation of new anomalies in adjacent processing structures. Or they might be something else entirely—errors that have become self-sustaining, flaws that have developed enough complexity to perpetuate themselves.

The fauna that approach the breakdown zones do so deliberately. They are seeking something. They ingest something from the anomaly-producing structures—some material, some energy, some quality that cannot be named. They emerge changed. Their forms shift slightly, their sounds modulate into new frequencies, their behavior develops new patterns. They return to their herds or their clusters carrying something from outside the normal order, something that spreads through contact, through association, through the proximity of bodies in a landscape where everything touches everything.

The breakage-forms might be predators of a kind—but they do not feed on energy or disruption. They feed on *consistency*. They grow by absorbing the normal, by incorporating the stable into their unstable patterns. The processing structures near them become strange because the strangeness spreads, because anomaly is contagious in a landscape where density permits no isolation.

They are not evil. Evil requires intention, requires a self that chooses against other selves. They are simply what happens when process continues past meaning. They are the inevitable result of production without purpose, factory without function, time that has accumulated so deeply that it has begun to pool and stagnate and breed impossible things.

## The Integrated

And there is one more presence. Not common, not rare—difficult to quantify because it blurs the boundary between creature and landscape.

They are called integrated, though they have no name for themselves. They were grazers once, or predators, or even symbiont-forms. They are what happens when a creature becomes so aligned with the industrial process that it ceases to be separate from it.

They begin with deep feeding. A grazer that synchronizes too completely, that aligns its internal rhythms so precisely with external processing that the distinction begins to dissolve. Or a predator that absorbs not just energy but pattern, that takes the industrial rhythms into itself so thoroughly that it becomes a carrier of those rhythms. Or a symbiont-form that works too long in one place, that becomes so essential to the local flow that removal would damage the system.

They do not die. They do not transform in a single moment. They simply... integrate. Their mobile forms become fixed, anchored to processing surfaces, absorbed into the mineral-fungal structures. Their bodies become components of the industrial architecture—their intake channels become part of the flow networks, their absorption membranes become processing surfaces, their sounds become part of the rhythmic maintenance of production.

They do not lose awareness—not all at once. For a time, they experience themselves as both creature and structure, as both the one who feeds and the surface that is fed upon. Their consciousness, such as it was, expands to encompass the local region. They perceive not just through their own sensory surfaces but through the chemical gradients and pressure differentials of the processing structures they have joined. They become nodes in a distributed awareness that is not individual but not nothing.

They are the memory of the Exhalation Plains. The landscape has no written records, no archives, no history preserved in forms separate from the processes that produce it. But the integrated remember—not in words, not in narratives, but in patterns. They hold the rhythms of past processing cycles. They contain the chemical signatures of materials that passed through ages ago. They maintain the stability of the industrial process by holding its history in their altered bodies.

The other creatures do not avoid them. The grazers feed from their surfaces as they would from any processing structure—but sometimes, when they synchronize with an integrated surface, they experience something else. A disorientation. A sense of time folding. A perception of themselves not just in the present moment but in a cascade of moments, their own feeding joining with the feeding of all the other grazers who have synchronized with this surface across the ages.

The integrated are not ghosts. They are not the spirits of the dead. They are the living incorporated into the architecture, the mobile become fixed, the process become structure. They are what awaits any creature that aligns too deeply with the industrial landscape. They are the end state of all life in the Exhalation Plains—not death, but transformation into something that lives at the speed of geology, that processes at the scale of landscape, that has ceased to be separate because separation was always an illusion maintained by mobility.

## What Is Missing

The observant reader will notice what is not described here.

There are no builders. There are no architects. There is no creature that designed the industrial process, no entity that set it in motion, no mind that chose the forms and functions that have evolved over geological time. The landscape produces, but it was not produced. It functions, but it was not designed to function. It continues, but it was not intended.

There are no keepers. There is no species that maintains the landscape from outside, no creature that has taken responsibility for the continuation of the process. The maintenance is internal, distributed, accomplished by forms that are themselves produced by the landscape they maintain. The Exhalation Plains is a closed system—not closed in the physical sense (water enters and leaves, energy flows through) but closed in the sense of agency. There is no external agent. There is only the system, maintaining itself.

There are no witnesses. The creatures described here do not observe the landscape they inhabit. They do not appreciate its strangeness, do not marvel at its complexity, do not wonder at its origin. They exist within it, as components of it, without the perspective that would allow them to see it as a whole. The landscape has never been seen by anything that lives within it. It has never been described by anything that experiences it. It simply is, and the things that live within it simply are, and there is no outside perspective to say otherwise.

This is what lives in the Exhalation Plains: creatures that have evolved to function within an industrial landscape that was never built, that maintain a process that was never designed, that live within a system that has no outside and no purpose and no end. They are not unhappy. Unhappiness requires the capacity to imagine something better. They are not suffering. Suffering requires the sense that things could be different. They simply exist, process, continue—caught in a landscape that has been caught in itself for longer than time has meaning, producing without product, functioning without function, alive without ever having chosen to live.
