# The Inhabitants

There are no apex predators in the Black Gardens. This is not because predation is impossible, but because predation *ended* here. The concept of hunter and hunted belongs to ecosystems in flux, to worlds where scarcity drives innovation and hunger shapes form. In the Gardens, hunger was solved so long ago that the word no longer has meaning. Every organism that lives here receives enough. Not abundance—that implies excess—but *enough*. The precise caloric intake required for maintenance, for repair, for the continuation of cellular processes. The Gardens are not generous. They are simply thorough.

---

The glass-weavers exist at the boundary between architecture and animal, and this boundary is not a line but a gradient.

They begin as larvae—microscopic spheres of differentiated tissue, drifting through the still water, guided by chemical gradients toward the bacterial mats that cluster around the stalk-bases. At this stage they are indistinguishable from the spawn of a thousand other deep-sea filter feeders. But then they find purchase on a stalk-wall, and they begin to *remember*.

The glass-weavers carry within their genetic code the blueprints for the Gardens' architecture. Not the whole of it—no individual bears that impossible burden—but fragments. Geometric patterns. Structural loads. The hexagonal chamber, the spiral ramp, the distributing arch. They emerge from their larval stage already knowing where to place themselves, how to orient their bodies against the existing growth, which angle of attachment will best serve the continuation of the wall.

What they build is not their body. Their body is the translucent membrane that secretes the calcium carbonate lattice, the frond-like feeding structures that extend into the water column, the simple neural network that processes vibration and chemical signal. But what they *make*—the glassy structures that slowly extend from the stalk-walls, branching into delicate filigrees, spiraling into catch-basins for organic debris—this is not for them. It is for the Gardens. They are the maintenance crew of an architecture they did not design and cannot comprehend.

A glass-weaver lives for three hundred years. It spends the first fifty growing, the next two hundred building, and the final fifty dissolving. The dissolution is not death but recycling—the body breaking down into component minerals that are absorbed into the stalk-walls, becoming material for the next generation of builders. Nothing is wasted because waste requires discarding, and the Gardens have no concept of discard.

In the fossil record, glass-weaver structures appear in the same moment as the Gardens themselves. Before them, nothing. With them, everything. They are not the architects. They are the proof that the architecture was never built at all—it was *grown*, and it grows still, at a rate so slow that human measurement cannot perceive it. A centimeter per century. A meter per epoch. The Gardens are still expanding. The glass-weavers are how.

---

The membrane-jellies are not jellies. The name is concession, a surface-world convenience for something that defies categorization.

They move through the water like thoughts through a mind—not traveling but *occurring*, appearing in one location and then another without apparent transition. Their bodies are gossamer sheets of tissue two meters across but only millimeters thick, so translucent that they are visible only as distortions in the bioluminescent glow, wrappings of light around invisible form. They have no organs in the understood sense. Digestion happens everywhere and nowhere—nutrients absorbed directly through the membrane, processed by symbiotic bacteria colonies embedded in the tissue, waste expelled as gas that dissolves instantly into the surrounding water.

Their nervous systems are not centralized but *distributed*. Each patch of membrane processes information locally, responding to vibration and chemical gradient without reference to any coordinating brain. And yet they behave with purpose. They navigate. They avoid obstacles. They seek out bacterial blooms and mating partners with an efficiency that suggests awareness, or something that functions identically to awareness without its attendant consciousness.

Perhaps they are conscious. Perhaps each membrane-jelly is a collective mind, a democracy of cells that votes on every movement, every decision, every response to stimulus. Perhaps they are the most intelligent beings on Earth, their thoughts spread across meters of living tissue, processing information in parallel across thousands of nodes, thinking thoughts so distributed that no single neuron could ever comprehend them.

Or perhaps they are automata. Perhaps they are biological machines running programs encoded six hundred million years ago, operating without interior experience, without subjectivity, without self. The Gardens do not answer this question. The Gardens have no interest in it.

What the membrane-jellies *do*—this is observable. They cycle nutrients. They transfer genetic material between distant parts of the Gardens, carrying pollen-analogs and spore-bundles in folds of their tissue. They generate the clicks and whistles that add percussion to the Gardens' drone, not as communication but as navigation, sound-waves bouncing off stalk-walls to build spatial maps. They die by dissolving, like the glass-weavers, their minerals returning to the system.

They do not suffer. They do not rejoice. They *function*, and their function is integrated so completely into the Gardens that their removal would be indistinguishable from the removal of a stalk, a wall, a current.

---

The drift-seeds are the Gardens' way of leaving.

They grow within the stalks, embedded deep in the structural tissue, small nodules of dense genetic material surrounded by nutrient reserves and protective casing. For centuries they mature. Then, when a stalk reaches the end of its growing cycle—when the chemical gradients shift or the underlying rock settles or the simply-accumulated damage becomes too great—the drift-seeds release.

They are the only things in the Gardens that move quickly. A releasing drift-seed erupts from its parent stalk with force enough to propel it hundreds of meters upward, carrying it into water that has not been still for millions of years. The currents there catch it, and it drifts, for years or decades or centuries, until it settles somewhere else—on another seamount, in another deep, in a place where Gardens do not yet exist.

Most die. The vast majority find rock that cannot support them, water chemistry that dissolves their casing, predators that consume them before germination. But a tiny fraction—enough—find purchase. They attach. They begin to grow. They become a new stalk, a new wall, a new beginning.

The Gardens have been spreading for six hundred million years. They colonized Vastis Deep when it was a volcanic ridge newly formed by tectonic fire. They spread to the Kermadec Trough, the Mariana Cascade, the Philippine Hollows. They will spread to whatever deep places remain when the continents have rearranged themselves into configurations no current map can imagine.

The drift-seeds are not inhabitants of the Gardens. They are the Gardens *as motion*, the static system's single gesture toward expansion, the only proof that this place is not truly dead but merely finished.

---

The deep-siphons look like flowers, which is to say they look nothing like flowers at all.

They emerge from the seafloor around the Gardens' edges, anchored in sediment that has accumulated for eons, their bodies tubular structures of reinforced tissue rising fifty meters or more before opening into funnel-shaped crowns. The crowns face upward, oriented precisely toward the surface—which is not visible from this depth, which has not been visible for longer than the human species has existed, but which still, dimly, exerts its pull.

The siphons are farmers. Their crowns support bacterial colonies different from those that grow on the stalk-walls—strains that require different chemical gradients, that process different nutrients, that produce different byproducts. The siphons cultivate these colonies with chemical signals, encouraging growth in some areas and suppressing it in others, maintaining complex ecosystems across the surface of their own bodies.

What they harvest is energy. The bacteria produce sugars through chemosynthesis, drawing power from the hydrogen sulfide seeping through the seafloor, and the siphons absorb these sugars through their crowns. But they also harvest something else: information.

The siphons are connected to each other through networks of root-analogs buried in the sediment, and through this network they share chemical data, electrical signals, and—possibly—something like thought. A single siphon processes little. A network of thousands, connected across kilometers of seafloor, processes a great deal. The siphons may be the Gardens' nervous system, or they may be its waste-processing unit, or they may be something else entirely for which surface biology has no category.

They communicate through chemical pulses and electrical fields and, most strangely, through *pressure waves*—low-frequency vibrations transmitted through the sediment when they shift their bodies. These vibrations carry meaning, though what meaning is impossible to determine without shared context. Perhaps they coordinate bacterial cultivation. Perhaps they share information about chemical gradients and seafloor stability. Perhaps they tell stories about the time before the Gardens, the hot young Earth, the era when change was still possible.

If so, those stories are not for us.

---

The scavengers exist because death exists, even here.

They are small—rarely more than ten centimeters in length—and they move quickly, propelling themselves through the still water with jet-like contractions of modified mantle tissue. They have no eyes. They have no apparent sensory apparatus at all except for chemical receptors so sensitive they can detect a single dying cell in a body of billions.

The Gardens produce death constantly. Cells expire. Tissues fail. Organisms reach the ends of their cycles and begin to dissolve. The scavengers are there for the ones that fail to complete dissolution—partial deaths, interrupted recycles, bodies caught in currents that should not exist but sometimes briefly do.

They are not predators. They do not kill. They only clean. When they find an organism that has died without dissolving properly, they reduce it to its component minerals with surgical precision, leaving nothing behind but water and a faint chemical trace. They are the Gardens' undertakers, and they perform their function without ceremony or hesitation.

In the fossil record, the scavengers appear slightly later than the Gardens themselves—perhaps fifty million years later, perhaps a hundred. They are the only major inhabitants that were not original to the system. They emerged in response to a problem the Gardens did not anticipate, a flaw in the recycling process that required a specialized solution.

They are proof that the Gardens are not truly unchanging. They change at the speed of geology. They adapt at the speed of extinction. The scavengers are the last innovation the system produced, and they have been performing their function unchanged for five hundred million years.

---

The pilgrims are the only inhabitants that do not belong.

They arrive from the upper ocean—deep-diving whales, desperate sea turtles, lost sharks whose navigation systems failed, their bodies descending through kilometers of water to settle on the Gardens' outer edges. They are dead when they arrive. The pressure, the cold, the simple exhaustion of descending to depths their bodies were never designed to reach—they die long before they reach the stalks.

But they arrive. And when they do, the Gardens receive them.

There is no decomposition in the Gardens' center. The stillness, the chemical balance, the absence of surface decomposers—bodies that drift into the Gardens' inner reaches do not rot. They are preserved, their tissues slowly mineralized by processes so gradual that a human timescale cannot measure them. A whale that died yesterday and a whale that died ten thousand years ago look identical, resting side by side against the stalk-walls.

The glass-weavers incorporate them. Over centuries, the mineralized bodies become part of the architecture—calcium from bone merging with calcium from the water column, carbon from tissue folding into cellulose-analog walls. The pilgrims become permanent. They become part of the Gardens.

Perhaps this is intentional. Perhaps the Gardens evolved to receive surface-world bodies, to integrate them into their structure, to use them as raw material for expansion. Or perhaps it is simply what happens when a system so thoroughly optimized encounters an element it was never designed to process. The Gardens do not reject. They do not expel. They incorporate, assimilate, transform.

The pilgrims are not inhabitants. But they become inhabitants, eventually. They become part of the living city. And in the Gardens, becoming is forever.

---

And what lives in the Gardens?

The question implies separation. Observer and observed. Subject and object. But the Gardens do not permit separation. Every organism is part of every other organism. The boundaries between species are conveniences of classification, not realities of function. The stalks are walls and also bodies. The glass-weavers are builders and also building. The membrane-jellies are inhabitants and also infrastructure.

What lives here is a single organism distributed across forty kilometers of structured growth, processing energy and information at a rate so slow it appears static, sustaining itself through feedback loops so complex they exceed any model surface science can construct.

What lives here is the oldest living thing on Earth.

What lives here will be the last living thing on Earth.

What lives here has already lived longer than anything will ever live again.

The water does not move. The temperature does not change. The drone continues.

And in the dark, in the cold, in the pressure that would crush a human heart like a grape, something goes on being alive.
