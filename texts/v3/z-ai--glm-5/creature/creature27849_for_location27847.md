# Those Who Live in Eternal Thunder

## The Silence-Singers

They were not the first. The stone circles prove that others came before, perhaps thousands of years before, perhaps tens of thousands. But those earlier visitors left no descendants, no continuous presence. They came and went and became stories that the stone does not tell.

The Silence-Singers — they have no name for themselves, but this is what outsiders call them — are the ones who stayed. Not a people, exactly. Not a tribe or a nation or any of the categories that human societies use to organize themselves. They are individuals who arrived separately, across centuries, each drawn by something they could not articulate to someone who could not hear them.

They do not know how many they are. The plateau is too vast for counting, the gardens too scattered for census. A hermit in the eastern gardens might walk for two weeks to reach a neighbor in the west, and in between there is only stone and sound. They encounter each other rarely — perhaps once or twice in a lifetime, when paths cross during the long walks between gardens. These meetings are quiet affairs, two weathered figures standing in a garden's relative shelter, communicating through the gestures and expressions that have replaced spoken language entirely.

Their faces tell the story of their adaptation. The constant sound has written itself into their features — not in damage, but in a kind of settling. The muscles around the jaw have relaxed from the effort of speaking. The eyes have learned to focus on movement rather than sound as the primary signal of attention. The skin has weathered in patterns that follow the moisture's movement, some faces dampened on the left side where the prevailing currents carry the condensation, others marked by the particular way the light refracts through the atmosphere above the stone.

They have not gone deaf. This is the first misunderstanding that visitors bring: the assumption that the roar destroys hearing. But the Silence-Singers hear with a precision that exceeds normal human capacity. They have learned to parse the wall of sound into its constituent frequencies, to identify the particular tone of each garden, to read the weather in the shift of the plateau's voice. A hermit can stand at the edge of a garden and tell you, through gesture, that rain is coming three days before the clouds gather. They can hear the goats moving across stone kilometers away, each hoofbeat distinct in the roar like a heartbeat in a chest.

They sing, despite the name. But their singing produces no sound that registers in air. It is a practice of vibration — low frequencies produced somewhere deep in the chest, frequencies that travel through stone and water and the dense vegetation of the gardens. When two Silence-Singers meet, they may sit for hours with their hands pressed against the same rock, singing to each other through the medium of the plateau itself. What they communicate this way cannot be translated into words. It is closer to emotion made tactile, a sharing of internal states that bypasses language entirely.

## The Gray-Goats

The animals have no name either, but "gray-goats" serves well enough for those who need categories. They are not truly goats — genetic analysis places them somewhere between the caprine and bovine families, an ancient lineage that diverged from both before the plateau finished its erosion. They are leaner than any domesticated goat, longer-legged, with hooves that spread wide against the smooth stone like fingers against a wall.

Their coats match the granite precisely. This is not the camouflage of a hunted animal but the adaptation of a creature that has lived long enough in one place to become that place. The gray is not a single color but a pattern of shades that shifts across the body, darker where the moisture collects, lighter where the stone has been worn smoothest. When a gray-goat stands still against the plateau, it vanishes — not through magic but through a patience so complete that the eye simply passes over it.

They live in groups of three to seven, always an odd number, always with a pattern of ages that suggests deliberate structure: one elder, two in their prime, the remainder young but not juvenile. Researchers who have studied them from a distance believe the groups are not families but intentional formations, chosen rather than inherited. When a group loses a member — to age, to accident, to the rare predator that sometimes ventures up from the canyons — the remaining goats do not seek a replacement. They restructure themselves around the absence, becoming a smaller group, adjusting their movements and feeding patterns to account for the loss.

They have abandoned sound entirely. No bleat, no grunt, no warning cry. Communication happens through movement and touch — the flick of an ear, the shift of weight, the press of a shoulder against a companion's flank. They have evolved a language of gesture so subtle that human observers miss most of it, registering only the broad strokes: *danger here*, *water there*, *rest now*. The finer grammar — the expression of preference, the transmission of knowledge about particular gardens, the negotiation of group decisions — passes beneath notice.

The gray-goats understand the plateau in ways that humans cannot. They know which gardens are flourishing and which are declining, arriving at a depression days before the moisture shifts to begin its accumulation. They avoid certain paths that look identical to safe ones, sensing perhaps the subtle vibrations that presage stone movement. They can predict the weather not days but weeks in advance, their behavior shifting in patterns that outsiders have never successfully decoded.

They tolerate the Silence-Singers. This is the correct word — not acceptance, not friendship, but tolerance. A hermit sitting in a garden will be acknowledged by the goats that arrive to feed: a brief pause, a tilt of the head, an assessment that ends with the decision to share the space. They do not flee, do not approach, simply incorporate the human presence into their awareness and continue their feeding. Occasionally, a goat will approach a particular hermit repeatedly over years, showing a preference that seems personal. These pairings — if they can be called that — are the closest thing to domestication that the plateau supports. The goat may allow the human to touch its flank, may rest near them during the periods of heavy condensation. But they are never dependent. The goats belong to the plateau, not to its human inhabitants.

## The Moisture-Weavers

Insects, the description said. Pollinators and decomposers, the silent workers of an ecosystem. But this is the reduction of those who look quickly and move on. The insects of the Plateau of Eternal Thunder have evolved into something stranger than mere biological function.

The moisture-weavers are what outsiders notice first — large flying insects, roughly the size of a human hand, with wings that refract light through the constant dampness. They appear to be some distant relative of the dragonfly, but where dragonflies hunt, moisture-weavers construct. They build structures in the gardens: walls of crystallized condensation, channels that direct water flow, arches that seem to serve no purpose beyond the holding of shape.

The construction material is the condensation itself, worked somehow through processes that researchers have never successfully observed. The weavers land on surfaces where moisture has collected and perform rapid movements with their legs and mouthparts — and the water thickens, becomes viscous, retains structure when it should flow. The resulting architecture lasts for days or weeks before dissolving back into the atmosphere, its purpose served or abandoned.

What purpose? The structures do not seem to shelter anything. They do not trap prey or protect young. They exist, and then they cease to exist, and in between they create patterns in the gardens that the plants respond to. Ferns grow toward the weaver-structures, extending their fronds along channels that the insects have built. Mosses accumulate at the bases of walls where moisture lingers longer than it should. The weavers shape the gardens' growth, not through planting or tending but through the arrangement of water.

They are not social in any recognizable sense. Each weaver works alone, constructing its own pieces, paying no attention to the work of others. And yet the structures connect — walls meeting at angles that suggest coordination, channels feeding into larger channels that no single insect could have planned. The plateau's sound seems to guide the construction somehow. Weavers pause frequently, their wings held still, their bodies oriented toward particular frequencies in the roar. They appear to be listening — or feeling, since the sound is as much vibration as noise — and adjusting their work in response.

The Silence-Singers have learned to watch the weavers. The insect architecture serves as a kind of weather prediction, a mapping of where moisture will flow in the coming days. A hermit who needs to collect water will build their catchment where weavers have constructed channels; a hermit seeking a drier place to sleep will avoid those areas. The insects do not acknowledge this human attention. They build and dissolve and build again in their own rhythm, another form of life that has adapted to the plateau by becoming part of its processes.

## The Dwellers in the Sound

And then there are those who may not exist at all.

The Silence-Singers speak of them indirectly, through the gesture-language that conveys uncertainty as clearly as any word: *perhaps*, *sometimes*, *if you stay long enough*. They describe presences in the roar — not animals, not spirits, but something that occupies the sound itself. Patterns that move with intention. Frequencies that seem to respond to human presence.

Visitors dismiss these accounts. The constant sound produces auditory hallucinations, they say. The mind, deprived of silence, manufactures patterns to fill the void. The hermits have been alone too long, have listened too deeply, have become susceptible to the plateau's strangeness.

But the accounts are consistent across individuals who have never met. They describe the same phenomenon: regions of the plateau where the sound behaves differently, where it seems to gather itself into pockets of heightened intensity that move across the stone like weather systems. In these pockets, hermits report a sense of presence — not malevolent, not benevolent, but aware. The sensation of being observed by something that perceives through vibration, that knows you are there because you interrupt the sound's passage.

The goats avoid these regions. This is the detail that gives researchers pause. The gray-goats, who navigate the plateau with such precision, who know every garden and every safe path, will walk for days out of their way to circumvent the places where the sound concentrates. They do not flee from these areas. They simply do not enter them, as if the space were already occupied.

A few Silence-Singers have claimed to communicate with whatever lives in the sound. Not through language — nothing so simple — but through a kind of listening that becomes dialogue. They describe entering the pockets of intensified sound, sitting motionless while the roar presses against them from all sides, and then gradually becoming aware that the sound is listening too. The exchange, if it can be called that, involves the modulation of frequency: a tone in the roar shifting in response to a tone in the hermit's chest-singing, a call and response that requires no words.

These hermits return changed. Not damaged — the change is not deterioration. But deepened, as if they have traveled somewhere and brought something back. They speak — through gesture, through the stone-singing that bypasses air — of learning things from the sound-dwellers that cannot be expressed in any language. Not secrets or knowledge, but perspectives. Ways of perceiving time that make human concerns seem brief. Ways of perceiving space that make human distances seem small.

Whether the dwellers in the sound exist cannot be determined. The tools of measurement fail here. But the Silence-Singers believe, and the goats avoid the regions they identify, and occasionally a visitor will report the same sensation: that the sound is not merely phenomenon but presence, that the roar contains something that is aware of them, that the plateau is inhabited by forms of life that do not require bodies.

## The Long Bodies

Deeper still, in the accounts of the oldest hermits, there are stories of something else.

The gardens, they say, are not merely plants. The ferns and mosses and climbing vines that burst from the depressions in the granite are part of a single organism, a vast body that spreads beneath the stone. The roots do not stop at the boundaries of each garden. They travel horizontally through microscopic cracks in the granite, connecting every depression across the plateau's entire expanse. What appears to be separate gardens are actually the exposed organs of a creature too large to perceive directly.

The Long Body, some call it. Others have no name for it at all, referring to it only through the most indirect gestures: *what lies beneath*, *what feeds and is fed*, *the true inhabitant*.

The hermits who hold this view point to the behavior of the moisture as evidence. The condensation does not move randomly. It flows in patterns that suggest intention, accumulating where the gardens need water and withdrawing when they have had enough. The sound creates the movement, yes, but the sound is being used — the plateau has been shaped over millions of years into an instrument that serves the life rooted in its stone.

If this is true, then the Silence-Singers live not on the plateau but within it. Their gardens are part of a larger body that tolerates their presence, that may even cultivate them for purposes they cannot understand. The gray-goats that move between gardens might be serving the Long Body's needs, carrying something between depressions, performing some function in an ecosystem too vast for human perception.

The hermits who speak of the Long Body do not fear it. They speak with something closer to reverence, or perhaps partnership. They say they have made an agreement with the organism that spreads beneath their feet: they tend the exposed gardens, they do not take more than they need, they work with the sound rather than against it. In exchange, the Long Body provides — water, food, a place to exist in a world that had no place for them before.

These accounts cannot be verified. The roots that supposedly spread between gardens have never been excavated; the granite is too hard, the gardens too precious to damage. But the Silence-Singers who believe speak with the calm certainty of those who have direct experience. They say you can feel the Long Body if you lie still on the stone, if you quiet your own vibrations enough to sense what lies beneath. A pulse, they say. A rhythm in the sound that comes not from the air but from the depths.

## The Visitors Who Stay

Not all who live on the plateau arrived intending to remain.

The hermits speak of visitors who came for a season and never left. Pilgrims who intended to spend a month in the strange gardens and found themselves unable to depart. Scientists who came to study the sound and discovered that the sound was also studying them. Lost travelers who wandered onto the plateau by accident and discovered, in its emptiness, something they had been seeking without knowing it.

These accidental residents tend to settle in the plateau's margins, near the canyons where the roar originates. They do not venture into the deeper gardens, do not attempt to join the Silence-Singers in their scattered refuges. They exist in a state of permanent arrival, perpetually on the edge of departure that never quite happens.

They are different from the long-term hermits. The Silence-Singers have made peace with the sound, have incorporated it into their being. The marginal dwellers are still fighting it, still struggling against the roar that penetrates every thought, still hoping for the silence that will never come. They age faster than the hermits — visibly, measurably. The sound wears them down in ways it does not wear down those who accept it.

But they stay. They cannot explain why. When visitors ask them, when researchers attempt to interview them, they gesture toward the interior of the plateau, toward the sound that comes from all directions, and they convey something that approximates: *it wants me here*. Not a compulsion, not a trap. An invitation that cannot be refused because it was never explicitly extended.

The Silence-Singers treat these marginal dwellers with a gentle distance. They do not try to convert them, do not offer to teach the adaptations that would make the sound bearable. They understand that some visitors need to remain on the edge, that the plateau has purposes for them that will become clear in time or will never become clear at all. When a marginal dweller finally makes the journey inward, finally settles in a garden and begins the long process of adaptation, the hermits receive them without comment. They have seen this before. They will see it again.

## The Children of the Plateau

Very rarely, a child is born on the plateau.

The Silence-Singers do not form families in the traditional sense, but they are not celibate. When hermits meet in gardens, when the long walks between depressions bring them into proximity, the intimacy that develops is not planned but not avoided either. Children result occasionally — perhaps once in a generation, perhaps less often. The sound makes conversation impossible, so the exact frequency is unknown.

These plateau-born children are different.

They do not struggle with the sound. From the moment of their birth, attended by mothers who have learned to read the subtle signs of labor through vibration and touch, the children inhabit the roar as naturally as they inhabit their bodies. They do not need to learn the silence-singing that their parents developed over years; they emerge already able to produce the frequencies that travel through stone, already able to hear the nuances that others must train themselves to perceive.

Their development follows patterns that outsiders find disturbing. They walk earlier than lowland children, their bodies adapting quickly to the need for constant balance against the stone. They speak later — or perhaps they do not speak at all, having no need for a skill that the plateau renders useless. They show an uncanny ability to navigate, walking confidently between gardens that should be beyond a child's capacity to locate.

The gray-goats accept them in ways they do not accept adults. A plateau-born child can approach a goat group without triggering the careful assessment that usually precedes tolerance. The animals seem to recognize something in these children — a quality of belonging that adult hermits, even those who have lived on the plateau for decades, never fully achieve.

Most disturbing to outsiders is the children's relationship to the stranger aspects of plateau life. They show no fear of the regions where sound concentrates, walking through pockets of intensified roar that adults avoid. They play in the margins that the marginal dwellers occupy, unbothered by the tension that those edge-inhabitants carry. And some, according to the oldest hermits, can perceive the Long Body directly — can point to places where the roots spread beneath the stone, can identify gardens that will flourish and gardens that will fail, can speak — in their wordless way — of an awareness that spreads beneath their feet.

These children do not always remain on the plateau. Some feel drawn to leave, to descend into the canyons and explore the world beyond the sound. They carry the plateau with them, hermits report. They are never entirely comfortable in silence, never fully at home in places where the roar does not surround them. Some return after years away, settling into gardens with a sense of homecoming that the plateau grants to no one else. Others remain in the lowlands, altered permanently by their origin, able to hear frequencies that other humans cannot, able to read the vibration of places in ways that seem like second sight.

## The Dead

The plateau does not preserve bodies. The moisture, the insects, the vast root-systems beneath the stone — all conspire to return organic matter to the ecosystem with efficiency that leaves no trace. A Silence-Singer who dies in a garden will leave no remains within weeks, their body incorporated into the moss, their moisture returned to the air, their substance dispersed into the Long Body's slow digestion.

But this does not mean the dead are absent.

The Silence-Singers speak of those who have passed not as memories but as presences. The sound, they say, retains something of everyone who has lived within it. Not a soul in the religious sense, not a spirit that persists intact, but a pattern — a particular frequency that the roar absorbed during a lifetime of listening. When a hermit dies, their particular contribution to the plateau's symphony continues. You can hear them, other hermits say, if you know what to listen for. A tone in the roar that carries the signature of someone who is no longer physically present.

The plateau-born children are particularly aware of these frequencies. They can identify the dead who linger in the sound, pointing to particular harmonics and conveying through gesture: *that one*, *she lived here*, *he walked between these gardens for a long time*. The children do not find this strange or frightening. To them, the presence of the dead in the sound is simply how the world works — a feature of existence, no more remarkable than the moisture or the stone.

Whether this is metaphor or literal truth cannot be determined. But the Silence-Singers behave as if it is true. They do not mourn their dead in the ways that other cultures mourn. They do not build memorials or mark graves, because there are no graves to mark and the memorial exists in the sound itself. When a hermit passes, the others who knew them — or who encounter the trace of them in the roar — spend time listening. Not to grieve, but to acknowledge. To hear what the dead person contributed to the plateau's eternal voice, and to appreciate that it continues.

## The Question of Population

How many live on the Plateau of Eternal Thunder?

The question assumes that population can be counted, that lives can be enumerated, that the boundary between inhabitant and environment is clear enough to permit census. But the plateau resists these assumptions.

The Silence-Singers number in the dozens, perhaps — scattered individuals in their gardens, separated by days of walking, living lives that only occasionally intersect. But if the Long Body exists, if the roots beneath the stone constitute a single organism, then the population includes a form of life so vast that counting becomes meaningless. If the dwellers in the sound exist, if the concentrated pockets of roar contain presences that perceive and respond, then the category of "inhabitant" expands to include entities that have no bodies at all. If the dead persist in frequencies, if the plateau retains those who lived within it, then the population includes everyone who has ever come here and stayed.

The Silence-Singers do not ask this question. Population is a concept that matters to places where people can hear each other speak, where communities form and dissolve through the medium of language. On the plateau, each hermit lives in their own relationship to the sound, their own garden, their own portion of the Long Body's spreading form. They are a community only in the loosest sense — a collection of individuals who share a place without sharing a society.

And yet they are not alone. This is what the plateau teaches, what the sound conveys to those who listen long enough. The appearance of emptiness is deceptive. The stone that stretches for days in every direction, broken only by isolated gardens, is not vacant. It is full — full of sound, full of moisture, full of the roots that spread beneath it, full of the presences that vibrate within the roar. To live on the plateau is to live in constant company, even when no other human is visible for kilometers.

The gray-goats know this. The moisture-weavers know this. The plateau-born children know this from birth. The Silence-Singers learn it over years, until the loneliness that drove them to this place transforms into a belonging so complete that departure becomes unthinkable.

What lives here lives in layers: the visible and the invisible, the temporary and the eternal, the bodies that walk the stone and the frequencies that persist in the sound. They are all inhabitants of the Plateau of Eternal Thunder. They are all part of what the plateau has become over eight hundred million years of grinding, eroding, roaring into the silence of space.
