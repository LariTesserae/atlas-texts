# The Inhabitants of the Apical Cistern

## The Raft

There is a creature in the Apical Cistern that has never been given a name, because naming implies a category, and the Raft exists outside of categories. It is not singular. It is not plural. It is, simply, the Raft—a presence that occupies the upper region of the water column, fifteen to twenty meters below the ceiling, where the light is brightest and the particulate matter has not yet begun its descent.

The Raft is a collective organism, or perhaps an ecosystem in miniature, or possibly a single vast entity that has learned to present itself as many. It consists of interconnected nodules—gelatinous spheres ranging from the size of a fist to the size of a human head—linked by translucent threads that carry nutrients, chemical signals, and something that might be a form of bioelectrical communication. The nodules pulse. Each has its own rhythm, its own cycle of expansion and contraction, but the rhythms synchronize when the Raft is undisturbed, creating a wave of motion that passes through the entire structure like breath through a lung.

The Raft does not swim. It maintains its position through a combination of buoyancy regulation and graceful acceptance of the water currents. When the circulation cycles shift, the Raft moves with them—rotating slowly, drifting laterally, adjusting its position in the water column with the smallest possible expenditure of energy. It has learned the Cistern's rhythms. It knows, without conscious thought, when the intake valves will open and when they will close. It rises and falls with the water level as if the chamber itself were breathing around it.

The bioluminescence—the colors that ripple across the nodules' surfaces in patterns that seem too organized to be random—serves purposes that the Raft may no longer remember. In some forgotten era, perhaps, the light displays communicated warnings, or attracted mates, or confused predators that no longer exist. Now they simply continue, a behavior maintained long after its original function has been lost. The patterns cycle through the same sequences with minor variations—blue to violet to pale gold to white, then back to blue, in loops that span hours and never quite repeat.

The Raft feeds on the particulate snow that drifts down from above—organic debris, dead microorganisms, the constant rain of biological material that falls from the upper water column. The nodules filter this matter through porous surfaces, extracting nutrients with efficiency that suggests long refinement. Nothing is wasted. What the Raft cannot use is packaged into dense pellets and released to sink toward the floor, where other organisms will find it. The Cistern has no concept of waste. Everything becomes food for something else.

The Raft is old. Not in the sense of individual lifespan, though the nodules may live for decades before being replaced by new growth—it is old in the way that lineages are old. The Raft has existed in the Cistern for as long as there has been a Cistern to exist in. Its ancestors colonized this space when the machinery was new, when the engineers still walked the catwalks and checked the gauges and made notes on clipboards. It remembers, in its diffuse and distributed way, what it was like to be smaller, to be scattered, to be competing for space in a chamber that had not yet been claimed. Now the Raft simply is. It occupies. It persists.

It has no predators. The smaller fish that school beneath it do not feed on the nodules—the surface texture is unappealing, the chemical defenses are effective, the energetic return on investment would be poor. The larger predators—those flattened, eyeless hunters that cruise the middle depths—ignore the Raft entirely. It is not worth their attention. It moves too slowly to trigger prey responses. It offers too little nutrition to justify the pursuit. The Raft exists outside the food web, a presence that occupies space without participating in the violent exchanges that characterize life in the lower depths. It has achieved a kind of peace. It is uncertain whether this peace is enviable or merely stagnant.

The Raft sings. This is the wrong word—the sound it produces is not song in any human sense—but it serves as well as any other. The nodules contain gas bladders that can be inflated and deflated at will, and the air movement through connecting channels produces tones. The tones are low, felt more than heard, vibration in the water rather than sound in the ear. They shift across frequencies in patterns that suggest meaning, that suggest communication, that suggest something being said to an audience that may or may not exist. The Raft does not know if it is speaking. It has forgotten whether it expects an answer.

The singing changes with the light cycles. During the bright phases, the tones are higher, more frequent, suggesting activity or alertness. During the dim phases, the singing slows, deepens, becomes a drone that matches the thrum of the hidden machinery. The Raft has synchronized itself with the industrial rhythm. It does not question whether this synchronization serves a purpose. Purposes are for creatures that have not yet finished becoming what they will be. The Raft has been what it is for longer than memory. It is complete. It continues.

## The Stewards

There are creatures in the Apical Cistern that seem to have been designed for a function, though the function has long since become obsolete. They are called Stewards, though not by themselves—they have no language, no concept of naming, no need for categories. The name is applied by visitors, by observers, by those who find it significant that these creatures engage in behaviors that look, from a distance, like maintenance.

The Stewards are the largest mobile animals in the Cistern. An adult can reach two meters in length, though most hover around a meter and a half. Their bodies are flattened horizontally rather than vertically—broad, diamond-shaped forms that spread like blankets in the water, with irregular edges that suggest the erosion of time. They are not streamlined. They are not built for speed. They are built for patience, for hovering, for the slow accumulation of momentum that carries them through the water column with minimal effort.

Their coloration shifts across the teal-to-gold spectrum depending on their position in the chamber. Near the vegetation, they darken, matching the blue-green of the fronds. Near the ceiling, they pale to almost white, reflecting the industrial light. Near the floor, they become dusty gold, blending with the sediment. This coloration is not camouflage in the active sense—the Stewards do not change color rapidly in response to threats. It is a slow adaptation, a physiological response to environmental conditions that occurs over days or weeks. A Steward that has spent months near the vegetation will retain its teal coloration even if it moves to a different region of the Cistern. It is not hiding. It is simply existing in a particular way, and its body reflects that existence.

The Stewards navigate without eyes. They have never had eyes, not in any evolutionary history that can be reconstructed. The sensory organs clustered around their snouts—a series of raised nodes and ridged surfaces that look almost like a mask pressed into the flesh—provide them with a three-dimensional map of their environment that is more detailed than vision would offer. They sense electrical fields, detecting the muscle contractions of prey and the movement of water through the intake valves. They sense pressure changes, knowing when a current is approaching from behind, when another body is moving through their territory, when the water level is shifting. They sense chemical signatures, reading the water for information about food sources, about potential mates, about the health of the ecosystem around them. They know the Cistern in a way that no seeing creature could. They know its hidden currents, its temperature gradients, its secret pockets of warmth where the machinery runs hottest. They know the vegetation not by its appearance but by its electrical signature, by the chemical traces it leaves in the water, by the way it alters the current patterns as the fronds shift and settle.

The Stewards do not hunt in the conventional sense. They do not pursue prey across distances, do not engage in chases or struggles. They hover. They wait. They position themselves in the water column with precise attention to the currents, to the movement patterns of the smaller fish, to the rhythm of the Cistern's cycles. And when prey comes within range—when a silver-flashing shape moves close enough to be taken—they accelerate. The burst is sudden, powerful, driven by muscular contractions that turn the broad body into a blade cutting through water. The strike is over in seconds. The prey is swallowed whole. The Steward returns to its hover, to its patient waiting, to its slow navigation of currents that bring food to it rather than requiring it to chase.

The Stewards are not numerous. The Cistern can support perhaps twenty to thirty adults at any given time, along with a fluctuating population of juveniles. They maintain territories—not through aggression, but through simple presence. Each Steward claims a region of the water column, hovering there, moving there, feeding there, until other Stewards learn to avoid that space. The territories shift slowly over time, adjusting to changes in the vegetation, in the current patterns, in the distribution of food sources. There is no conflict. Conflict would waste energy. The Stewards have learned that patience is more efficient than competition. They occupy their spaces, and they allow other Stewards to occupy theirs, and the boundaries between territories are maintained through absence rather than assertion.

They communicate through electrical signals. The nodes around their snouts can produce brief pulses—voltage differentials that carry through the water and can be detected by other Stewards at range. The signals are simple. *Here. This space is claimed. Go elsewhere.* They do not signal more than this. They have no need to express affection or anger or fear. The Stewards do not experience these emotions in forms that would be recognizable. They experience something closer to location, something closer to satiation, something closer to the absence of wrongness. When all is well—when the water temperature is correct, when the food supply is adequate, when the territory is secure and the cycles are proceeding as expected—a Steward experiences a state that might be called contentment, though even that word implies more complexity than the reality. The Stewards are not happy. They are simply untroubled.

They are not solitary, but neither are they social. They tolerate the presence of other Stewards at distances that vary with the individual, coming closer during certain phases of the circulation cycle, separating during others. They do not form bonds. They mate without courtship, the females releasing chemical markers that attract males, the union brief and functional, the fertilized eggs deposited in protected crevices where they will develop without parental attention. The young, when they emerge, are miniature versions of the adults—already equipped with the sensory nodes, already capable of navigation, already possessed of the instincts that will carry them through their decades of patient hovering. They find their own territories. They learn their own rhythms. They become Stewards, continuing the pattern that has existed for as long as the Cistern has held water.

The Stewards have never seen the sky. They have never known weather, never experienced temperature change that was not part of a predictable cycle, never encountered a predator that could truly threaten them. They are the apex predators of their world, but they do not know this. The concept of apex, the concept of predator, the concept of threat—these require contrast, require an understanding of alternative states. The Stewards have no such understanding. They live in the Cistern. They hover. They wait. They eat when food comes. They reproduce when the cycles indicate. They die when their bodies fail, sinking toward the floor where the decomposers will find them, where their nutrients will enter the system again, where they will become the particulate matter that feeds the vegetation and the Raft and the next generation of silver-flashing prey. They are part of the system. They do not know they are part of a system. They simply are.

## The Silver Swarms

The small fish that school through the Apical Cistern are the most numerous of the visible inhabitants. They are the prey base, the foundation of the food web, the reason the Stewards can afford to wait. They are also, in their way, the most active and alive presence in the chamber—the quick flash of silver that catches the industrial light, the sudden coordinated movements that suggest intelligence without individual thought.

A single fish is unremarkable. Ten centimeters long, streamlined for rapid acceleration, equipped with visible eyes that function in the diffused light, their scales reflect the ceiling illumination in patterns that shift as they move—silver now, pale gold now, almost white when they turn at certain angles. They are built for speed, for escape, for the desperate burst of energy that carries them away from danger and into safety. They are built, in other words, to be eaten. They are built to survive long enough to reproduce, to contribute their genes to the next generation, to continue a lineage that has persisted in this chamber for longer than the Stewards, longer than the Raft, longer perhaps than the vegetation itself.

The individual fish lives a short life—perhaps two or three years if it avoids predation, though few avoid predation for that long. It spends its time in motion, not because motion is required, but because motion is what it is built for. The nervous system of a silver fish is tuned for rapid response, for the detection of threats, for the execution of evasive maneuvers. It cannot hover. It cannot wait. It must move, must feed, must school with its fellows, must respond to stimuli with bursts of speed that exhaust it and send it searching for food again. It lives in a state of constant alertness that might look, from the outside, like anxiety. But anxiety requires an understanding of what might happen. The silver fish do not imagine futures. They simply respond to presents.

The school is where the silver fish exist as something more than individuals. The school has properties that no single fish possesses—coordination, response time, the ability to shift direction as a single entity as if guided by a mind distributed across hundreds of bodies. The fish do not communicate through language. They do not need to. They sense the movements of their neighbors through pressure waves, through chemical signals, through the simple visual awareness of position and speed. When one fish shifts, the others shift. When one accelerates, the others accelerate. The school turns, and every fish in it turns at the same moment, at the same angle, arriving at the same new heading without discussion or hesitation.

This coordination is not conscious. The fish do not decide to school. They are compelled to school, drawn into proximity by forces older than thought, by evolutionary pressures that made the individual fish vulnerable and the school strong. A single fish, alone, will be eaten within hours—its movements predictable, its escape routes limited, its ability to detect threats reduced. A school, swimming together, is a confusion of targets, a shifting mass of silver that the Stewards and other predators cannot track effectively. The fish at the center of the school are protected by the fish at the edges, and the fish at the edges rotate position constantly, no individual remaining exposed for long. The school is a survival strategy, an emergent property of thousands of years of selection. It is not chosen. It simply is.

The silver fish school according to rhythms that have become instinct. They cluster near the intake valves during high-flow phases, when the fresh water brings organic particulates that form their primary food source. They disperse during low-flow phases, spreading through the water column, feeding on vegetation growth and the debris that drifts down from the Raft above. They sleep, after a fashion—entering states of reduced activity during the dim phases, hovering near the vegetation where the cover provides some protection, their awareness narrowed but not absent. They do not sleep deeply. Nothing in the Cistern sleeps deeply. Sleep is a vulnerability that evolution has not permitted to become fixed.

They reproduce in cycles, releasing eggs and sperm into the water column in coordinated spawning events that coincide with the circulation rhythms. The eggs drift, fertilized, settling into crevices and attachment points throughout the chamber. The vast majority will not survive. They will be eaten by crustaceans, consumed by filter feeders, swept into intake valves and carried away from the Cistern entirely. But enough survive to maintain the population—enough to replace the adults that die, enough to feed the predators that depend on them, enough to continue the endless cycle of birth and consumption that defines life in this sealed world.

The silver fish do not fear in any recognizable sense. They respond to threats, but the response is automatic, muscular, immediate. They do not think about the Steward that might be hovering in the dim light ahead. They do not worry about the passage of time, about the inevitability of death, about the meaning of their brief existence. They simply live. They swim. They feed. They school. They spawn. They die. They are present in the Cistern in a way that larger, more complex organisms cannot be—present in the moment, present in the body, present in the immediate sensory experience of warm water and diffused light and the pressure of neighbors moving nearby.

## The Scuttling Ones

The floor of the Apical Cistern belongs to the crustaceans—or the creatures that occupy the ecological niche that crustaceans would occupy in a world with more familiar biology. They are the Scuttling Ones, the armored presences that move across the sediment and the manufactured surfaces with slow, deliberate purpose.

A mature Scuttler spans thirty centimeters across its carapace, a broad oval of calcified armor that has accumulated the marks of age—scratches from territorial disputes with other Scuttlers, the pitted surface of bacterial colonies that have found purchase on the shell, the accumulated attachments of sessile organisms that treat the Scuttler as mobile real estate. The shell is not decorative. It is thick, heavy, built to resist the crushing grip of predators that no longer exist. The Scuttling Ones evolved in an era when the Cistern contained threats that could crack a carapace, extract the soft flesh within, leave nothing but scattered shell fragments. Those threats are gone—died out, perhaps, when the system was younger, or simply evolved into something less dangerous. But the armor remains. Evolution has not yet discovered a reason to thin the shell, to reduce the investment in protection that no longer serves a purpose. The Scuttling Ones continue to build their armor, generation after generation, because building armor is what they do.

They move on six articulated legs that spread from the carapace in a circular arrangement, allowing them to travel in any direction without turning. The legs end in hooked claws that grip the substrate—whether ceramic tile, metal surface, or vegetation stem—with equal security. They can climb. They can descend. They can cling to surfaces at angles that would defeat a less specialized body plan. They spend much of their time on the floor, sifting through sediment for organic debris, but they also patrol the walls, climbing to the mid-levels where the vegetation grows, feeding on the growth surfaces of the fern-kelps, scraping away the mossy accumulations that form on metal panels.

Their heads, such as they are, are buried beneath the forward edge of the carapace—a arrangement of mouthparts and sensory antennae that emerge from the armored protection to sample the environment. The antennae are long, flexible, covered in chemoreceptors that provide the Scuttling Ones with a detailed map of their immediate surroundings. They can detect food sources from meters away, tracking the chemical signature of decaying matter through the water. They can detect other Scuttlers, identifying individuals by their unique chemical profiles. They can detect changes in water chemistry, shifts in mineral content, the approaching signature of a circulation cycle that will bring fresh particulates into the chamber. They do not see. They do not need to see. The chemical world is richer than the visual world, more detailed, more informative for a creature that lives in the sediment.

The Scuttling Ones are not social. They tolerate the presence of others of their kind at distances determined by resource availability—closer when food is abundant, more dispersed when competition becomes relevant. They communicate through chemical signals, releasing markers that indicate territorial claims, reproductive availability, or simple presence. The signals are not sophisticated. They do not carry complex information. They serve only to establish boundaries, to prevent the energetic waste of unnecessary conflict.

They are scavengers and opportunistic feeders. They consume the organic debris that settles on the floor—the remains of dead fish, the waste products of other organisms, the fragments of vegetation that break free and drift downward. They consume the growth on the walls and the vegetation, scraping away the mossy accumulations with specialized mouthparts. They consume, occasionally, the eggs and larvae of other species, though they do not actively hunt. They are the cleaning crew of the Cistern, the organisms that ensure the floor does not accumulate a layer of debris that would choke the system. They transform waste into biomass, becoming food in turn for any predator capable of penetrating their armor.

Which is to say: they are food for nothing. The Stewards cannot crack their shells. The silver fish cannot threaten them. The only predators in the Cistern that could consume them are the filter-feeding colonies near the floor—those semi-autonomous biological infrastructure organisms that can absorb smaller individuals, can dissolve the shells with chemical processes, can extract the nutrients within. But the Scuttling Ones have learned to avoid the filter colonies. They sense the chemical markers, recognize the danger, navigate around the growth zones. They are safe. They are armored. They are untouchable, for now, by any threat that shares their world.

The Scuttling Ones live long—decades, potentially, for individuals that avoid disease and accident. They grow slowly, molting their shells at intervals that lengthen as they age, producing new armor that hardens over weeks. The molting process is dangerous. During the period when the new shell is soft, the Scuttler is vulnerable to threats that would normally bounce off its protection. They hide during molts, burying themselves in sediment, emerging only when the new armor has calcified sufficiently. The risk is worth it. The armor is essential. The Scuttling Ones continue their slow, armored existence, generation after generation, patient and deliberate, the floor-dwellers of a world that has forgotten why they needed to be so heavily protected.

## The Silent Architects

Beneath the sediment, in the deepest regions of the Cistern, in the spaces between the manufactured floor and the underlying infrastructure, there are presences that do not move. They are not visible to the casual observer. They are not, strictly speaking, alive—not in the sense that the Stewards are alive, or the silver fish, or the Scuttling Ones. They are something else. Something that was installed. Something that continues.

The Silent Architects are calcified structures that extend from the manufactured surfaces of the Cistern floor. They resemble coral, in form—if coral grew in geometric patterns, if coral incorporated metal elements into its growth, if coral followed algorithms encoded in its cells rather than responding to environmental conditions. They are pyramid-shaped, approximately, rising from attachment points on the original manufactured substrate, extending upward in layers that become progressively smaller, creating a faceted appearance that catches the dim light of the lower depths.

They are not individuals. They do not have awareness. They do not possess the capacity for response, for adaptation, for the change-over-time that characterizes living things. They grow, yes—but their growth is predetermined, encoded in genetic material that was designed rather than evolved. They process, yes—but their processing serves functions that were established millennia ago and have not been altered since. They exist. They continue. They perform their roles in the Cistern system without comprehension, without intention, without the possibility of alternative.

The Architects filter water. This is their primary function, the reason they were installed, the role they continue to serve. They draw water through porous surfaces, extract minerals and organic compounds, deposit waste products in crystalline formations that accumulate around their bases. They are part of the Cistern's maintenance system—the biological equivalent of the pumps and valves that circulate water through the chamber. They clean the water. They remove particulates that the other filter-feeders miss. They maintain the chemical balance that allows the vegetation to grow, that allows the fish to thrive, that keeps the system in the equilibrium it has maintained for centuries.

They reproduce through a process that might be called cloning, if cloning is the right word for organisms that were never individuals in the first place. The Architects release spores—microscopic packages of genetic material that drift through the water column until they encounter suitable substrate. When they find the manufactured surfaces, the attachment points, the locations designated for colonization by the encoded algorithms, they attach and begin to grow. They do not vary. They do not mutate. Each new Architect is identical to the parent, following the same growth pattern, producing the same geometric structure, performing the same function in the same location. There is no evolution here. There is only continuation.

The crystalline waste products that accumulate around the Architects have built up over centuries. In some areas, they form low hills, reshaping the floor of the Cistern, creating topography where the manufactured surfaces were originally flat. The crystals are valuable—or would be, if value meant anything in this sealed world. They contain concentrated minerals, extracted from the water by the Architects' processing. They are, in a sense, the only resource in the Cistern that is not renewable, the only product that accumulates rather than cycling. But the system has room for accumulation. The chamber was built large. The crystals will continue to grow for centuries more before they interfere with function.

The Architects do not know they are architects. They do not know anything. They operate according to encoded instructions, processing water, extracting minerals, producing crystals, releasing spores. They are infrastructure, as much as the pumps and valves and heating elements. They were installed. They have not been removed. They continue because continuation is what they do, because the alternative—stopping, dying, becoming inert—would require a deliberate action that no one is present to take. The Cistern maintains itself. The Architects are part of that maintenance. They are the reason the water stays clear enough for light to penetrate, the reason the chemical balance remains stable, the reason the ecosystem can persist in a sealed environment without external input. They are essential. They are ignored. They are silent.

## The Old One

There is a Steward—there has always been a Steward, according to the records that visitors sometimes bring—that the others treat differently. Not with deference, exactly. Not with respect. But with a recognition, a spacing, an awareness that this individual occupies a category apart.

The Old One is not larger than the other Stewards. It is not visibly different—its coloration shifts across the same range, its sensory organs cluster in the same arrangement, its broad body spreads through the water with the same patient grace. But it has been here longer than any other inhabitant of the Cistern. It was here when the first observers arrived to document the ecosystem. It was here, already mature, when the machinery was younger and the light arrays were brighter. It was here before the sediment layers had accumulated to their current depth, before the vegetation had reached its current scale, before the Scuttling Ones had evolved their current armor.

The Old One does not age as other Stewards age. This is not immortality—the body shows the accumulated damage of centuries, the pitted skin where infections have healed, the scars where territorial disputes left their marks, the gradual erosion of the sensory nodes that time has worn down. But it continues. It does not die. The other Stewards live their decades and sink to the floor, becoming food for Scuttling Ones and filter colonies. The Old One continues, swimming its territory, hovering in the currents, feeding on the silver fish that school beneath its position.

The other Stewards avoid the Old One's territory. They do not challenge it. They do not compete for space in the regions where it hovers. This is not submission, not in any conscious sense. It is instinct, perhaps, an evolved response to something that the Old One carries in its electrical signature, in its chemical markers, in the subtle cues that Stewards use to identify each other. *Do not approach. Do not challenge. This space is occupied by something other than a rival.*

The Old One is not aware of its age. It does not count cycles or track generations. It exists in the present, as all Stewards do, responding to immediate stimuli, navigating by current and chemical signal, feeding when food is available, resting when the light dims. It has no memory of the Cistern as it was centuries ago—no memory of the engineers who maintained the machinery, no memory of the original populations that colonized the chamber, no memory of the changes that have accumulated across the decades of its existence. It simply is. It continues. It has always been here, and it will continue to be here, and the concept of ending is as foreign to it as the concept of sky.

But something has changed in the Old One's behavior across the centuries of its existence. It has become more still. It hovers longer, moves less, spends more time in states of minimal activity. Its hunting bursts are shorter, less frequent, adequate for the reduced caloric needs of a body that has slowed its metabolism to match the available resources. It has learned, somehow, to require less—to draw the minimum from the system, to take only what is needed, to persist without demanding more than the Cistern can provide. This is not a conscious adaptation. It is the natural endpoint of a life that has continued past the point where continuation should have become impossible. The Old One has become the Cistern, in a sense—an organism that has achieved perfect equilibrium with its environment, that has stopped struggling against the limits of the system and simply accepted them.

The Old One will die eventually. Everything dies. But the Old One has not died yet, and the Cistern shows no signs of failing, and the machinery continues its cycles, and the light continues to diffuse through the water column, and the Old One continues to hover in its territory, patient and present and ancient beyond reckoning. It is not a god. It is not a guardian. It is simply a Steward that has lasted longer than any Steward should, and the other inhabitants of the Cistern give it space without knowing why, and the system continues around it, and time passes without meaning, and everything remains exactly as it has always been.

## The Absent Host

There is an absence at the center of the Apical Cistern's ecosystem. A shape that should be present, a presence that would explain the design, that would justify the maintenance, that would account for the care taken in constructing and preserving this environment.

The Cistern was built for something. The machinery, the lighting, the temperature regulation, the chemical balance—all of it was designed to support a population, to maintain conditions for a specific purpose. But that purpose is absent. The host is not present. The reason for the Cistern has been removed, or has departed, or has died out, or was never installed in the first place. The machinery continues to maintain the conditions, but the conditions are for nothing. The ecosystem has filled the vacuum, has evolved to occupy a space designed for something else, has made itself at home in a room prepared for a guest who never arrived.

The inhabitants do not know there is an absence. They do not know that the warm water should be warmer still, that the light should follow different cycles, that the chemical composition should favor organisms with different metabolisms. They have evolved to fit what is present. They have adapted to conditions that were not intended for them, have found niches in a system designed for purposes they cannot imagine. The Stewards hover in water that was meant for something larger. The silver fish school in spaces designed for different schooling patterns. The Scuttling Ones climb walls that were supposed to support different organisms entirely. They do not know. They cannot know. They simply live in the world they have, making use of what is present, continuing because continuation is what living things do.

But visitors sometimes sense the absence. They feel, without being able to articulate, that the Cistern is waiting for something. That the machinery is maintaining conditions for a purpose that no longer exists. That the ecosystem has colonized a vacancy, has moved into a room that was furnished for a different occupant. The sense of wrongness that some visitors report—the unsettling quality that they cannot name—may come from this. The Cistern is functioning. The ecosystem is healthy. The inhabitants are adapted to their environment. But the environment was not designed for them. And something in the system remembers, in the way that machinery remembers, in the way that infrastructure retains the shape of its intended function. The Cistern is waiting. The Cistern has always been waiting. The Cistern will continue to wait, maintaining conditions for a guest who will never arrive, supporting an ecosystem that has made itself at home in the absence, running because stopping was never designed into the system.

The inhabitants do not mind. They do not know there is anything to mind. They swim through warm water, illuminated by ancient light, feeding on the particulate matter and the vegetation and each other, continuing the cycles that have continued for centuries. They are present. They are alive. They have claimed this space, made it theirs, evolved into forms that fit the conditions that exist rather than the conditions that were intended. They are not the host. They are the inheritors of an abandoned estate, the squatters in a house prepared for someone else, the life that has grown in a garden that was planted for different flowers. They do not know this. They will never know this. They simply continue, as the machinery continues, as the light continues, as the water continues to circulate through the chamber that has always been and will always be.

This is who lives in the Apical Cistern. These are the presences that occupy the space, that fill the water column with movement and sound, that transform the manufactured environment into something resembling a natural world. They are not aware of the paradox of their existence—the way they have evolved to fit a space designed for something else, the way they have made themselves at home in a room prepared for an absent host. They do not need to be aware. They simply live. They continue. They are the Cistern's inhabitants, its ecosystem, its life. And they will continue to be, for as long as the machinery runs, for as long as the light shines, for as long as the water circulates through this sealed and ancient chamber at the top of something that no one remembers.
