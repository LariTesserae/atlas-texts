# Who Lives Here

## The Damp-Walkers More Closely

You watched them from a distance, but distance deceives in this light. Close now—close enough to see the details that the overview obscured—you understand that "damp-walker" is a name that tells you nothing about what they are.

Their skin is not skin. It is a living membrane, semipermeable, constantly exchanging moisture with the air. You see now that they do not merely drink from the water sheets; they absorb water through every surface of their bodies, the process visible as a subtle shimmer in the humid air, a continuous osmosis that means they never truly stop drinking. Their large dark eyes are compound, facets you mistook for smooth surfaces, each one reflecting a different fragment of the world. They do not blink. They have no eyelids. The air is too wet for eyes to dry, and so they have evolved away the protection that other creatures need.

Their mouths fascinate. Not jaws but complex structures of ridged cartilage, flexible but strong, designed to grip and strip. When one feeds on a fern-frond, you see the process: the mouth opens not horizontally but vertically, the ridges closing on the waxy surface like aclamp, then drawing back to peel away the outer layer in long wet strips. The exposed flesh beneath is pale green, almost white, and the damp-walker consumes it with deliberate efficiency, chewing in a slow circular motion that takes minutes for each mouthful.

They vocalize. Not often, and not loudly—their calls are low rumbles that you feel in your chest rather than hear with your ears—but they do speak to one another. You notice the pattern now: one animal stops feeding, raises its head slightly, and produces a sound that seems to come from somewhere deep in its body, a resonance that the dense air carries to the others. They respond in turn, each call slightly different in pitch or duration, a conversation you cannot interpret but whose existence is unmistakable. They are saying something. They are agreeing, perhaps, or warning, or simply confirming that they are all still here, still present, still together.

And they are not as indifferent to you as they seemed.

When you move suddenly—a step that breaks the slow rhythm of your observation—the nearest damp-walker tenses. You see the ripple of motion across its body, a wave that passes from head to flank, every muscle engaging simultaneously in a preparatory tension. It is not fear, not as you understand fear. It is readiness. They have no predators, but they have not forgotten what threat feels like. Somewhere in their lineage, something hunted them. Somewhere in their memory, preserved in instincts they no longer need, is the knowledge that large moving things can bring harm.

The tension passes. The animal relaxes, returns to its feeding. But you have learned something: the damp-walkers are not passive. They are patient. There is a difference.

## The Glider-Folk

You cannot catch them. You have tried, moving with all the slowness the heat allows, hands extended toward the surfaces where they rest—but they are always elsewhere by the time you arrive, launched into the dense air on membranes that span between wrist and ankle, fingers spread wide to maximize the surface that catches the updrafts.

But you have observed them. In the evenings, when the light grows somehow even more diffuse, they gather in the highest fronds, dozens of them clustering on the same tree-fern, their small bodies clinging with feet that have two toes facing forward and two back, a grip that holds even on the wettest surfaces. They are social. They groom each other with movements so fast they blur, tongues or teeth or something you cannot quite see working through the membranes and fur that cover their bodies. They are not mammals—their surface is wrong for that, a pelt of something finer than hair, almost like the soft scales of a butterfly's wing—but they are warm, and they are social, and they care for each other in ways that suggest bonds beyond mere utility.

Their calls, which seemed simple at first, resolve into complexity when you listen long enough. They harmonize. Individual voices join and separate, creating patterns that repeat with variations, phrases that appear and return transformed, a music that has structure and intention. They are singing. Not for territory, not for mating—at least not only for those things—but because singing is what they do, because the dense air carries sound so well that it has become their primary medium, because in a world where visibility is limited by fog and vegetation and the perpetual diffusion of light, sound is how they know each other, how they find each other, how they remain a community spread across the canopy.

You found a dead one once, a glider that had fallen—or been dropped, or had simply reached its end—onto the stone surface where you walked. It was smaller than it had seemed in flight, its body no longer than your palm, its membrane collapsed against itself like wet fabric. You knelt to examine it, and the smell hit you: not rot, not decay, but something sharper, chemical, a scent that stung your nostrils and made your eyes water. Even in death, it was defended. The pelt, the membrane, the very skin contained compounds that discouraged predation, that made the small body a mouthful of regret for anything that might consider eating it.

Perhaps that is why they have no fear. Perhaps they have never needed fear, because they taste of warning, because their bodies carry messages that say *do not*, because they evolved in a world where being small and toxic and fast was enough.

You left the body where it lay. Within an hour, the crawling vines had begun to grow over it, their cup-leaves extending toward the small corpse, the pale flowers turning in its direction. The plateau does not waste its dead. Everything returns, everything is absorbed, everything becomes substrate for the next generation of growth.

## The Things in the Water

The transparent amphibians are not the only inhabitants of the pools.

You discovered this when you knelt to drink—against your better judgment, your thirst overwhelming your caution—and saw movement in the water that did not match the darting forms you had observed before. Slower. Larger. Deliberate in a way that suggested intention rather than instinct.

The creature that surfaced was not amphibian. It was something else entirely.

Flat and wide, its body spread across the surface of the pool like a leaf floating on still water, its edges fringed with tendrils that moved independently, testing the air, tasting the moisture. In its center, a raised portion that might have been a head, though it had no eyes you could see, no mouth, no features except a pattern of pores that opened and closed in a rhythm that matched your breathing. You froze. The creature did not move. The pores continued their opening, their closing, their invitation to... something.

Then it sank, slowly, gracefully, its flat body descending through the warm water until it disappeared into the biofilm layer at the pool's bottom, vanishing into the iridescent scum that covered every submerged surface. You pulled your hand back from the water. You did not drink.

Later, you observed the pools from a distance, and saw that the flat creatures were common, present in nearly every depression where water collected. They moved at night—or what passed for night in this cloud-swathed world—rising to the surface when the light dimmed, spreading their bodies across the water like stars emerging in a dark sky. They were beautiful, in their way. They were also clearly not passive. Things that do not eat do not grow to that size. Things that do not defend themselves do not survive in water shared by other creatures.

What did they eat? What ate them? You did not know. You suspected that knowing would not make you feel better about sharing their pools.

And there were smaller things. Much smaller.

The biofilm itself—the iridescent sheets that coated every submerged surface—was alive, not with one species but with thousands, a community of microorganisms so dense and so diverse that the water itself could be said to be living. You saw this when you lifted a stone from a pool and watched the slime that dripped from its underside: not uniform but patterned, layers of different colors intermingling, strands of something filamentous connecting patches of something gelatinous, the whole mass responding to the air with movements that seemed too coordinated to be random. The microorganisms were communicating. Through chemicals, through touch, through means you could not perceive, they were exchanging information about your presence, about the disturbance you had caused, about the conditions of their world.

You replaced the stone. You did not disturb the pools again.

## The Builders, or Their Remains

You cannot avoid the question forever: who made this place?

The infrastructure is too obvious once you know to look for it. The dark veins in the limestone, carrying heat and energy through channels that could not have formed naturally. The platforms, the towers, the subtle modifications of atmosphere that keep this plateau in a steady state that defies the physics of elevation and latitude. Someone engineered this. Someone built a world inside the world, a terrarium at the scale of a mountain range, a garden that has grown wild for so long that the gardeners may no longer be present.

But are they gone? Or have they simply changed?

You find evidence in the structures themselves. The bone-white material that forms the platforms and towers is not uniform. In places, it has grown—new extensions emerging from old surfaces, fresh branches reaching toward the sky, the architecture continuing to develop according to some internal plan. In other places, it has retreated—surfaces flaking away, sections crumbling into the water sheets, the material returning to whatever components it was made from. Growth and decay, construction and deconstruction, a cycle that suggests not abandonment but adaptation, the structures reshaping themselves to meet conditions that have changed over time.

And near one tower, you find something that stops you.

It is small, easily overlooked, the size of your thumb, embedded in the bone-white surface at eye height. At first you think it is a flaw, an inclusion, a piece of debris that was trapped in the material when it formed. But when you look closer, you see that it is deliberate: a shape, curved and smooth, like a shell or a fragment of pottery, placed in the tower's surface with intention. And when you look closer still, you see that the shape is not hollow. It contains something.

Bone. Actual bone, yellowed with age, dense and heavy. And beside it, something that might be a tooth, worn flat on one surface, dark with minerals from the water that has flowed over it for centuries.

An offering. Or a memorial. Or a message.

Someone died here. Someone was placed into the tower, into the material that conducts energy and regulates atmosphere, and the tower grew around them, incorporated them, made them part of its structure. You do not know if this was burial or sacrifice or simply the way this culture—this species—honored its dead. But the presence is unmistakable. The tower is not only infrastructure. It is also a graveyard, a monument, a library of the individuals who built and maintained and loved this place.

You step back. The tower's hum continues, unchanged, a note that has been sounding for longer than you can imagine. The embedded remains stare out at you, the hollows of bone-darkness catching the diffuse light. You feel observed. You feel judged, perhaps, by presences that cannot see you but whose existence implies a standard against which you are being measured.

You are walking through someone's home. You are touching someone's belongings. You are breathing someone's engineered air.

And they may still be here.

## The HIDDEN Ones

You do not see them. You are not certain they exist. But the evidence accumulates, and the conclusion becomes difficult to avoid.

The vegetation is too managed. Not pruned, not planted in rows, but shaped—certain species encouraged in certain areas, certain growth patterns redirected, the forest floor curated by hands or by processes that have the effect of hands. You notice it first in the clearings around the platforms, the way the fern-trees curve away at exactly the angle that preserves sight lines, the way the crawling vines approach but never cross boundaries that have no physical barriers. Then you notice it elsewhere: the paths that wind through the thicket, wide enough for damp-walkers but not for anything larger, the pools that are deeper than natural depressions would allow, their bottoms smoothed by something that was not erosion, the distribution of flowers in clusters that seem random until you map them and realize they form a gradient, a transition, a deliberate arrangement of species across space.

Someone tends this garden. Someone moves through it, observing, adjusting, maintaining the balance that keeps the plateau's systems functioning. They may be few. They may be slow, working on time scales that make their interventions invisible to a visitor who stays only hours or days. They may have forms you do not recognize as bodies, may have evolved or engineered themselves into shapes that blend with the environment, may be the moss or the lichen or the biofilm itself, distributed intelligences that have no center and no boundary, only presence.

Or they may be watching you right now, from behind the fronds you think are empty, from within the water sheets you think are pure, from the towers whose hum you have learned to ignore. They may be deciding what to do about you, whether you are a threat or an curiosity or simply another element of the environment to be incorporated into their management. They may have been watching since you arrived, may have tracked your path across the plateau, may know more about you than you know about yourself.

You call out once, experimentally: "Hello?" The word sounds strange in the dense air, flattened, distorted, arriving at your own ears from multiple directions at once. There is no response. No echo, even. The plateau absorbs your voice and gives nothing back.

But the next morning, when you return to the platform you found on your first day, something has changed. The water that pooled in its concave surface has been cleared—drained or drunk or simply removed—and in its place, a small arrangement of objects. A stone, smooth and white. A frond, freshly cut, its edges still wet. A flower, pale and trembling, its bell hanging downward as all the flowers here do.

An offering. Or a greeting. Or a warning.

You do not touch the objects. You do not know the protocol, do not know what response is appropriate, do not know if the objects are gift or test. You sit beside the platform for an hour, watching, waiting, but nothing moves except the damp-walkers in the distance, the gliders in the canopy, the water in its endless circulation. If someone is there, they do not reveal themselves. If someone is watching, they do not intervene.

You leave the objects as they are. You walk away. Behind you, the platform waits, its offering undisturbed, its purpose unclear.

## The Children of the System

There is another possibility, one you consider on your third day, as you watch a damp-walker give birth in the shade of a fern-thicket.

The process is slow, unhurried. The animal does not lie down but stands, its legs splayed wider than usual, its body rippling with contractions that seem to cause no distress. A sac emerges first, translucent, filled with fluid that catches the light. Then the young, smaller than you expected, its membranes already absorbing the moisture of the air, its compound eyes open and observing. It does not cry. It simply stands, shaky at first, then steadier, its body finding its balance on the wet stone.

The mother does not nuzzle it, does not lick it clean. She simply waits, her body continuing its contractions, pushing the sac fully out, letting it fall to the ground where it begins immediately to dissolve, the fluid releasing into the water sheet, the membrane crumbling into components that the biofilm will consume. The young is already walking, already moving toward the fern-fronds, already beginning to feed.

But it is not what you expected. Its coloration is wrong—not the gray-green of the adults but something darker, something with a blue undertone that matches the veins in the limestone, that matches the infrastructure that runs beneath the surface of the plateau. And as you watch, you see that the young animal's skin, where it touches the stone, leaves a faint residue, a shimmer that persists after it moves on, a trace of something that the biofilm absorbs and incorporates with unusual speed.

The damp-walkers are not merely adapted to this place. They are part of its systems. They have evolved—or been evolved—to serve functions within the infrastructure, to carry compounds from one location to another, to distribute resources through their feeding and their movement and their waste. They are not livestock, not pets, not wild animals. They are components. They are mechanisms of flesh and blood, designed to perform tasks that the static infrastructure cannot.

And if the damp-walkers are components, what about the gliders? What about the flat creatures in the pools? What about the transparent amphibians and the biofilm organisms and the crawling vines and the tree-ferns? What if the entire ecosystem is a machine, every species engineered to perform a function, every interaction a process in a system too large and too complex to be perceived as artificial?

This would mean that the builders are not gone. This would mean that the builders are everywhere, that their presence is expressed in the bodies of the living things that populate the plateau, that they have become their creation, have dissolved themselves into their infrastructure, have achieved a form of immortality by becoming the garden itself.

It would mean that the damp-walkers are not animals. They are ideas given flesh. They are intentions made manifest. They are the builders, still building, still maintaining, still watching—through compound eyes, through pores that open and close, through tendrils that taste the air—still present after all this time.

You watch the young animal feed, its dark-blue-tinged body absorbing the wetness of the frond, its skin already beginning to shift toward the gray-green of the adults. It is joining the system. It is becoming what it was designed to be. In a few hours, it will be indistinguishable from the others, will browse and drink and rumble-call with the rest of the herd, will live and die and be absorbed back into the plateau when its time comes.

You watch, and you wonder: what were they trying to build? What were they trying to become? Did they succeed? Or did they simply become lost in their own creation, dissolved into the wet heat and the slow cycles and the perpetual present of a world that no longer remembers them as distinct?

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything lives here, and nothing is separate from anything else.

The distinction between organism and environment breaks down in a place like this. The water is alive, thick with microorganisms, conducting chemical signals between pools that have no physical connection. The stone is alive, threaded with infrastructure that has grown and adapted and incorporated organic material into its structure. The air is alive, saturated with spores and pollen and the shed cells of a million bodies, a medium through which life passes as much as it passes through the water or the vegetative layer.

To ask what lives here is to ask the wrong question. Nothing lives here *individually*. Everything lives here *together*, a continuous community that has no boundaries, no separations, no individuals that can be understood apart from the whole. The damp-walkers are the ferns are the water are the stone are the towers are the builders are the dead whose bones rest in the walls.

You are here too, now. You are breathing the engineered air, drinking the mineral-rich water, absorbing the heat that rises from the infrastructure beneath your feet. You are part of the system, if only briefly. You are being observed by the microorganisms that coat your skin, being tasted by the tendrils of the flat creatures that live in every pool, being incorporated into the biofilm that will remain after you leave.

What lives here?

You do. For now.

And when you go—and you will go, eventually, descending through the weeping cascades to whatever lies below—you will carry this place with you. In the microorganisms that have colonized your gut, in the compounds that have accumulated in your tissues, in the memories that will surface in dreams for years to come. You will be a vector, a transmission, a spore spreading the essence of the plateau to wherever you travel next.

The system will use you. The system uses everything.

That is what lives here: a system so complete, so integrated, so perfectly designed for its own continuation that it does not need to be maintained. It maintains itself. It grows and adapts and incorporates and evolves without intervention. The builders, if they still exist as individuals, are passengers now, observers of a process they set in motion but no longer control.

And you—visitor, observer, temporary component—are simply the latest element in a process that has been running since before your species learned to write, that will continue running long after your species is gone.

What lives here?

The question answers itself, in the end. *Here* lives. Here is alive, in a way that makes your categories meaningless, in a way that reminds you that life is not a thing but a process, not a noun but a verb, not a collection of individuals but a continuous unfolding of relationship and exchange.

You are standing in something that has no word in your language. You are breathing it, touching it, becoming it.

The plateau lives. And you, for this moment, are part of its life.
