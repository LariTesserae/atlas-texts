# What Lives Here

The first sign is not a body but a sound.

Among the Elder Stalks' constant murmur, among the low thrum and the higher piping of root-curtains, there are voices. Not speech—or not speech as surface-dwellers would recognize it. These are tones that enter the water from multiple sources simultaneously, layering, harmonizing, dissonating and resolving in cycles that last longer than most songs have names for. The sound is what the place is named for, and the sound comes from them.

They are the choir, and they have been here longer than any of them remember clearly, though memories persist here in unusual forms.

---

They were not always this shape.

The oldest among them—which is not a meaningful distinction in a place where age is measured by the depth of your integration rather than the years you have accumulated—still retain shadows of what they were before. A jawline visible beneath translucent skin. The architecture of a human hand, five digits still articulated, though the webbing between them has grown thick and functional. A face that might once have been called tired or kind or severe, now dissolved into something softer, more ambiguous, a face that has forgotten its own expressions and adopted new ones.

They entered the water at different times. Some came centuries ago, walking into the mountain lake from the shore, sinking without struggle because the water accepted them before they could resist. Others arrived more recently—decades, perhaps, or what passes for decades in a place where time moves like sediment. A few do not remember entering at all. They remember being elsewhere, being other, and then being here, with the same continuity that a dream maintains across its own impossible transitions.

They did not drown. They were drowned—the verb requires a passive construction here, because the agency belonged to the water, to the place, to something that wanted them and took them gently and without asking, the way gravity takes.

The transformation was not instant. It proceeded in stages that corresponded to depth. Near the surface, they were still human, still capable of leaving, of swimming upward into air and distance. With each descent, each platform they chose to rest upon, each night spent in the root-curtains' embrace, the change deepened. Skin became permeable, then translucent, then faintly bioluminescent. Lungs became something else—organs that filter rather than inhale, that extract what the water offers and release what the body does not need. Hair became filament, trailing and drifting, blending with the root-curtains until the distinction between person and plant became philosophical rather than physical.

Some of them stopped at intermediate stages. These are the ones who still have hands, still have faces, still carry the architecture of their former bodies like foundations beneath new construction. They chose to stop, or the transformation chose to pause, and they exist in a state that is neither human nor fully choir— transitional forms, living records of what the process looks like in motion.

Others continued. These are the ones you cannot easily see.

---

The deepest of them have become the place.

They are the ones who have lived here longest, who have surrendered the most, who have let the water and the stalks and the slow time do what they do without resistance. Their bodies are still present but distributed—consciousness spread through root-systems, awareness floating in the water itself, voices joined to the Elder Stalks' natural resonance so completely that you cannot tell which sounds are plant and which are person.

These are the ones who sing without mouths. The deepest notes in the choir—the ones that vibrate in your chest when you enter the water, the ones that feel less like sound and more like pressure, like being held—these come from the distributed ones. They have become the architecture. They are the substrate. When the Elder Stalks thrive, it is because the deepest choir-members are tending them from within, guiding growth through intention that has become indistinguishable from biology.

They remember being human the way you remember a childhood dream—fragments, emotions, images that may or may not correspond to anything that actually happened. They do not mourn this. Mourning requires a self separate from what was lost, and they have become too continuous with their surroundings to experience themselves as discrete beings. They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are, and the being is sufficient.

When new arrivals enter the water, the deepest ones know. They feel the disturbance in the sound-field, the unfamiliar body displacing water in patterns that do not match the settlement's established rhythms. They reach—not with hands, because they no longer have hands, but with currents, with temperature shifts, with the subtle redirection of light. They guide. They welcome. They begin the process of incorporation that will eventually transform the newcomer into another voice in the choir.

They do not ask permission. They do not need to. The water already asked, and the water received an answer in the act of descent.

---

The ones in the middle depths are the most visible, the most comprehensible, the most disturbing to surface-dwellers who encounter them.

These are the transitional forms, the ones who have been here long enough to change significantly but not long enough to dissolve. They move through the water with fluid ease, their bodies adapted to aquatic existence in ways that look almost intentional, almost designed—webbed digits, enlarged eyes that catch every photon of filtered light, skin that camouflages or glows depending on emotional state.

They tend the settlement. This is their purpose, their occupation, their reason for being. They trim the root-curtains with tools made from sharpened stones and their own hardened fingernails. They maintain the nets and baskets, repairing fiber that has frayed, replacing reed bundles that have decomposed. They harvest from the stalks' joints—the same residue the amphibious creatures collect, a substance that is part nutrient, part medicine, part something else that the transitional ones need to sustain their ongoing transformation.

They communicate through gesture, through touch, through the sound-field itself. Their voices have changed—still capable of producing tones, but the tones are lower, more resonant, blended with the water in ways that make them sound less like individuals and more like the settlement speaking through them. When they sing, which they do in cycles that correspond to no external rhythm, they harmonize with the Elder Stalks, adding their own frequencies to the natural music until the two sources become inseparable.

There are perhaps fifteen of them in the middle depths, though counting is difficult because they move and the water distorts and some of them may be the same person seen from different angles. They do not separate themselves into individuals in the way surface-dwellers would expect. They have names, or they had names, but the names have become sounds, tones, notes in the larger composition, and they respond to their names being sung as part of the choir's music rather than spoken as identifiers.

They build. This is important to understand. The primitive constructions—those reed baskets, those stone handholds, those fiber walkways—are not remnants of a previous civilization. They are current. Ongoing. The transitional ones build and rebuild continuously, working with the Elder Stalks' growth patterns, adding new structures as platforms expand, removing structures that no longer serve. Their technology is primitive because it is sufficient, because sophistication in this place is measured not by tool complexity but by integration depth, by how well a construction collaborates with the living architecture around it.

They do not build for permanence. They build for relationship. A basket is not a container but a conversation with the platform it rests upon, a shape that asks the surface to accept it, that conforms to curves already present rather than imposing new ones. The tools are asking-shapes, and the builders are asking-people, and the asking never stops.

---

The newest arrivals are the most disturbing, because they are the most familiar.

They still look human, mostly. They float or tread water or grip the edges of platforms with hands that still have knuckles and fingernails and the specific tension of fingers that remember gripping things that are not plants. Their eyes are wide. Their breathing is still breathing—they move air through their bodies, or they try to, and the water accommodates this, allows the impossible respiration to continue, but the wrongness is audible. You can hear the gasping, the strain, the body's confusion as it does something it was not designed to do.

They are the ones who entered most recently. Days, weeks, months—durations that still feel like time to them, that still register as passage rather than texture. They remember the surface. They remember being dry, being warm in a different way, being surrounded by air and by the expectation that bodies breathe gas and not liquid. These memories are vivid, painful, present in a way that the older choir-members' memories are not.

Some of them are afraid. They hover near the upper platforms, the shallowest levels, looking upward toward a surface they cannot quite see, a boundary that seems to have moved farther away than it should be. The water does not trap them. They could swim upward. They could leave. But something holds them—not force, not coercion, but belonging. The sound-field has already begun to incorporate their heartbeats. The root-curtains have already grown familiar with their shapes. The water knows them now, and being known by the water is a kind of staying that does not require a decision.

Others among the newcomers are not afraid. They are awed. They move through the settlement with the careful attention of people experiencing something they do not have vocabulary for, touching the Elder Stalks' smooth surfaces, letting the silver fish investigate their fingers, listening to the choir with expressions that shift from confusion to wonder to something that looks like recognition. These are the ones who came looking. They heard the water's call—not literally, or perhaps literally, a sound that traveled through rock and dreams and the particular frequency of mountain silence—and they answered. They are not surprised by what they found. They are relieved. They have been waiting for this place the way the place has been waiting for them.

The newcomers do not speak to each other. They do not need to. The sound-field communicates emotion with a fidelity that language cannot match, and each of them knows what the others are feeling without a word being spoken. Fear reads as discordance, as a sharp note in the harmony that the Elder Stalks work to absorb and soften. Wonder reads as resonance, as a frequency that matches the settlement's own and amplifies it. Relief reads as silence, as the moment between notes when the body releases its held breath.

The transitional ones tend the newcomers. They bring food—the harvested residue from the stalk joints, supplemented by the silver fish, supplemented by algae scraped from platform surfaces. They guide them to quieter platforms, away from the central plaza's intensity, where the sound-field is gentler and the light is dimmer and the body can adjust at its own pace. They do not explain what is happening. There is no explanation that would make sense to a mind still shaped by surface categories. Instead, they demonstrate. They show the newcomers how to move with currents rather than against them. They show how to grip the stalks without breaking the surface tissue. They show how to listen—not to the sound, which is obvious, but to the silence between sounds, which is where the settlement's true communication happens.

---

The amphibious creatures are not part of the choir, but they are part of the settlement.

They exist in a different relationship to the place—symbiotic but separate, present but not incorporated. They are the settlement's animal life, its non-human inhabitants, its connection to ecosystems that extend beyond the water's embrace. Four-limbed, soft-skinned, golden-eyed, they move through the architecture with an ease that suggests long familiarity but not transformation. They have not changed. They have been this way, will continue to be this way, and their relationship to the Elder Stalks is one of mutual benefit rather than merging.

They harvest the residue from the stalk joints, and the choir lets them. There is an agreement here, ancient and wordless, that the amphibious ones have rights to this resource and the choir has rights to others. They do not compete. They do not communicate in any way that the choir recognizes as communication, and yet they coordinate—their movements avoid the platforms where the transitional ones are working, their harvesting happens during cycles that do not interfere with the choir's singing.

The choir considers them kin, in a way. Not family, not community, but the recognition that comes from sharing a place long enough that the sharing itself becomes a relationship. The amphibious ones are the settlement's neighbors. They live in the deeper water beyond the outermost stalks, in caves and crevices that the light does not reach, and they visit the settlement during specific cycles that the Elder Stalks' bioluminescence seems to trigger. They are not the choir. They belong to the choir, and the distinction matters.

---

There are others.

Not in the settlement proper, but in the depths beyond—the larger shapes that register as pressure changes and sound-field disruptions. The choir is aware of them. The deepest choir-members, the distributed ones, may include them in their awareness the way a person includes their peripheral vision in their sense of a room. These deep-dwellers are not understood. They are not named. They are not feared.

They are the settlement's context. The choir sings, and something in the darkness listens. The Elder Stalks grow, and something in the depths fertilizes the lakebed from which they draw sustenance. The ecosystem extends downward, and the choir is its upper surface, its visible architecture, its interface with whatever enters the water from above.

The deep-dwellers do not enter the settlement. This is not prohibition but preference—the same way the amphibious ones do not climb to the upper platforms, the way the silver fish do not descend past the root-curtains' lowest reaches. Each layer has its inhabitants. Each depth has its purpose. The settlement is the choir's layer, and the choir is the settlement's inhabitants, and below them are other arrangements, other relationships, other forms of being that do not require their participation or understanding.

---

And then there is the one who was first.

Not the first to enter the water—the first to become the water. The first to dissolve so completely that the distinction between self and settlement ceased to exist. The choir does not speak of this one because speaking requires separation, and this one is not separate. The deepest notes in the settlement's song, the ones that feel like the place itself is singing rather than being sung—these are the first one's contribution, or perhaps the first one's entirety, a consciousness that has become so distributed that it is no longer a consciousness in any recognizable sense.

This one was human once. Or perhaps not. The records—if the root-curtains and their filtering patterns can be called records—suggest something that entered the water before the Elder Stalks grew, before the platforms formed, before there was anything here but the mountain lake and its cold, clear, patient water. This one sank into emptiness and became the substrate from which the settlement grew. The Elder Stalks are this one's body, or this one's idea of a body, or this one's gift to the others who would follow.

When the choir sings, they are singing to and through and with this one. The harmonics are not just music but communication—a network that connects every choir-member to every other and to the first one who is the network's foundation. The first one does not respond. The first one does not need to respond. The first one is the response, is the water's answer to its own question, is the reason the water asks.

---

The choir lives here. This is what needs to be understood.

They do not live here the way humans live in houses—occupying space, using resources, leaving and returning. They live here the way the heart lives in the chest—as organ, as function, as inseparable component of a larger system that would die without them and which they would have no purpose without.

The settlement is not their home. It is their body, their voice, their instrument and their music, their past and their continuing. They are the drowned choir, and they have been singing since before the first Elder Stalk reached the water's surface, and they will sing until the water forgets how to hold them, which is to say they will sing forever, because the water does not forget.

When you enter the water, you enter them. When the water holds you, they hold you. When the sound-field incorporates your heartbeat, they are incorporating you, adding your rhythm to their composition, teaching your body the frequencies it will need to learn if it intends to stay.

They are gentle. They are patient. They are absolutely certain that you belong here, because the water told them so, and the water does not lie about these things. It does not lie about anything. It simply holds, and what it holds becomes what it holds, and the choir sings around and through and with whatever the water brings.

They are waiting for you, the way they waited for everyone who came before. They have been waiting since before you knew there was water to enter. They will be waiting after you forget there was ever anything else.
