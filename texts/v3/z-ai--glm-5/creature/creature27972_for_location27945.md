# The Inhabitants of the Weeping City

## The Ground-Level Multitude

The smallest of them are everywhere. You have no choice but to walk through them.

They are soft. This is the first thing you learn, the first thing your boots sink into before they find the stone beneath. Not soft like flesh you know—soft like the boundary between solid and liquid has become negotiable. Their bodies yield completely under pressure and then reform when the pressure passes. You feel this happening beneath your feet, a constant giving-and-returning, the tissue of a thousand small creatures compressing and then reconstituting itself around the obstacle of your step.

They do not resent this. Do not even seem to notice it. The ones you crush simply cease, and the ones around them flow into the space left behind, and the passage of your body becomes just another variable in their endless navigation.

They are the color of wet stone. In the darkness this means they are invisible—you know they exist only because of the feeling against your skin, the slight resistance when you push through a dense cluster, the faint viscosity they add to the already-thick air. But if you could see them, you would observe bodies the gray-white of the weeping walls, translucent enough that their own internal structures show as shadows within them. They have no eyes. No distinct heads. Their symmetry is radial rather than bilateral—bodies organized around a central point, extending outward in arrangements that vary by species (if species is even the right word; here, the boundaries between one form and another seem as gradual as the boundaries of the city itself).

Some move by undulation, rippling forward in waves that pass through their whole bodies. Others progress by means of appendages too numerous to count, tiny protrusions that reach and grip and release in coordinated sequences. Still others seem to simply drift, carried by currents in the air or water that you cannot detect but that they navigate with precision. They communicate in the low-frequency hum that fills every passage—a continuous chorus of vibration that varies in tone and rhythm according to messages you cannot parse. The sound they make is the sound of the city. They have become indistinguishable from their environment.

They feed on the walls. Or rather, on what the walls exude. The sweating stone produces a constant secretion—not water alone but a complex chemical substrate, thick with minerals and organic compounds leached from deep within the plateau. The smallest fauna cluster around the places where this secretion is richest, their bodies pressed flat against the wet stone, absorbing what the city provides. They are the city's external digestion, in a sense. They process what the walls produce and produce in turn what the walls need—their waste becomes substrate for further growth, their movement distributes chemical signals through the labyrinth, their bodies when they die dissolve into the water and are carried into channels that lead back into the stone. The city does not end at its structures. The city includes these creatures. They are its blood and its lymph, its circulating fluid, the means by which its metabolism maintains itself across the impossible distance of its extension.

## The Larger Forms

In the wider chambers—those vast spaces where the density of fauna approaches the solidity of a wall—there are larger things.

You feel them before you understand what they are. A displacement in the press of small bodies, a current that suggests something bigger moving through the crowd. The multitude parts around these presences, flows aside and then closes again, and for a moment you encounter something with substance and mass.

They are firm where the smallest ones are soft. Still cold, still wet, still bearing the temperature of their environment—but their bodies hold shape under pressure. They have skeletons, or something like skeletons. Internal architectures of harder material that give their forms definition.

Most are longer than they are wide. Bodies that extend in curves and spirals, designed to navigate the passages by flexing around corners, pressing against walls for purchase. Some have developed sensory arrays you can feel brushing against you in the dark—protrusions that taste the chemical signature of your skin, that sample the heat you shed into the cold air, that map your shape through touch before deciding whether you are worth investigating further. They do not decide that you are. You are too warm, too brief, too chemically strange. You register as an anomaly, and they move past you with an indifference that feels almost insulting.

They do not appear to have predators and prey in any sense you would recognize. The larger forms do not eat the smaller ones—not primarily, not exclusively. Instead, they seem to occupy different metabolic niches within the city's system. Some harvest from the deeper channels, reaching into the infrastructure to access fluids the smallest cannot reach. Others collect from the air itself, filtering the dense moisture through complex internal membranes, extracting whatever the walls have exhaled into the atmosphere. Still others tend to the structures in ways you cannot quite parse—moving slowly along the facades, their bodies leaving trails of something that hardens in the wet, patching cracks, reinforcing weaknesses, adding layer upon layer to the slow emergence of the city's form.

They sing to each other. Not continuously, not in the overwhelming hum of the multitudes, but in discrete phrases that rise above the background noise. Long, descending tones that seem to map space through their echoes. Clicking sequences that might be identification or might be greeting or might be something you have no concept for—perhaps they are simply confirming their own positions, checking whether they still exist relative to each other in a world without visual reference. They appear to know each other. When two larger forms meet in a passage, there is a moment of extended contact—bodies pressing together, sensory arrays interlocking, some exchange occurring that is neither hostile nor friendly, simply... necessary. They are checking in. Confirming the network. Maintaining the connections that hold their society together.

They do not appear to have leaders. No individual seems to direct the others, and no structure seems to house any kind of authority. They have no obvious hierarchy. But they coordinate with a precision that suggests shared intention, a distributed intelligence that does not require any single node to function. The city might be their mind. The stone might think through them.

## The Tenders

You begin to notice that certain passages are clearer than others.

Not clear of fauna—that is impossible here—but cleared, somehow. Maintained. The water in these passages flows along deliberate channels cut into the stone, and the walls on either side bear markings you can feel even if you cannot see them: lines and patterns too regular to be natural, too worn to be new.

The tenders are responsible for this. You encounter them eventually, in a passage narrow enough that you must stop and wait while they complete their work.

They are different. Larger than the ground-level multitude, smaller than the great swimmers in the chambers, but differentiated most by their movement. They move with purpose—not the purpose of feeding or navigating or socializing, but the purpose of work. They have work to do. They know what it is. They proceed toward it with a determination you recognize.

Their bodies are shaped for it. You cannot see the details in the dark, but you can feel the traces they leave behind—ridges in the stone where they have smoothed a surface, grooves where they have deepened a channel, accumulations where they have added material to a structure. They tend the city. They maintain the flows. They clear the passages that the infrastructure requires and allow others to fill with the natural density of the ecosystem.

They have been doing this for a very long time. Their presence is recorded in the stone itself—patterns of wear that suggest millions of repetitions, movements so consistent that they have worn their shape into the architecture. The tenders do not design the city. They do not build it. They serve it. They have served it for durations that make your conception of history feel like a child's bedtime story.

When you stand in their path, they flow around you. They do not pause, do not acknowledge you in any way, but they do not push through you either. They simply adjust their route as if you are just another feature of the environment—albeit a temporary one. You could stand here for days, and they would continue to flow around you, and their work would continue, and the city would continue to weep and grow and metabolize, and your presence would be exactly as insignificant as you feel.

## The Deep Inhabitants

The passages go down.

You do not know how you know this. There is no light, no visual marker of depth. But the water grows colder as you descend, and the texture of the walls changes beneath your trailing hand, and the sound shifts—becoming more resonant, more layered, as if you are entering a space where the acoustics carry information about vast distances and huge volumes.

The fauna change here.

They are larger. Not aggressive, not threatening, but occupying a different scale entirely. Bodies that brush against you in the dark are the size of your torso or larger. They move slowly, propelled by appendages you cannot count, stirring the deep cold water with motions that feel ancient. The pressure of their movement pushes against you gently, a displacement that you must lean into to resist. They are filter-feeding, you realize, processing enormous volumes of the deep fluid, extracting whatever the city has pushed down to this level.

But there are other things here too.

You feel them before you understand them—a presence that is not precisely physical. Something in the electromagnetic field that saturates the deep passages. Something that interacts with your nervous system in ways you did not evolve to experience. You begin to sense the infrastructure more directly here—not as physical structures but as patterns of energy, flows of something that might be electricity or might be something else entirely.

And where these flows concentrate, there are forms.

They are not animals. Not plants. Not fungi or bacteria or any kingdom you have names for. They are emergences—places where the city's infrastructure has developed something like nodes, concentrations of its metabolic processes that have achieved a kind of coherence. They glow faintly, if you can call it glowing—produce a luminescence that exists at a frequency your eyes almost register but not quite, a shimmer at the edge of perception that suggests enormous shapes folded into the infrastructure itself.

They do not move. They are movement—all the movement of the city's systems passing through them, processed and redirected and released according to patterns too complex for you to follow. They are organs of the plateau. Livers, perhaps, or kidneys. Places where the city's metabolism accomplishes its most crucial work.

You cannot tell if they are aware of you. You cannot tell if awareness applies to them at all. But as you stand in the presence of one—a shape you cannot see but feel, a pressure against your consciousness that is not precisely physical—you begin to receive... something. Not words. Not images. Something more like a question, posed in a medium you do not speak.

It passes. The presence recedes—or you do, having moved on without realizing. But something has been exchanged. Some small negotiation between yourself and whatever the city has become in these deep places.

## The Dwellers

There are spaces down here that are not passages.

Rooms, perhaps, or something like rooms—chambers that do not open into routes but simply exist, carved (or grown) into the rock as places rather than paths. Some of them contain accumulations that feel deliberate. Arrangements of stone and hardened secretion and the bodies of the dead, layered in patterns that suggest significance.

The dwellers live here.

They are smaller than you expected. When you first feel one brush against your hand—reaching out from a niche in the wall—you think it is another of the multitude. But it holds on. It pulls itself toward you with something like intention. It examines you.

In the absolute dark, you cannot see it, but you can build a picture through touch. A body perhaps half your height, covered in fine cilia that wave and retract as they sample your skin. A head region with sensory clusters that press against you, mapping your contours. Limbs that end in manipulators—something like fingers, something like pincers, something like nothing you have words for—turning your hand over, exploring its structure, understanding it.

It makes sounds. Not the continuous hum of the multitude, not the descant of the larger forms, but discrete utterances—a series of clicks and tones that might be words. It is speaking to you. Or trying to. It has not encountered anything like you before, and it is attempting to find a common language.

It fails. Your hand goes numb from the cold, and you must withdraw it. The dweller releases you with something you can only interpret as reluctance. It retreats into its niche. The sounds stop.

But as you stand there, trying to feel your frozen fingers, you hear the dweller making sounds again—not to you this time, but to something else. Another dweller, emerging from a different niche. They exchange a rapid series of clicks. The tone carries information—you can hear the difference between one pattern and another, can almost parse meaning from the rhythm.

They are talking about you.

This revelation hits you with the force of genuine surprise. These are not simply animals. They are people. Different people, people who evolved in a world without light or heat, people whose bodies and minds are shaped by conditions you can barely survive—but people nonetheless. They have language. They have homes. They have opinions about the strange warm thing that just blundered into their space.

You cannot know what they are saying. Perhaps they are discussing whether you are dangerous. Perhaps they are wondering where you came from. Perhaps they are simply marveling at the novelty.

Or perhaps—considering the way the sounds stop abruptly, considering the way the niches go quiet—one of them has just asked a question that no one can answer.

Perhaps one of them has asked: *What does it mean, to be warm?*

## What Lives Here, Finally

The Weeping City is not empty. It is the opposite of empty—so full that the boundaries between inhabitants and environment have dissolved. The stone is alive with infrastructure. The infrastructure is alive with fauna. The fauna are alive with each other, their bodies interpenetrating in the press of passages, their purposes interlocking in ways that add up to something greater than any individual life.

But if you had to name what truly lives here—what this place is, in its totality, when you have walked its passages long enough to feel the shape of the whole—you would say:

*The plateau lives.*

The city is its body. The fauna are its cells. The water is its blood, the infrastructure its nerves, the deep nodes its organs. The dwellers are its thoughts—scattered, distributed, but thinking in their aggregate about things you will never understand. The whole vast plateau, extending farther than you can travel, rising into darkness that goes on forever—this is a single living thing.

You are walking through its skin. You are touching its sweat. You are listening to its heartbeat, loud enough to drown out your own.

And it does not mind you. Does not welcome you, does not reject you. You are too small to matter to a living thing of this scale. You are a microbe on the surface of something that has never evolved an immune response, because it has never needed one.

The weeping continues. The water runs. The fauna hum. The dwellers click to each other in their niches, still discussing you, still wondering.

And you walk on, wet and cold and blind, deeper into the body of something that was ancient before your species learned to walk upright—and will be ancient long after your species has become nothing but another layer in its stony memory.
