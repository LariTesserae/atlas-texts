# What Lives in the Thermal Garden

The bacteria came first. They were here before the ice withdrew, in fact—dwelling in the marginal zones where thermal vents met glacial margins, living in the narrow interface between freezing meltwater and boiling fissures, survivors of a much older ecosystem that existed before the mountain was covered, before the ice came in the first place. Some of them remember that time. Not in any way that we would recognize as memory, but in their structures, their chemical pathways, the shapes they take. They carry within them the echo of an ecosystem that existed millions of years ago, a world that is gone and has been gone for longer than any animal has been alive, and when the ice retreated they simply expanded, spreading outward from their refuges into the newly exposed territory as if they had been waiting. Which they had. Which is what bacteria do—they wait, and when conditions change, they grow.

The thermophiles cluster around the hottest vents, the places where steam emerges at temperatures near boiling, where the water would scald any animal that touched it. They form mats, these bacteria, layers and layers of them building up over the years since the ice withdrew, each generation laying down its material over the remains of the last. The mats are thick now in places—centimeters deep, soft and yielding, giving slightly underfoot like wet carpet, though no foot has ever stepped on them. They are colored by the minerals they process: orange where they metabolize iron, yellow where they oxidize sulfur, green where photosynthetic bacteria have managed to establish themselves in the limited light, creating a patchwork across the wet ground that looks deliberate, designed, a map of chemical processes made visible.

The bacteria do not know they are here. They do not know anything in the way that knowing is understood by those who have nervous systems. But they respond. They respond to temperature, to chemical concentration, to the presence of the minerals they need and the absence of those they cannot tolerate. They respond to the cycles of the geyser by shifting their position, their growth patterns, their rate of metabolism. They are aware, in their way, of the rhythms of this place, and they have organized themselves accordingly, finding the thermal gradients that suit each species and occupying them with a precision that looks like intelligence. It is not intelligence. It is something older and simpler than intelligence, a way of being in the world that does not require understanding, only response.

The mats have a smell, though no one has ever smelled them. They smell of sulfur, of iron, of the particular musty tang of bacterial metabolism, the waste products of billions upon billions of single-celled organisms processing energy from chemical bonds, releasing gases as they work. The smell mingles with the steam and becomes part of the atmosphere of the cirque, part of the chemical signature of the place, another element in the complex mixture that makes the air here different from the air anywhere else on the mountain.

Above the mats, where the ground is merely damp rather than actively steaming, the mosses have established themselves. They arrived as spores, carried on the wind that funneled into the cirque in the first years after the ice withdrew, and they found conditions that suited them: constant moisture, warmth from below, protection from the harsh UV radiation at this altitude. The mosses are not individual plants in the way that trees are individual. Each patch is a colony, millions of tiny plants growing so close together that they form a single surface, a continuous skin of vegetation that coats the rock and creeps across the mineral formations with a patience that transcends any individual lifespan. The mosses grow a few millimeters a year, extending their edges, filling in gaps, colonizing new surfaces when old ones become unsuitable. They have been doing this since they arrived, and in the decades since the ice withdrew, they have covered nearly every available surface in the cirque, blanketing the stone in a thick soft layer that absorbs sound as well as water.

The mosses are dark green in the few hours when enough light filters down to reveal their color, but in the perpetual twilight they read as black, as absence, as if the rock were simply not there beneath them. They are interrupted in places by mineral staining—streaks of orange where iron-rich water flows over them, patches of red where sulfur deposits form, spots of white where condensation has left its mark—and these stains are not harmful to the moss. The mosses have adapted to the chemical environment, tolerating levels of acidity and mineral concentration that would kill their relatives in gentler places. They incorporate the minerals into their structures, which is why the patches look different from one another depending on where they grow, each one a record of the particular chemistry of its location, a living map of the thermal garden's internal variations.

The mosses feel the steam more than they feel anything else. They orient themselves toward the heat, growing more thickly on the sides of rocks that face the vents, thinning out on the cooler faces. They are not moving toward anything, not seeking, not desiring—they are simply growing more successfully where conditions are more favorable, and the cumulative effect of thousands upon thousands of individual growth decisions is a pattern that looks purposeful. The mosses have no purpose. They simply grow, and in growing they fill the space available to them, and in filling that space they create the environment for the other organisms that have made this place their home.

Liverworts came later, or perhaps they came at the same time as the mosses but spread more slowly. They are here now regardless, occupying the dampest zones, the places where water pools and trickles and the ground is more mud than rock. They grow differently than the mosses—not in dense carpets but in overlapping plates, each thallus a flattened sheet of tissue that spreads across the surface and presses against its neighbors, forming a tiled pattern that looks artificial, deliberately arranged. The liverworts are thicker than the mosses, fleshier, more substantial. They hold water in their tissues in ways that the mosses cannot, and this allows them to survive in zones that flood periodically, where the mineral water rises and falls with the geyser's eruptions, where other plants would be scoured away or drowned.

The liverworts have a color that is difficult to describe because it changes with their water content. When saturated, they are a deep rich green that verges on black. When they dry slightly between floodings, they become paler, almost translucent in places, and their internal structures become visible—complex networks of cells and channels that transport water and nutrients through the thallus. They are ancient organisms, liverworts, among the first plants to evolve on land, and they have not changed much in the hundreds of millions of years since. They are well-suited to marginal environments, to places where conditions fluctuate, to zones where other plants cannot compete. In this thermal garden, they have found a niche that suits them perfectly, and they occupy it with the quiet persistence of a form that has survived mass extinctions, continental rearrangements, and the rise and fall of entire ecosystems.

The ferns are the most visible inhabitants of the cirque, the ones that would catch the eye if there were eyes here to see them. They rise from the moss and liverwort layers in spirals, their fronds uncurling in the perpetual warmth, reaching upward in fronds that can grow a meter long in the deeper shade, longer where the steam is thicker. They are pale—nearly white in the dim light—and their leaves are thin enough that the veins show through, pale blue traces against translucent tissue. They are not albino, not exactly. They have chlorophyll, but very little of it, and what they have is masked by other pigments that have evolved to protect them from the mineral-rich water and the occasional burst of intense UV that penetrates the cloud layer at this altitude. They are adapted to a world without much light, and they have found another source of energy: the heat itself.

This is not photosynthesis as it is understood in other places. The ferns have symbiotic relationships with bacteria that live in their roots, bacteria that can metabolize sulfur compounds and release energy that the ferns can use. It is a partnership that evolved independently in several places on earth, but rarely as successfully as here. The ferns draw energy from the bacterial processing of minerals, and the bacteria benefit from the structures the ferns provide, the stable environment, the access to water and dissolved gases. Together they form a system that needs no sun, that can thrive in perpetual twilight, that has created a forest of pale fronds in a place where no forest should be able to grow.

The ferns move in ways that plants in other places do not. They grow toward the steam, orienting themselves toward the heat sources, and over time they shift their position as the vents change. A fern that establishes itself near a vent may find that vent dying after a few years, the mineral deposits sealing the crack, and it will send out new fronds toward the nearest living vent, slowly migrating across the cirque floor in a kind of slow-motion pursuit of warmth. The older fronds die and decay, feeding the fungi, while the new ones reach ever further. Some ferns have been tracked—by the patterns they leave in the moss, the trails of dead stolon and decaying matter—moving ten meters or more over the decades, following the thermal system as it shifts beneath the surface.

The ferns are sensitive in ways that are difficult to explain. They respond to the geyser's eruptions, their fronds orienting toward the source of the steam, their pores opening to catch the moisture and the minerals it carries. They respond to changes in temperature, growing faster during warm periods, slowing or stopping when cold air descends into the cirque. They respond to the presence of other ferns, growing more densely in some areas and thinning out in others, creating patterns that look like deliberate spacing but are actually the result of competition for resources, for space, for access to the steam. They are not conscious. They are not aware. But they are responsive, and their responses create the appearance of intention, of design, of a kind of intelligence that operates on a timescale too slow for humans to perceive directly.

In the dark zones, the places where the steam is thickest and the light is lowest, the fungi dominate. They have found a niche in the organic matter that accumulates in the thermal garden—dead fronds, decaying moss, the remains of liverwort thalli, all of it breaking down in the warm damp environment and providing food for organisms that can process it. The fungi are many things here: shelf fungi that bracket the mineral formations, growing in ridged layers that add new growth each year; thread-fungi that spread across damp surfaces in pale webs, connecting everything to everything else; mushrooms that grow in dense clusters in the darkest corners, pressing against each other until they form mounds of tissue that look like a single organism but are actually thousands.

The shelf fungi are the most visible. They grow on the mineral formations, on the rock faces, on the ledges where steam condenses and runs down in streams. They are orange and purple and white, their colors coming from the minerals they process, the compounds they accumulate in their tissues as they break down the organic matter that feeds them. They are patient, growing in slow layers, adding millimeters each year to their brackets, extending outward into the air in ridges and shelves that catch the condensation and direct it toward their growing edges. Some of them are decades old now, established in the first years after the ice withdrew, and they have built up complex structures over the years, layer upon layer of tissue that records the history of the place in its patterns of growth.

The thread-fungi are less visible but more extensive. They spread across every damp surface in the cirque, forming networks of hyphae that connect the moss to the liverworts to the ferns to the bacterial mats, creating a web of biological connection that underlies everything visible in the thermal garden. They are the infrastructure of the ecosystem, breaking down organic matter and releasing nutrients that the other organisms can use, transporting water and minerals from one zone to another, communicating information about changes in the environment through chemical signals that pass along their networks. They are not conscious. They are not aware. But they connect everything to everything else, and in connecting they create a system that responds as a whole to the changes that affect any part of it.

The mushrooms are the reproductive structures of some of these thread-fungi, and they emerge when conditions are right—when the humidity reaches a certain level, when the temperature is stable, when enough organic matter has accumulated to support the energy cost of producing fruit. They grow in clusters because they are fed by the same network, drawing resources from the same source, and they press against each other as they grow because space is limited in the corners and crevices where they establish themselves. The result is mounds of mushrooms, hundreds of stems growing so close together that they cannot be separated, caps fused into continuous surfaces, gills overlapping and interweaving. From above, they look like a single organism. From below, they are revealed as thousands, each one growing from its own connection to the network below.

The mushrooms release spores when they mature, sending clouds of reproductive cells into the steam-filled air, where they are caught by the updrafts and carried throughout the cirque. Some of them land in suitable places and germinate, sending out new networks of hyphae that spread across available surfaces, looking for organic matter to process. Most of them do not land in suitable places—they fall on dry rock, on mineral formations, on surfaces too hot or too cold or too chemically extreme for growth. But enough of them find purchase that the fungi continue to spread, continue to colonize new surfaces, continue to process the organic matter that the other organisms produce.

The bacteria in the mats and the bacteria in the fern roots and the bacteria suspended in the steam and the bacteria coating the mineral formations are all connected by the thread-fungi, by the water that flows through the cirque, by the air that circulates in the confined space of the basin. They exchange genes, exchanging capabilities, creating new combinations of metabolic pathways that allow them to exploit new resources, survive new conditions. They are evolving constantly, faster than any organism with a nervous system could evolve, responding to changes in the environment with the flexibility of things that can reproduce every twenty minutes, that can swap genetic material with any other bacterium they encounter, that can go dormant for years or decades when conditions are unfavorable and revive when conditions improve.

This is what lives in the thermal garden: not individuals, not really, but systems. The fern is not separate from the bacteria in its roots, which are not separate from the fungi that connect them to the liverworts, which are not separate from the moss that provides the substrate for all of them to grow on. The bacterial mats are not separate from the water that flows over them, which is not separate from the minerals that dissolve in it, which is not separate from the steam that carries it. Everything connects to everything else, and the boundaries between organisms are not the clear boundaries that exist in other ecosystems. Here, in this place where the rules are different, where the energy comes from below rather than from above, the inhabitants have evolved to blur the lines between self and other, between one organism and the next, between the living and the non-living world.

The thermal garden does not need anything from outside. It is self-contained, self-sustaining, a closed loop of energy and matter that could continue indefinitely if the conditions remained stable. The heat from below provides energy. The minerals dissolved in the water provide nutrients. The organisms that live here provide the infrastructure for processing that energy and those nutrients, recycling everything, wasting nothing. The only inputs from outside are the thin air that flows into the cirque and the diffuse light that filters through the clouds—neither of which is essential. The garden could survive in absolute darkness, in the complete absence of atmosphere, as long as the thermal system below continued to provide heat and mineral-laden water. It is a world unto itself, a fragment of an older earth, a remnant of the ecosystems that existed before photosynthesis transformed the planet.

And yet the garden is not static. It changes constantly, growing and shifting and adapting, following the thermal system as it evolves, incorporating new organisms that arrive on the wind, losing organisms that cannot survive when conditions shift. It is alive in a way that is difficult to explain to those who think of life as individual organisms with clear boundaries and defined lifespans. It is alive the way a forest is alive, the way a coral reef is alive—the aggregate of thousands of organisms interacting with each other and with their environment, creating something that is more than the sum of its parts, something that has characteristics that none of its components possess individually.

The thermal garden breathes. Not with lungs, not with any organ designed for the purpose, but with the geyser's eruptions, with the rise and fall of steam, with the daily cycle of condensation and evaporation that moves water through the system. The garden metabolizes. Not with a digestive system, not with any centralized processing, but with the bacterial mats and the fungal networks and the fern roots, all working together to process energy and materials, to grow and repair and reproduce. The garden responds. Not with a nervous system, not with any mechanism for thought, but with the collective responses of all its organisms, shifting their growth patterns, their metabolic rates, their distribution across the available space.

This is what lives in the thermal garden. Not a collection of individual plants and bacteria and fungi, but a single system, a single organism, a single entity that has been growing and changing and adapting since the ice withdrew and will continue to grow and change and adapt until the thermal system beneath it changes or fails or the mountain itself changes in some way that destroys the cirque. It is alive, and it is alone, and it is complete in itself, needing nothing, wanting nothing, expecting nothing. It simply is, and in being, it fills this space on the mountain with a presence that is not human, not animal, not even properly plant in the way that plants are understood. It is something else. Something older. Something that has found a way to exist in a place where nothing else could survive, and has made that place its own.
