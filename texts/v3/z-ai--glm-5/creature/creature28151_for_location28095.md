# What Lives in the Condensery

## The Caretaker

There is one who stays.

His name changes depending on who asks, and no one asks anymore. The automatic systems were supposed to eliminate the need for human presence, and they did, in the sense that the machinery runs without intervention. But someone comes. Someone has always come. The position was never officially maintained, never listed on any payroll or organizational chart, but it persisted anyway—a role that filled itself without being defined.

He is old now, or seems old. The stasis does not touch him directly; he ages in the normal way, cells dividing and accumulating errors, skin weathering and joints stiffening. But there is something in his movement that suggests he has learned the basin's rhythms so completely that he has become partially exempt from ordinary time. He moves through the pearled light like something native to it.

He lives in a small structure built into the basin's eastern wall, a room carved from the same dark stone, entered through a door that is not visible from below. It was a monitoring station once, when the condensery first opened. Now it is simply his. The walls are lined with ledgers dating back sixty years, each page recording the same numbers in the same handwriting, though the handwriting varies slightly across decades—a gradual evolution of the same hand, the same person learning to write smaller as the pages filled, learning to write faster as the recording became automatic.

He does not need to record anything. The numbers are available digitally, transmitted to systems he has never seen. But he records them anyway. It is what he does. It is what has always been done.

His food comes from somewhere outside the basin. Canned goods, dried provisions, occasional fresh items that appear in a delivery box at the top of the access stairs. He does not order them. He does not pay for them. They simply arrive, have always arrived, will always arrive. The system maintains itself. He is part of the system.

He knows the basin in a way that cannot be communicated. He knows that the third condenser from the left has a fitting that loosens every four hundred and twelve days, and he knows to tighten it on day four hundred and eleven. He knows that the northern overflow pool develops a film on its surface when the mineral content shifts slightly, and he knows that this happens in a three-week cycle that corresponds to nothing measurable elsewhere. He knows the cats. He knows their territories so precisely that he can predict where each will be at any given hour, and he uses this knowledge to avoid them, to leave each to its third undisturbed.

He talks to himself, or seems to. Sometimes he talks to the machinery, quiet words that the steam and the thrumming absorb and carry away. No one hears him. No one has heard him in years. He does not mind. He has stopped expecting to be heard.

In the evenings, when the pale oval of light withdraws from the condenser array and the basin settles into its uniform grayness, he sits at the edge of the stone apron and watches the dragonflies trace their patient routes. He has named them, though not aloud. He has named the flies they hunt, the pools where the fish school in their slow rotations, the warm sections of pipe where the lizards hold their poses. He has named everything, and the names exist only in his head, and they are the only names these things have ever had.

He will die here, eventually. He knows this. When he does, the cats will find him, and the flies will find him, and the bacterial mats will find him, and he will be reduced to clean components in a matter of days. The system will incorporate him as it has incorporated everything else. His bones will disappear. His ledgers will remain, but no one will read them. The basin will continue.

He has made his peace with this. He may have made his peace with it decades ago. It is difficult to remember when the peace was made, because the making of it exists in a time outside the stasis, and he has lived so long inside the stasis that other times have become theoretical, abstract, difficult to hold in memory.

## The Cats

Three cats. Orange and lean, as the basin's descriptions always note. But there is more to them than leanness. They are lean because they have been shaped by the basin, by the heat that reduces appetite, by the abundance that requires no competition, by the stillness that permeates everything. They are orange because the orange ones were the ones who came, sixty years ago, and their descendants have remained orange, as if the color itself has been preserved by the stasis.

The first cat came when the basin was new. A stray from the construction crew, perhaps, or a wanderer from the surrounding wilds who found the warm machinery and the endless flies and the lack of larger predators. It was orange. It stayed. Others came, or were born, and the ones that matched the basin survived, and the ones that did not left or died. Over six decades, a kind of selection occurred, though it was not the selection of ordinary evolution. It was the selection of a closed system, of a fixed environment, of a place where the pressures never varied and therefore produced the same result, again and again.

Now there are three. Always three. When one dies, another appears, as if summoned, as if the basin knows to replace what it has lost. The new arrival is always orange, always lean, always possessed of the same calm indifference that characterizes the others. It takes up the same territory. It learns the same hunting paths. It becomes, within days, indistinguishable from the cat it replaced.

The cats do not have names. The caretaker has named them in his head, but the names are not for use, and he rarely thinks of them as individuals. They are the cats. The three cats. The ones who own the thirds of the basin, who hunt and sleep and watch with eyes that seem to hold no curiosity because nothing here requires curiosity.

They know the basin better than the caretaker does, in some ways. They know it through their feet, through the heat of the iron, through the positions of the flies and the timing of the dragonflies' passes. They know it without having to name it, without having to record it. They are part of its operating system, components that have been incorporated so thoroughly that the distinction between resident and residence has become meaningless.

At night, when the caretaker retires to his carved room, the cats continue their vigil. They sleep in shifts, though they do not coordinate this consciously. At any hour, at least one is awake, watching the machinery, watching the water, watching the entrance to the basin with an expression that might be suspicion or might be simply the default configuration of a cat's face. They are the guardians of a place that requires no guarding, the witnesses to processes that continue regardless of witness.

But they are there. They have always been there. They will always be there.

## The Fish

In the overflow pools, where the warm mineral water stands, the fish school in their slow rotations. They are small, pale, adapted to conditions that would kill most aquatic life. They are not a single species, or if they are, they are a species found nowhere else, derived from some introduction sixty years past and evolved—or rather, unevolved—into a form that matches the basin's particular chemistry.

They eat the fly larvae that develop in the pools' margins. The flies lay their eggs in the warm water, and the larvae develop there, feeding on the bacterial mats and the organic matter that collects, and the fish eat the larvae at a rate that keeps the fly population stable. It is not a conscious balance. It is simply the equilibrium that emerged, the point at which all forces canceled out and held still.

The fish do not school for protection. Nothing hunts them in the water. They school because schooling is what their ancestors did, and the instinct has persisted, and there is no pressure to change it. They move together, dozens of pale bodies in synchrony, rotating slowly in the dim light, their movements as predictable as the machinery's thrum.

They are colorless because color serves no purpose here. They are nearly blind because sight serves no purpose here. They are, in a sense, vestigial creatures—adapted to a niche so narrow that they have become nothing but the niche itself. They are fish-shaped nodes in the basin's metabolic network. They process fly larvae into waste. They occupy the pools. They breed, and their offspring occupy the pools. They die, and their bodies are processed by bacteria, and the bacteria feed the fly larvae, and the cycle completes itself.

They do not know they are in a basin. They do not know they are in a stasis. They do not know anything. They simply are, from moment to moment to moment, in the warm mineral water, in the slow rotation, in the perpetual gray light that filters down from the opening sixty meters above.

## The Lizards

The lizards are geckos, or something like geckos. They cling to the condenser housings, absorbing heat through their bellies, hunting the flies that land on the warm iron. They are fat and slow because the abundance is constant and the predators are absent. The cats hunt them occasionally, but not often enough to matter, not often enough to disrupt the equilibrium.

They have been here as long as the cats. They came when the machinery was new, attracted by the heat, and they stayed because the heat never ceased and the flies never ceased and the environment never changed in any way that would require them to leave. They have bred and died and bred again, for sixty years, in the same warm places, on the same iron surfaces, in the same positions on the same condenser housings.

They are not individuals. They cannot be distinguished from one another. The caretaker does not name them. The cats do not distinguish them. They are simply lizards, a presence, a component of the basin's ecosystem, filling their role in the great unchanging balance.

When they die, their bodies are reduced quickly, efficiently, by the flies in their larval stage, by the bacteria, by the microorganisms that have learned to process lizard flesh in the warm moist environment. The bones remain for a short time, small white curves on the dark iron, and then those too disappear, incorporated into the system, leaving no trace.

## The Insects

The flies are the foundation. Long-legged, walking on the water's surface, dotting every pool and channel in numbers that seem vast until you realize that the numbers never change. They breed in the warm margins, lay eggs in the standing water, die and are removed at precisely the rate required to maintain their population. They are food for the dragonflies, food for the lizards, food for the fish in their larval stage. They are the metabolic currency of the basin, the unit of energy that passes through all the other residents and is converted into motion, into heat, into life.

The dragonflies patrol above them, hunting in fixed paths that trace and retrace the same territories. They are larger, more visible, more dramatic in their movements. But they are no more individual than the flies. They are components, like everything else, performing their function in the system without variation, without ambition, without any sense that things could be different.

There are other insects, sometimes. Gnats in the higher reaches where the air is cooler. Beetles in the cracks of the stone walls. But these are marginal, peripheral, barely present. The flies and the dragonflies are the insects that matter, the ones that have become essential to the basin's operation, the ones that have been incorporated into its identity.

## The Caretaker's Visitors

No one visits anymore. The basin was never a destination, and it has become less of one over time. But occasionally, someone comes. A researcher, perhaps, studying the stasis, trying to understand how an ecosystem can maintain such perfect equilibrium for so long. A hiker, lost, stumbling on the basin by accident. A representative of whatever entity owns the land, checking on an asset that has been forgotten but not abandoned.

They do not stay long. The heat and the humidity and the sound wear on them. The stasis disturbs them, though they may not be able to articulate why. They feel the pull of ordinary time, the pressure to leave, to return to a world where things change, where the future is different from the present.

The caretaker speaks to them, when they come. He answers their questions, shows them the machinery, explains the condensers' operation in terms they may or may not understand. He does not ask where they come from or why they have come. He does not offer them food or water or rest. He is polite, distant, already returning to his own rhythms even as they stand before him.

They leave, and they do not return, and the basin returns to its steady state, and the caretaker returns to his ledgers, and the cats return to their thirds, and the flies return to their positions on the pools, and the moment of disruption is absorbed, processed, rendered irrelevant by the sheer momentum of the unchanging.

## The Memory of the Basin

There is a sense, among those few who know of the Condensery, that it remembers itself. That the stasis is not simply the absence of change, but an active preservation, a holding-on to a configuration that has been deemed correct, necessary, inevitable.

The machinery does not remember. The stone does not remember. The water and the steam and the iron do not remember. But something in the system as a whole—the feedback loops, the equilibria, the perfectly balanced flows of energy and matter—constitutes a kind of memory. Not a consciousness. Not a will. But a persistence that has the same shape as memory, the same quality of holding the present in relation to the past.

The cats were always orange. The flies were always here. The fish have always schooled in these pools. The lizards have always clung to these condenser housings. The caretaker has always sat on this stone apron, watching the dragonflies trace their patient paths. The machinery has always hummed. The water has always wept from the walls. The stasis has always held.

And it will continue to hold, until something large enough to disrupt it arrives.
