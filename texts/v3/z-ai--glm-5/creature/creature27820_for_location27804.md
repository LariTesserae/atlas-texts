# The Residents

They are not what you would expect.

---

The first thing to understand is that no one is born here.

Oh, children are born— the maternity ward at the Municipal Hospital sees to that, three or four arrivals per week, cat-slide in the steam-smeared windows—but they don't stay children for long. Not in the way children stay children elsewhere. The Quarter's children grow at the rate of their surroundings, which is to say faster than seems right, and with more uncertainty about their final shape.

A child born in the lowlands and brought up on the cable railway at age six will have a different relationship to the city than a child born in the Quarter itself. The imported children retain the shape their origins gave them. They remain, essentially, lowland children in miniature— certain of what a wall is, what a street means, what the relationship between a door and its frame ought to be. They are useful children, later, for the work that requires precision. They become the engineers, the measurers, the ones who keep the ledgers.

The children born here are different. They are native to the indecision. They navigate the soft spots without noticing them. They play games in streets that will not commit to being level, and they never stumble. Their bodies grow in sympathy with the growing mountain— taller, often, than their parents, their bones slightly more porous, their blood carrying something that isn't quite hemoglobin but serves the same purpose with greater efficiency at altitude. They do not experience altitude sickness. They do not, strictly speaking, experience the altitude at all. They belong to it.

When they descend— if they descend— to the lowland cities for education or work, they grow ill. Not dramatically, not dangerously, but persistently. A queasy wrongness, as though their bodies know they have been taken somewhere too solid. They recover slowly, and they never quite acclimate. They write letters home that speak of a suffocation they cannot name, a sense that the ground beneath them has finished too early, has closed off possibilities they did not know they were counting on. Most of them return. The ones who don't become something else— lowland people, eventually, their Quarter-born strangeness settling into mere eccentricity. They are the ones who tell stories nobody quite believes.

---

The adults who come here come for reasons they cannot always articulate.

There is work, yes. The mining operation employs hundreds, and the processing facilities hundreds more, and the cable railway requires its crews, and the municipal government its functionaries. But work alone does not explain the particular quality of the Quarter's adult population. There is work in a hundred grim industrial towns, places of soot and struggle where the young leave and the old remain out of inertia. The Quarter is different. People choose it.

They come from somewhere else— the lowland cities, the coastal towns, the agricultural valleys far below— and they arrive with a set of expectations about what a person is supposed to be. They have professions. They have histories. They have, often, wounds: failures of vocation, failures of love, failures of nerve. They have come to the Quarter because they have heard rumors, or because they knew someone who knew someone, or because they looked at a map and felt the altitude calling to them like a finger pressing against their chest.

The first weeks are difficult. The breath comes short. The food tastes of stone. The ground shifts beneath the bed. Newcomers wake in the night certain that the building is falling, only to find that it has merely settled— which here means both "come to rest" and "decided to be something different." The soft spots find them. They walk down a street and lose an hour, or find themselves on a different street with no memory of turning, or stand in their kitchen watching the wall breathe and wonder if they are losing their minds.

Most of them leave. This is not spoken of as failure. The cable railway carries down a steady stream of people who tried the Quarter and found it incompatible with their constitution. They return to the lowlands shaken, vaguely embarrassed, unable to explain what drove them away. They describe it to their friends as "the altitude" or "the isolation" or "the work," and their friends nod and accept the explanation, and nobody mentions the way the returning traveler's eyes slide away from certain questions, the way they flinch when they see spiral patterns in ordinary moss.

But some of them stay.

---

The ones who stay have a look.

It is not a single look, not a uniform— the miners have their own weathered quality, the engineers their precise habits, the botanists their distracted intensity— but there is something shared beneath the surface differences. A tolerance for ambiguity that has calcified into a kind of faith. They have learned to live in a place that has not finished being itself, and in learning this, they have themselves become unfinished in useful ways.

They are not unreliable. Quite the opposite: they are the most reliable people you will ever meet, in the ways that matter. They show up. They do the work. They keep their promises with a ferocity that borders on desperation. What they do not do is finish. Their projects remain perpetually in progress. Their houses have rooms that are "being renovated" in a state that has not changed for a decade. Their conversations circle around conclusions without landing. Their relationships persist in a state of mutual becoming that never resolves into marriage or separation, simply continues, year after year, as two people gradually shape each other into something neither of them expected.

They have a word for this in the local dialect, which has evolved over forty years into something distinct from the lowland tongue. The word is *havri*, and it translates roughly to "the state of being temporarily permanent." A *havri* resident is one who has chosen to stay but has not committed to having stayed. A *havri* relationship is one that continues without the assumption that it will end. A *havri* life is one that accumulates meaning without demanding finality.

The oldest residents— the surveyors who came with the original geological assessment, the engineers who built the cable railway, the miners who first excavated the strange ores— are the most *havri* of all. They have been here since the beginning, and they will be here until the end, and they have no opinion about when the end might be. They have stopped thinking in terms of departure. They have also stopped thinking in terms of permanence. They exist in the Quarter the way the Quarter exists in the mountain: as a feature of something still becoming, necessary to the process, uncertain in its ultimate form.

---

There is a woman who runs the measurement office.

Her name is Mother Aldric, though she is nobody's mother, and the title is honorific, earned through decades of attention. She came up the cable railway as a young surveyor's assistant forty-three years ago, and she has been measuring the mountain's movement ever since. Her office is in the basement of the Municipal Building, where the foundation is anchored deepest into the bedrock, and the walls are lined with ledgers going back to the Quarter's founding.

Every Thursday, she walks the eastern escarpment with her instruments. She measures the tilt of the spires, the width of the gaps, the angle of the slopes. She records the data in ledgers that only she can read, because she has developed her own notation, a way of marking not just position but tendency— not just where the rock is, but where it seems to want to go. She can look at a formation and tell you, with unsettling accuracy, what it will look like in a year, or a decade. She has predicted subsidence events that the engineers missed. She has identified soft spots before anyone reported them. She says the mountain speaks to her, and she means it literally, not metaphorically, and nobody who has watched her work doubts her.

She is old now. Her spine has curved in the same spiral pattern as the moss, and her eyes have the pale intensity of the local hawks, and her hands are stained with the dust of rock that has not finished being rock. She does not intend to retire. She does not intend to die, particularly— she just intends to continue, the way the mountain continues, slowly and in sympathy with the forces that shaped her.

When newcomers ask her how long she plans to stay, she looks at them with her pale eyes and says, "Until I know what this place is." And then she adds, "It hasn't told me yet."

She has been saying this for thirty years.

---

There is a man who tends the cable railway's upper terminus.

His name is Dolvin, and he is not an engineer, not a miner, not a measurer. He is simply the man who greets the cars as they arrive and waves them off as they depart. He has lived in the terminus building for twenty-two years— he has a cot in the back room, a hot plate, a collection of books about geology that he reads without fully understanding. He is not paid a salary. The railway company long ago gave up trying to figure out who authorizes his presence; he is simply there, a feature of the terminus, as much a part of the machinery as the cables and gears.

He is not a large man. He is not a remarkable man. But he has a quality that newcomers notice immediately, which is that he does not seem entirely present. He is friendly and helpful and eager to chat, and he remembers every face that passes through his terminus, and he can tell you the history of the railway in precise detail, and he is always, always, slightly translucent. Not in a way you can point to— not in a way that would show up in a photograph— but in the way that a person is translucent when they are standing in front of a bright window, their edges a little uncertain, their features a little softer than they ought to be.

The children think he is a ghost. The adults know better, because ghosts do not eat or sleep or complain about the damp, and Dolvin does all three. He is simply a man who has been in the Quarter too long, who has absorbed too much of its indecision into his own being. He has become slightly less solid than a person ought to be. He has become the kind of man who might, if you looked at him from the wrong angle, not quite be there.

He knows this. He has made his peace with it. "I'll solidify when the mountain does," he says, when anyone asks, and he laughs, and his laugh is a little too faint, as though it has to travel through more distance than the air between you.

---

There is a child who was born in the soft spot on Carning Street.

Her name is Ves, and she is eleven years old, and she does not know she was born in a soft spot because no one has told her. Her parents know— they were living on Carning Street when she arrived, three weeks early, in an apartment that has since been condemned because the walls kept shifting during the night. They moved to a different street, a harder street, a street where reality had finished making up its mind. But Ves had already been shaped by her arrival.

She is not less real than other children. She is differently real. When she tells you something, you have to pay attention, because the thing she tells you might become true in a way that ordinary statements do not. Not because she is lying— she is an honest child, scrupulously so— but because the soft spot where she was born left a mark on her, gave her a connection to the mountain's process of becoming that runs deeper than language.

She says things like "I think the rock wants to be a garden" and a week later, a patch of the spiral moss begins to grow in a formation that looks like deliberate cultivation. She says "the goats are waiting for something" and the goats gather at the eastern edge of town and stand there for three days, patient and unmoving, until something shifts in the mountain and they disperse as though a signal has passed. She says "I had a dream about a street that doesn't exist yet" and the surveyors find a new fissure opening exactly where she described, a gap that will become a passage when the rock decides what it wants to be.

Her parents are proud of her and terrified of her in equal measure. They love her the way you love a child who might grow up to be extraordinary or might grow up to be something you do not have a word for. They have discussed sending her to the lowlands, to the universities, where people study children like her, but they cannot bring themselves to do it. She would wilt there. She would fade. She belongs to the Quarter in a way that cannot be transplanted.

Ves does not know any of this. She is simply a child who lives in a city that is still becoming, and she is becoming alongside it, and she assumes that everyone else is doing the same. She catches the glowing insects in jars and releases them before they die. She feeds the goats from her hand. She walks through the soft spots without noticing them, without ever getting lost, without ever losing an hour or taking a wrong turn. The soft spots know her. They open for her. They always have.

---

There are others. There are always others.

There is the blacksmith who forges the processed ore into ingots and has developed a technique for reading the metal's future, for seeing what the alloy will become before it has cooled. There are the three sisters who run the boarding house near the eastern escarpment, who have been in the Quarter longer than anyone can remember, who have not aged in any way that shows, who speak in unison when they are tired and finish each other's sentences when they are not. There is the surveyor who went into the high reaches above the terminus and came back with his eyes changed, the brown gone to gold, seeing things that others cannot see, refusing to discuss what he encountered on the slopes where the mountain is newest.

There are the ordinary people, too, the ones who came for work and stayed because the work was good and the pay was better and the altitude eventually stopped making them breathless. They are the majority, the background, the population that makes the Quarter a city rather than an asylum. They have their gardens and their grievances and their municipal politics. They argue about tram schedules. They complain about the smoke. They go to the market on Saturdays and buy tubers and spiral moss and meat from the goats that are culled when the herds grow too large. They fall in love and out of love and sometimes back into love again. They have children who grow up strange and children who grow up ordinary and children who leave and children who stay.

They are not unaffected by the Quarter. No one is unaffected by the Quarter. But they are functional, which is its own kind of strength. They live in a place that has not finished being, and they have learned to be people who have not finished being, and they have made that work.

---

And then there are the ones who were always here.

This is not something the residents discuss. It is not something the visitors notice. But if you stay in the Quarter long enough, if you walk its streets at the hours when the light is strangest, if you pay attention to the corners where the soft spots cluster, you begin to see them.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts are remnants of what was. These are remnants of what might have been.

They are the people the mountain considered making before it settled on the current configuration. They are the residents who existed in the potential the Quarter held before it became the Quarter. They walk the streets that will exist in a thousand years, when the geology has finished its current phase and moved on to the next. They live in the buildings that are being built in the mountain's imagination, the structures that will rise when the current ones have subsided into the slopes.

You cannot speak to them. You can barely see them— just a flicker at the edge of perception, a sense of a figure standing where no figure should be, a doorway that exists for a moment and then is gone. But they are there. The Quarter is full of them, crowded with its own potential population, layered with its possible futures. Every soft spot is a place where one of them almost emerged, where a might-have-been person nearly crystallized into being before the mountain changed its mind.

The children can see them sometimes. Ves has described them, matter-of-factly, as "the ones who aren't yet." The adults pretend not to hear. They have learned not to look too closely at the soft spots, not to pay attention to the flickers at the edge of their vision, not to wonder about the people who might exist if the rock decided differently.

They are not dangerous, these almost-people. They are simply present, the way everything in the Quarter is present: provisionally, potentially, in sympathy with the forces that are still making up their minds. They are part of the population. They are not counted in the census, but they are there, and they have always been there, and they will be there long after the current residents have become someone else's might-have-beens.

---

The Quarter's residents live in a city that has not finished being built on a mountain that has not finished rising in a world that has not finished deciding what it wants to be.

They are the people who chose uncertainty. They are the people who looked at a place that was still becoming and said, I will become alongside it. They are engineers and miners and botanists and mothers and children and old surveyors with curved spines and translucent men in railway termini and girls who were born in soft spots and sisters who speak in unison and blacksmiths who read the future in cooling metal and surveyors with gold eyes who have seen the things that move in the mist above the cable railway's highest point.

They are ordinary people living ordinary lives in a place where nothing is quite ordinary, where the rock sings at dusk and the plants grow in spirals and the goats watch with knowing eyes and the ground shifts by centimeters per year and reality itself has not quite hardened into certainty.

They are *havri*. They are temporarily permanent. They are staying until they know what this place is.

The place, of course, is staying until it knows what they are, too.
