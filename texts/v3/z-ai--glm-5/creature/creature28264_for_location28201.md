# Those Who Remain

They are not old in the way people in towns are old. Town-old is a condition of diminishment, a slowing from some remembered velocity. These people have no memory of velocity. They were born into slowness or arrived here before slowness became a choice, and now their age is something else: a geology, an accumulation. Their faces have the same weathered roundness as the building-stones. Their hands know the temperature of things without touching them.

---

There are five. Or four and a presence. The count depends on whether you include Màiri, and whether you count by breath or by weight or by the space a body makes in a room when it sits in the same chair at the same hour, decade after decade, until the chair's shape remembers the body better than the body remembers itself.

Call it five.

---

Donnachadh came first, or his father did, or his father's father—the story has worn down to its spine, the details lost to the same wind that carries everything else west. He lives in the easternmost building, the one with the best roof and the door that still closes. He is the one who tends the goats, or tolerates them, or exists in a negotiated peace with them. The distinction between ownership and cohabitation has never mattered here. The goats browse where they please. Donnachadh knows where they browse and walks those same paths later, checking without interfering, observing without judgment. He has a relationship with the animals that resembles a very long marriage in which both parties have largely forgotten the other is separate from themselves.

His hands are large and pale, the knuckles swollen in a way that suggests work done in cold water. He does not speak much, not from reticence but from a kind of efficiency—words are stones, he seems to think, and should not be moved unless they need to be moved. When he does speak, his voice is low and flat, tuned to the frequency of the wind so that it carries only as far as necessary. He says things like "rain before dark" or "the grey one's favouring her left" and these statements contain all the information they need to contain, nothing more.

He remembers more than he lets on. He has a memory for weather that predates his own lifetime—storms described by his grandfather, droughts measured by the depth of the burn, the winter the stone at the yard's center cracked along its north face from the cold. These memories live in his hands as much as his head. He can feel weather coming in his left wrist, a deep ache that has never been wrong.

---

Òg is the youngest, which is a relative term. Forty, perhaps. Or fifty. Age calcifies differently here, where the days are so similar they stop counting as separate units and become instead a kind of continuous medium, like water or light. He arrived from somewhere else—this much is known because he still carries the posture of someone who has been elsewhere, a slight forward lean, as if he's still expecting to walk into a room and find it different from the room he left. The others do not have this posture. They have settled.

He lives in the structure nearest the burn, the one with the collapsed back wall that has become a kind of courtyard, open to the slope below. He sleeps in the intact front room and spends his days in the ruined section, watching the water, or the sky, or the intermediate distances where birds sometimes pass. He does not speak at all. This is not a vow or a condition, merely a habit that hardened into a fact. His throat works perfectly well; he has been heard to cough, to clear his throat when the fog sits heavy, to make small sounds of exertion when he lifts things. But he does not speak, and after a while, the others stopped expecting him to, and after a longer while, they stopped noticing that he didn't.

He grows the vegetables. Or rather, he tends the plots that produce the vegetables—the soil does most of the work, such work as soil can do at this altitude, and the vegetables grow or they don't, and Òg's interventions are minimal and precise: a redistribution of stones, a channel cut to guide condensation, the careful redistribution of the goats' leavings into the thinnest patches. He moves through the gardens with a slowness that makes his progress nearly invisible. If you watched him for an hour you might think he hadn't moved at all, but if you looked away and looked back, you would find him elsewhere, doing something else, his hands dirty and his expression unchanged.

He came from the south, from somewhere with a name. He walked here, alone, over the ridge, in the rain. He did not explain why and no one asked. That was years ago, or what passes for years here. He has not left since.

---

Màiri is the question. She lives in the central building, the one that faces the standing stone across the yard, and she is there most of the time, a stillness that might be age or might be something else. Her hair is white and long, pulled back in a way that was fashionable in her youth and is now merely practical. Her eyes are pale, a colour that shifts with the light, sometimes grey, sometimes a washed-out green, sometimes no colour at all. She does not move much. She sits in the doorway when the weather permits, watching the yard, or watching something in the yard that only she can see.

The others bring her food. They do this without announcement or ritual; they simply leave things near her hand—a boiled root, a piece of cheese made from the goats' milk and aged in the cool interiors of the storage structures, sometimes a bit of preserved meat from an animal that died naturally and was not wasted. She eats. Her hands work well enough. Her eyes track movement. Whether there is a mind behind those eyes, or what kind of mind, is not a question the others ask. She is Màiri. She is here. She has been here as long as any of them can remember, which means she may have been here before there was a "them" to remember.

Sometimes she speaks, but not to anyone present. Her mouth moves, forming words in a rhythm that suggests conversation—pauses for response, slight nods as if acknowledging a point made, occasional sounds that might be disagreement or amusement. What language this is, if it is a language, is unclear. It has the cadence of speech but not the recognizable shape. The others have learned not to listen, or rather, they have learned that listening yields nothing, and so they have stopped expecting it to.

She might be ninety. She might be older. She might have been young once, or she might have emerged into the world at this age, like a god in a story, already ancient. She does not discuss it. She does not discuss anything. But she watches, and sometimes she smiles at something, and sometimes she weeps without sound, and sometimes she sits so still that the moss might begin to grow on her the way it grows on the standing stone—north face only, shade-cast, patient.

---

The twins are not twins. They are two women who arrived together, decades ago, from the same place, walking at the same pace, and who have continued to move at the same pace ever since. They live in adjacent buildings and spend their days in each other's presence with a comfort that requires no acknowledgment. Their names might be Eachna and Enat, or those might be names for their grandmothers, passed down and applied to them by default. They answer to the names, or they do not answer but turn their heads slightly when the sounds are made, which serves the same purpose.

They are the keepers of the structures. Not the builders—no one builds here anymore—but the maintainers, the noticers of small shifts. They walk the perimeter of the crescent each morning, separately but simultaneously, starting from opposite ends and meeting in the middle, near the standing stone. They do not speak during these walks. They touch the walls as they pass, pressing a palm to the granite, feeling for movement. If a stone has shifted, they know. If a roof-beam has developed a new sag, they know. They make repairs without urgency, using materials that have been here longer than they have—stones found loose on the slope, timber salvaged from structures beyond repair, turf cut from the hillside in thin sheets and laid over gaps.

They are older than Òg, younger than Donnachadh, perhaps the same age as Màiri was when Màiri's age stopped being a meaningful measure. Their hands are permanently stained with soil and stone-dust, the creases filled with grit that no amount of washing removes. They move with a precision that seems almost choreographed—when one reaches for a tool, the other hands it to her without being asked; when one pauses to examine a crack in a wall, the other pauses too, waiting, watching, seeing the same crack from a different angle.

They do not speak much, but they can, when necessary. Their voices are similar—mid-range, unhurried, with an accent that belongs to this particular altitude and no other. They say things like "third course, loose" and "frost before morning" and "Màiri needs water." They do not say things like "I remember when" or "I wish" or "I hope." Those tenses do not seem to exist for them. There is only the present, repeating, and the present contains everything necessary.

---

The goats are not people, but they are inhabitants, and they know the settlement better than any visitor would. They are small and hardy, with coats the colour of the hillside—grey-brown, mottled, capable of disappearing against the granite and turf. They have lived here for generations, unmanaged and largely unmolested, browsing the scrub that grows in the shelter of the walls, drinking from the burn, sleeping in the lee of the ruined structures when the wind is hard.

They have names, after a fashion. Donnachadh knows them as individuals, distinguishes them by subtle variations in horn-shape and gait. He has not named them aloud, but in his mind they are the grey one, the lame one, the bold one, the one that watches. These designations shift over time as individuals die and new ones are born, but the roles remain constant, as if the goats are not discrete animals but positions in a structure, occupied sequentially by whoever is available.

They are not pets. They are not livestock. They are cohabitants. When a goat dies, it is left for the birds and the weather, or it is used, depending on the season and the need. The survivors browse on, unmarked by loss. They have no apparent concept of past or future. They exist entirely in the immediate, in the next mouthful, the next movement of air, the next patch of sun. In this they are not unlike the people.

---

There may be others. The settlement has space for others. The path shows wear in places that don't match the known patterns of the five, and sometimes, at the hours between dark and light, sounds emerge from structures that should be empty—a cough, a footfall, the particular creak of a door that cannot, by itself, swing. Whether these are echoes, or ghosts, or the simply the settlement remembering itself, no one investigates. The living have learned that some sounds are best left unexamined.

Outsiders come, occasionally. Walkers, lost and redirected. Researchers, drawn by the standing stone or the architecture or the peculiar quality of the place. They do not stay long. The settlement offers nothing that resembles hospitality—no inn, no shop, no welcome of any kind. The people do not turn them away, but they do not invite them in. They coexist for the hours or days the outsiders remain, and then they watch them leave, walking east over the ridge, diminishing into the grey-green distance, gone.

The people do not wonder why the outsiders leave. They do not wonder much about anything. Wonder requires a conception of alternatives, and alternatives have become difficult to imagine. This is the place. These are the people. The wind comes from the west. The water gathers and leaves. The stones hold each other up. The moss grows. The days repeat until they don't, and then there is another day, indistinguishable from the one before, and that is sufficient.

It has always been sufficient. It will continue to be sufficient for as long as "continue" means anything here, which may be longer than the word "here" means anything anywhere else. The settlement at Lurrach Mòr does not measure itself against survival. It simply survives. The people, in their way, do the same.
