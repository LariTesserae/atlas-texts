# The Inhabitants

There is a specific moment when a closed system becomes aware of itself. Not conscious—nothing so dramatic as that—but when the interdependent processes within it achieve enough complexity that the system begins to behave like a single organism. The Cistern of Lower Valance reached that moment approximately sixty-three years after the last human worker climbed the ladder and sealed the rusted hatch. No one marked the occasion. The occasion was marked anyway, in the accumulated mineral deposits, in the shifting population dynamics, in the slow redistribution of chemical energy from one niche to another.

---

## The Swimmers

What lives in the water has forgotten that water was not always home.

The largest of them—they have no name for themselves, but a biologist might call them something like *Paludicola tardigrada* or might give up on taxonomy entirely and simply describe what she sees—move with the deliberation of things that have never known urgency. There is no predator here large enough to threaten them. There is no prey here that requires speed. They have achieved what every organism in a stable environment eventually achieves: an equilibrium between energy expenditure and energy acquisition that makes violence unnecessary. They are not aggressive. They are not peaceful. They are simply efficient, and efficiency in a closed system looks like patience.

They have six limbs, though the front two have evolved into something more like sensing appendages—long, thin, covered in chemoreceptors that read the water the way a reader reads text. They can taste the approach of another creature from across the pool. They can taste the minerals dissolving from the walls. They can taste the subtle shifts in pH that indicate a change in the bacterial colonies living in the sediment, and they know what those shifts mean for their own digestion.

Their gills are their most beautiful feature, though beauty is not a concept they possess. They are feathered, elaborate, each filament capable of independent movement to maximize water flow. They fold flat against the neck when the creature submerges fully, and they open like ferns when the creature surfaces—fronds unfurling, testing the air, pulling oxygen from the humidity. In the dim bioluminescence they produce themselves, the gills look like ghostly flowers, opening and closing in rhythms that sync with the heartbeat.

The bioluminescence serves multiple purposes. It is primarily metabolic—a release of excess chemical energy that has no other outlet in an environment where movement is minimal. But over generations, it has become something else. The ones that can control it—the ones that can pulse their chromatophores in specific patterns—have more offspring. Not because the light attracts mates, but because it indicates health. A strong pattern means a strong metabolism. A strong metabolism means more eggs, more viable sperm, more successful reproduction.

The patterns themselves are complex. You cannot read them, but they are reading each other constantly. A single pulse can carry information: I am here. I have found food. The water is warm in this corner. There is a vibration coming from above. They communicate in a language of light and timing, a language that has developed entirely in darkness and will never be translated because there is no one to translate it. They do not know they are communicating. They simply do it.

They surface when they need to breathe, and they submerge when they need to feed. The feeding happens in the sediment at the bottom of the pool, where organic matter accumulates: the remains of insects, the waste of the colonial organisms, the occasional body of one of their own kind that has died of old age or illness. They are not sentimental about the dead. They eat them. The dead are still useful, and nothing in the cistern is wasted.

They are not social animals. They do not form bonds, do not recognize individuals, do not remember their own offspring once the eggs are laid and fertilized. They are not solitary either—they exist in a state of constant proximity, swimming past each other, touching in the dark, acknowledging each other's presence with pulses of light before moving on. They are, in the strictest sense, a population: individuals who share a space, who coordinate without intention, who have achieved something that looks almost like community but is actually just overlap.

They lay eggs in sheltered places, and they choose those places with care. The gelatinous masses—fifty to a hundred eggs per clutch—require a surface that is stable, protected, and close to a food source. The corroded pipes provide all three. The metal has rough surfaces that the eggs can adhere to, and the interior is protected from the larger swimmers who might accidentally disturb the clutch. When the eggs hatch, the larvae emerge into a ready-made shelter, and they stay there for weeks, feeding on the organic film that accumulates inside, growing their limbs in stages, learning to move in the confined space before venturing into the open water.

The transition from pipe to pool is the most dangerous moment of their lives. The small ones are vulnerable—too small to be uninteresting to the scavengers, too slow to escape. Many are taken in the first days. Those who survive grow quickly, adding mass with every meal, becoming the things that the scavengers will no longer touch. The process takes months. In that time, they learn the shape of the cistern—the location of the warm seeps, the areas where the water moves in helpful currents, the stone formations that provide cover. They learn it by moving through it, by tasting it, by mapping it with their bodies rather than with sight.

They do not see well. Their eyes are present—small, dark, positioned on either side of the head—but they have evolved in darkness for enough generations that vision has become secondary. They perceive light and shadow, movement and stillness, but they do not form images. The world to them is a pattern of chemical signatures and vibrations, a map of gradients and flows. They know where everything is. They simply do not know what anything looks like.

## The Growth on the Walls

If anything in the cistern could be said to have a history, it is the colonial organisms that cover the stone.

They were not planted. They emerged—spores or cells or simple biological matter that drifted in on the water, that settled in the dampness, that found the conditions favorable and began to grow. They are not one thing. They are several things, living in symbiosis, layers of different organisms that have become interdependent over time. A biologist would call them a lichen, which is technically accurate but misses the strangeness of what they have become.

The base layer is fungal—a network of filamentous structures that penetrate the microscopic pores of the stone, anchoring the colony in place. It holds water better than the stone does, creating a reservoir of moisture that the other layers can draw from during dry periods. The dry periods are rare; the cistern's humidity is nearly constant. But the fungus remembers dryness. It retains the structures that would allow it to survive if the water level dropped, if the walls stopped weeping, if the long darkness were interrupted by something as devastating as sunlight.

On top of the fungal layer, there are bacteria—millions of them, billions of them, organized in sheets and films that process the minerals in the water. They are the ones that give the growth its color: iron-orange where the water carries dissolved metal from the rusted infrastructure, copper-green near the old fittings, manganese-purple in the corners where the ventilation shafts deposit trace elements from the surface. They do not photosynthesize. There is no light to photosynthesize with. They oxidize. They find chemical energy in the water and they extract it, building it into their own bodies, excreting waste products that feed the fungus, that feed the next layer, that feed the insects that live on the surface.

The surface layer is something else entirely. It looks like algae, but it is not. It is a layer of cells that produce a waxy coating, a protective barrier that keeps the colony from drying out, from being eaten too quickly, from being disturbed by the movements of passing creatures. It is the visible part of the growth, the part that you see when you look at the walls—the yellow-gray lobes and fingers, the flat sheets that spread across the stone like skin.

They grow slowly. Millimeters per year, in some places. In others, where the water flows more freely, they can grow centimeters in a month, responding to the abundance with aggressive expansion. They compete with each other for space—the colonies are not unified, are not cooperative, are constantly expanding and contracting based on the resources available to them. If you could watch them over years, compressed into minutes, you would see something like warfare: colonies advancing and retreating, boundaries shifting, one organism overwhelming another and then being overwhelmed in turn. But the warfare is slow. It is geological. It happens on a timescale that makes it invisible to the creatures that live in the water, that live on the walls, that fly through the air.

The insects live on them. Not in them—the growth is not deep enough to provide shelter—but on them, feeding on the surface layer, scraping away the wax with mandibles that have evolved for exactly this purpose. The insects provide waste. The waste provides nitrogen, phosphorus, trace elements that the mineral-rich water lacks. The growth provides food. The insects provide fertilizer. The relationship has been stable for as long as either species can remember, which is not long at all for the growth and only a few generations for the insects, but the stability is real. It has been tested. It continues.

The growth also processes the dead. When an insect dies, when a moth-thing falls from the air, when the shed skin of a swimmer drifts against the wall, the colony absorbs it. The fungus breaks it down. The bacteria incorporate it. The surface layer grows. The cycle completes. There is no waste in the cistern, only transition from one form to another.

If you touch the growth—something the original human workers would have done, would have had to do, would have tried to clean from the surfaces they needed to maintain—it feels like wet leather, slightly warm from the bacterial metabolism, slightly resilient from the fungal structure beneath. It does not resist. It does not fight back. It simply is. It will grow back over whatever mark you leave. It will grow back over your hand if you hold it there long enough. Not aggressively. Not violently. Just persistently. Just inevitably.

## The Insects

There are eleven distinct species of insect-like creatures in the cistern, and they have divided the available space among themselves with a precision that would impress an urban planner.

The smallest—the ones that live on the wall growth—move constantly. They are the size of a fingernail, pale, segmented, equipped with legs that have evolved for gripping stone and mandibles that have evolved for scraping. They work in patterns that look random but aren't. Each individual follows a trail laid down by the one before it, tasting the surface with feet and antennae, finding the areas where the growth is richest, scraping away the surface layer and consuming it. Behind them, other individuals follow—different species, different mandibles, equipped to process what the first group left behind. It is an assembly line. It is a production system. It is efficient.

They do not coordinate intentionally. The coordination is genetic, hardwired, an evolved behavior that maximizes the use of resources without requiring the cognitive capacity to understand why. They simply do what their parents did, and their parents' parents, and the ancestors before them who first colonized this space and found that it worked.

They communicate through chemical trails and physical contact. When one finds a particularly rich area, it leaves a marker—a specific compound in its waste—that others can detect. They converge. They feed. They move on. They reproduce quickly, completing their life cycles in weeks, dying in piles that accumulate in corners, providing food for the scavengers, providing material for the wall growth, continuing the cycle.

The largest of the insect-things are different. They live in the deeper crevices—the spaces between stones, the gaps in the corroded metal, the hollows created by collapsed walkways. They are predators. They hunt the smaller insects, using speed and surprise rather than any sophisticated strategy. They are still small—the cistern could not support large predators—but they are visible, and they are purposeful, and they will eat the smaller insects in numbers that keep those populations from overwhelming the wall growth.

They do not fly. Nothing in the cistern's insect population flies. The air is too thick with moisture, the space too confined, the surfaces too cluttered. Flight would be inefficient. Flight would be dangerous. So they walk, they climb, they jump short distances when necessary. They have evolved for stability, for traction, for the ability to move across wet stone without slipping. Their feet have hooks, their legs have spines, their bodies are low and wide.

They molt regularly, shedding their carapaces as they grow, leaving behind the empty shells that accumulate in drifts. The shells are almost translucent. In the bioluminescent glow of the swimmers in the pool below, they look like ghosts, like echoes, like memories of creatures that have moved on to other forms.

The scavengers—crab-like but not crabs—patrol the edges where water meets walkway. They are the cleaners of the cistern, the ones who process everything that falls, everything that dies. They are opportunists. They eat the shells. They eat the dead insects. They eat the occasional bird, stripping the flesh from the bones with mandibles that have evolved for shearing, leaving the skeleton to settle into the sediment, to be covered by the waste of the swimmers, to become mineral deposit, to eventually become part of the stone.

They are not aggressive. They will not attack living things unless those things are already injured, already dying. They know the difference between alive and dead—chemical signals, movement patterns, the specific taste of distress in the water. They are patient. They can wait. When something dies, they will be there. Not immediately. But soon.

They have a social structure, though calling it that implies more intention than it possesses. They cluster in groups of six to twelve, sharing territory, cooperating in the processing of large food items, tolerating each other's presence in ways that the swimmers do not. They communicate through touch and through vibrations—tapping their legs on the metal grating, creating signals that travel through the solid surfaces of the cistern. *Food here.* *Danger here.* *Safe here.* The signals are simple. The messages are essential. The system works.

## The Flying Things

The moths—or the things that were moths once, that have become something else—navigate by sound and by heat.

There is no light to navigate by. The darkness is absolute, or as close to absolute as makes no difference, and the creatures that fly through it have abandoned vision entirely. Their eyes are vestigial, covered in scales, non-functional. They perceive the world through antennae that have become enormously complex—feathered structures that can detect the faintest temperature differential, the slightest air current, the almost imperceptible vibrations of other creatures moving nearby.

They are not numerous. The cistern cannot support a large population of flying animals. There is not enough food in the air, not enough space for the territory that flying things typically require. But they persist, a small population that has found a niche and held it, feeding on the airborne particles that the bacterial colonies release, on the waste products of the insects, on the organic matter that drifts up from the water.

They are pale. Everything in the cistern is pale—the darkness has removed the evolutionary pressure for pigmentation, and so the creatures have lost what they did not need. Their wings are covered in fine scales that accumulate mineral deposits over time, making them heavier, making flight more difficult, making their lives shorter than they might otherwise be. They do not live long—weeks at most—but they reproduce quickly, laying eggs on the walls, in the crevices, in the accumulated organic matter that the insects have processed.

The spider-things hunt them.

The spiders are not numerous either, but they are patient. They build their webs in the upper regions of the cistern, anchoring them to the dead light fixtures, to the rusted brackets, to the ceramic insulators that once carried electricity and now serve only as attachment points for silk. Their webs are different from those of surface spiders—they hold moisture rather than shedding it, creating a surface that is sticky with condensation, that catches not by glue but by water tension, by the simple physical property of wet thread against dry wing.

The geometry is complex. The spider-things do not build flat webs because flat webs would not work in this space, would not take advantage of the three-dimensional environment, would not maximize the chances of catching the moths that fly erratically through the darkness. Instead, they build structures—webs within webs, layers of silk that create a maze, that reduce the space available for flight, that funnel the moths toward the areas where the spider waits.

The spider does not hunt actively. It waits. It can wait for days, suspended in its web, motionless, conserving energy. When a moth flies close, when the vibrations reach the spider through the silk, the spider moves. It is fast when it needs to be. It wraps the moth in additional silk—thicker silk, storage silk—and it feeds when it needs to feed, or it saves the prey for later, maintaining a larder of immobilized insects in various states of consumption.

The spider-things are not social. They do not tolerate each other's presence. Each web is a territory, defended against intruders, maintained with constant repairs. When a spider dies—when it reaches the end of its natural lifespan, when it is killed by one of the larger swimmers that occasionally climbs the walls looking for food—the web is abandoned. Other spiders will avoid it, will build new structures in new places, will not reclaim the silk of the dead. The abandoned webs remain, collecting moisture, accumulating debris, eventually becoming part of the infrastructure of the cistern, providing surfaces for bacterial growth, for insect navigation, for the continuation of the cycle.

## The Ecosystem

What lives in the cistern is not a collection of individual species. It is a single system, interdependent, balanced, complete.

The swimmers eat the insects. The insects eat the wall growth. The wall growth eats the waste. The scavengers eat everything that dies. The flying things eat the airborne particles. The spiders eat the flying things. The bacteria eat the minerals. The fungus eats the stone. And the system continues, has continued, will continue—barring intervention from outside—until something fundamental changes.

The water chemistry is stable. The mineral content is stable. The temperature is stable. The humidity is stable. The population levels fluctuate, but within narrow ranges, and the fluctuations themselves are predictable: more insects when the wall growth is thick, more swimmers when the insect population peaks, more scavengers when the swimmer population is large enough to produce significant waste, more wall growth when the scavenger population processes enough material to provide fertilizer. It is a cycle. It is a loop. It is, in the strictest sense of the word, a closed system.

But closed systems are not truly closed. They are merely closed enough to maintain equilibrium over observable time. The cistern receives water from the water table—water that brings new minerals, new organic matter, new life in the form of bacterial colonies and microscopic organisms that enter the system from outside. The ventilation shafts—clogged, damaged, mostly non-functional—still allow some air exchange, still bring in the occasional bird, the occasional insect from the surface, the occasional spore that settles on the wall growth and introduces genetic diversity into the colony.

The system is not static. It is dynamic. It changes constantly, responding to inputs from outside, adjusting its internal processes to maintain the balance that keeps everything alive. The swimmers have changed over generations—their limbs have become longer, their gills more elaborate, their bioluminescence more complex. The insects have changed—their mandibles have evolved to process specific layers of the wall growth, their life cycles have accelerated or decelerated based on food availability. The wall growth has changed—it has become more efficient at processing minerals, more resilient in the face of predation, more aggressive in its expansion across the stone.

The system is evolving. It is becoming more itself with every generation, every cycle, every transition from one form of energy to another.

## The Human Residue

The humans are gone, but they are not absent.

The infrastructure they left behind has been incorporated into the ecosystem. The corroded pipes provide shelter for swimmer eggs. The broken ceramic insulators provide protection for the scavengers. The abandoned cables provide anchor points for spider silk. The wall growth has spread across the surfaces that were designed for human access, covering the walkways, climbing the handrails, turning the industrial architecture into something organic, something lived in.

There are other residues. The documents that the insect-things have colonized—the words on them are still visible in places, preserved by the mineral deposits, readable in the dim light that you carry with you. Most are administrative: maintenance schedules, equipment logs, the bureaucratic documentation of a working facility. One is different. It is a personal note, handwritten, preserved in a dry corner where the wall growth has not yet reached:

*The sound is not the pumps. I've checked all of them. Three times now. The sound is coming from below.*

There is no below. The cistern is built on bedrock, on the same stone that forms the walls. The sound the worker heard was probably the water—the movement of underground streams, the settling of the structure, the echo of something distant and unidentifiable. Or it was something else. It was something else that the worker could not explain, that the worker felt compelled to document, that the worker left behind when they climbed the ladder for the last time and sealed the hatch behind them.

The bootprint in the hardened mineral accumulation is human-sized, human-shaped. The depth of the impression suggests someone standing in one place for a long time, waiting, watching, doing whatever the workers did in this space before it was abandoned. The print is permanent now—preserved by the same processes that formed the travertine curtains, the stalactites, the flowstone that covers the walls. The person who made it is gone. The print remains.

The graffiti carved into the stone is in shorthand—actual shorthand, the kind used by clerks and secretaries in the middle industrial period. It is a notation system, a way of recording information quickly, and it was used here for reasons that are not clear. The carvings are near the workstation, near the lunch box, near the junction box with its spilled contents. They might be inventory codes. They might be personal notes. They might be the same kind of message that appears on the preserved document—a record of something the worker noticed, something that seemed important, something that needed to be written down before the worker left.

They have not been deciphered. The shorthand is obsolete, the code unknown, the meaning locked in a language that no one speaks anymore. The cistern holds the message. The cistern offers no translation.

## The Sound

There is a heartbeat in the cistern.

It is not a literal heartbeat—there is no single organ large enough to produce it, no creature with the capacity to make a sound that would echo through the chamber in regular intervals. It is a mechanical process, or it is the illusion of one: water moving through pipes that were never fully sealed, pressure changing in chambers that were designed for different purposes, the structure itself settling into the earth in ways that create rhythm.

The sound is faint. You might not hear it at first—your ears need time to adjust to the acoustics of the space, to separate the intentional sounds from the ambient ones. But it is there. It has always been there. It was there when the workers maintained the facility, and it is there now, unchanged by the decades of abandonment, continuing its pattern regardless of who is listening.

The inhabitants of the cistern do not hear it—or if they do, they do not respond to it. It is outside their sensory range, outside their concern. It does not affect the water chemistry. It does not affect the food supply. It does not affect the cycles of their lives. They continue to swim, to feed, to reproduce, to die, and the sound continues beneath it all, a rhythm that they have incorporated into the background of their existence.

But for you, standing on the metal grating, watching the bioluminescence pulse in the dark water, the sound is present. It is mechanical. It is patient. It has been repeating for longer than you have been alive, and it will continue to repeat after you leave. It is the sound of the cistern itself—the sound of a place that has become a world, that has achieved a kind of life through the accumulated processes within it.

*Thump. Thump.* Pause. *Thump.*

It might be a pump. It might be a heart. It might be something else entirely, something that has no name, something that emerged from the convergence of systems and processes and time.

The cistern knows. The cistern is not telling.
