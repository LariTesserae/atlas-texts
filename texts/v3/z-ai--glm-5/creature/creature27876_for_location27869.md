# The Keeper of Patterns

She is not old. This is the first thing to understand, because it would be easy to mistake her weathering for age. Her skin has the grain of something that has been in water a long time—not wrinkled, but *smoothed*, the fine lines erased by moisture until her face has the soft polish of river stone. Her hair grows in dark hanks, matted with minerals from the water, and she wears it loose because there is no one here to see, because the wind would undo any braid within hours, because she stopped caring about such things so long ago that the memory of caring feels like a story someone else told her.

She is not old. But she has been here since before the last pilgrim stopped coming.

---

The first thing she does each morning—before the light floods in, before the birds begin their spiral, before her body remembers it exists—is check the channels.

There are forty-seven of them. She knows this number the way she knows her own breath, the way she knows the particular sound each pool makes when the water reaches its proper level. The channels were carved by hands she has never seen, in a time she cannot name, but she maintains them with the same devotion those original carvers must have felt. Each morning, she walks the terraced steps with her fingers trailing in the cold water, feeling for obstructions. A leaf. A dead crustacean. A shard of stone fallen from above. She removes these things gently, places them in the small woven basket at her hip, and moves on.

The water must flow. This is not a belief. It is not a prayer. It is simply the truth of the place, as fundamental as the light, as the cold, as the stone itself.

When she first came here—how long ago? The question has no meaning anymore—she was taught the channels by someone whose name she never knew. A woman. No, a shape that moved like a woman, that spoke in words that were half-language and half-water-sound. That shape showed her where the water pools, where it quickens, where it slows to match the carved spirals on the walls. *Here*, the shape said, or didn't say, *here is where the pattern becomes itself.*

She did not understand. She still does not understand. But she tends the channels anyway.

---

Her body has adapted in ways she does not think about anymore.

She does not feel the cold. Or rather—she feels it, but it does not hurt. The cold is information. It tells her where the water is moving too slowly, where ice might form if she does not intervene. She reads the cold the way a sailor reads the wind, adjusting her body, her movement, her attention. When she sleeps, curled on one of the higher terraces where the air is warmest (and even here, warm means merely *less cold*), she sleeps the thin sleep of something that might need to wake at any moment. Her breath makes no cloud. She has learned to breathe shallow, to keep the heat inside.

Her feet are something else entirely. They have not worn shoes in—again, the question has no meaning. The woven grass sandals she arrived with rotted off within the first month. Since then, she has walked barefoot on the wet stone, and her soles have become something like leather, something like bark, something that grips even the slick surfaces where the water sheets down. The spaces between her toes have webbed, just slightly. Not enough to be grotesque. Just enough to notice, if you were looking. If anyone were ever looking.

She has not seen her own reflection in longer than she can remember. The pools are too dense with life, the surface too disturbed by motion, and she has learned not to peer into them too closely. The creatures that live in the water—the pale crustaceans, the threading spiders—have their own business to attend. She will not intrude.

---

She eats what the Alcove provides.

This was the hardest thing, in the beginning. Not the hunger—she had expected hunger—but the *particularity* of what this place offered. The crustaceans, harvested with patience and precision, taste of cold water and something metallic. The birds, when they die naturally (she would never kill one; this is not a rule she was taught, but a rule she *knows*, the way she knows the channels), provide meat that is dark and dense, full of the energy of their constant motion. The plants that grow in the crevices outside—she makes journeys when the light is right, never in full sun, never in the deepest shadow—to harvest the tough succulents and small hardy grasses.

She has developed a relationship with each form of life here.

The crustaceans she respects. They are the foundation of everything, the base of the pattern. She watches them pulse through the pools and feels something like kinship. They move in currents, in spirals, in shapes that match the ancient carvings, and she has spent years—decades?—trying to understand why. When she harvests them, she takes only what the Alcove can spare, and she thanks each one with a touch, her finger briefly grazing its translucent shell.

The spiders she tolerates. They walk on water, which seems impossible, which seems like a violation of some natural law, but they do it. Their legs dimple the surface, and she has spent long hours (the light does not change enough here to mark time, but her body knows when hours have passed) watching them trace their geometries. They eat the crustaceans. This used to distress her, but she has come to understand that this is their purpose. Everything eats something here. Everything is eaten in turn.

The birds she loves. This is a weakness, perhaps. A violation of the distance she should maintain. But their constant motion, their dense bodies, their slate-grey feathers—something in her responds to them. When one lands near her, when it tilts its head and regards her with its black eye, she feels seen. Acknowledged. She speaks to them sometimes, in the thin piping voice she has developed, the voice that the cold air can carry. They do not answer, but they do not fly away either.

---

She does not remember how she came here.

This is not exactly true. She remembers fragments: the cold wind, the thin air, the climb that seemed to take years. She remembers arriving at the peak and seeing the opening carved into the stone. She remembers descending the worn steps, her feet finding the dips where ten thousand feet had fallen before her. She remembers the light flooding in, the water gleaming, the sense of entering something that had been waiting.

But she does not remember *why*.

She was not a pilgrim. This much she knows. Pilgrims came in groups, carrying offerings, speaking prayers in languages she could almost understand. She came alone. She carried nothing. She wore the woven sandals that would rot off within a month. Her purpose—if she had one—has dissolved into the water, smoothed away like the fine lines of her face.

Sometimes, in the thin sleep, she dreams of hands. Not human hands. Something older. Something that moved through stone the way she moves through water, carving channels and pools and niches with a patience that terrifies her. In the dreams, she is being shaped. Her body is becoming something else. Her edges are being worn down.

She wakes from these dreams with her hands in the water, her fingers trailing in the cold, the crustaceans gathering around her skin as if she is something they recognize.

---

The pilgrims stopped coming long ago.

She remembers the last group: three figures, heavily clothed, their faces wrapped against the cold. They descended the steps with the practiced ease of those who had made this journey before, and they moved through the Alcove with purpose. One collected water in copper vessels. One arranged offerings in the niches. One—a child, she realized, a child who moved differently than the others—stood at the edge of the largest pool and watched the crustaceans spiral.

The child saw her.

She is certain of this. Their eyes met—her dark, water-smoothed face and the child's bright, curious gaze—and for a moment, something passed between them. Recognition, perhaps. Or acknowledgment. Or simply the strange gravity of two beings occupying the same space.

The adults did not see her. Or if they did, they showed no sign. They completed their rituals, gathered their water, and climbed back out into the thin mountain light. The child lingered, watching the pools, watching her, and then was called away. The steps echoed with their departure, and then there was silence.

She has not seen another person since.

She does not know how long ago that was. Time moves strangely here, looped in on itself like the spirals in the water. She thinks decades, at least. Perhaps longer. The Alcove does not age, and neither does she—not in the way she once did. She thinks she might be something other than human now, or perhaps she is simply what a human becomes when it stays in one place long enough, tending the same patterns, breathing the same cold air.

---

At night—when the light fades to a thin grey glow, when the birds settle into their alcoves, when the water runs dark and smooth—she watches the patterns.

This is the closest she comes to prayer. She sits at the edge of the largest pool, her legs folded beneath her, her eyes following the crustaceans as they move through their spirals. She watches the water sheet down the walls, finding the same channels it has found for centuries, pooling in the same hollows. She traces the carved designs with her gaze, feeling the way they match the movement of the creatures, the flow of the water, the cracks in the ceiling.

Something is being remembered here.

She does not know what. She does not know if she is the one remembering or if she is simply part of what is being remembered. But she feels the weight of it, the density of purpose that presses against her from all sides. The Alcove holds something—the shape of a thought, perhaps, or the echo of a word spoken before language existed—and she tends it the way she tends the channels. Gently. Patiently. Without understanding.

Sometimes, in the deepest dark, she whispers into the water. Not words. Sounds. Shapes of breath that the water receives and holds. She does not know why she does this. She does not know what she expects to happen. But the crustaceans gather when she whispers, and the spiders pause in their geometries, and for a moment, the patterns align—water, stone, creature, voice—and she feels something click into place.

It does not last. It never lasts. But it is enough.

---

She does not think about leaving.

The question has no meaning here. Leaving would require a self that exists outside the Alcove, and she no longer has that. She is the channels she tends. She is the pools she watches. She is the cold that does not hurt and the water that does not freeze and the patterns that repeat and repeat and repeat, trying to remember what they are.

She is the Keeper of Patterns. She did not choose this title. She did not choose anything. But here, in the Weeping Alcove, in the bright cold light, in the silence that holds the weight of centuries, she tends what needs tending.

And she waits.

For what, she does not know.

But the water is still weeping, the patterns are still forming, and the Alcove is still holding its breath.
