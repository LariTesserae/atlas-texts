# The Workers

They arrive by helicopter, most of them. Two-week shifts. The rotation is fixed, predictable—you learn the schedule in training and you keep it until you transfer or quit or forget there was ever another way to live.

The first thing you lose is conversation. Not the ability—the habit. The roar makes talking difficult, and after a while, the effort of shouting over the pumps, of repeating yourself, of watching words get swallowed by the noise—it wears you down. You develop a shorthand. Gestures. A look that means *check the intake* or *pressure's dropping* or *I'm going to the barracks*. The workers communicate in glances, in movements, in the shared language of people who have lived in sound long enough to stop trying to live above it.

Some of them talk to themselves. You hear it in quiet moments between the stations—muttering, half-formed sentences, the sound of a voice remembering it exists. Most keep their thoughts internal. The plateau teaches you to hold things in.

There's a type of person who ends up here. Not by accident, never by accident—nobody stumbles into this job. You have to seek it out, answer an advertisement most people scroll past, sit through an interview where they explain the conditions in detail, the isolation, the noise, the damp, the light that never softens. You have to sign forms acknowledging you understand. You have to want to come.

So who wants to come?

---

People who need to disappear for a while. The plateau is good for that. No phones work reliably—the communications array handles official traffic, and personal calls happen on a schedule, rationed, supervised. You can't binge on distraction here. You can't fill the empty spaces with noise because the noise is already full.

People who need money. The hazard pay is significant. The isolation pay. The altitude pay. You can clear more in two weeks here than in a month of ordinary work, and if you keep your head down, if you don't break anything, if you last—there are workers who've been rotating through for years. They have routines. They know the stations. They've stopped thinking about the outside except as a place they visit between shifts.

People who made mistakes. This is not official, but you learn to read the silences. A worker who doesn't talk about his life before, who flinches at certain questions, who receives no calls and sends none—he's not here for the scenery. The plateau holds people the way it holds water: because they have nowhere else to go.

---

There's a woman named Marta who works the eastern quadrant. Third rotation, five years running. She walks her check route at the same time each morning—04:00, before full light, headlamp cutting through the mist. She knows every pump by its sound. Can tell you which bearing is wearing thin before the gauges register the change. The other workers say she dreams the machines, that she wakes up knowing what's about to fail.

Marta doesn't talk about herself. Doesn't talk much at all. But if you walk with her on her route—if you keep pace, if you match her silence—she'll point out things you'd miss on your own. A patch of moss that's slightly different in color, marking a spring you can't see. A pattern in the pools that indicates where the water table's shifting. The place where two species of liverwort are competing for territory, fighting a slow war across a square meter of ground.

She notices things. The plateau taught her to notice, or maybe she came here because she already knew how. Either way, she reads the surface like a text—every variation a word, every pattern a sentence. The workers respect her. She's the one you call when something's wrong and you can't figure out what. She walks in, listens, points. There. That's your problem. The bearing. The seal. The crack in the concrete that's been growing for six months and you didn't see it because you were looking at the gauges instead of the ground.

---

There's a man named Cob who handles the heavy repairs. Big man, thick hands, face weathered from years of exposure. He came up here after something happened—nobody knows what, and asking isn't done. He's pleasant enough, professional, reliable. He just doesn't exist outside of his shifts. No family calls. No friends visit. He arrives, works, leaves, returns. The plateau is his entire life, and he seems content with that.

Cob has a relationship with the water. That's the only way to describe it. When he works on a pump, he moves differently—more slowly, more deliberately. He touches the housing before he opens it. He watches the flow before he adjusts anything. The other workers joke that he's asking permission, and maybe he is, in his way. The machines respond to him. Breakdowns that should take hours take minutes when Cob is there. Things that should be broken suddenly work.

He's not religious. He'll tell you that directly if you ask. But something happened to him up here, or maybe before he came, some experience that taught him to treat the world as if it's alive. The vegetal mat, the water moving through it, the pumps pulling and pushing—Cob moves through the system like he's part of it. Like he's another valve, another channel, another temporary shape the water has taken.

---

The night shift workers are different. Self-selected. The ones who request the darkness, the isolation, the stars so bright they cast shadows. You have to be a certain kind of person to choose the night—more comfortable alone, less dependent on the social rhythms that structure ordinary work. The night workers report to each other through written logs. They see each other only briefly, during shift changes. They walk the routes in darkness, headlamps off, memorized paths, feet knowing where to step without eyes to guide them.

A night worker named Vren described it once, in the barracks, during overlap: *The plateau talks at night. Not words. But the sound changes. You can hear the breath in it. The way the water moves differently when the sun goes down. The way the pumps labor harder or easier depending on something you can't see. You learn to listen. You learn what normal sounds like, and then you hear the variations. That's how you know when something's wrong. Not the gauges. The sound.*

The day workers don't always believe them. The logs are thorough, though. When a night worker writes *something different about Station 7, can't pin it, recommend inspection*, the day team inspects. And there's always something. A seal that's starting to weep. A vibration that shouldn't be there. A patch of moss that's begun growing across an intake screen.

---

The barracks are functional. Bunk beds, lockers, a communal space with a few chairs, a table, a small screen for watching approved entertainment on a limited schedule. The walls are thin—you can hear the roar through them, always, though you stop noticing after the first few days. Your brain edits it out. It becomes background, like a heartbeat, like breath.

But the barracks are sinking. Every year, they settle a little deeper into the vegetal mat. The doors drag. The floors tilt. You can set a marble on a table and watch it roll toward the eastern wall. The workers have adjusted. They sleep with their heads slightly elevated. They walk with a lean. They've learned the geometry of slow descent, the angle at which a building gives up and decides to become part of the landscape.

The administration talks about reinforcement. New foundations. A plan to stabilize the structures before they're swallowed entirely. The workers nod and don't believe it. They've seen the moss grow over a pump housing in a single season. They've watched the water find every weakness, every seam, every place where something made of metal and concrete touches something made of living tissue and resolves to absorb it.

There's a comfort in the sinking. The workers talk about it sometimes, in the quiet hours, in the shared language of people who've stopped expecting permanence. *We're going down*, they say. *Slowly. Together.* And there's something peaceful about it. Something honest. The plateau doesn't pretend to be something it isn't. It's growing. It's wet. It's alive. Everything that comes here eventually joins it. The workers have just arrived early enough to watch the process.

---

Not everyone lasts. Some workers arrive for their first shift and leave halfway through, unable to tolerate the sound, the wet, the isolation. They don't talk about what drives them out. They just go. The helicopter returns early. The empty bunk gets reassigned. The plateau doesn't notice.

Others last longer and then don't—a year, two years, five—and then they stop coming. The rotation schedule shifts. A new name appears where theirs used to be. Life continues. The roar doesn't change.

A few workers have asked to stay past their shifts. To extend. The administration approves it sometimes, if staffing allows, if the worker seems stable. These are the ones who become part of the plateau, who start to move like the landscape moves—slowly, deliberately, in rhythm with the water. They develop the attentiveness of Marta, the patience of Cob, the listening of the night workers. They grow into something shaped by this place.

Whether that's good or bad depends on what they were before. The plateau gives you time to think. Sometimes that's a gift. Sometimes it's not.

---

The workers age faster up here. The thin atmosphere, the intense light, the constant damp—it shows on their faces, their hands. They develop a look after a few years: weathered, leveled, stripped of unnecessary expression. The roar flattens something in them. The isolation settles. They become people who've spent too much time listening to water move, and it changes the shape of their thoughts.

They forget names. Not the names of things—the names of people, places, lives they had before. A worker will reach for the name of a town where he grew up and find it gone, replaced by the sound of a particular pump at Station 12, the tone of it, the way it shudders when the intake screen needs clearing. The plateau fills you up with itself and leaves less room for whatever you were before.

Most workers don't notice until they leave. A helicopter descends through the mist layer, touches down in the lowlands, and they step out into a world that's suddenly, shockingly quiet. They reach for the roar and don't find it. They reach for the habit of silence and find themselves surrounded by people talking, laughing, making the ordinary noise of ordinary lives. It's overwhelming. It's wrong. Some of them turn around and go back up immediately, requesting another shift, any shift, just let them stay where the sound is.

Others adapt. They go home. They try to remember what their lives were before. They look at the sky and miss the stars, the real stars, the ones you can only see from a high thin place where nothing softens the dark. They turn on taps and think about where the water comes from, the journey it took, the living ground it passed through on its way down.

They never entirely leave. Some part of them stays on the plateau, walking a check route, listening to pumps, watching the moss grow over infrastructure. The workers who return for shift after shift after shift—they've found something they can't name, something that doesn't exist in the places people are supposed to belong. They've found a place that holds them the way it holds water: because they fit there, because they've become part of its slow, patient, living system.

And the plateau, for its part, accepts them. Doesn't distinguish between the vegetal mat and the workers walking across it. Doesn't differentiate between the water pooling in the catchments and the blood moving through human hearts. It's all the same to a landscape this young, this alive, this uninterested in the categories people use to separate themselves from the world they live in.

Up here, there are no separate things. There's just the growing, and the water, and the endless, endless roar.
