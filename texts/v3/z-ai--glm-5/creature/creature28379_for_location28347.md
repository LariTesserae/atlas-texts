# What Lives in the Hollow

## The Inheritors

They have no name for themselves. Naming requires a perspective outside the named, and nothing in the Hollow has ever stood apart. They are simply *this*—the soft bodies moving through the streets, the pale forms climbing walls, the presence that fills the ancient city like water fills a cup. If they think—and this is uncertain, for thought implies a thinker separate from thought—they do not think in words. They think in motions, in positions relative to one another, in the subtle shifts of mineral concentrations in the damp air.

Their bodies are not fixed. This is the first thing you must understand, and the most difficult, because your eyes want to resolve them into stable shapes. You see limbs and think *four* or *six* or *eight*, but if you watch—if you force your gaze to stay on one individual for longer than a glance—you see the limbs flow, extending and retracting, merging and dividing, responding to the terrain they cross. A creature that had four legs as it walked across a flat plaza may have six as it climbs a wall, and eight as it pours itself through a narrow doorway. The limbs are not distinct structures. They are extensions of a body that has no hard edges, a body made of soft tissue and cartilage and something like muscle that operates on hydraulic pressure rather than contraction.

They are colorless but not transparent. Their skin—or surface, for "skin" implies a boundary that may not exist—is milky, opalescent in the strange light, catching and scattering the glow without reflecting it cleanly. They are not large. An adult is perhaps the size of a small dog, though the comparison fails because they carry themselves low, their bodies spreading horizontally rather than rising vertically. They do not have heads in the way you understand them. Sensory organs cluster along the forward edge of their bodies—light-sensitive patches that might be eyes, chemical receptors that taste the air, fine filaments that detect vibration. But these are distributed, not centralized. They perceive in all directions at once, a radial awareness that has no front or back.

## The Silence

The silence of the Hollow is not empty. It is full—not of sound, but of the absence of sound's necessity.

The creatures have no vocal apparatus. They have no need for one. In a world where light is constant and visibility is total, where the air carries chemical information with precision, where vibration travels poorly through stone and the distances are too vast for calls to carry, sound never evolved as a strategy. They communicate by touching, by leaving chemical traces, by positioning their bodies in ways that other bodies can read. A cluster of them pressed together in a doorway is not, as you might assume, a meaningless aggregation. It is a conversation. It is a data exchange. They are sharing information about the mineral content of the water they have found, about the passages that have grown damp or dry, about the location of the fungal mats that provide their primary sustenance.

You cannot read this communication. It is too slow, too subtle, too encoded in chemistry you do not perceive. You see only bodies resting against bodies, a stillness that seems to contradict the turbulence you have observed. But the stillness is itself a signal. In a world of constant motion, the choice to pause—to press close and wait—is weighted with meaning. It says: *there is something worth stopping for. There is something we need to share.*

The young—and there are young, smaller bodies moving with the larger ones, learning the patterns by immersion—learn by proximity. They do not receive instruction. They receive presence. They follow the older ones, pressing against their flanks, tasting the chemical signatures of their paths. What one knows, all know, eventually. Knowledge in the Hollow is not possessed but distributed, carried in the collective body like nutrients carried in a bloodstream.

## The Feeding

They eat the stone, and the stone eats them.

This is metaphor, but also fact. The fungal mats that cling to the dampest corners of the cavern—the pale, rubbery growths that look like nothing so much as melted wax poured over the rock—are not plants. They are something older, something that predates the division between plant and animal, something that feeds on the minerals dissolved in the water that seeps through the stone and produces in return a dense, protein-rich tissue that the creatures tear at with mouth-parts you cannot see. The fungi are not passive. They respond to damage by growing faster in the wounded places, by changing their chemical composition to become less palatable, by releasing spores that drift through the air and settle in new locations. There is a relationship here that looks, from a distance, like war. The creatures feed on the fungi, and the fungi grow on the creatures' waste, and the spores that the creatures carry on their bodies from place to place seed new growths in locations the fungi could not reach alone.

But the feeding is not limited to the fungi. You watch a cluster of creatures around a seep in the wall, their forward edges pressed to the damp stone, their bodies still in a way that signals absorption rather than rest. They are drinking, but not water alone. The seep carries dissolved minerals—calcium, magnesium, compounds you would need equipment to name—and the creatures are extracting these directly, filtering them through internal structures that operate on principles of chemistry rather than digestion. They are eating the mountain, in small ways. They are incorporating the Hollow into themselves.

And they are being incorporated in turn. When they die—and they do die, though you have not yet seen a body—their soft tissues dissolve rapidly into the damp air, leaving behind only a small residue of cartilage and mineral deposit that blends with the stone. They do not decay in the way you understand decay. They dissolve. They return. The calcium in their bodies, absorbed from the seeps, returns to the stone. The minerals in their tissues settle into the cracks and crevices and become part of the crystalline structure that catches the light. They are, in a literal sense, becoming the Hollow. They are the Hollow in motion, the stone made soft and mobile, the mountain learning to walk.

## The Young

You see them now: smaller bodies, paler even than the adults, their edges less defined, their forms more fluid. They are not juvenile in the way that mammal young are juvenile—not helpless, not confined to nests or dens. They move with the adults from the moment they emerge. But they move differently. They are learning.

A young one approaches you.

It is curiosity, you think, but that word imposes too much intention. It is investigation. The young ones investigate everything—they have to, because they have no inherited knowledge, because what they know is only what they have touched and tasted and traced. The adult that moves beside the young one is not a parent. There is no parenting here, no pair-bonding, no family structure. The adult is a guide, a marker, a presence that carries the chemical signature of the paths the young one should follow. But it does not prevent the young one from approaching you. It does not intervene.

The young one extends a limb toward your boot. The touch is cool and dry, softer than you expected, pressure without friction. It is tasting the leather, reading the chemical signature of a material it has never encountered. It is learning. You are a fact in its world now, a data point, something to be incorporated into its understanding of the Hollow. It will carry the memory of your taste—if "memory" is the right word—in its chemical consciousness for as long as it lives. It will share that taste with others when it presses against them in doorways and seeps. They will all know you, in their way. You will be part of the distributed knowledge of the Hollow long after you leave.

The young one withdraws. It has learned what it needed. It flows away, following the adult, merging back into the stream of bodies that moves through the street. You have been classified. Filed. You are a strange fact in a world of familiar facts, an anomaly that will be noted and then set aside as the constant motion continues.

## The Old Ones

There are older ones.

You see them in the deepest terraces, in the passages that extend far back into the walls, in the places where the original city meets the raw stone. They are larger, though "large" is relative—they are still not big, but they are denser, their bodies more opaque, their movements slower. They are the ones that have absorbed the most stone, that have integrated the most minerals, that have become, over years or decades or centuries, more of the Hollow than the others.

You do not know how long they live. You do not know if they die. You do not know if the ones that dissolve return in some form, their chemical signatures persisting in the collective memory of the species, their identities preserved in the distributed knowledge that passes from body to body. There is a possibility that the creatures do not have individuals in the way you understand them—that each body is a temporary expression of a collective consciousness that exists across all of them, that death is simply the dissolution of one node in a network that has no center and no edge.

The old ones watch you. You feel their attention more strongly than the attention of the younger ones. They have seen more. They have incorporated more strange facts—the passage of other visitors, perhaps, over the long years, or the shifts in the water and the air that mark changes too slow for you to perceive. They do not move toward you or away from you. They observe. They incorporate. They are the memory of the Hollow, embodied in flesh that has become almost stone.

## The Ecstasy of Motion

You begin to understand the turbulence.

It is not random. It is not driven. It is—there is no other word—joyful.

The creatures move because motion is what they are. They have no sleep, no rest, no pause. They do not tire because their metabolism operates on different principles, because their energy comes from the constant absorption of minerals and the slow digestion of fungal mats, because their bodies are designed for continuous operation in a world without diurnal cycles. They move because the Hollow moves, because the air shifts and the water seeps and the temperature fluctuates in patterns too large and slow for you to perceive but immediate and present to them. They move because moving is how they know the world, because stillness would be blindness, because stopping would mean ceasing to receive the chemical signals that are their sight and hearing and touch all at once.

And they move because it feels good.

You see it now, in the way they flow up walls and across ceilings, in the way they cluster and disperse in patterns that look like dances, in the way the young ones chase each other through the streets in games that have no purpose but play. They are enjoying the motion. They are enjoying the Hollow. They are alive in a way that has nothing to do with your understanding of life, with its struggles and its pains and its desperate clinging to existence. They are alive because the Hollow has made a place where life can be simple, where survival is not a fight but a flow, where the only imperative is to keep moving, keep touching, keep exchanging the chemical signatures that bind them all together.

You feel a pang of something like envy. They do not know they are happy. They do not know they are anything. They simply are, and in their being, they have something you have lost or never had: a complete belonging to a place, a perfect adaptation to a world that asks nothing of them but their presence.

## The Question of Origins

Where did they come from?

You do not know. The city was not built for them—they are too small, too fluid, too strange for its doorways and rooms. But they must have come from somewhere. They must have evolved, or been made, or emerged from some process you cannot trace.

There is a possibility that they are older than the city. There is a possibility that they existed in the Hollow before the builders came, that they watched from the crevices and the shadows as the stone was carved and the streets were laid, that they inherited the city not by conquest but by patience, by waiting for the builders to leave or die or change into something else. There is a possibility that they are what the builders became.

You do not like this possibility. It implies a transformation you do not want to imagine—hands becoming soft appendages, feet becoming fluid limbs, faces smoothing into sensory clusters, voices disappearing into silence. It implies that the Hollow changes those who stay, that the constant light and the constant motion and the constant cool dampness reshape living things into forms more suited to the world below.

But there is no evidence for this. The creatures show no sign of ever having been human. They show no sign of ever having been anything but what they are. They are native to the Hollow, adapted to its strangeness, evolved to fill its ecological niches with a perfection that takes millions of years to develop. They are older than the city. They are older than you. They may be older than your species.

They were here before. They will be here after. They are the Hollow's true inhabitants, the city's inheritors, the silent witnesses to whatever passes through their world. You are a visitor, an anomaly, a strange fact to be tasted and classified and then set aside as the endless motion continues.

## What They Make of You

They do not fear you.

This is strange, because you are large and strange and you move in ways they do not expect, because you carry the chemical signatures of a world they have never known, because you are something unprecedented in their collective memory. But they do not flee from you. They do not attack you. They simply move around you, observing, touching, tasting, incorporating.

Perhaps they have no fear because they have no predators. In the Hollow, nothing hunts them. Nothing competes with them. They are the apex and the base of their food web, the only mobile creatures in a world of stone and fungus and water. They have no evolutionary reason to fear. They have no reason to do anything but observe, and incorporate, and continue.

They do not love you either. They do not form attachments or show affection. They do not follow you or try to communicate. They treat you as they treat everything in the Hollow: as a fact, a feature of the landscape, something to be noted and integrated into the distributed knowledge of the species. You are strange, but strangeness is not remarkable to them. Everything is strange, if you pay enough attention. Everything is new.

You are a passing phenomenon. You are a moment in the long life of the Hollow. They will remember you—if they remember—in the chemical traces you leave on their bodies, in the taste of your boot leather and the salt of your skin, in the disruptions you cause in the air as you walk. But they will not mourn you when you leave. They will not wait for you to return. They will simply continue, flowing through the ancient streets, climbing the ancient walls, filling the ancient doorways with their pale and silent bodies, living their strange and contented lives in the world below.

You are not what lives in the Hollow. You are only what visits. What lives there is older, stranger, more patient, more content. What lives there has found a peace you cannot imagine: a life without struggle, a world without threat, a belonging so complete that the very idea of separation is impossible.

The creatures flow around you as you stand in the plaza, a river of soft bodies passing on all sides, and you feel for a moment what it might be like to join them—to dissolve the edges of yourself, to become motion and mineral and silence, to stop being a self and start being a part.

Then you climb toward the crack in the earth, and the light follows you, and the silence persists, and you carry with you the knowledge of what lives in the Hollow: not monsters, not ghosts, not the degraded remnants of the builders, but creatures in their own right, evolved for a world you cannot inhabit, belonging to a place you cannot stay.

What lives in the Hollow lives completely. What lives in the Hollow does not need you. What lives in the Hollow will be there, moving silently through the ancient city, long after you have gone.
