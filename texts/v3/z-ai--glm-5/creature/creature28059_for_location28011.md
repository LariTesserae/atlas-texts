# What Lives Here

## The Ботанос

They are not trees. They are not a forest. Each one is a territory unto itself—a vertical nation with borders measured in days of travel. The smallest of them rises four hundred meters before branching; the oldest have forgotten how to branch at all, their surfaces rising smooth and unbroken until they dissolve into the permanent haze. Their bases sprawl outward into root-lakes that interlock with neighbors in slow, tectonic negotiation. The space where two root-systems meet is not a boundary. It is a conversation. Roots intertwining over centuries, exchanging nutrients, exchanging information through fungal networks so dense they constitute a second nervous system underlying the entire plateau.

An individual ботанос lives long enough to watch civilizations rise and dissolve below. They predate the uplift. Their seeds—spores? propagules? the word implies something too small—drifted in the shallow seas that existed before the land tore itself upward. They evolved for pressure, for darkness, for the slow nutrition of seafloor decay. When the plateau rose, they did not adapt. They *remembered*. The darkness above the cloud-layer was not different enough to require change. They simply continued, growing larger in an environment where nothing existed to limit them.

Their surfaces are ecosystems. The channels and grooves that guide water downward harbor moss-equivalents, lichen-analogues, small plants that have evolved to live on the skin of larger plants. Insects live there—not the insects of the below-world, but creatures that have lost the capacity for flight because flight requires spaces smaller than the emptiness between pillars. They crawl through the water-channels, drinking from the flow, laying eggs in the small pools that form where channels widen. Some have evolved to survive freezing; their eggs can spend years encased in ice, hatching when warmth returns. The ботанос does not mind them. They clean the channels. They bring small nutrients. They are, in their way, part of the vascular system.

The people who hollow living spaces in the lower trunks do not harm the ботанос. They work with the plant's own growth patterns, creating chambers that the botanical tissue can grow around, incorporating the intrusion into its structure. A platform carved this decade will be grown-over within a generation, the edges softened, the shape integrated. The people know this. They build knowing their work is temporary on the plant's timescale. They are making a shelter they will return to their host when they no longer need it.

The flowering happens once per human generation. A ботанос that has reached the end of a long reproductive cycle sends energy upward through its vascular system—a process that takes months, visible as a darkening of the channels, a thickening of the sap that flows through them. The flower, when it emerges at the summit, is not a flower in any recognizable sense. It is a structure the size of a small building, a complex architecture of tissue that unfurls over weeks, releasing propagules into the upper atmosphere. These drift on currents the people cannot perceive, traveling distances measured in the plateau's own vast geography, settling on distant surfaces to begin growth. The people mark time by flowerings. A child born during a flowering carries that botanical calendar in their name; they are "Fifth-Flowering" or "Of-the-White-Bloom," and their age can be calculated by any who know how many flowerings have passed since.

## The Твари

They call them твари—the word means "creatures" but carries an undertone of "things made" or "things shaped," as though they were crafted for this environment rather than evolved within it. The largest of them have wingspans that would be ridiculous anywhere else, fifteen meters of membrane stretched across hollow-bone frameworks so light that a strong breath could shift them. They cannot take off from flat ground. They need height, need the thermal currents that rise between pillars, need the wind-channels carved by ботанос geography. They climb to launch-platforms—the upper branches of smaller pillars, the edges of shelves carved into larger ones—and they drop into space, falling until the air catches them and they become weight.

They eat the insects that live in the botanical channels, the small flying creatures that swarm in the dim light, and occasionally each other. Their beaks are long, curved, designed for probing into spaces too narrow for their bodies. They navigate by sound more than sight; their calls map the emptiness, returning information about pillar-locations, obstacle-distances, the movement of other твари through shared airspace. The eight-second descending cry is not communication. It is a mapping tool. The sound travels outward, reflects off surfaces, returns to ears evolved to process three-dimensional echoes. A твари can fly through complete darkness and never strike a pillar. It already knows where everything is.

They nest in the upper reaches of the ботанос, where the surfaces widen into platforms and the light grows marginally brighter. Their nests are structures built from their own shed feathers, from the silk-sap the people harvest, from ice that forms and reforms in the cold. They lay one egg per season. The chick emerges into a world where falling means death, and it must learn to fly within weeks or it will never learn. The parents teach by demonstration. They launch from the nest-edge, catch the air, return. They launch again. The chick watches, and eventually follows. Some do not make it. The ground beneath the nesting-grounds is scattered with the small corpses of those who misunderstood wind, who trusted air that did not hold them, who fell into the emptiness and could not recover.

The people hunt them with net-traps strung between pillars, with silk-sap lines that tangle wings, with patience. A тварi caught alive is worth more than one killed; their feathers carry medicinal properties, their hollow bones make tools too light to feel in the hand, their membranes can be cured into translucent sheets that hold warmth better than any woven fabric. But the твари are not easy prey. They learn. They remember trap-locations. They call warnings to others that propagate across the pillar-network in cascades of descending notes. A hunter who takes too many from one area will find that area empty for generations. The твари will redirect their migration-routes, shift their feeding-grounds, disappear into the plateau's vastness and not return.

There are stories of твари that have learned to ride the Чешуйник's kills. They follow the predator, wait for it to feed, descend to steal scraps before it can finish. The stories say these твари are considered cursed by their own kind, that they fly alone, that their calls are distorted—higher, faster, the mapping-sound twisted into something desperate. No one has confirmed this. The Чешуйник's hunting-grounds are not places people visit.

## The Чешуйник

The predator has a name that translates roughly as "the scaled thing," but the word "scale" in this language carries meanings the below-world's languages do not. A scale can be a physical covering, yes—the predator is sheathed in membranes that overlap like feathers, like armor, like the shingles of a roof designed by something that had never seen a roof. But "scale" also refers to music, to measurement, to the process of climbing. The Чешуйник is all of these. It is scaled in its covering. It moves through musical frequencies the твари use for navigation, intercepting their own mapping-calls to locate them. It operates at a scale that distorts perception, close enough to see that it is enormous, far enough that it could be small. It climbs the ботанос pillars with an ease that suggests the plants were designed for it rather than it evolved to navigate them.

It is silent when it hunts. The твари call; the call travels; the Чешуйник intercepts, calculates position, drops from above. The impact is the first sound—the wet compression of bodies meeting in midair, the crack of hollow bones shattering. The твари does not have time to understand what happened. It is already dead, clutched in talons that have evolved to pierce membrane and shatter skeleton in a single motion, already being carried to a feeding-ground somewhere in the pillar-maze.

The low call after a kill—the satisfied register that vibrates through the air for thirty seconds—is not communication. It is territorial. It is the predator announcing that it has fed, that it is not hungry, that other predators should hunt elsewhere. The people have learned to read these calls. A single long note means one kill, one Чешуйник, an area safe to travel for several hours. Two notes in quick succession mean a pair, possibly a mating-group, possibly a parent teaching young to hunt. These areas are avoided. Three notes or more mean something else entirely—something the oldest hunters will not discuss with outsiders.

The Чешуйник does not hunt people. This is not because it cannot. A predator that can take a тварi mid-flight can take a person climbing between pillars. It simply does not. The people have theories. They smell wrong—the fermented-fruit residue on their skin, the smoke from their ritual fires, the copper they work into their tools. They are too small—a Чешуйник that fed on people would need to hunt constantly, and the predator is built for efficiency, for single kills that sustain it for days. They are not worth the effort. Or—this is the theory the people prefer, the one they tell their children—the Чешуйник remembers. It remembers the people who cultivate the fungi that protect the ботанос from parasites, the people who clean the water-channels, the people who are part of the ecosystem rather than prey within it. It remembers, and it does not eat what it remembers.

No one has ever seen a Чешуйник nest. No one has seen a juvenile, a mating pair, a death. They appear in the air between pillars, hunt, call, vanish. Their lifespans are unknown. Their origins are unknown. The people have stories—that they emerged from the seafloor when the plateau rose, that they were always here, waiting in the darkness for land to rise around them, that they are not animals at all but processes, geological forces given wings and hunger. The standing stones predate the uplift. The Чешуйник may as well.

## The People

They call themselves something that translates poorly—roughly "those who are inside," but "inside" here means something more like "dwelling-within-the-body-of." They do not have a word for "on" that applies to land. Their language was built for this place, and in this place, you do not live *on* a ботанос. You live *in* it, *with* it, *as part of* it.

Their population is larger than the landscape suggests. A traveler moving through pillar-cluster-thirty-seven might encounter a single family in a week of walking, might assume the plateau is nearly empty. But the family is one of hundreds living in that cluster alone—hundreds of families spaced across botanical territory so vast that they are functionally invisible to each other. They know their neighbors exist. They communicate through sound-calls that travel the pillar-network, through messages carved into botanical tissue that will be read decades later when the channels heal, through the trade-routes that connect distant clusters in slow, seasonal journeys. But they do not see each other often. The emptiness is too large.

A child is born into a family-unit that may include biological parents, may include parents who adopted them from a neighbor who could not feed another mouth, may include no parents at all—a child born during a migration, taken in by a passing group, raised without knowing their origin. The plateau does not require lineage. It requires survival. A child who can climb, who can read the weather in the feel of air on skin, who can hear a Чешуйник call and know what it means, is a child who will live to adulthood. A child who cannot learn these things will not, and no amount of parental protection will change that. The plateau does not negotiate.

Their material culture is built from what the botanical world provides. The silk-sap that bleeds from ботанос wounds—harvested carefully, never taking enough to harm the plant—can be spun into thread, woven into fabric, cured into sheets that hold warmth and resist the constant moisture. The fiber-vines that grow between pillars, drinking the condensation, reaching toward distant neighbors, can be twisted into rope strong enough to bear a person's weight across the emptiness. The fungi that grow in the water-channels—cultivated by the people, protected from competitors, traded between clusters as genetic stock—provide food that survives freezing, that can be stored for years, that carries nutrients the botanical diet lacks. The stone they carve comes from the plateau's exposed faces, from the faults that run like surgical cuts through rock, from the scree-slopes that haven't learned to settle. Their tools are stone and copper and bone, and they have learned to make tools that perform as well as steel—a copper blade tempered in botanical sap, a stone knife ground to an edge that can shave hair from a твари feather.

They do not have writing in the below-world sense. They have carving—inscriptions on botanical tissue that will last until the plant heals over them, on stone that will last until the plateau shifts again. They have sound—calls that encode information, songs that carry history, a tonal language where pitch determines meaning and the pillar-network provides resonance that amplifies messages across distances. A child learning to speak is learning to navigate, learning to communicate across the emptiness, learning to survive in a world where being heard matters more than being seen.

They have stories. The standing stones were built by those who came before—before the uplift, before the ботанос, before the Чешуйник. Those who came before knew what was coming. They prepared. They vanished. The people do not know where they went. They do not know if they died or transformed or simply walked so far into the plateau's vastness that they have not yet been found. The stories say they are still here. The stories say they live in the far pillars, in the clusters no trade-route reaches, in the regions where the ботанос grows so dense that navigation becomes impossible. The stories say they built the stones to call something, to hold something back, to mark a place where the boundary between worlds grew thin. The stories say many things. The people listen, and leave offerings, and do not investigate.

They have religion, but "religion" implies a separation between sacred and profane that does not exist here. The ботанос is not worshipped. It is lived within. The Чешуйник is not feared. It is avoided. The твари are not revered. They are harvested. The world is not holy. It is simply present—vast, cold, operating by rules the people did not make and cannot change. They do not pray for intervention. They do not seek meaning. They understand that meaning is something you make for yourself in a place that does not provide it.

Their dead are given to the emptiness. A body wrapped in silk-sap fabric, weighted with stones, carried to the edge of a platform and released. The fall takes minutes. The body will land somewhere in the root-lakes, will decompose into the fungal network, will become nutrient for the botanical towers that sustained the life that ended. This is not ceremony. This is efficiency. This is return. The people do not mourn the dead; they acknowledge them, speak their names into the pillar-resonance, incorporate their memory into songs that will last generations. And then they continue. The emptiness does not grieve. Neither do they.

## The Fungi

They do not have a name in the people's language. They are simply *there*—present in every water-channel, growing in every sheltered space, threading through the soil that accumulates in the root-lakes. They are white, pale gray, sometimes tinged with the lavender of the perpetual sky. They do not look like food. They look like mineral deposits, like frost that learned to grow upward, like something the ботанос exuded and forgot.

But they are alive. They are the basis of everything the people eat that is not твари meat or botanical sap. They grow in cultivation-chambers the people maintain—hollow spaces in the lower pillars, lined with stone, kept moist by water channeled from condensation surfaces. They grow slowly, adding mass over months, over years. A single cultivation-chamber can feed a family for a generation if properly maintained. The knowledge of maintenance—what water to add, what temperature to maintain, what competitors to remove—is passed from parent to child, is traded between clusters, is the basis of wealth in a society that does not have currency.

They have medicinal properties the people have learned through long experimentation. Some heal wounds—packing a fungal poultice into a cut accelerates healing, prevents infection, leaves a scar that fades within years. Some reduce fever—chewing the fibrous tissue releases compounds that lower body temperature, that ease the ache of cold-induced illness. Some are poison—a single species, identifiable only by the slight blue tint at its edges, causes paralysis that lasts for days, that leaves the victim aware but unable to move, vulnerable to cold, to predators, to the slow freezing that kills faster than the poison itself. The people use this poison on their arrow-tips, on their trap-lines, on the rare occasions when conflict between clusters requires violence. They do not use it often. Violence is inefficient. The emptiness is large enough that anyone can simply leave.

The fungi communicate with the ботанос. This is known—known through observation, through the way a pillar's health improves when fungal networks are cultivated nearby, through the way pillar-disease spreads when fungal networks die. The fungi are not parasites. They are partners. They draw nutrients from the air, from the water that flows through botanical channels, from the decomposition of dead material the ботанос cannot process itself. They return nutrients in forms the botanical tissue can absorb. They protect against parasites that would bore into the plant's vascular system. They are, in the terms the people use, the botanical immune system. And the people are the doctors.

## The Night-Callers

When the light shifts from gray-lavender to gray-violet, the твари go quiet. Their eight-second descending calls fade, replaced by a different soundscape—lower, slower, the calls of animals that have evolved for the coldest hours. The people call them night-callers, but this is not a single species. It is a category. It is everything that wakes when the твари sleep.

The largest of them is a creature that moves along the botanical surfaces, climbing with limbs that have evolved to grip the channels and grooves. It is not the Чешуйник. It is smaller, slower, less dangerous to the people because the people are too small to be worth its attention. It eats the insects that swarm in the dim light, the small creatures that live in the water-flows, the occasional твари that has died of natural causes and fallen onto a branch. Its call is a low rumble, a vibration in the botanical tissue that travels through the pillar-network and emerges as a feeling more than a sound. The people can sense it—a pressure in the chest, a tightening in the skull. It means the night-callers are active. It means stay in the shelter. It means the darkness is not empty.

The smallest of them are insects that fly in the perpetual twilight, their wings beating so fast that they generate heat, that they appear as flickering points of light in the gray-violet gloom. They are not bioluminescent. They are simply warm, warm enough to glow in a world where everything else is cold. The people catch them in nets, crush them into paste, spread the paste on surfaces that need to remain ice-free. The heat lasts for hours. The insects die, but there are always more. They breed in the water-channels, in the small pools that form where condensation accumulates. They are infinite, renewable, a resource that does not require cultivation because it cultivates itself.

Between these extremes—the large surface-climbers, the small heat-generating insects—there are creatures the people have never fully catalogued. They hear them. They see traces of them—tracks in the fungal soil, waste-products left on branches, the remains of meals abandoned when something larger approached. They know these creatures exist. They have not named them because naming implies knowledge, and knowledge implies observation, and observation requires getting close enough to see, and getting close enough to see is dangerous. The night is the night-callers' time. The people stay inside. The people survive.

## The Below-World Peoples

They come up sometimes. Trade-caravans from the valleys beneath the cloud-layer, bringing copper and cloth and preserved foods that taste like sunlight—a flavor the plateau people recognize only as an absence, as something their food lacks. The below-world peoples consider the plateau holy, or cursed, or both. They call it the Place Where Clouds Are Born, the Domain of the Silent Gods, the Land That Should Not Be. They come up because the trade is valuable—ботанос silk-sap, fungal medicines, твари feathers that carry properties the below-world healers prize. They come up despite their fear. They come up, make their trades, return to the world below as quickly as they can.

The plateau people do not go down. They can—the root-lakes connect to the plateau's edges, to slopes that descend into the cloud-layer—but they do not. The below-world is too bright. The below-world is too warm. The below-world has a sun you can see, stars that appear at night, a horizon where sky meets land. The below-world is wrong. The plateau people go down for necessity only—to trade, to acquire copper they cannot source above, to meet with below-world representatives who will not climb. They return as quickly as they can. They return to the gray-lavender light, to the pillar-resonance, to the cold that feels like home.

The below-world peoples tell stories about the plateau. They say a person who stays too long will change—will grow taller, will lose the ability to feel warmth, will start to hear the botanical frequencies that the plateau people treat as environment. They say the plateau people are no longer human, that centuries of living inside living architecture have transformed them into something the below-world does not have words for. They say this with fear, with reverence, with the tone of people describing something they do not understand and do not want to. The plateau people hear these stories. They do not correct them. Let the below-world fear them. Let the below-world stay away.

They speak different languages. The plateau language is tonal, built for distance, built for the pillar-network that amplifies certain frequencies and swallows others. The below-world languages are tonal too, but differently—their tones carry emotion, carry grammatical information, carry meanings that the plateau language encodes through other mechanisms. Trade-language exists, a simplified pidgin that neither side speaks well, that serves for negotiating copper-prices and medicinal-exchanges, that fails completely for anything more complex. The plateau people do not want more complex. They do not want the below-world's stories, its histories, its records of a world they cannot see. They want copper. They want cloth. They want to complete the trade and watch the caravan descend back into the clouds.

## The Standing Stones

They are not alive. They were, once—quarried from seafloor rock, shaped by hands that worked with tools the plateau people do not have, arranged in patterns that require aerial perspective to perceive. But they are not alive now. They are simply present. Monuments to something that existed before the uplift, before the ботанос, before the people who now leave offerings at their bases.

The stones do not move. When the plateau rose, everything shifted—the seafloor becoming cliff-face, the shallow waters becoming the cloud-layer, the life that existed below finding itself thrust into a different kind of darkness. The stones remained. Their arrangements held. Their patterns—visible only from heights no local person has reached—stayed coherent, as though they had been designed to survive exactly what happened. The people find this significant. The people are right to find this significant.

The stones are not monuments. This is known because monuments commemorate, and the stones do not commemorate anything the people can identify. They are not temples. Temples require worship, and the stones do not suggest worship—they suggest function, geometry, the practical arrangement of resources for purposes that have not yet become clear. They are not warnings. Warnings communicate danger, and the stones do not communicate danger—they communicate only presence, only the fact of themselves, only the patient waiting of things that do not need to explain why they exist.

The people leave offerings anyway. It costs nothing. The fermented fruit-liquor the people make, the carved tools they do not need, the occasional тварi feather-arrow placed with intention at the base of the nearest stone. The stones accept these offerings or do not—the people cannot tell the difference. The offerings remain until weather removes them, until fungal growth absorbs them, until something that lives in the stone-fields carries them away. The stones themselves do not change. They do not acknowledge. They simply wait.

The oldest of the people say the stones are waiting for something that has not yet happened. The uplift was not the event they were built for. The uplift was a prelude, a geological overture to something larger, something that has not yet arrived. The stones can wait. The stones have waited three thousand seasons already. The stones can wait three thousand more. The people cannot. The people live and die in the shadow of structures whose purpose they will never know, whose builders they will never meet, whose function—if function there is—will outlast every living thing on the plateau.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Nothing. A place where the distinction has dissolved into something else entirely.

The ботанos is the substrate and is alive. The people live inside it, with it, as part of it. The тварi navigate the emptiness between pillars, calling into frequencies that map a world too large to see. The Чешуйник hunts the hunters, glides through the cold air, calls territorial warnings that resonate for kilometers. The fungi grow in the water-channels, feeding the people, protecting the plants, threading through every surface with networks of pale fiber. The night-callers emerge when the light shifts, climbing the botanical surfaces, rumbling their low calls through tissue that transmits vibration better than air. The below-world peoples come up sometimes, trade, return, tell stories about a place they consider holy and cursed and wrong. The standing stones wait for something that has not yet happened, built by hands that knew what was coming, built to survive what arrived.

And beneath all of it, in the root-lakes where the botanical bases interlock, in the fungal networks that thread through every living surface, in the water that flows through channels too small to see—there is something else. The people do not have a word for it. They do not have a concept for it. It is not a god. It is not a spirit. It is not a force or a presence or a being. It is simply the awareness that the plateau is one thing—that the ботанос and the твари and the Чешуйник and the fungi and the people and the stones are all expressions of a single organism, a single system, a single living process that extends beyond the ability of any component to perceive it.

The people know they are small. They know they are cold. They know they are alive for a brief time in a place where life has become the rock on which they stand. They do not need to know more than this. They live inside the body of something larger than themselves. They will die inside it. They will return to it. And the plateau will continue, will flower once per generation, will sing its bass-note resonance through the emptiness, will hold the standing stones in their patterns until whatever they were built for finally arrives.

This is who lives here. This is what lives here. The answer is the same as the question, and the question is the landscape itself.
