# What Lives Here

## The Moot

They are not what you expected.

The first one you see properly—at close range, close enough to touch—stops walking when you stop. It has been following you for some time. You noticed it the way you notice things here: indirectly, through the displacement it causes in the dimness, through the slight settling of the root-mat under its weight. When you turn, it stands not ten meters away, watching.

It is the size of a large dog or a small deer, but the proportions are wrong. The legs are shorter, the body longer, the neck substantial. Its head sits low and forward, not raised on a long neck but carried at the same level as its spine, giving it a forward-facing attention that feels oddly familiar. The eyes dominate the face—saucer-large, adapted to the permanent twilight—but the mouth is also prominent, broad and mobile, capable of shapes that suggest expression.

Its fur is not fur.

This is what strikes you first. The coat is a complex colony: dense undergrowth of actual hair, yes, but rising from that base are mosses, lichens, fine-threaded fungi, patches of something that might be algae. The effect is a living pelt, continuously photosynthesizing (dimly, under these conditions) and continuously respiring. The creature carries a garden on its back. When it moves, small clouds of spores drift from its flanks.

Its color shifts as you watch—not dramatically, not chameleon-fast, but slowly, like mood given vegetal expression. Patches of the moss-colony darken or lighten in response to something. Temperature? Emotion? The presence of a stranger? You cannot tell.

It makes a sound. Not a chirp—the grazers in the distance chirp, small communicative blips—but something more complex. A hum that rises and falls, a statement with syntax. It is speaking.

You do not understand.

It waits. It is patient. It has been patient for a very long time.

## The General Moot

They call themselves something that translates roughly to "the Moot," though that word carries connotations in English—gathering, assembly, meeting-place—that the original does not. Their name for themselves is more fundamental: it means something like "those who continue together."

There are millions of them.

The plateau supports a population you cannot see from any single vantage point because the space is too vast and too partitioned. But they are everywhere: in the canopy, traversing the grown walkways; in the hollow spaces between stems, moving in herds; in the root-mat itself, living in chambers excavated from the living floor. The creatures you glimpsed from your resting shelf were a small group, a family-unit perhaps, moving toward one of the reservoirs. But they are only a fraction of the total.

The Moot are not a single species in the way you understand species. They are a genus, a radiation, dozens of variations on a basic body plan adapted to different niches within the cultivated architecture. Some are large—the size of cattle—and live exclusively on the root-mat, grazing on the nutrient-rich surface. Some are small—rat-sized—and never leave the canopy, living their entire lives in the dim green ceiling. Some are long and many-legged, adapted to the shelf-roads that connect the stems. Some have become nearly immobile, rooted to single spots where they serve functions that the architecture requires.

But they all recognize each other. They all speak, in their various ways, the same underlying grammar.

And they all tend the garden.

## The Gardeners

This is the truth that emerges slowly, through observation and eventually through communication:

The Moot did not inherit this place. They built it.

Or rather—their ancestors did, and they continue to maintain it. The distinction between "builder" and "descendant" has eroded over the millennia, dissolved into a continuous practice. Every Moot is a gardener. Every gardener is the architecture's child and its keeper.

The tools you found evidence of—stone blades, primitive implements—these are still in use. You see them now: carried in the mouths of certain Moot, worn smooth by generations of handling. Some blades are ancient beyond reckoning, passed down through lineages so long that the stone has begun to develop a kind of polish, a patina of intention. They are not made anymore. They do not need to be. There are enough blades for the work that remains.

And what work is that?

Pruning. Shaping. Training. The same tasks the original architects performed, performed still by their descendants who have themselves become part of the system they tend. They prune the stems to maintain the canopy's structure. They clear the water-channels when condensation clogs them. They guide new growth along paths laid down millennia ago, following patterns embedded in the architecture itself.

But more than this: they prune themselves.

## The Cultivation of the Self

The Moot practice autografting.

You learn this when one of them—the same one that first approached you, perhaps, or another that looks identical, they are difficult to distinguish—allows you to observe its routine. It stands still. It uses its mouth, its flexible lips, its small front teeth, to manipulate the moss-colony on its own flank. It is pruning itself. Removing dead growth. Encouraging new growth in specific patterns. Grafting one patch of lichen onto another to create hybrids that never occur in nature.

They cultivate their own bodies.

Every Moot carries a personalized ecosystem, a unique colony tailored over its lifetime to its needs and preferences. Some have moss-patches that produce specific compounds—antibiotics, analgesics, mood-altering metabolites. Some have bred their fur-gardens to glow faintly, creating personal lanterns that light the dimness around them. Some have cultivated symbionts that pre-digest their food, or supplement their nutrition, or protect them from the few parasites that exist here.

And they trade. When Moot meet, they exchange cuttings. They share cultivars. They graft promising strains onto each other's flanks, cross-pollinating their personal gardens. The moss-colonies are language, medicine, currency, and identity all at once.

You realize now why the creature that approached you seemed to change color. It was not mood. It was communication. Its fur-colony was reshuffling itself, carrying information about you, about the encounter, about what it meant. Every Moot is a walking archive, a living record of every meeting it has ever had.

## The Root

There is, however, a more fundamental mystery.

The Moot tend the garden. The Moot maintain the architecture. The Moot prune themselves and each other and the world they live in. But who tends the Moot?

You see it eventually, when you have been here long enough to notice patterns: the way certain Moot move with a different quality of attention, a slower deliberateness; the way they are treated by others—not with deference exactly, but with a kind of recognition; the way their fur-colonies glow more brightly, carry more complex pigments, seem to *know* more.

These are the elders. Not elders in age—age is difficult to determine here, time moves strangely—but elders in the other sense. The ones who have gone deep.

They live in the root-mat.

There are chambers down there, excavated from the living substrate, lined with specialized growths that the Moot have cultivated for millennia. The elders reside in these chambers, partially embedded in the floor itself, connected to the mycelial network that underlies the entire plateau. They have grafted themselves into the architecture.

This is not death. This is not the immobility of decline. This is a choice.

The elders have become nodes in the system. They feel what the roots feel. They taste the digestion happening meters below. They sense the moisture moving through the irrigation channels, the stress on the canopy, the flow of nutrients through the stems. They are the nervous system of the cultivated height, distributed across continental distances, communicating through chemical signals carried in the substrate itself.

And they remember.

## The Memory of the World

The plateau is older than human civilization.

This is what the elders hold: the memory of every event that has occurred here since the first stems were guided into place. They remember the climate shifts. They remember the die-offs and the recoveries. They remember when the cloud-layer thickened and the light began to fail, and they remember how they bred the bioluminescent patches that now glow in the canopy's darkness. They remember when the instability first started—the first time reality flickered, when a stem existed in two places at once and the Moot learned to navigate uncertainty.

They remember the architects.

This is the most carefully held memory, preserved through chemical encodings in the root-mat, passed down through generations of elders, accessible now only through deliberate communion: the Moot were not always the Moot. There was something before. Something that came from below.

The plateau, it turns out, was not built from nothing. Two miles below—assuming below exists, assuming the distance means what you think it means—there was a surface. A world. The ancestors of the Moot lived there, or something like them did. They began to grow.

They grew upward.

Not for any reason you would recognize. Not for shelter or resources or defense. They grew because growing was what they did. They grew because the alternative was not-growing, and not-growing was unacceptable. They grew because something in their ancestors' marrow—a literal marrow, in those days, before the fur-colonies and the grafting and the cultivation of the self—demanded continuation.

They grew for so long that they forgot why they started.

They grew for so long that the world below became a myth, then a rumor, then nothing at all. The elders remember this. They hold the memory of descent, even though no Moot has ever descended. They hold the memory of the ground, even though ground is just a word for something that yields under your feet.

## The Practice of Instability

You ask—through gestures, through the slow pidgin that emerges between you and the Moot who first found you—about the flickering. The way matter here seems uncertain, the way location is approximate.

The Moot responds with a hum that takes several minutes to complete. It is teaching you.

The instability is not a problem to be solved. It is a condition to be inhabited.

The Moot have lived with ontological uncertainty for longer than they can remember—literally longer, because the instability itself degrades memory, makes precise recall difficult. They have adapted. Their minds work differently than yours. They do not expect objects to remain in one place. They do not expect identity to remain constant. They are comfortable with approximation.

A stem is here. A stem is also thirty centimeters to the left. Both positions are true. The Moot navigate by occupying both positions simultaneously, walking paths that exist only partially, trusting that the root-mat will catch them when reality settles.

They have taught their fur-colonies to do the same. The mosses and lichens they carry are bred for uncertainty—they can exist in multiple configurations, shifting between strains, adapting to the flickering architecture. The Moot have become practiced at being approximately themselves.

This is why they do not fear you. Your solidity, your strange density, your refusal to flicker—these mark you as alien, yes, but not threatening. You are simply more stable than the environment can easily support. You stand out. You are a rock in a river.

You will erode eventually. The Moot know this. They have seen it before.

## The Outer Moot

There are Moot who leave.

This is the last thing you learn, and it comes from an elder—a half-embedded form in a chamber deep in the root-mat, glowing with chemical communication, ancient beyond reckoning. It shows you images through direct contact: Moot who have traveled to the edges of the plateau. Moot who have descended. Moot who have explored the space beyond the cultivated architecture.

They do not return.

This is not because they die. This is because they change. The stability of the world beyond the plateau—assuming there is such a world, assuming stability is possible—transforms them. They become something else. They lose the fur-colonies that mark them as Moot. They forget the practice of uncertainty. They solidify.

The elder does not judge this. The elder presents it as information: this is what happens to those who leave. You may leave if you choose. You may return to the world below, if it exists, if you can find it. But you will not return as yourself.

You consider this. The Moot around you—the one who first approached, the others who have gathered nearby, watching—wait patiently. They are good at waiting. Waiting is most of what they do.

The plateau breathes. The warm damp air fills your lungs. Somewhere in the canopy, something pale and many-legged traverses a grown walkway. Somewhere in the distance, a herd of grazers chirps their small communicative sounds.

The elder holds you in its awareness, considering you with eyes that have seen centuries, that have watched the architecture flicker and settle and flicker again. It offers you a cutting from its own fur-colony: a small patch of moss, genetically complex, carrying information you cannot decode.

An invitation. A graft.

You could stay. You could learn the practice of uncertainty. You could cultivate yourself. You could join the Moot in their endless patient work, tending the architecture that tends to them.

Or you could descend.

The plateau does not care. The plateau will continue regardless. It has continued for longer than memory can hold, and it will continue for longer again. It does not need you. But it will accept you, if you choose to stay.

The root-mat yields slightly under your feet. The warm air presses against your skin. The Moot wait, their many-chambered hum filling the dim space, patient as stone, patient as growth, patient as the slow metabolism of the world.
