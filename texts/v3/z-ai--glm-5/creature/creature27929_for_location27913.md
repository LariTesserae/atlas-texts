# The Inhabitants

## The Workers

They did not arrive. They accreted.

This is the first thing to understand about the people of the Ascendant Manufactory—their presence is a function of the place rather than a choice they made. Ask any worker how they came to be here and you will receive a pause, a flicker of something like uncertainty, and then a practical answer about assignment protocols or rotational schedules. The pause is where the truth lives: they do not remember deciding. They do not remember arriving. They were elsewhere and then they were here, and the transition has worn smooth in their memory, like a stone tumbled in a river until its edges became suggestion rather than shape.

Physically, they read as almost-right. The same almost that characterizes the fauna—the birds with too many joints, the scuttling things with clicking lenses. The workers have not developed extra joints or compound eyes, but they have adapted in ways that accumulate the longer you look. Their skin carries a persistent sheen from the damp, not wet but never quite dry, and it has taken on a texture that resists the moisture rather than fighting it. Their hands have thickened in particular ways—from gripping rails, turning valves, tending vegetation with tools meant for machines. The calluses form patterns that would not develop in any other environment.

Their eyes have changed the most. The constant bright—the watching light that never warms, never flickers—has taught their pupils a new range of contraction. They can see in the exposure without squinting. They can read the fine print on assembly instructions in conditions that would dazzle someone from elsewhere. But they have lost something, too: the ability to see properly in true darkness. In the rare spaces where the light does not reach, they move with hands extended, trusting touch over sight, and you can see something like relief in their faces when they return to the illumination.

## Movement and Navigation

The geometry teaches them new habits early, or it weeds them out.

New arrivals—new assignments, new rotational staff, however they are called—move wrong. They walk at the pace of people who expect distance to remain constant. They trust their eyes to tell them when they have arrived. They turn corners expecting the corridor to continue in a straight line from what came before. They do not understand that space here has learned to be flexible.

The workers who remain have developed a grammar of movement that reads as superstition to outsiders but is actually observation, codified. They count their steps in long corridors, and they have learned the specific counts that indicate reality is holding stable versus the counts that suggest the corridor is stretching. They touch the walls in certain ways—not for comfort but for verification, checking whether the surface beneath their palm matches the surface in their peripheral vision. They avoid certain walkways at certain hours, and they cannot fully explain why, only that the numbers feel wrong there at those times.

The phrase "the numbers feel wrong" is common here. It indicates a category of understanding that is neither purely mathematical nor purely intuitive. Workers describe the geometry of a space by how it feels—cramped when the dimensions suggest spaciousness, extended when the tape measure reads correct. They have learned to trust feeling over measurement, because measurement assumes that reality is consistent, and reality here has not earned that assumption.

Children born in the manufactory—if there are children born here—navigate differently than those who arrived as adults. They move through the unstable corridors without the hesitation that characterizes their elders. They do not expect consistency because they have never known it, and this lack of expectation makes them fluent in a way that learned adaptation cannot match. They are more like the animals than like their parents, perceiving geometry as a fluid condition rather than a solid truth.

## The Work Itself

Every worker has an assignment. This is the foundational fact of their days. They wake at intervals determined by the slow-turning schedules, they move through the near-touching layers to their designated station, they perform the tasks that maintain the systems or feed the production lines or tend the struggling vegetation. The work is continuous. It is necessary. And its purpose is often unclear.

This is not to say the workers do not understand their immediate function. A worker assigned to water filtration can explain every stage of the process, can trace the path of moisture from collection channels to holding tanks to the intricate systems of membranes and pressure chambers that render it drinkable. But ask where the processed water goes beyond their station, what systems it feeds, what final product it enables, and the answer becomes hazier. They know their section. The broader purpose is received in fragments, or not at all.

Some workers have learned to find meaning in the continuity itself. The manufactory produces—this is what it does, what it has always done, what it will continue to do on scales that outlast individual attention. To maintain one small section of that production is to participate in something vast, even if the full shape remains obscure. These workers often develop a kind of contentment that reads as eerie to outsiders—too calm, too patient, too accepting of the glacial pace. They have matched themselves to the slow dynamic, and they find satisfaction in the turning of systems.

Other workers chafe. They ask questions that cannot be answered. They try to map the whole of the production process, tracing components through assembly lines and transport systems, always running into barriers—physical, informational, or spatial. A worker who follows a component downward through the platform layers will eventually encounter a door that does not open, or a corridor that leads back to a floor they have already passed, or a stairwell that descends further than any map suggests. The manufactory does not want to be fully known, or it cannot be known fully, and the workers who push against this boundary find themselves worn down by the effort.

## Relationships to the Animals

The workers share space with the emergent fauna, and the relationship is neither hostile nor exactly harmonious. It is negotiated.

The birds with their too-jointed wings have become a kind of ambient presence. They land on railings during breaks, watching the workers with their compound lens-eyes, the focal length clicking and adjusting with an audible tick. The workers have learned to read these ticks as a form of expression—faster clicking indicating agitation or interest, slower rhythms suggesting calm or indifference. They do not speak to the birds, exactly, but they will mutter acknowledgments when passing, and the birds will dip their heads in response, and this passes for conversation in a place where the animals are more present than other humans.

The scuttling things in the maintenance tunnels are less visible but more essential. Workers assigned to machinery maintenance often find the particulates already cleared from corners, the filters already wiped, the tight spaces already investigated by small bodies that can fit where humans cannot. Some workers have developed specific relationships with individual scuttlers—leaving small offerings of food-substitute near favored work sites, receiving in return a kind of prioritized attention to their assigned equipment. It is not domestication. It is mutual acknowledgment.

The larger presences in the upper gantries are avoided. Workers assigned to the highest levels learn to time their movements around the thing's patterns—its footsteps too regular, its breathing too rhythmic. They do not speak of it directly. They reference "the upper activity" or "that time of rotation" and everyone knows what they mean. Occasionally a worker will claim to have seen it more clearly—a shape moving between the gantries, something that might be fur or might be insulation material trailing from damaged conduits—but these accounts are received with careful neutrality. Better not to look too closely. Better not to name what prefers to remain unnamed.

## The Social Fabric

The density of the manufactory's architecture creates a corresponding density in human connection. Workers live in quarters pressed against workspaces pressed against maintenance corridors. Privacy is a relative concept—the walls are thin, the spaces cramped, and the constant damp carries sound in unexpected ways. Everyone knows everyone's schedule, everyone's habits, everyone's struggles with the geometry.

And yet isolation persists. The workers share space without truly sharing lives. They eat together in designated areas, they converse in the pauses between tasks, but there is a quality to their connection that suggests people waiting for something else—waiting for an assignment to change, waiting for a rotation to complete, waiting for whatever condition brought them here to finally release them. They care for each other in practical ways—covering shifts when illness (or something like it) intervenes, sharing resources when the distribution systems lag—but the deep bonds of chosen community are rare. They are together because the manufactory put them together, and they make of that proximity what they can.

Romantic and sexual relationships do form, but they operate within the strange temporality of the place. A couple might be together for years by the counting of shifts and rotations, but they will struggle to say how long that is in any external measure. Time moves differently here—not faster or slower, but differently, broken into units that do not translate. Partners learn to navigate each other's geometry the way they navigate the corridors: checking what feels stable, avoiding the areas that stretch and shift, trusting feeling over measurement.

Children are... uncommon. Not absent, but uncommon. Those who are born here are raised collectively, passed between workers as schedules allow, taught the grammar of movement and the wisdom of not-looking from the earliest age. They do not ask why they are here because they have no concept of elsewhere. The manufactory is their world—its damp air and watching light, its slow-turning systems and emergent animals. To them, the unstable geometry is simply how space works, and they move through it with an ease that their parents find both impressive and unsettling.

## The Researchers

A distinction must be made between the workers and the researchers, though the boundary is less clear than either group would claim.

The researchers came to study the manufactory—its physics, its anomalies, the way reality buckles under accumulated density. They have equipment that measures what the workers feel, instruments that quantify the wrongness in the corridors and the potential in the structures. They speak of gravitational anomalies and controlled collapse, of substrate breakdown and possibility-space visualization. Their language is precise, technical, and reassuringly scientific.

But the researchers have been here for a long time. Longer than their rotation schedules would suggest, though no one points this out. They have adapted to the slow dynamic in ways they do not acknowledge. Their experiments run on timescales that initially required patience and now simply require presence. They have developed the same sheen to their skin, the same adaptation to the watching light, the same tendency to trust feeling over measurement when they think no one is watching.

The workers regard the researchers with a mixture of respect and skepticism. Respect because the researchers have language for what the workers experience—words that make the strangeness feel knowable, if not actually known. Skepticism because the researchers still believe, at some level, that the strangeness can be solved. They still operate as if the right measurement will stabilize the geometry, as if the correct theory will render the manufactory comprehensible. The workers know better. The manufactory does not want to be solved. It wants to continue, and it will continue, with or without understanding.

Some researchers have begun to realize this. They are the ones who start to sound like workers, who start speaking of "how the numbers feel" and "the shape of the space." They are the ones who stop trying to map the impossible corridors and start learning to navigate them. They are in the process of becoming what they study, and they know it, and they do not know how to stop.

## The Knowledge They Carry

Ask a worker what the manufactory produces and they will give you a partial answer. They can name components, substances, devices—they can describe the objects that pass through their stations and the processes that shape them. But the final purpose, the ultimate destination, the reason for all this continuous industry—these remain opaque.

This opacity is not ignorance. It is something more specific: a learned disinterest. Workers who have been here long enough have discovered that asking what the production is for leads nowhere productive. The answer is always deferred—another department, another level, another system beyond their clearance or their comprehension. And because the manufactory has its own time, because the systems continue whether or not they understand, they have learned to make peace with not knowing.

But they know other things. They know which corridors hold and which ones drift. They know the sounds that indicate stable machinery and the sounds that suggest a system beginning to fail. They know the particular rhythm of the birds' clicking lenses and what each rhythm means. They know how to read the erosion that has not yet occurred—the potential gaps between panels, the future weaknesses in joinings. They carry an accumulated wisdom that is neither technical nor intuitive but something between, a practice of perception developed over time and through experience.

This is what they have instead of purpose: a deep fluency in the place itself. They belong here in ways that cannot be explained to those who have never lived in the watching light, never navigated the shifting geometry, never adapted to the slow dynamic. They are part of the manufactory now—another system in the dense press of structures, another process that turns and maintains and continues.

## What They Are Becoming

The manufactory changes those who remain. This is not a threat or a warning—it is a description, as neutral as the damp air and the bright light.

The workers who have been here longest have become difficult to distinguish from the environment. Not physically, not yet—though their skin has changed, their movements have changed, their perception has changed. But the deeper transformation is harder to see. They think at the pace of the systems. They process time in units of production cycles and moisture collection. They have stopped expecting elsewhere, stopped remembering arrival, stopped anticipating departure. They are present in a way that is difficult for those from outside to comprehend—fully occupied by their here and now, their assignments and rotations, their small negotiations with the fauna and their careful navigation of the geometry.

If you ask them what they want, they will pause. They will flicker with something like the uncertainty that accompanies questions about arrival. And then they will give you a practical answer—better rations, more reliable equipment, a shift in the distribution schedules. They will not say they want to leave. They will not say they want to stay. They have reached a state where wanting itself has been subsumed by the continuity. The manufactory produces, and they are part of that production, and the question of desire has become irrelevant.

This is what lives in the Ascendant Manufactory: people who have been incorporated. Not against their will—there is no will here that could resist, no self that remains separate enough to resist. They are not prisoners and they are not believers. They are inhabitants in the deepest sense of the word—people who have become the place they live, its dampness and its density, its slow time and its watching light, its emergent life and its emergent strangeness.

They are what the manufactory made after it made itself.
