# What Lives Here

The first answer is: the mat. The mat lives here.

But that is too simple, and it is also not simple enough, because what lives here is not one thing and has never been one thing, and the mat — that continuous, tangled, grey-green fabric that covers the fitted stone like moss covers a gravestone — is not an organism. It is a community. It is a civilization, if that word can be stretched far enough, and every word here needs stretching, because the vocabulary was built for a world of surfaces and sunlight and bodies that move, and none of those things apply.

---

Start with what you can see.

The tendrils. They rise from the mat — not vertically, because vertical has no meaning here, but *outward*, in the direction the current permits, which is mostly north, gently, the way a flag leans in a wind so slow it is more rumor than weather. Each tendril is the width of a finger, perhaps a little more, and as long as an arm, perhaps a little longer, and they do not grow individually. They grow in clusters, in bundles, in what might be called colonies if colonies did not imply separate organisms cooperating, and these do not cooperate because cooperation requires a self that chooses to help another self, and what lives here does not choose. It does what it does. The doing is the being.

Each tendril is translucent at its tip and opaque at its base, where it joins the mat. The translucence is the growth — the new tissue, still forming, still building itself from the dissolved mineral the currents carry through the grid. The opaqueness is the age, the accumulation, the layers of tissue laid down over years — over decades — that have compressed into something dense and almost woody. If you were to slice a tendril open (and nothing here has ever been sliced open; nothing here has ever been cut; the concept of cutting belongs to a world of tools and edges and intentions, and this is not that world), you would see rings. Not like tree rings, not exactly — tree rings are annual, tied to the rhythm of seasons, and there are no seasons here. These rings would be finer, more numerous, tied to no rhythm at all except the rhythm of growth itself, which is to say: the rings appear when they appear, and they mark nothing but the passage of time, and time here passes so slowly the rings are almost touching.

The bioluminescence lives in the tendrils. Not throughout them — only in the outermost layer, the skin, the surface that meets the water. The cells that produce the glow are small, smaller than the cells of any surface plant, and they are densest at the tip, where the growth is newest, and sparsest at the base, where the tissue is oldest and thickest. This is why the glow is uneven, why each tendril seems to carry a small lantern at its end — because it does. The light is brightest where the organism is youngest, and it dims as the tissue ages, and by the time the tendril has become part of the mat, the glow is gone, absorbed into the general shimmer, the ambient luminescence that rises from the whole surface like heat rises from skin.

The pulse is not a heartbeat. It has no center. It is not coordinated by any nervous system, any clock, any pacemaker. Each tendril pulses on its own, and the pulse is a function of the cell's chemistry — the slow accumulation of a luminescent substrate, the slow trigger that releases it, the slow depletion that follows, the slow recovery. The cycle takes minutes. But because each tendril is on its own cycle, and because the tendrils are packed so densely that hundreds are visible at any moment, the aggregate effect is not a pulse at all. It is a shimmer. A mottling. A slow, breathing variation in the light, like clouds passing over a moon, except there are no clouds and there is no moon, and the variation is the life of the place expressing itself in the only language it has: light.

Nothing sees the light. This is important. The light is not for seeing. The light is not *for* anything. It is a byproduct of the biochemistry, the way heat is a byproduct of metabolism — a waste product, an excess, a thing the organism produces because it cannot help producing it, because the reaction that sustains the cell releases a photon as a side effect, and the photon leaves the cell and enters the water and travels two meters and is absorbed by the darkness and is gone. The light does nothing for the mat. It does not attract. It does not communicate. It does not reveal. It is simply what the living looks like when the living is this kind of living, in this kind of dark, at this kind of cold.

Except — and this is the thing that nags — the light is not random. The pulse is not a single chemical reaction running its course. It is complex. There are phases. There is a bright phase, a dim phase, a bright phase, a dim phase, and the transition between them is not linear; it follows a curve, a sigmoid, a shape that suggests regulation, feedback, control. Something is managing the light. Something within the cell, or within the colony, or within the mat, is modulating the bioluminescence, adjusting it, keeping it within a range. Not because the range matters — the light does nothing — but because the chemistry that produces the light is connected to the chemistry that sustains the growth, and the growth must be regulated, and so the light is regulated, and the regulation is visible, and the visible regulation is the closest thing this place has to a face.

---

Go deeper. Go into the mat.

The mat is not a surface. It is a volume. It has depth — meters of depth, in the basins, less on the rises — and within that depth, the structure is layered, and the layers are distinct, and the distinction is the distinction between life and death, except that death is not quite the right word, because nothing here dies the way things die on the surface, with a cessation, a moment, a before and an after. Here, death is a gradient. The tissue at the top of the mat is alive. The tissue at the bottom is not alive. Between them is a continuum, a slow transition, a fading — the way a sound fades, the way a color fades, the way a memory fades — from the full intensity of living biochemistry to the mineral stillness of what was once alive and is now becoming something else.

The something else is the substrate. The peat-analogue. The compressed, partially decomposed, still-organic-but-no-longer-living matter that sits on top of the fitted stone and that is, in the deepest basins, several meters thick. This is where the decomposition happens. This is where the dead vegetable matter is reduced — slowly, so slowly — to the nutrients the living mat can use.

And here is where the second answer begins: the decomposers live here.

---

They are not animals. They are not plants. They are not fungi, though the analogy to fungi is the closest available, and even that analogy is wrong in fundamental ways. They are — perhaps — something like what bacteria are on the surface, single-celled or simple-multicellular, heterotrophic, consuming organic matter, but they are not bacteria in any taxonomic sense, because bacteria belong to a tree of life that is rooted in a world of oxygen and sunlight, and this world has neither, and the tree of life here — if there is a tree — is a different tree, with different roots, in different soil, and we do not have the taxonomy for it because we did not build our taxonomy for this.

What we can say: they are small. They are very small. The largest of them is perhaps the size of a grain of sand, and most are much smaller — microscopic, submicroscopic, approaching the scale of the chemical reactions that constitute them. They do not move. Or rather, they move the way all small things in water move: by diffusion, by the random motion of molecules, by the slow drift of the current. They do not swim. They do not crawl. They exist in the water that permeates the substrate, in the spaces between the dead plant tissue, in the film that covers every surface — every stone, every dead tendril, every mineral grain — and they do what they do, which is: they take.

They take organic molecules from the dead tissue. They break the bonds — the carbon-carbon bonds, the nitrogen bonds, the bonds that held the living cells together — and they use the energy released to build themselves, to maintain themselves, to continue. They are not eating. Eating implies a mouth, a gut, a process of ingestion and digestion and excretion. This is not that. This is chemistry. This is surface chemistry — reactions at the interface between the dead tissue and the water, catalyzed by the structures these organisms have built for themselves, which are not bodies but *surfaces*, reactive surfaces, molecular scaffolds that hold the right atoms in the right positions to make the right reactions happen.

They are the decomposition. They are the reason the dead matter becomes nutrient. Without them, the dead tendrils would accumulate, would pile up, would choke the living mat, and the whole system would stall — the living would die, the dead would remain dead, and the Drowned Lattice would be a graveyard. Instead, the decomposers work, and the dead becomes nutrient, and the nutrient rises through the mat — carried by the slow diffusion of water through tissue — and the living takes it up, and grows, and dies, and the cycle continues.

They have been here as long as the vegetation. Perhaps longer. Perhaps they were here first — perhaps the first organisms to colonize the fitted stone were not the plants but the decomposers, arriving in the water, settling on the stone, building their reactive surfaces on the bare mineral, waiting for something to die so they could begin their work. Or perhaps they arrived together, the plants and the decomposers, arriving as a system, arriving as an ecology, arriving the way an ecosystem arrives on a new volcanic island — not piece by piece but in fits and starts, in waves of colonization, each wave bringing what the next wave would need.

It does not matter. They are here now. They are the dark matter of this world — invisible, uncounted, present in every handful of substrate, in every liter of water, on every surface. They are the reason the cycle turns. They are the smallest inhabitants, and they are the most necessary, and they are the least visible, and this seems right, for this place: that the most important thing is the thing you cannot see.

---

There is a third thing that lives here, and it is harder to name.

It is not an organism. It is not a collection of organisms. It is not the mat, which is a collection, or the decomposers, which are a community. It is the *system* — the whole of it, the interlocking, self-regulating, self-sustaining network of stone and water and plant and decomposer and current and mineral and light and cold.

The system lives. Not metaphorically. Not in the way a philosopher might say a ecosystem "lives" to make a point about interconnectedness. The system lives in the way that any sufficiently complex feedback network lives: it maintains itself. It adjusts. It compensates. If the current slows — if a channel fills with silt — the vegetation in that area grows less, dies more, the decomposition slows, the nutrient release slows, the mat thins, and the thinning mat allows more water to reach the channel, which flushes the silt, which restores the current, which restores the growth, which restores the mat, which thickens, which slows the water, which drops the silt, and the cycle returns to its starting point. This is homeostasis. This is what a body does when it regulates its temperature, its pH, its blood sugar. The Drowned Lattice does this. It is a body. It is a body the size of a continent, made of stone and water and plant tissue, and it has been maintaining itself for longer than the builders were builders.

This is the thing that lives here that is most alive, in the sense that "alive" means "maintaining a state against entropy through the use of energy." The mat is alive the way a cell is alive — it does its thing, it grows, it dies. The decomposers are alive the way enzymes are alive — they catalyze, they process, they turn one thing into another. But the system is alive the way a *body* is alive — it holds itself together, it responds to perturbation, it heals. Not consciously. Not intentionally. But functionally, mechanically, in the way that a thermostat is functional, that a valve is mechanical, that a heart is automatic.

The system is the largest inhabitant. It is the only inhabitant that has a boundary — because the mat has no boundary (it extends to the edge of the water, to the edge of the stone, to the place where the construction ends and the natural seafloor begins, wherever that is), and the decomposers have no boundary (they are everywhere the water is), but the system has a boundary. It is the boundary of the grid. Where the channels end, the system ends. Beyond the grid, the water is different — it carries different minerals, different temperatures, different organisms. The grid is the membrane. The system is the cell. The Drowned Lattice is a cell, and it has been alive for a very long time, and it is not dying, and it is not growing, and it is not changing. It is maintaining. It is holding its state. It is being the thing it is, with the quiet conviction of something that has been itself for so long that being has become the same as existing.

---

And the builders?

The builders do not live here. The builders have not lived here for a time so long it makes the word "not" feel insufficient. The builders are gone, and they are gone the way a species is gone — not destroyed, not killed, not erased, but *ended*, finished, completed, the way a sentence is completed. They did what they did. They made the stone. They cut the grid. They built the structures whose stumps now stand beneath the mat like bones beneath skin. And then they were done, and being done, they left — or died — or changed into something else — or were always something that could not stay — and the place they made went on without them, and the place grew over, and the growth became the place, and the place became what it is.

But there is a sense — and this is the fourth thing that lives here, the most difficult thing, the thing that is barely an inhabitant and more of a residue — there is a sense in which the builders persist. Not as ghosts. Not as memories. As *pattern*. The grid persists. The fitted stone persists. The tool marks persist. The intention persists — the idea that the floor should be level, that the channels should be straight, that the structures should be placed at intervals, that the blocks should be small, hand-scale, carry-able. These are not just physical features. They are decisions. They are the record of decisions made by beings who are no longer here, and the decisions have outlasted the deciders, and the decisions are still being followed — by the water, which still flows through the grid; by the vegetation, which still grows on the stone; by the system, which still maintains itself within the boundary the builders set.

The builders live here as intention. As the shape of the thought that someone had a very long time ago, pressed into stone, held in water, maintained by the patient, cold, slow continuation of the plan. The builders are the thought. The mat is the thinking. The decomposers are the forgetting. And the system — the whole vast, cold, dark, glowing, humming, barely-alive system — is the continuation, the way a sentence continues after the speaker has stopped speaking, carried by the momentum of its own grammar, completing itself according to rules the speaker set in motion but no longer controls.

---

So what lives here?

The mat lives here. Thousands of millions of tendrils, each one a slow cold lantern, growing and dying and growing, the skin of the world.

The decomposers live here. The invisible ones, the molecular ones, the ones that turn death into life without ever being seen.

The system lives here. The whole of it — the cell, the body, the continent-sized thing that maintains itself the way a heart maintains its beat, automatically, without intention, with the deep conviction of a process that has found its rhythm and will not stop.

And the builders live here. As pattern. As intention. As the fossilized grammar of a mind that is no longer a mind, speaking through stone, through water, through the slow green glow of a world that has outlasted its makers and become its own reason.

Four things. Or one thing in four registers. Or a thousand things — because each tendril is a thing, and each decomposer is a thing, and each block of fitted stone is a thing, and each moment of the hum is a thing, and the cold is a thing, and the dark is a thing, and the water is a thing, and the slowness is a thing, and together they are the thing that lives here, which is not a creature and not a community and not a system but a *place* — a place that is alive the way a mountain is alive: slowly, massively, without urgency, without direction, without any need to be anything other than what it is.

The Drowned Lattice lives here. It is what lives here. It is the only thing that lives here. It has been living here for longer than living has a name, and it will continue to live here for longer than that, and the living it does is the living of stone and water and the slow green glow and the hum that is below hearing and the cold that is below feeling and the patience that is below patience, which is to say: it is the living of something that has been alive so long it has forgotten what it means to be alive and has become simply, completely, what it is.
