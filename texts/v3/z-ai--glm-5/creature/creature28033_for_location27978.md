# What Lives Here

---

## The Lichens

They were here first. Not first among the animals, not first among the visible things—first among everything that persists. The lichen mats in the shadow zones are organisms that have been growing for longer than the concept of agriculture has existed. Some patches may be older than the human species itself. They spread at rates measured in millimeters per century, and the largest of them—those dark circles swelling across the granite where the canyon rims cast their longest shadows—have been expanding since before the last ice age receded from these elevations.

What lives in a lichen is a negotiation. A fungus provides structure, a photosynthetic partner provides food, and together they become something neither could be alone. The lichens on the plateau are crustose—flat, tight to the stone, their bodies pressed against the granite as if trying to merge with it. They are dark gray, almost black, with occasional flushes of rust where mineral content varies. Their surfaces are minutely textured—bumpy, ridged, sometimes cracked into mosaic patterns where the thallus has aged and fragmented.

They live in the shadows. This is their rule. The direct sun would desiccate them in minutes; the condensation that forms in the pre-dawn cold would evaporate before they could absorb it. But where the canyon rims throw their long reach across the plateau each afternoon, where the stone fins channel shadow into troughs that stay dark for hours, the lichens flourish. They flourish as slowly as anything has ever flourished. They are patient in a way that has nothing to do with waiting, because waiting implies the expectation of change. They are simply growing. They have always been growing. They will continue to grow until the shadow moves or the stone erodes or the world shifts beneath them.

If you crouch beside one of the larger patches and look closely—closer than most visitors ever look—you can see the internal structure. The lichen is not a single surface. It is a landscape, a terrain of small mounds and depressions, of cracks and fissures where the thallus has split and begun to heal. Tiny fruits—apothecia, cup-shaped reproductive structures—dot the surface like craters on a distant moon. These produce spores that will almost never germinate on the bare granite, because lichens rarely establish themselves on new stone. They grow outward from where they are. They do not colonize; they expand.

And inside them, in the matrix of fungal hyphae and algal cells, something else lives.

## The Insects

The lichen mats are not barren. They are habitat.

Springtails—tiny, soft-bodied arthropods no longer than a grain of sand—live in the microspaces within the lichen's structure. They are so small that the lichen's texture is their terrain, its cracks their valleys, its mounds their mountains. They feed on the lichen itself, on the detritus that accumulates in its depressions, on each other. They exist in numbers that would seem impossible if you could see them, but you cannot see them. They are pale, translucent, nearly invisible against the dark thallus. They are the foundation of the plateau's animal food web, and they are almost nothing.

With them live mites—eight-legged, armored, slow. They crawl across the lichen's surface with a deliberation that seems excessive for their size, pausing to feed, pausing to rest, pausing for no observable reason. Some are predators, hunting the springtails with a patience that matches their substrate. Others are scavengers, consuming the dead and the dying. They have been here as long as the lichens, or nearly. Their ancestors crawled across the same mats when the mats were half their current size.

There are also ticks. Not the parasites of lowland fame, but a genus unique to high elevations—flat, dark, almost geometric in outline, living in the cracks of the lichen and emerging to feed on whatever blood-bearing creature passes close enough. They are few. They are patient. They wait for the birds.

And beetles. Small, dark, compact beetles with fused wing covers and legs built for gripping stone. They cannot fly. They do not need to. They live their entire lives within a few square meters of lichen, walking from patch to patch in the shadow zones, feeding on the smaller insects, hiding in the lichen's fissures when the light shifts. Their bodies are shaped like the stone they live on—flattened, streamlined, polished. If you pick one up and hold it against the granite, it disappears.

The insects are the reason anything larger lives here. They are the invisible substrate of the plateau's animal life, the tiny machines that convert lichen and condensation and dead matter into the protein that feeds everything else. They do not know this. They are simply living.

## The Burrowing Mammals

In the deeper cracks—the fissures where the granite has split cleanly, forming channels that extend meters into the stone—something else lives.

They are small. Smaller than mice, larger than shrews. Their bodies are elongated, their legs short and powerful, their fur a pale gray that matches the stone's patina so precisely that a living individual and a piece of debris are indistinguishable at any distance. They are rodents, or near-rodents—their taxonomy is disputed, and the few specimens collected and studied show enough divergence from any known lineage that their classification remains unsettled. They may be relics. They may be the last representatives of a group that was once widespread, pushed upward by changing climates and competitors until only this remained: the highest, harshest, emptiest place they could hold.

They live in the cracks. That is their entire world. The fissures in the granite—some natural, some expanded by generations of patient clawing—form networks that extend deep into the stone, connecting through channels too narrow for any predator to follow. They emerge at dusk and at dawn, the two periods when the temperature hovers near freezing and the condensation forms on the stone's surface. They drink from the moisture that beads in the shallow bowls. They eat the insects that emerge from the lichen mats. They gather seeds and plant fragments from the sparse vegetation and cache them in their deepest chambers.

They are social, in their way. Colonies occupy connected crack systems, with multiple individuals sharing the same network. They communicate through ultrasonic calls—frequencies too high for human ears, lost entirely in the roaring of the wind—marking territory, signaling alarm, maintaining contact. They breed slowly. A female produces one litter per year, of two or three young, in a chamber deep in the stone where the temperature is stable and the wind cannot reach. The young are born blind and furless, and they mature slowly by rodent standards, remaining in the nest for weeks before emerging to explore the crack system.

They are not the plateau's only mammals—others pass through occasionally, or did historically. But they are the only mammals that live here, that are here, that belong. They are as much a part of the stone as the lichen, as the mica, as the wind-smoothed grooves. They have no other home.

They do not have a name in any human language, because no human has studied them closely enough to name them.

## The Grasses and Succulents

The plants are not the story, but they are present.

In the protected troughs between stone fins, where wind speed drops and shadow lingers, tufts of stiff grass emerge from cracks in the granite. They are not like lowland grasses. Their blades are thick, almost succulent, their surfaces covered in a waxy cuticle that reflects light and seals in moisture. They are silver-gray, the same patina as the stone, and they move only in the strongest gusts. Their roots extend into the cracks where condensation collects, reaching into micro-fissures in the rock where water persists for hours after the surface has dried. They grow slowly. They reproduce rarely. Most individuals are clones—offshoots from a single root system, connected underground by rhizomes that thread through the stone's fractures. A single genetic individual may occupy meters of trough, its clones spreading along the crack system like a slow green tide.

They are old. Not as old as the lichens, but old. The largest clones may be centuries old, their root systems extending through networks of cracks that have been stable since before the current climatic regime stabilized. They have survived because they do not need to change. They are already optimized for this place—for the cold, the light, the dryness, the wind. There is nothing to adapt to. They are adapted.

The succulents are different. They live in the mineral bowls—the shallow depressions where water collects and evaporates, leaving rings of precipitated minerals that mark the stone like growth rings in a tree. They are flat, almost two-dimensional, their rosettes pressed against the stone's surface as if trying to be invisible. Their leaves are hard, almost stonelike, their surfaces textured with mineral deposits that accumulate as water evaporates from their tissues. They store the condensation that pools in the bowls, and they bloom once a decade—maybe less frequently—with pale flowers that last three days.

Three days, once in ten years. The flowers are small and white and unremarkable by any standard of lowland beauty. But they are the only flowers on the plateau, and the insects emerge from the lichen mats to visit them, and the burrowing mammals gather the seeds, and the cycle continues.

The succulents do not seem to know they are rare. They are not waiting for their decade. They are simply growing, slowly, in the mineral bowls, and when the conditions align—moisture, temperature, some internal clock that has been counting since the last bloom—they flower. Then they stop. Then they grow.

## The Stone-Runners

The birds.

They are the plateau's visible life, its obvious abundance, the thing that any visitor notices first and remembers longest. They are everywhere along the canyon rims, and they are the reason the plateau feels as it does—full at the edges, empty at the center, a place where life has found a single impossible niche and exploited it beyond all reasonable limits.

They are corvids. This is clear from their anatomy—the strong bills, the heavy bodies, the intelligence visible in their black eyes. But they are corvids the way a whale is a mammal: adapted so completely to their environment that the relationship to their cousins is not immediately apparent. Their bodies are the size of ravens, but their legs are longer, proportionally, than any other corvid's. They walk as much as they fly. Their feet are large, with long toes that grip the stone's surface, and they move across the granite with a fluid, unhurried gait that seems almost human. When they fly, it is with purpose—to reach a nesting ledge, to chase an insect, to shift from one section of the rim to another. They do not soar. They do not play in the wind. They are not here for the sky.

Their feathers are pale gray above—matching the granite so precisely that a roosting bird is invisible at ten meters—and white below, reflecting the intense light away from their bodies. Their bills are dark, almost black, and their eyes are the same black, bright, watchful. When they look at you, they are looking at you. They are assessing. They are remembering.

They nest in the canyon walls—every ledge, every crack, every scooped hollow where a beak can shape the stone. The nests are substantial structures built of stone fragments, lichen fragments, and the stems of the sparse grasses, cemented with guano and the mineral dust that accumulates in the nesting sites. They are built to last. Some nests have been occupied for generations, accreting material over decades or centuries until they form substantial structures projecting from the cliff faces like shelves. The colony extends for kilometers along the plateau's edge—a continuous city, dense and noisy and permanent.

They eat everything the plateau offers. Insects from the lichen mats—probed from the lichen's surface with deliberate strokes of the bill, the birds walking across the dark patches with the focused attention of foragers who know exactly where the food is. The burrowing mammals—caught at dawn and dusk when they emerge to drink, the birds dropping from the rim to the crack openings and waiting, patient, still, for a movement that triggers the strike. Seeds from the grasses and succulents. Carrion, when it exists—though on the plateau, little dies that is not quickly consumed. They are opportunistic, adaptable, intelligent. They solve problems. They remember solutions.

They are social in ways that are still being documented. The colony is not a single group but a complex of overlapping social units—families, clans, coalitions—with shifting alliances and territories that seem to be maintained more through vocalization than through direct conflict. Their calls are varied and specific. Contact calls that maintain group cohesion in the wind. Territorial calls that announce ownership of a nesting ledge or a foraging patch. Alarm calls that distinguish between aerial threats and terrestrial ones, that specify the direction and distance of the danger. Mating songs that are individually distinct, learned, and refined over years.

And the constant, unceasing vocalization that is not any of these things—a continuous low churring that seems to serve no function except presence. The birds call to confirm that they are here. They call to confirm that the others are here. They call because calling is what they do, and the plateau without their voices would be a different place, would be wrong in a way that the plateau itself does not know how to be.

They do not migrate. They do not leave. They have never left, or if they have, they have always returned, and the return is so established that it is not a return but a continuation. They are here in the winter, when the temperature drops far below freezing and the wind reaches speeds that would tear feathers from a lesser bird. They press together on their ledges, sharing warmth, their bodies forming dense aggregations that are visible from a distance as pale patches on the cliff faces. They are here in the brief summer, when the sun beats down and the condensation forms and the insects emerge and the cycle ticks forward another increment.

They mate for life, or for the practical equivalent of life. Pairs form in their second or third year and remain together until one dies. The surviving partner may take a new mate, but the interval is long—months, sometimes years—during which the survivor continues to occupy the nesting ledge alone, calling the mate's song in a voice that is, or seems, diminished. They raise one clutch per year, of two or three eggs, in the substantial nests on the canyon walls. Both parents incubate. Both feed. The young fledge after six weeks and remain with the parents for months, learning the plateau's geography, the foraging routes, the nesting sites, the calls. They are taught. They are educated. The plateau's knowledge is held in the birds, passed from generation to generation in a tradition that is older than any human record of the place.

They are ancient here. Not as old as the stone, not as old as the lichens, but old. The colony has existed along this rim for longer than the canyon system has looked the way it does now. The birds have been here while the wind carved the grooves deeper, while the stone fins shifted by millimeters, while the climate cycled through changes that are invisible at the scale of a human lifetime but visible at the scale of a bird lineage. They have adapted when adaptation was necessary and not adapted when it was not. They are adapted now.

They are the plateau's voice. The wind provides the instrument—the canyon walls, the fluted channels, the gaps and tunnels—but the birds provide the sound that the wind carries. Or they seem to. The roaring is the wind, is the stone, is the birds, is the combination of all three into something that none of them alone could produce. The birds call into the wind and the wind shapes their calls and the stone shapes the wind and the birds live in the stone, and the sound is one thing with many sources.

## The Absent Ones

There were people here. Not many, not often, but some.

They left tools. Stone flakes, hand-axes, blades of surprising delicacy—worked from the plateau's own granite and from chert and quartzite that does not occur here, that must have been carried up from the lowlands. They are old. Older than metalwork, older than settled life, older than any tradition that still exists. They were made by hands that knew the stone the way the birds know the wind—intimately, practically, with a competence that is visible in the work.

The tools lie in the deep troughs where the shadows are longest. They were dropped, or placed, or lost—and they have remained where they are, unchanged by the slow weathering that affects everything on the plateau. The equilibrium holds them. They are as they were when they fell.

Who made them? The question is not answerable. There are no bones, no marks on the rock faces, no other traces. The people who passed through left nothing but tools, and the tools tell us only that they came, they worked, and they left. They did not stay. The plateau does not support staying, not for creatures with human needs. The elevation is too high, the cold too extreme, the resources too sparse. They came for something—stone, perhaps, or views, or reasons that left no archaeological trace—and they left, and the plateau returned to its previous state.

But the tools are here, and they are not nothing. They are evidence of a kind of attention, a kind of presence, that the plateau's current inhabitants do not practice. The people who made these tools looked at the stone and saw something in it. They saw the way it fractured, the way it held an edge, the way the mica caught the light. They worked with it. They shaped it. They made it into something it was not, and then they left it behind.

The birds do not do this. The lichens do not do this. The burrowing mammals do not do this. Only people do this, and the people are gone.

The tools lie in the shadows. The mica in their surfaces catches the light that reaches them, and they shine, faintly, in the way that stone shines when it has been worked by hands that understood it.

## The Wind

The wind lives here too.

This is not metaphor. The wind is a presence on the plateau in a way that it is not in other places—not a force that acts upon the landscape but a condition that is the landscape. It does not arrive and depart. It is always here, moving through the canyon rims at speeds the lowlands never experience, pouring through channels in the rock, playing the stone like an instrument. It is a resident. It has been here longer than the birds, longer than the lichens, longer than the stone's current shape. It has been here since the plateau rose.

It does not live in the way that organisms live. It does not eat, does not breed, does not die. But it has a behavior. It has patterns. It has preferences—channels it returns to, gaps it seeks, surfaces it favors. It shapes what it touches. It is shaped by what it touches. It and the stone have been in conversation for so long that the conversation has become a kind of stillness—the stillness of equilibrium, of two forces that have found their balance and now simply maintain it.

The wind does not know the birds. It does not know the lichens or the mammals or the insects. It moves through them as it moves through the stone—indifferently, constantly, with a force that is not personal because it is not a person. But the birds know the wind. They live in it. They nest in the walls it shapes, they drink from the condensation it helps form, they call into it and hear their calls returned, transformed. The wind is their medium, the way water is a fish's medium. They are adapted to it. They could not exist without it, not here, not in these numbers. The wind creates the conditions that allow the colony, and the colony has no idea that this is the case.

The wind screams. It has been screaming for so long that the screaming is not screaming anymore. It is sound. It is the sound of this place, the baseline, the condition. Everything that lives here lives in the wind, and the wind lives in everything that lives here, and the distinction between them is not useful.

## What Lives Here

What lives here is a system. Not a community, not an ecosystem, not a collection of species—though it is all of those things. A system. The stone and the wind and the lichens and the insects and the mammals and the grasses and the succulents and the birds and the absent people and the tools they left and the cold and the light and the condensation that forms in the pre-dawn dark: all of these are parts of a single thing, a thing that has been running for longer than any of its components have existed in their current forms.

The system does not know it is a system. It does not know anything. It is a process—a slow, stable, frozen process that has reached equilibrium and now simply continues. The wind carves the stone by millimeters per century. The lichens expand by millimeters per century. The birds call, and the calls are the same calls, and the generations pass, and the calls are still the same. The tools lie in the shadows and do not change.

What lives here lives at the edge. This is the landscape's primary rule, and everything in the system follows it. The birds: at the edge. The lichens: at the edge of shadow. The mammals: at the edge of the cracks. The grasses: at the edge of the troughs. The insects: at the edge of visibility. The tools: at the edge of memory. The wind: at the edge of the plateau, where the land falls away and the air pours through.

The center holds nothing. The center is stone and light and the wind moving across a flat, empty surface. The center is the space between presences, the hollow that makes the abundance at the edge feel like abundance rather than density. The center is the reason the edge exists.

What lives here is the relationship between the emptiness and the fullness. The relationship is the thing. The relationship is what has been here for a very long time. The relationship is what will be here for a very long time more.

The birds call. The wind screams. The light beats down. The lichens grow. The mammals drink from the condensation pools. The insects move across the dark mats. The tools lie in the shadows. The stone holds.

This is what lives on the Singing Plateau.
