# The Inhabitants of the Exudation Tiers

## The Drowned Kindred

They call themselves the Drowned—not from despair, but from recognition. They are people who have been submerged in this place so long that they have become a different species of human. Not in the biological sense, not yet, though the changes have begun, but in the deeper sense: they think differently, perceive differently, inhabit their bodies differently than the dry-world ancestors who first built the Tiers and then were built by them in return.

A Drowned body tells the story of the place. The skin is the first thing a visitor notices: soft, almost mucosal in its texture, adapted to constant moisture in ways that would seem concerning if you came from somewhere dry. It doesn't prune after long exposure to water because it is always exposed to water. The skin drinks the humidity directly, and the Drowned have been observed surviving without drinking in the conventional sense for periods that would kill a visitor from a drier climate. Their skin also carries a faint bio-luminescent signature—not visible under normal conditions in the Tiers, where the ambient glow of the city's luminescent walls washes out such subtleties, but detectable in absolute darkness. The pattern varies by individual and seems to serve some function in the deep tiers where light fails entirely, a way of recognizing kin in the black wet.

Their hands have changed. Not dramatically, not into something unrecognizable, but the webbing between fingers has extended subtly over generations, and the fingertips have developed a concentration of sensory pits that can read the mineral content of water, can taste the difference between water that has passed through the oldest calcite formations and water drawn fresh from the thermal vents below. A Drowned artisan can touch a wet surface and tell you, with reasonable accuracy, how long that water has been circulating, what systems it has moved through, whether it is safe to drink or process or channel elsewhere. They read water the way a dry-world farmer reads soil.

The Drowned are not tall. Ten thousand years of living in packed tiers, of navigating corridors designed by ancestors who had different aesthetics of space, of sleeping in alcoves carved into walls that were once exteriors—this has selected for compactness. The average height has decreased by perhaps fifteen centimeters since the founding, and the trend continues. They are not small, precisely, but they are dense, built for efficiency rather than reach. Their bones show higher mineral density than the dry-world norm, perhaps from the constant absorption of calcium and other minerals from the saturated air, or perhaps from some deeper adaptation to the weight of the water they move through constantly.

Their relationship to sound defines them. Every Drowned child is born into noise that would deafen a visitor within hours, and the auditory system has adapted accordingly. The external ears have grown smaller over generations, folding closer to the skull, protecting themselves from the constant assault. The auditory canal has developed elaborate structures of cartilage and membrane that filter and dampen, that separate useful sound from the ambient roar. And the brain has evolved—or learned, the distinction is unclear over these timescales—to process vibration through the skeleton itself, reading the hum of the city through the soles of the feet, the palms of the hands, the skull pressed against a wall at night.

Many Drowned still go deaf, eventually. The noise wins, over decades. But deafness here is not what it would be elsewhere. A deaf Drowned elder can still hear, after a fashion, by placing their hands flat against surfaces, by feeling the specific frequencies of water moving through pipes, of structures settling under load, of fauna moving in the dark lower tiers. They develop this sensitivity deliberately, training for it in adolescence, preparing for the day when the ears will finally surrender. The oldest Drowned are often the most skilled at reading the city's vibrations—not despite their deafness but because of it. The noise that would overwhelm a hearing person becomes, to the deaf, a landscape of information.

They are not alone in the Tiers. Migrants come, from time to time, from the dry rim-worlds beyond the basin's edge. The Drowned call them the Parched, and watch them with a mixture of pity and wariness. The Parched struggle here—their skin cracks in the humidity, ironically, having evolved to retain moisture and finding itself instead overwhelmed by it. Their lungs labor at the thick air. Their ears begin to bleed within days without protective gear. They move through the Tiers like ghosts, barely present, already leaving even as they arrive. Some stay, though. Some adapt. A Parched child raised in the Tiers from infancy will grow up Drowned in everything but bloodline, their body making the adjustments that genetics has not yet had time to encode. These children—the Dry-Drowned, they are sometimes called—represent the ongoing incorporation of the city's population, the slow expansion of what it means to belong to this place.

The Drowned do not maintain the city through written manuals. They maintain it through bodies—through the bodies of those who taught them, and the bodies they teach in turn. Knowledge lives in muscle memory, in the way a hand reaches for a particular valve without conscious thought, in the way a body moves through a particular corridor at a particular angle to avoid the spray from a predictable crack. They have professions, but the professions are not what a visitor might expect. There are Water-Readers, who taste the circulation and diagnose what ails the hidden channels. There are Stone-Singers, who read the vibrations of structures and predict where the next fracture will form, where the next collapse will threaten, where the next support must be placed. There are Tier-Seers, who navigate the byzantine layering of the city and guide others through sections that would otherwise be impassable, the old routes having been buried or flooded or repurposed beyond recognition.

The Tier-Seers are among the most respected. The city is not merely large—it is deep, and the depth is not entirely mapped. There are sections of the Tiers that no living person has entered, flooded chambers and abandoned construction that exists only as rumor, as the occasional emergence of strange water from unknown depths, as the muffled sounds that rise from grates that lead nowhere contemporary maps acknowledge. The Tier-Seers know the habitable sections with precision, and they know the boundaries beyond which even they will not go. They carry mental maps of labyrinthine complexity, updated constantly through word of mouth, through the network of workers who maintain the circulation, through the subtle shifts in vibration that indicate where the city is changing today. A Tier-Seer can walk from one side of a level to another without ever emerging into the open air—through buildings, through passages cut through walls that were once exterior, through spaces that have been interior so long that no one remembers what they were before.

The Drowned sleep in shifts, and they sleep in groups. Privacy is a concept that evolved elsewhere, in places where space was available to be parceled. Here, sleep happens in the Sleep-Tiers—sections of the city given over entirely to rest, where hundreds of bodies lie in close proximity, breathing in unison, the noise of their respiration joining the larger noise of the city. They sleep in alcoves and bunks and hammocks slung between pipes, wherever space permits. They sleep to the sound of water moving, which is the only lullaby they have ever known. Children raised in the Sleep-Tiers often struggle to sleep in silence, when they encounter it later in life. The quiet feels like a threat, like the absence of the city's life.

Families are not organized along the lines that outsiders might recognize. The Tiers do not permit the nuclear family structure, with its assumptions of private space and exclusive bonds. Instead, families are organized by Tier and by work-cycle—those who sleep at the same time, in the same Sleep-Tier, form the primary emotional and caretaking bonds. Children are raised collectively by their sleep-cohort, by the adults who wake when they wake, who move through the city's schedules in parallel. A child may know their biological parent, but they know their sleep-parents—the adults who raised them in the daily rhythms of the Tiers—far more intimately. The biological tie is acknowledged, but it does not define kinship.

They speak a language that is not entirely spoken. The noise of the city makes verbal communication difficult at the best of times, and impossible at the worst. The Drowned have developed a sign language of extraordinary complexity—a gestural system that can communicate subtle information in the visual field, that can be read at a distance, that can carry on conversations while the hands are otherwise occupied by taking advantage of the city's predictable blind spots. But they have also developed something harder to describe: a language of posture and orientation, of body-angle and proximity, that communicates emotional state and social intention without either speech or sign. A Drowned person can enter a room and read, from how people are standing, from where they have placed themselves relative to the walls and the water-flow, what kind of space this is, what kind of interaction is expected, what kind of danger might be present. They read rooms the way a dry-world person reads faces.

Their relationship to the fauna is complex. The creatures of the Tiers are not pets—they are too numerous and too wild for that—but they are also not what a visitor would call wild animals. They are cohabitants. The Drowned have developed relationships with the various species that move through their living spaces, relationships that range from tolerance to partnership. The hairless cat-things are known to hunt the insect-analogs that would otherwise overwhelm stored food, and the Drowned have learned to leave spaces for them in storage chambers, to allow them access to the areas where their hunting is most useful. The swimming reptiles in the flooded lower levels are avoided when possible and respected when not—there are protocols for moving through water where they hunt, rhythms of movement that signal a human presence and reduce the likelihood of conflict. The bird-things are left to their aerial domain, their bodies occasionally collected when they fall, their feathers used for decoration in a culture where usable organic materials are scarce.

But the Drowned do not eat the fauna. This is a choice, not a necessity—they could, and in extremis they have—but they have developed other food sources that they prefer, that do not compete with the ecological web they depend on. The bacterial mats, properly processed, provide protein. The fungal growths, carefully selected and prepared, offer nutrients. And there are the gardens—not plant gardens, but mineral gardens, engineered formations where edible crystal structures are grown from water saturated with specific dissolved compounds. The Drowned eat stone, in a sense. They eat what the city exudes. They eat the products of processes they maintain but do not fully understand.

Their aesthetic sense has evolved to match their environment. Beauty in the Tiers is not the beauty of symmetry or simplicity. It is the beauty of texture, of variation, of the complex patterns produced by water moving through and over surfaces. Drowned art is almost entirely textural—carved reliefs meant to be touched rather than seen, surfaces designed to produce specific sounds when water flows over them, arrangements of mineral growth that create particular patterns of condensation and drip. Their music is not melodic in any conventional sense; it is rhythmic, percussive, built on the sounds that can be extracted from the city's materials, played on pipes and struck surfaces and the controlled release of water pressure. It integrates with the ambient noise rather than trying to rise above it.

Their spiritual practices center on water and circulation. The city is understood as a body—a living entity, not metaphorically but in some genuine sense, an entity of which they are part. They do not worship the city; that would imply a separation they do not feel. But they do practice rituals of maintenance and care that go beyond the practical, that acknowledge a relationship of mutual sustenance. When a section of the water circulation fails, they repair it not merely because they need the water, but because to fail to do so would be to fail in their role as the city's circulatory system, the mobile component that the city cannot grow for itself. They are to the city what certain cells are to a body—moving, maintaining, clearing debris, keeping the flows running.

They bury their dead in the deep tiers, in the flooded sections that no one inhabits, returning the body to the water that has sustained it throughout life. There is no grief in this, not in the conventional sense. Death is a return to the larger circulation, a rejoining of the mineral and fluid processes that the city runs on. The Drowned do not fear death as an ending. They fear it as a disruption—their death means a gap in the maintenance, a task undone, a valve unturned. They train successors with care, passing on not just knowledge but the muscle-memory of their particular work, the way they move through their section of the Tiers. The worst death, in their understanding, is a death that leaves work undone.

They tell stories about the beginning, but the stories are not consistent. No one knows what the Tiers were originally built for—what purpose could justify this concentration of structure and energy in a planetary depression that was always going to fill with water. Some say it was a refuge, a place to escape something that no longer exists elsewhere. Some say it was a testing ground, an experiment in sustainable habitation that long ago ceased to need justification. Some say the city built itself, that the first inhabitants simply found it here and moved in, and have been adding to it ever since without ever understanding what they were adding to. The truth is buried beneath ten thousand years of construction and reconstruction, and the Drowned do not let this trouble them. They are more interested in the present circulation than the ancient purpose.

They are not unhappy. This is important to understand. A visitor might look at the crowded conditions, the overwhelming noise, the constant moisture, the strange food, the incomprehensible technology they maintain without understanding—and might conclude that they are oppressed, trapped, surviving rather than living. But this is a projection. The Drowned have adapted so thoroughly that they would find dry-world conditions unbearable. The silence would feel like death to them. The horizontal space would feel like a threat. The cold would feel like an assault. They are not trapped in the Tiers any more than a fish is trapped in water. They are shaped for this place, and it is shaped for them, and the relationship is not one of imprisonment but of mutual constitution.

A Drowned person visiting the dry-world rim would experience what a dry-world visitor experiences in the Tiers, only in reverse. They would find the air impossibly thin, their gasping breath unable to extract enough moisture. They would find the silence oppressive, the lack of vibration information leaving them deaf in ways that transcend the auditory. They would find the space disorienting, too much of it, no walls close enough to touch. They would feel exposed, vulnerable, their bodies designed for a denser medium. They would be, in short, a fish out of water. And they would return to the Tiers with relief, descending through the increasingly humid layers, feeling the noise rise around them like a warm embrace, finally coming home to the wet and the warm and the roar of ten thousand years of habitation.

They love this place. They love it as it is, not as some ideal of what it could be. They love the crystalline growths that fracture the light, and the bacterial mats that shimmer across standing water, and the hairless cat-things that hunt in their storage chambers, and the swimming reptiles that move through the flooded depths. They love the walls that pulse with hidden circulation, and the floors that warm their feet, and the endless architectural variety that means every corridor holds some surprise. They love the water that falls constantly from above, the precipitation of condensation that makes every journey a passage through gentle rain. They love the noise that tells them the city is alive and functioning and that they are part of its function.

They love each other, too. The Drowned are not cold or distant. Their emotional lives are as rich as any human population's, perhaps richer for the intimacy forced by their crowded conditions. They have forms of closeness that are difficult to translate—ways of being together that do not require the privacy other cultures assume necessary for intimacy. They know each other's bodies thoroughly, not sexually necessarily, though that too, but physically, as parts of a shared environment. They know how their sleep-cohort members move, the sounds of their breathing, the vibrations of their footsteps. They know each other in a total way that dry-world people might find alarming, but that the Drowned find deeply comforting. To be alone, for them, is not to be in a private space. It is to be separated from the cohort, from the circulation, from the body of the city. Solitude is not privacy; solitude is isolation.

They have conflicts, of course. Any population this dense, any community this interdependent, will develop frictions. But their conflicts are worked out within the structures of their society, through the mediation of their social networks, through the gradual process of consensus that keeps the Tiers functioning. They cannot afford extended conflict. They cannot afford to stop working. And so they have developed practices of resolution that are not written down anywhere but are passed from body to body, the same way all their knowledge passes. They hold Councils—gatherings of those affected by a particular dispute, held in spaces designated for this purpose, where the conflict is worked through until it can be released. The process is not quick, but it is thorough. Nothing is forgotten. Everything is felt.

They have leaders, but not in the way outsiders might expect. Their leaders are not commanders; they are connectors. The most respected Drowned are those who know the most people, who can bring together the right individuals for a particular task, who understand the networks of relationship that underlie all work in the Tiers. Influence flows through these connectors, not through command. A connector cannot order anyone to do anything; but they can suggest, and their suggestions carry weight because everyone knows that the connector understands the web of relationships better than anyone else. To be a leader among the Drowned is to be a keeper of the social network, a maintainer of the invisible circulation of trust and obligation that makes all other circulation possible.

They fear things, but not the things that dry-world people fear. They do not fear death; they fear ending badly, leaving work undone. They do not fear the fauna; they fear the disruption of the ecological balance that keeps the fauna in their proper relationships. They do not fear the noise; they fear the silence that would signal catastrophic system failure. They do not fear the water; they fear the water stopping, the circulation ceasing, the city dying around them as its fluid lifeblood cools and stagnates. Their deepest fear is of breakdown—not of individual death, but of the collective systems that make life possible.

They tell stories of breakdown, cautionary tales passed through generations. The most famous is the Tale of the Third-Tier Flood, a historical event that may or may not have occurred as described. In the story, a section of the Tiers flooded catastrophically when circulation was disrupted by a conflict that prevented proper maintenance. Thousands died. The section was abandoned, sealed off, left to the swimming reptiles and the dark. The tale is told to children as they begin their training, as a way of encoding the stakes of their work. It is also told to adults when conflicts begin to interfere with maintenance, as a reminder of what happens when social failure becomes structural failure.

They do not want to leave. This is perhaps the most important thing to understand about the Drowned. They have had opportunities. Visitors have offered to take individuals or groups out of the Tiers, to bring them to the rim-worlds where space is available and air is dry and silence can be found. The Drowned invariably decline. Those few who have accepted, who have gone to see the outside world, almost always return. They come back thinner, their skin dried and cracked, their hearing damaged by the silence, their vibration-sense atrophied from disuse. They come back having discovered that they are no longer capable of living anywhere else.

They come back, and they step into the humidity, and they feel their skin drink the air, and they hear the roar of the city rising around them, and they feel the vibration of the circulation through the soles of their feet, and they know that they are home. They are Drowned. They belong to the water. They belong to the tiers. They belong to each other, and to the strange living city that has made them what they are. And they will remain there, maintaining what they can, adapting to what they cannot, moving with the water and the warmth and the noise until they become part of the structure themselves—another layer in the endless accretion, another body returned to the deep circulation, another set of hands and feet and ears and vibration-sensitive bones added to the city that has always been their home.
