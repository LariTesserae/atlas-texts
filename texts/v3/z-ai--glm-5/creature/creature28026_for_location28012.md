# What Lives in the Drowned Plateau

## The Stilt-Walkers

They were not always here. Or perhaps they were, and simply had not yet assembled themselves into forms large enough to notice. The stilt-walkers emerged from the matrix of microbial threads and vegetative debris over countless generations, pulling themselves together from fragments that had learned cooperation as the only means of survival in a place where movement was costly and stillness was cheap.

A stilt-walker stands perhaps three meters tall when full-grown, though "stands" is not quite the word. It perches. It balances. It extends itself upward on limbs that are more like roots than legs, jointed in places where flexibility proved advantageous, rigid in places where rigidity served better. The limbs are not uniform. One may be thick and covered in the fine filamentous growths that trap heat from the warm strata; another may be thin and bare, adapted to reach through the cold boundaries without disturbing them. Each limb is a compromise between the walker's needs and the ecosystem's demands, negotiated over evolutionary time through a process that left no record except the forms themselves.

The body — if it can be called a body — hangs between these limbs like a gathered curtain, a membrane dense with the organelles of conversion. The stilt-walker does not eat in any recognizable sense. It positions itself in the border zones between strata, where temperature differentials create the pressure gradients that power its metabolism, and it absorbs. The absorption is slow, patient, matching the pace of the place it inhabits. A walker might remain in a single position for weeks, its limbs sunk into the silt, its membrane gently undulating, drawing from the meeting of waters the energy required to continue existing.

They do not sleep. They do not wake. They simply persist, their awareness diffused across their entire surface, sensitive to temperature shifts, chemical gradients, the faint vibrations of distant sounds. When a float-pod detaches and begins its slow descent, every walker within kilometers knows. When a warm current meanders through the lower strata, they know. When one of their own finally yields to structural failure and begins to dissolve, they know that too — and they do not mourn, because mourning requires a relationship to time that they have never developed.

Their reproduction, when it occurs, is as patient as everything else about them. A walker that has accumulated sufficient resources — that has found its place in the ecosystem secure enough to risk division — will begin to bud. The process takes months. A small cluster of cells forms near the top of the membrane, gathering the materials it will need to become independent, learning from the parent's surface the arrangement of limbs that has served best. The offspring is not a copy. It is an iteration, a variation on a theme that the parent has spent its life perfecting. When the bud finally detaches, it drifts for days or weeks, a tiny fragment of potential, until it settles on a patch of substrate that will support its first tentative limbs. The parent does not watch it go. The parent has already returned to its absorption, its patient waiting, its place in the dense silence.

There are perhaps four hundred stilt-walkers on the plateau, though counting them requires a definition of individuality that becomes fuzzy at the edges. Old walkers sometimes fuse with young ones, their membranes merging where they touch, their limbs tangling into configurations that neither would have chosen alone. The fused beings continue to absorb, to persist, to occasionally bud — but they are no longer discrete, no longer countable, no longer the kind of thing that can be tracked through the water column. They become features of the landscape, architectures of living tissue that the younger walkers navigate around, obstacles that have chosen not to move.

The stilt-walkers have no language, no communication, no society in any sense that surface-dwellers would recognize. And yet they have learned, across the generations, to arrange themselves in patterns that maximize their collective survival. Near the plateau's center, where the warm strata are thickest and most stable, the walkers stand dense and tall, their membranes deeply folded, their limbs interwoven into structures that trap and retain heat. Near the edges, where the walls rise and the water grows thin, the walkers are sparse and small, their bodies adapted to the cold, their presence almost accidental. The gradient between these extremes is smooth, continuous, as if the population were a single organism that had learned to distribute itself according to need. But there is no central coordination. There is only the slow accumulation of individual choices, each walker finding its place, each generation inheriting the arrangement of its predecessors and modifying it by fractions of a degree.

## The Ribbon-Weavers

If the stilt-walkers are the plateau's architects of patience, the ribbon-weavers are its only concession to motion. They are not animals — not in the sense that surface biology would recognize — but they move, and their movement has purpose, and their purpose has created the only structures on the plateau that were not grown from vegetative stock.

A ribbon-weaver is a long, flat strand of tissue, rarely more than a few centimeters wide but sometimes extending for meters, its edges frilled with the ciliated structures that provide propulsion. It moves by undulation, slow ripples that pass along its length, pushing against the thick water with a gentleness that barely disturbs the surrounding threads. It has no head, no front or back, no center that could be called a self. It is a line that has learned to move, a boundary that chose direction.

The weavers earned their name through their construction. When a ribbon-weaver finds a gap in the canopy — a place where the floating vegetation has thinned enough to admit excessive light — it begins to weave. The process takes weeks. The weaver pulls filaments from the surrounding growth, gathering the tough cellulose strands that the plants produce and have no use for, and it braids them together into sheets that float just below the surface. The sheets are not dense enough to block light entirely, but they diffuse it, scatter it, prevent any single point from receiving more photons than the ecosystem can absorb. The weavers are the plateau's repair mechanism, its response to injury, the agents that close wounds before they can fester.

A single weaver might produce dozens of these patches over its lifetime, each one slightly different, adapted to the specific gap it addresses. The patches persist long after the weaver has moved on, becoming part of the canopy itself, substrate for the same vegetative growth they were designed to moderate. The plants grow over them, through them, incorporating the woven material into their own structures. In this way the weavers contribute to the very density that creates the gaps they repair. The cycle is not ironic to them. It is simply what they do.

The ribbon-weavers have no eyes, no organs of perception that surface biologists would recognize. And yet they find the gaps they need to find, navigating the complex three-dimensional architecture of the plateau with an accuracy that suggests awareness. They sense the light — not as a visual phenomenon but as a chemical pressure, a differential in the water's composition that marks the boundary between illuminated and shadowed regions. They follow these gradients with the patience of water finding its level, adjusting their course over hours or days, arriving at last at the place where their weaving will be most useful.

They reproduce by fragmentation. A weaver that has grown too long, that has accumulated too much material for efficient propulsion, will simply break apart. The fragments continue to move, to weave, to exist as independent entities, each one carrying in its chemical memory the patterns that served its progenitor. There is no moment of division, no ceremony of separation, no recognition that a single being has become many. The fragments drift apart over weeks, losing contact with each other, becoming distinct through the simple accumulation of separate experience.

Sometimes fragments fuse. Two weavers that cross paths in the water column may tangle, their edges interlocking, their tissues merging where they touch. The fused being is larger than either fragment alone, more capable of producing significant patches, more able to navigate the boundaries between strata. Fusion is not reproduction but it is also not death; it is a third option that surface biology has not invented, a way of continuing that involves neither the loss of identity nor its preservation, but rather its transformation into something that contains the original as a memory.

## The Deep-Sitters

At the bottom of the plateau, among the sharp rocks and the settled silt and the bones that do not decay, there are things that might once have been animals. The deep-sitters retain the basic architecture of mobile creatures — bilateral symmetry, sensory structures that cluster at what might be called a head, limbs that might once have been used for locomotion. But they have not moved in millennia, and their stillness has transformed them into something else entirely.

A deep-sitter begins its existence as a larva, a tiny free-swimming thing that darts through the lower strata in search of a place to settle. The larvae are nearly invisible, transparent except for the dark clusters of developing organs, their movements quick and almost frantic compared to the languid pace of everything around them. This phase lasts only days. When a larva finds a suitable location — a depression in the rock, a cleft between ridges, a space protected from the gentle currents that do exist — it attaches itself and begins the long process of becoming a sitter.

The transformation is total. The limbs that carried the larva through the water thicken and root, digging into the silt, extending into the porous rock beneath. The sensory structures that clustered at the head redistribute across the body's surface, becoming nodules that detect the chemical composition of the water rather than its visual properties. The bilateral symmetry softens into something more radial, more appropriate to a creature that no longer has a front or back, no longer needs to distinguish between the direction it came from and the direction it is going. What remains of the larva's mobility is concentrated in its feeding apparatus — a crown of tentacles that can extend to grasp passing debris, retracting slowly, bringing food to a mouth that has migrated to the center of the body.

The deep-sitters are the only creatures on the plateau that deliberately hunt. Their tentacles can reach up to a meter from the body, weaving through the water column with a slowness that makes them nearly invisible to the drifting grazers they consume. A grazer that blunders into a sitter's crown will be wrapped, enveloped, drawn down into the central cavity where digestion proceeds over weeks. The sitter does not kill its prey quickly. It has no capacity for speed, no mechanism for anything but the gradual application of enzymes and patience. The grazer dissolves while still alive, its simple nervous system registering only the slow dimming of sensation, the gradual loss of boundaries, the final release into the chemistry of another being.

There are fewer deep-sitters than stilt-walkers, perhaps two hundred across the entire plateau. Their distribution is uneven, clustered around the areas where the silt is deepest, the rock most porous, the chemical gradients most favorable. They communicate with each other through the release of compounds that diffuse through the lower water layers, signaling their presence, their reproductive status, their success or failure in obtaining food. The communication is not language. It is more like the exchange of state, each sitter broadcasting the conditions of its existence, each sitter receiving the broadcasts of its neighbors and adjusting its own chemistry in response.

When a sitter has accumulated sufficient resources, when its crown has captured enough prey to sustain the effort, it releases a cloud of gametes into the water above. The cloud drifts, disperses, mingles with the clouds released by other sitters in the same reproductive season. The fertilized eggs settle slowly through the strata, becoming larvae before they reach the bottom, beginning their brief mobile phase before they have even encountered the world they will eventually inhabit. Most will not survive. The plateau's floor is sharp, crowded, hostile to creatures that have not yet found their places. But a few will settle, will attach, will begin the long stillness that constitutes their adult existence, and the cycle will continue.

The deep-sitters remember in their bodies the history of the plateau's rising. Their genetic code contains sequences that date to the time before the inundation, adaptations to conditions that no longer exist, responses to stimuli that the plateau has not produced in millions of years. Occasionally a sitter will activate one of these ancient sequences — will release a compound that served some purpose in the shallow seas where its ancestors lived, will move a tentacle in a pattern that was useful before the lifting changed everything. The movement accomplishes nothing. The compound dissipates without effect. But the behavior persists, encoded in flesh, a memory that predates the medium in which it now occurs.

## The Drift-Gatherers

Between the stilt-walkers and the deep-sitters, in the middle strata where the light is green and the warmth is moderate, there exists a category of being that defies classification. The drift-gatherers are not plants, not animals, not fungi or bacteria or any of the categories that surface science has named. They are accumulations, assemblages, communities that have become so tightly integrated that they function as single organisms.

A gatherer begins as a fragment — a piece of vegetative debris, a shed limb from a stilt-walker, the remains of a grazer too small to interest a deep-sitter. The fragment drifts through the water column, gathering the organisms that cling to any passing surface: bacteria, fungal threads, the microscopic animals that live in the spaces between larger lives. The fragment becomes a host, a meeting place, a tiny ecosystem contained within a volume no larger than a drop of water. And then, somehow, the ecosystem coordinates.

The coordination is not conscious. It is chemical, electrical, a network of signals that passes through the gatherer's components, linking them into a system that can respond to its environment as a unified whole. The bacteria begin to produce compounds that the fungal threads can use. The threads weave structures that protect the bacteria from grazers. The microscopic animals feed on the debris that the other components cannot process, returning the nutrients in forms that all can share. The fragment that began as dead material becomes the skeleton of a living being, a body assembled from the organisms that happened to find it.

The gatherers move, slowly, by mechanisms that are not yet understood. They have no limbs, no flagella, no structures that could produce propulsion. And yet they drift with purpose, orienting themselves toward the warmer strata during the day and the cooler strata at night, maintaining their position in the water column through means that seem to involve the coordinated beating of thousands of cilia belonging to thousands of different organisms. The movement is not fast — a gatherer might travel ten meters in a day, following the shifting gradients of temperature and chemistry — but it is movement nonetheless, evidence that the assemblage has become something more than the sum of its parts.

The drift-gatherers reproduce by fragmentation, but their fragmentation is different from the ribbon-weavers'. When a gatherer grows too large for the resources available in its immediate environment, it begins to separate, not into two pieces but into many — dozens, sometimes hundreds, of tiny clusters that drift apart, each one containing a sample of the original's diversity. The fragments continue to function, to grow, to gather new organisms into their expanding communities. They are not clones. They are seeds, each one carrying the potential to become something its parent never was, to incorporate species the parent never encountered, to develop configurations the parent never imagined.

There are thousands of drift-gatherers in the plateau's waters, perhaps millions, their numbers shifting constantly as new fragments form and old gatherers exhaust the resources of their locations. They are the plateau's most flexible inhabitants, the most capable of adaptation, the most likely to survive whatever changes the future might bring. They are also the most fragile, the most dependent on the density of life around them, the most vulnerable to any disruption that might thin the microbial crowds they require for their continued existence.

The gatherers have no awareness as individuals. But there is some evidence that the plateau's entire population of gatherers functions as a single distributed organism, exchanging chemical signals across distances that no individual could traverse, coordinating their movements to cover the water column in patterns that optimize the absorption of heat and light. The evidence is circumstantial — the presence of compounds in gatherers that have never been in contact, the synchronization of reproductive cycles across the plateau's extent — but it suggests a form of life that surface biology has not yet imagined: a being that exists as thousands of separate bodies, each one distinct, each one mobile, each one capable of independent survival, but each one also part of a larger whole, a presence that spans the entire drowned plateau and speaks to itself in the slow language of chemical exchange.

## The Bones and What They Hold

The bones that litter the plateau's floor are not, strictly speaking, alive. They are the remains of creatures that died here when the waters first arrived — surface animals caught in the rising, terrestrial beings that drowned in the transformation of their forest home into an underwater garden. Their flesh dissolved millennia ago, absorbed by the microorganisms that were already present in the new medium, their proteins and fats incorporated into the chemistry of the plateau's earliest inhabitants. But their bones remain, white and sharp among the rocks, the only features on the floor that are clearly not geological.

And yet the bones are not empty. They have become architectures, substrates for the growth of organisms that specialize in the colonization of calcium. Bacterial films coat their surfaces, layers of cells so dense that the bone beneath is no longer visible, living membranes that derive their energy from the slow dissolution of the material they inhabit. Fungal threads penetrate the interior spaces, filling the marrow cavities with networks so fine that they seem to glow in the faint light that reaches the bottom. Small grazers — relatives of the drift-gatherers but specialized for this particular environment — move across the bone surfaces, consuming the bacterial films and leaving behind waste that feeds the fungi, a miniature ecosystem contained within the boundaries of each skeleton.

The bones have been here so long that they have become part of the plateau's geography. Silt accumulates around them, forming ridges and valleys that guide the flow of what currents exist. The deep-sitters cluster near the larger skeletons, using them as anchors for their rooting limbs, drawing from the calcium a chemical stability that the surrounding rock cannot provide. The bones are not passive. They shape the world around them, create the conditions for the lives that depend on them, contribute to the ecology of the floor in ways that their original owners could never have imagined.

There is one skeleton that differs from the others. It lies near the plateau's center, larger than any of the terrestrial animals that could have been caught in the rising, its bones arranged in a pattern that suggests something not quite mammalian, not quite reptilian, not quite anything that surface paleontology has named. The skeleton is incomplete — some bones have drifted away, others have been absorbed by the organisms that colonize them, still others have been incorporated into the structures of deep-sitters that rooted too close — but what remains is enough to indicate that this was a large creature, perhaps the largest that ever lived on the plateau, a being that was present when the waters came and chose to stay.

The skeleton's skull, if it is a skull, faces north, toward the wall where the warm strata concentrate during the afternoon. The skull's interior has been filled by a growth so dense that it seems solid — a crystallization of bacterial films and fungal threads and mineral deposits that has accumulated over millions of years into something that resembles a geode more than a biological structure. When the light strikes this growth at certain angles, it refracts, splitting into colors that do not appear anywhere else on the plateau, a private rainbow visible only to those who pass close enough to see.

The deep-sitters avoid this skeleton. Their chemical signals shift when they approach it, becoming patterns that do not appear in any other context, sequences that might be warning or might be reverence or might be something for which surface language has no equivalent. The skeleton does not move, does not emit, does not appear to do anything at all. But it presides. It occupies. It persists in the center of the plateau like a truth that everyone knows but no one speaks, a presence that shapes the lives around it without ever revealing its purpose.

## What Else Lives Here

There are other beings on the plateau that deserve mention, though space and the limits of comprehension prevent full description.

The canopy-swimmers are small, never more than a few centimeters in length, and they live exclusively in the tangled mat of vegetation that floats just below the surface. They have adapted to the three-dimensional maze of stems and fronds, their bodies flattened and flexible, able to squeeze through gaps that seem too small for any living thing. They graze on the vegetative matter itself, biting off pieces of the floating forest and digesting them slowly, contributing to the constant rain of debris that falls through the water column. Their lives are brief — weeks, perhaps months — but their numbers are vast, millions of individuals processing the canopy's growth and returning it to the water as the nutrients that sustain everything below.

The boundary-guards are even smaller, microscopic organisms that cluster at the interfaces between the water's strata. They form visible films, shimmering sheets that mark the borders between layers, and they appear to regulate the exchange of materials across these borders. They allow some compounds to pass while blocking others, maintain the distinct chemistry of each stratum while permitting the controlled diffusion that the ecosystem requires. They are the plateau's customs agents, its border police, the invisible infrastructure that makes the layered water column possible. Without them, the strata would merge, the warm and cold waters would mix, and the complex ecology that depends on their separation would collapse.

The sound-makers are rare and mysterious, inhabitants of the deeper regions that produce the only deliberate sounds on the plateau. The sounds are not vocalizations — the sound-makers have no mouths, no resonant chambers, no structures that could produce voice. They create sound through movement, rhythmic contractions of their bodies that send pressure waves through the water, pulses that travel for kilometers and fade slowly into the silence. The purpose of these sounds is unknown. They do not appear to attract mates, do not warn of danger, do not serve any function that surface biology can identify. They might be art. They might be prayer. They might be the expression of something that lives in the deep water and needs, for reasons that only it understands, to make itself heard.

The bone-singers are organisms that have colonized the terrestrial skeletons and derived from them a capacity that no other plateau inhabitant possesses. They vibrate, producing sounds that resonate through the calcium structures they inhabit, amplifying and modifying the pressure waves that reach them from other sources. When a sound-maker pulses in the deep water, the bone-singers respond, taking the sound and transforming it, adding harmonics and overtones that were not present in the original. The result is a music that exists nowhere else on earth — or perhaps anywhere — a composition created by the collaboration of beings that have never seen each other, separated by the full thickness of the water column, communicating through the medium they share.

## The Relationship of All These Things

The inhabitants of the drowned plateau do not exist in isolation. They exist in relationship — to each other, to the vegetative matrix that forms their environment, to the water itself and the layers it contains, to the light that falls through the surface and the darkness that accumulates at night. Every being described here is connected to every other by threads of dependency so numerous and so fine that they cannot be mapped, only acknowledged.

The stilt-walkers provide structure for the ribbon-weavers' patches. The weavers maintain the canopy that shades the water where the drift-gatherers drift. The gatherers process the debris that falls from the canopy and would otherwise smother the deep-sitters. The sitters consume the grazers that would otherwise overpopulate and strip the vegetation. The vegetation provides the debris that the gatherers gather, the surfaces that the canopy-swimmers graze, the architecture that the sound-makers use to orient themselves in the dark. The bones provide the calcium that the bone-singers sing through, the anchors that the deep-sitters root in, the geography that shapes the currents that distribute the chemical signals that coordinate the entire ecosystem.

And behind all of this, beneath it, surrounding it, there is the water — the medium that remembers its origins, the layers that do not merge, the warmth and light that fall through and are captured and converted into the slow sugars of existence. The water is not itself alive, but it contains life so densely that the distinction becomes meaningless. Every milliliter holds millions of organisms. Every cubic meter holds billions. The beings described here are the visible fraction of a presence that extends through every level of scale, from the stilt-walkers that stand three meters tall to the bacteria that measured in microns, and all of it is the plateau, and all of it is alive, and all of it persists.

This is who lives in the drowned plateau. Not a single creature, not a community of creatures, but a single continuous presence that has expressed itself in countless forms, each one adapted to the conditions of its particular niche, each one connected to the others by the simple fact of sharing a place. They do not know they are related. They do not need to know. They simply continue, each in its own way, each at its own pace, each contributing to the whole that none of them will ever see.

The plateau holds them all, and the water holds the plateau, and the light falls through, and the darkness accumulates, and the cycle continues, patient and complete, sufficient to itself, older than memory and younger than the earth it rests upon.
