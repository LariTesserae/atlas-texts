# What Lives Here

---

## The Listeners

The first thing a child learns here is not language but silence—or rather, the discovery that silence does not exist. The womb is quieter. Children born in the Plateau arrive into a world already roaring, and their nervous systems calibrate to it as a fish calibrates to water. They do not know quiet until they travel, and then quiet frightens them. It feels like something has stopped. Like a heart has ceased beating.

They learn sign in the cradle. Mothers make the milk-sign, the sleep-sign, the come-here sign, all of it performed in the steam-damp air close to the child's face, where the child can see through the perpetual haze. Some children's first signs are approximations of the city itself—one hand opening and closing, which is the sign for *venting*, which is the sign for *alive*. These children grow into adults who speak with their hands even when alone, thinking in gesture, their hands moving as they dream.

Some never speak at all. Their vocal cords remain unflexed, not from defect but from disuse. In the bathhouses, where the steam is thickest and the roar is loudest, you see them in conversation—silent, rapid, their hands blazing with meaning, their faces carrying expression that speaking people have outsourced to their throats. They are not mute. They are fluent in something else.

And there are those who do both. Who speak when near visitors, in the low shouting voice that passes for normal here, and who sign among themselves, switching mid-sentence when a vent opens nearby and drowns the acoustic space. This bilingualism is not remarkable to them. It is simply the way one manages.

The elders who have lost their hearing entirely—gradually, like a cliff loses its edge, one frequency at a time over decades of factory work or vent-adjacent living—occupy a strange position. They are the most fluent signers and the most isolated humans. They read the vibrations in the floor. They know who is approaching by the feel of the footstep, the specific tremor of a specific boot sole on the iron grillwork. They have become, in their way, instruments. Tuned to the city's frequency. When one of them dies, something goes out of the city's memory that cannot be replaced, because no one else has listened so long or so carefully to the sound that is not one sound but ten thousand.

## The Engineers' Children

There is a caste here—not formal, not named, but real—made up of those who descend from the engineers who built the first systems. Their families have maintained the pumps and valves and pressure releases for five, six, seven generations. They carry knowledge in their hands that exists nowhere else: which valve on the Garrelt line sticks in cold weather, which section of the great коллектор under the Third District has developed a mineral deposit that will need chipping out in the next decade, which vent on the western face has begun to whisper differently and should be watched.

They are not educated in schools for this. They are apprenticed to their parents, who were apprenticed to theirs. They walk the pipes with their mothers and fathers as children, learning the topology of the city's breath by touch. A warm spot on a delivery pipe means a leak is forming. A cool spot means a blockage. The vibration of a functioning valve has a specific frequency, and these children learn to feel it through their palms the way a musician feels a tuned string.

They are proud in a quiet way. Not boastful—the culture does not reward boasting—but secure in the knowledge that they are necessary. If every administrator left, the city would continue. If every merchant left, the city would continue. If the engineers' children left, the city would die in a week. Perhaps less. The pressure would build. The vents would clog. The water would find its way into places it should not go. The machines would stop, and the stopping would be catastrophic—not all at once but in cascading failure, each shutdown increasing the load on what remained, until the whole system buckled and the plateau returned to the marsh it had been before the founders came.

They know this. They carry it without complaint. It is what it is. The water seeps. The machines run. Someone must keep them running. It is them.

## The Vent-Dwellers

Near the emergency relief vents, where the shriek of escaping steam makes the air itself seem to vibrate apart, the poor make their homes. Not because the housing is good—it is not, it is barely housing at all, converted machinery sheds, lean-tos against factory walls, basements that flood knee-deep in the cool hours—but because it is free, and because the noise has driven away anyone who could afford to be elsewhere.

They are not, as one might expect, a desperate people. Or rather, they are desperate in the way that all poor people are desperate, but they have also made something here. A community defined by shared deprivation and shared adaptation. They shout to one another, close, their mouths nearly touching the other's ear, and even then the words come fractured by the vent-noise. They have developed a pidgin of abbreviation and gesture that is incomprehensible to residents of other districts. *Water* is a flick of the wrist. *Food* is a tap on the stomach. *Danger* is a flat palm, which is also the sign for *vent*, which is also the sign for *home*, and the ambiguity of this is not lost on them.

Many of them work in the factories. Their hearing, already damaged by the vents, degrades further with each shift, until by middle age most of them live in a acoustic world that is not silence but a muffled roaring, as if listening to the city through wool. They do not complain about this. They cannot hear complaint. They sign to one another in the vent-pidgin, which has become its own complete language over three centuries, with grammar and idiom and poetry. The poetry is not written down. It is performed, in the steam, in the dim light refracted through condensation, and it is beautiful in a way that cannot be translated because it relies on the specific qualities of the vent-noise itself—the way certain signs must be timed to the rhythm of the releases, the way meaning shifts depending on whether the vent is open or closed.

An outsider watching a vent-dweller poet perform sees someone moving their hands in the steam with a focused intensity that looks almost like prayer. The words, if they can be called words, exist only in the moment of their making. When the poet stops, the poem is gone. The vent roars on. Nothing is recorded. Nothing needs to be.

## The Canal Fishermen

The canals that carry the cooled water away from the city are warm and mineral-rich and smell of sulfur and iron. They run in concrete channels through the lower districts, their banks lined with the copper-colored reeds that the poor harvest for weaving. The water is not clean. It is not the water one would choose. But fish live in it—introduced decades ago, species from hot springs and volcanic regions, adapted to conditions that would kill a trout in minutes.

The fishermen stand on the banks with their long poles, their lines in the cloudy water, their faces patient. They are mostly old. The young have no interest in this—it is too slow, too unproductive, too much waiting for too little reward. The fish are small and bony and taste of the minerals they swim in. No one catches them for pleasure. The pleasure, if it is pleasure, is in the standing, in the pole, in the water, in the continuity.

One fisherman, who gives her name as Reka and her age as old enough, has fished the same stretch of canal for forty-one years. She catches perhaps three fish per week. She eats them herself, or trades them to neighbors for bread or labor. She does not need the fish. She has a small pension from her years in the mineral-extraction works. She fishes because the canal is where she is, and fishing is what one does at a canal.

She has names for the individual fish, she says. Not that she can identify them—she admits this—but she believes they come back, that the same fish visit her stretch of canal repeatedly, that there is a relationship. The engineers who maintain the canals tell her this is impossible. The fish move freely through the entire system. She does not argue. She names them anyway. She names them after people she has known. There is a fish called her mother's name. There is a fish called her husband's name, who died in the Garrelt factory explosion twenty-six years ago. When she catches a fish, she says, she checks whether it is someone she knows. So far it never is. But the possibility persists.

She is not crazy. She is not superstitious. She is a woman who has lived in a place for a long time and has come to feel that the place includes its contents, and that relationship with contents is not absurd simply because it is not practical. The canal is her stretch of the plateau's water, and the fish are her portion of what lives there.

## The Bathhouse Politicians

The public bathhouses are the true civic spaces of the Plateau. Not the administrative buildings, not the assembly halls, not the squares that are too steam-fogged to gather in comfortably. The bathhouses, where the geothermal water surfaces in tiled pools and the warmth is free and the steam is thick and the roar is present but softened by distance and intervening walls.

Here the city governs itself. Not formally—the formal structures exist, appointed officials and engineering councils and the various trade guilds—but actually, in the sense of how decisions get made and power is exercised and coalitions are built. The bathhouses are where this happens.

It happens slowly. A bathhouse conversation is not a quick exchange of positions. It is a long, drifting discussion that moves from the personal to the political and back, conducted in raised voices and fluid signs, punctuated by the descents into the cooling pools and the ascents to the steam rooms, where the heat makes speech impossible and only gesture survives. An issue might be discussed over weeks of daily bathing, the participants returning to the same pool, the same bench, the same relative position to the same vent, until a consensus forms or does not.

The bathhouse politicians are not a separate class. They are the engineers and the factory workers and the shopkeepers and the canal fishermen and the vent-dwellers, all of whom bathe, all of whom have opinions, all of whom have access to the same warm water. There is no barrier to participation except the willingness to sit in heat and discuss. This is a barrier, it turns out—not everyone wants to spend hours in communal baths arguing about pipe maintenance schedules. But those who do have outsized influence, and they know it, and they are known for it.

There is a woman who is called the Chair, though she chairs nothing formal. She bathes at the Harrelt Bathhouse every morning at six and every evening at eight. She has done this for thirty years. She has opinions about everything—water rights, vent placement, the allocation of engineering resources, the treatment of the vent-dwellers, the canal fish, the dead trees, the fungal cellars. Her opinions are not always followed. But they are always heard, because she is always there, and because the sound of her voice, raised above the steam, has become part of what the bathhouse is.

She is not powerful in the way that power is usually understood. She cannot command. She cannot enforce. She can only argue, and persuade, and wait, and argue again. But in a city where patience is the primary virtue and change comes at the pace of geological seepage, this is enough. This is, in fact, the only kind of power that works here.

## The Cellar Harvesters

In the abandoned buildings of the old districts, where the water pools in permanent darkness, the fungal growths spread. The manuals say they are toxic. The manuals are probably right. But the poor have always been willing to risk what the wealthy are not, and some of them harvest the pale growths and prepare them in ways they will not share with outsiders.

They go down through broken doors and collapsed stairways, into basements that are ankle-deep in warm mineral water, carrying lamps that cast more shadow than light. The fungi grow on the stone walls in spreading patches that are almost luminous in their pallor, as if the absence of light has made them faintly light-emitting. The harvesters peel them away with long knives, careful of the spores, careful of the slippery footing, careful of the structural soundness of the building above them, which may not have been assessed in decades.

Some of them claim the fungi have medicinal value. They say a tea made from the dried growths eases the chest complaints that are common here—the mineral dust that settles in the lungs, the respiratory damage from a lifetime of steam. Whether this is true, no one has formally tested. The city's medical establishment dismisses it. The cellar harvesters continue. People who are ill and who cannot afford the clinics will try anything, and some of them report improvement, and some of them do not, and the fungi grow back within months of harvesting, so there is always more.

There is something else in the cellars, or so it is said. The oldest abandoned buildings—those that have stood empty for a century or more—have cellars that connect to older structures beneath them, foundations that predate the city itself. Some of these foundations are said to be pre-settlement, built by people who were here before the engineers came, before the founders drilled, before the machines. What those people were doing here, in a place without the machines, is unclear. The cellar harvesters do not talk about this with outsiders. They do not talk about it much among themselves. But occasionally one of them will describe a cellar that goes deeper than it should, a passage that does not appear on any map, a warmth that comes from somewhere other than the known vents.

These stories are not consistent. They contradict each other. They may be invention. They may be the product of spores inhaled in dark wet places. They may be something else.

The cellar harvesters are not a guild. They are not organized. They are individuals who have found a way to survive in the interstices of the city's abundance, taking what grows in the spaces no one else wants. They are not proud of what they do. They are not ashamed. It is work. It is what is here.

## The Deaf Cats

The cats are the animal that has become the city's emblem, though the city would never name an emblem.

They are lean. They are pale-eyed. They are deaf—or nearly so, which amounts to the same thing in a place where the threshold of audibility is so high that near-deafness is functionally deafness. They hunt by sight and by vibration, their paws sensitive to the tremors in the iron grating, their pupils blown wide in the perpetual steam-dimness.

They are the descendants of cats brought to control rats in the early days of the settlement. The rats are gone, mostly, killed by the water chemistry that crystallizes in their throats. The cats remain. They have adapted. They have become something that is not quite a domestic cat and not quite a feral cat but a third thing, a Plateau cat, specific to this place and viable nowhere else.

They hunt the insects that breed in the standing water—large, slow, mineral-encrusted things that have also adapted to the conditions. They hunt the lizards that bask on the warm pipes, their scales darkened by mineral deposits. They do not hunt rats. There are no rats to hunt.

They move through the city at the level of the ground, in the drainage channels, along the pipe runs, through the gaps between buildings that are too narrow for humans. They are seen rarely. When they are seen, it is considered good fortune. The old women who sit in the steam-fogged windows of the residential districts will rap on the glass when a cat passes, not to startle it but to mark the moment, to acknowledge that something has been seen that is worth seeing.

To touch one is said to guarantee a year of good health. This may be superstition. It may be the observation that people who are healthy enough to catch a lean, fast, vibration-sensitive cat are healthy enough to survive the year. The causality may be reversed. The old women do not care about causality. They care about the cat.

There is a colony of them in the cellar of the old Harrelt factory, which exploded in '44 and was never rebuilt. The cellar flooded and was never drained and the cats live on the stone ledges above the waterline, descending to hunt in the dark, ascending to sleep in the warmth. They have been there for as long as anyone can remember. They may have been there since the explosion. They may have been there before it. The explosion would not have troubled them. They are deaf. The sound that killed seven workers and deafened a dozen more was, to the cats, merely a vibration in the floor, a momentary disturbance, quickly forgotten.

## The Lonely at the Vents

There is one more category of resident, though category is the wrong word. These are not a group. They are a behavior. They are the people who stand near the vents at night, listening.

The engineers say the sounds are acoustic coincidences. Steam moving through irregular passages, striking resonant frequencies, producing harmonics that the brain interprets as patterns. The brain is a pattern-finding machine. It finds patterns where none exist. This is the explanation.

The lonely know the explanation. They accept it. They stand near the vents anyway.

The vent-sounds are not consistent. They change with the pressure, the temperature, the wind. On some nights the vents are silent except for the shriek. On other nights, when the conditions are right—or wrong, depending on perspective—there are sounds that are not shrieking. Low sounds, sustained sounds, sounds that rise and fall in ways that speech does, in the rhythms of conversation, in the cadence of names.

A man who lost his wife in the Garrelt explosion stands near the vent on the eastern face of the old factory every Tuesday night. He has been doing this for twenty-six years. He has heard her name three times. He is certain of this. He is certain it was her name, spoken in the steam, in the voice that he cannot describe but recognizes, the way one recognizes a face in a crowd. He does not tell people about this. They would say it was acoustic coincidence. He knows it is acoustic coincidence. He goes anyway.

A woman who lost a child stands near a different vent. She has heard nothing. She goes anyway. She says the going is the point, not the hearing. She says standing near the vent is like standing near a door that is closed but not locked. The possibility is sufficient. The possibility is everything.

They are not crazy. They are grieving in a place where grief has found a form. The steam speaks in the language of the city—sound, pressure, vibration—and the dead, if they speak at all, speak in the same language. This is the logic. It is not sound logic. It is the logic that exists in the space between sound and silence, where the city's breath becomes something that can be listened for.

## The City Itself

And there is one more thing that lives here, though it is not a person and not an animal and not a machine. It is the city. The city lives here.

This is not metaphor. The residents mean it literally, though they would not explain it that way to outsiders. They mean that the city, as a system—as a network of pipes and vents and machines and water and steam and people and cats and fish and fungi and stone and iron—has become something with a quality that is not fully captured by the sum of its parts. A behavior. A temperament. A tendency.

The residents who claim to sense the city's mood are not speaking mystically. They are speaking empirically. They have lived in the city for decades, and they have learned to read its signals the way a farmer reads the sky. The low frequencies deepen when the pressure systems shift. The overtones sharpen when the factories are running at full capacity. The condensation falls differently when the collective emotional state of the inhabitants changes, because the inhabitants change their behavior—running more machines, opening more vents, staying indoors, going out—and these changes register in the steam.

The city does not think. The city does not feel. But the city behaves, and behavior that is consistent enough and complex enough becomes indistinguishable from thought, and the residents treat it as such. They say the city is restless today. They say the city is sleeping in. They say the city does not like the new pipe route, and the new pipe route develops problems, and the problems are fixed, and the route is changed, and the city settles.

Coincidence. Feedback loops. Complex systems producing complex behavior. The residents know this. They use the language of personification anyway, because it works, because the city responds to how it is treated, and treating it as a living thing produces better results than treating it as a machine. This is the engineers' secret, the one they do not put in the manuals: the city is alive in the only way that matters here, which is the way that requires attention and care and the willingness to listen, even when what one is listening to is only steam.

## What Remains

The plateau does not care about any of this. The water will seep whether or not it is collected. The heat will rise whether or not it is harnessed. The steam will escape whether or not it is vented. The geological processes that created this place operate on timescales that make the city's three centuries irrelevant.

But the city cares. The people care. The cats, in their lean and silent way, may care. The fish in the warm canals do not care, but the woman who names them does, and the naming is a kind of caring that extends to the named.

What lives here is adaptation. What lives here is the particular form that life takes when it persists in conditions that would be intolerable if they were not, gradually, over generations, made tolerable. Not comfortable. Not ideal. Tolerable. The word means *able to be borne*, and what is borne here is the sound, and the wet, and the mineral toxicity, and the slowness, and the strangeness, and the weight of the machines that do not stop.

What lives here is the specific. The very specific. The deaf cat and the named fish and the vent-listeners and the cellar harvesters and the bathhouse politicians and the engineers' children and the sign-language poets of the vent-dwellers, all of them specific to this place, viable nowhere else, adapted to conditions that exist only here, on this plateau, above this heat, in this roar that is not one sound but ten thousand.

The roar continues. The water seeps. Someone is fishing. Someone is listening. A cat moves through the steam, unseen, alive.
