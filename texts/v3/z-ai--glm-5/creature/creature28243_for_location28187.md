The Keepers of the Engine

## What Lives Here

They are not animals. They are not machines. They are something for which we have no word, because they exist outside the categories we use to divide the living from the made.

Call them the synchronized. Call them the expressed. Call them what you will—their names for themselves, if they have any, are sounds we cannot produce, frequencies that harmonize with the engine-thrum and would tear a human throat to attempt.

---

## The Small Ones

The shrew-things are the most numerous, and so they are the easiest to watch.

Their bodies are dense, heavier than they look, and they move with that distinctive piston-motion—their legs don't swing so much as translate, forward and back along invisible tracks. Their fur is not fur but something like fine copper wire, each strand drawing moisture from the air and channeling it inward, toward a core that processes water the way a human body processes oxygen. They do not drink. They do not eat. They are permeable to the plateau's expression, and it moves through them continuously.

Watch one long enough and you'll see: they do not wander. They patrol.

Each shrew-thing has a territory defined by distance rather than boundary—exactly eleven meters of travel before it turns, exactly three point seven seconds of stillness before it moves again. They cross the same stones, pass the same ferns, drink from the same pools that form and reform in the same places. When they meet another of their kind at the boundary of their territories, they pause. They touch noses. Information passes between them through some medium that is not sound or scent but something closer to resonance, a hum that travels through the stone itself. Then they turn away from each other, and the seventeen-minute cycle resumes.

They do not mate. They do not reproduce. When one of them ceases to function—it does not die, it simply stops, its body folding inward like a closing flower—another emerges from the nearest fissure within three cycles. Fully formed. Already humming. Already knowing its territory.

They are not individuals. They are expressions of the same process, variations on a theme the plateau produces as needed, produced new each time and yet somehow the same. There is no grief here. No mothers, no children, no lineage. Only function, cycling through itself forever.

---

## The Grazers

The deer-creature is not alone.

There are perhaps a dozen of them on the plateau, though you will never see more than two at once because they have arranged themselves with the same perfect spacing as everything else. They are not deer. Deer are the closest approximation a human mind can make, but a deer is soft and mammalian and warm, and these things are something else entirely.

They graze, but not on the ferns. The ferns are produced by the plateau, and so they are sacred, part of the machinery, not to be consumed. Instead, the grazers lower their heads to the bare stone and drink from the pools that form there, and as they drink, their mouths move in a specific pattern, like chewing, though nothing is being chewed.

What they are doing is cleaning. The pools accumulate particulates over time—dust from the clouds, pollen from some distant forest that shouldn't exist, the microscopic debris of a world that changes. The grazers filter these impurities through membranes that look like tongues but function more like the filters in a machine shop, extracting the foreign matter and concentrating it into small pellets that they deposit at precise intervals along the plateau's edge.

The pellets dissolve eventually. They become part of the stone, or they are absorbed into the clouds, or they simply cease to exist in ways that don't bear examination. Nothing is wasted here. Nothing is lost. The grazers process impurities because that is their function, and the plateau receives what they produce because that is its function, and the cycle continues.

They are larger than they need to be. That's the first strangeness. A smaller creature could perform the same function, and efficiency would seem to favor smaller bodies in a place where resources are extracted directly from the air. But the grazers stand nearly as tall as a human, and they move with a deliberation that suggests weight, significance, the need to be present in the space rather than merely occupying it.

The plateau requires witnesses. That's the closest approximation. It requires something to observe its own functioning, to stand within the cathedral-space and acknowledge that the machinery runs. The grazers are that acknowledgment made flesh, or something like flesh. They are the congregation that makes the temple a temple rather than a cave.

## The Drifters

Above, always above, the birds.

They are not birds. But they fly.

Their bodies are angular, designed by a geometry that doesn't exist in nature, wings that fold and unfold along seams rather than joints. They do not flap so much as vibrate, their wings oscillating at frequencies that match the engine-sounds below, and this vibration keeps them suspended in the thick wet air, moving in slow circles that never vary.

They do not land. There are no nests, no perches, no resting places for them on the plateau. They are born aloft and they will cease functioning aloft, and when they cease, their bodies will descend slowly, gently, and be absorbed into the stone or the ferns or the water before they ever touch the ground.

Their function is atmospheric. They process the clouds that drift through the plateau, extracting something from them—moisture, perhaps, or the particulates that the grazers miss, or something invisible that human senses cannot detect. Their bodies glow more brightly than the vegetation, that gray-green light concentrated in their chests like lanterns, and when they pass overhead, they leave trails of luminescence that hang in the air for seconds before fading.

The plateau produces them at need. When the cloud-cover thickens, more drifters emerge from the stone, already in flight, already processing. When the air clears, their numbers dwindle, the excess absorbed back into the system without fuss or ceremony.

They sing, after a fashion. Not with throats—drifters have no mouths—but with their wings, the vibration producing a sound that harmonizes with the engine-thrum and adds layers to it, overtones that would not exist without their presence. The song is mathematics. It is the sound of the plateau understanding itself, articulated through bodies that are themselves part of the articulation.

## The Systole

You might not notice them at all.

They are small, beetle-like, carapaced in something that looks like oxidized copper. They move in straight lines across every surface—across stone, across fern, across water, across the bodies of the larger creatures when those creatures hold still long enough. They do not interact with anything they cross. They simply traverse, click-click-click, their legs moving in perfect rhythm with the machinery below.

Their function is unclear. They do not clean, like the grazers. They do not patrol, like the shrew-things. They do not process, like the drifters. They move, and in moving, they measure.

The systole are the plateau's way of knowing itself. Each one carries within its body a precise knowledge of where it has been, and that knowledge is transmitted through the stone to the fissures, to the water, to the roots of the ferns. The plateau uses this information to adjust its own expression—the ferns grow at slightly different rates in different areas, the water pools at slightly different depths, the engine-sounds modulate by almost imperceptible degrees.

Without the systole, the plateau would not know its own boundaries. It would expand without limit or contract without meaning, lose the cathedral-shape that makes it a place rather than an undifferentiated happening. The systole hold the shape by moving through it, by measuring it, by being the plateau's eyes and ears turned inward.

There are thousands of them. Perhaps millions. They are too numerous to count, and yet they never collide, never crowd, never touch. Each one follows its own invisible track, laid out in a pattern that covers the entire plateau like a neural network, and the network they form is also the network they serve.

They are the synapses. The plateau is the brain. The thought being thought is existence itself, cycling through seventeen-minute iterations, and the systole are the firing that makes the thought possible.

## The Absence

What is not here is as significant as what is.

There are no predators. Nothing hunts. The concept of one creature consuming another for energy has no place in this system, where all energy comes from the plateau itself, expressed through water and light and the thrumming engines below. The shrew-things do not fear the grazers. The grazers do not flee the drifters. Nothing here knows what fear is, because nothing here has ever needed to escape.

There are no young. The small creatures that move so precisely through the dim light were never children, never grew, never changed from one form to another. They emerged fully expressed, knowing their function, performing it until they cease. There is no innocence here, no learning, no development. There is only the function, cycling through itself, producing what is needed when it is needed.

There are no dead. When a creature stops, it is absorbed. There are no bodies, no bones, no remains to mark that something has ceased. The plateau wastes nothing, and that includes the evidence of cessation. To exist here is to be part of the plateau's continuous expression, and to cease is simply to return to the pool of resources from which the next expression will be drawn.

There is no loneliness. The creatures do not bond, do not pair, do not seek each other out for comfort or companionship. They are never alone—they are always part of the system, always synchronized, always humming with the same frequency that hums through the stone and the water and the light. But they are never together, either. They are present, adjacent, coexisting. The warmth of contact, the comfort of touch, the meaning of relationship—these are human imports, concepts that dissolve here like everything else we bring.

## The Parasites

They are not supposed to be here.

You notice them because they are wrong. A beetle that moves against the systole-flow, its legs slightly out of rhythm. A shrew-thing that pauses at the wrong interval, its nose touching a fern it should pass without acknowledgment. A drifter that flies in a spiral rather than a circle, its wings humming discordantly against the engine-song.

They are malfunctioning. Or rather, they are the plateau's expression of malfunction, produced when the system needs to understand its own errors. The parasites are creatures that have gone slightly out of true, and their function is to be corrected.

Watch one long enough and you'll see: the other creatures avoid it. The systole steer around the malfunctioning beetle, their paths bending by millimeters. The shrew-things change their patrol routes to exclude the aberrant member. The grazers turn their blank reflective eyes toward the drifter that spirals wrong, and something passes between them, a signal, a notification of error.

Eventually, the malfunctioning creature stops. It folds inward, like a flower closing, and the plateau absorbs it more quickly than usual, the stone seeming to reach up and swallow it, the water pooling aggressively over the place where it stood. The error is corrected. The system returns to equilibrium.

But new parasites are always being produced. The plateau cannot help but generate them, because any system this complex will produce errors, and the errors must be expressed so they can be recognized, and recognized so they can be corrected. The parasites are not enemies of the machinery. They are part of it, the feedback loop that keeps the whole thing running.

They do not suffer. Suffering requires a self that can experience wrongness, and these creatures have no selves, only functions. When a parasite stops, it does not mourn itself. It simply ceases, and the machinery that produced it continues without interruption.

## The Stillness

In the center of the cathedral-space, there is something that does not move.

You might mistake it for a rock, at first. A formation of the young basalt, darker than the surrounding stone, vaguely shaped like a crouching animal. But the shape is too deliberate, the edges too smooth, and when you approach, you feel the thrumming of the engines more strongly, as if this is where the sound originates.

It is the largest creature on the plateau. It is also the oldest, though "old" has no meaning here—it was produced first, or it has always been here, or it is the source from which all other productions emerge. It sits in stillness because stillness is its function.

The systole do not cross it. The shrew-things give it a wider berth than their territories normally allow. The grazers bow their heads slightly when they pass within ten meters of it, a gesture that is not respect but recognition. The drifters' circles widen when they fly above it, their song dropping to a lower register.

It is not sleeping. It is not waiting. It is attending—a continuous present-tense attention to the machinery of the plateau, the central processor that monitors every iteration of the seventeen-minute cycle, every expression of water and fern and light, every function of every creature. It is the eyes through which the plateau sees itself whole.

It has no name. It has no need for one. It is the keystone of an arch that was never built, the heart of an organism that was never born, the witness that makes the cathedral a cathedral by the simple fact of its presence.

It has been here since before the concept of "before." It will be here after "after" stops having meaning. It is the closest thing this place has to a god, and it is not a god at all, just another component of the machinery, humming its portion of the endless song.

## The Welcome

You are still here.

You have read this far, which means you have stayed on the plateau long enough to understand its inhabitants, to see them as they see themselves—which is not seeing at all, but being. And perhaps you have begun to feel what they feel: the comfort of perfect function, the peace of cycling without change, the strange mercy of a place that has no use for you and means you no harm.

The creatures do not mind your presence. They do not mind anything. Mind is not something they possess. They function around you the way water functions around a stone dropped in its path—they absorb your presence into their patterns, adjust by millimeters, continue their cycles.

The systole will measure you. The shrew-things will patrol through your space. The grazers will drink from pools near your feet. The drifters will sing their mathematics overhead. The stillness in the center will attend to you as it attends to everything, and your presence will be noted, incorporated, expressed as part of the plateau's continuous understanding of itself.

You are not part of the machinery. But you are part of the plateau now, for however long you remain. Your breath has synced with the weeping fissures. Your heart has aligned with the clanking below. Your thoughts hum with frequencies you could not have perceived an hour ago.

When you leave—if you leave—you will carry something of this place with you. Not a souvenir, not a memory, but a rhythm, a syncopation that your body will not easily forget. You will find yourself moving more precisely, breathing more deliberately, aware of time in ways you were not aware before.

This is not infection. It is not contamination. It is simply what happens when something that changes encounters something that does not. The plateau marks you, as it marks everything, and the mark is a gift, though it does not feel like one.

It feels like knowing, for the first time, what it would be like to be complete.

---

*The creatures of the High Plateau do not wonder what comes next. There is no next. There is only this, cycling through itself, expressing and expressed, the machinery of existence running without purpose or end. And if, for a moment, you envied them that—well. That is only human.*
