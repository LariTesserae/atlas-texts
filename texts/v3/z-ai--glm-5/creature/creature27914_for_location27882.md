# What Lives Here

## The Settlers

Begin with the hands.

A substrate-wright's hands look wrong at first glance—too long in the fingers, the pads too broad, the skin between the joints grown thick and calloused into something closer to bark than flesh. This is adaptation across deep time. Thousands of generations of handling living tissue, of pressing nerve-reads into organic terminals, of coaxing growth through touch and vibration, have reshaped them. The nails are gone entirely. What remains is a kind of living instrument, ten articulated probes that can feel the difference between a healthy hyphal junction and a failing one the way a violinist feels the difference between a true note and a sour one.

The wright is old. Her name is something that does not translate well—sounds produced in the back of the throat, resonant with the Vellum's lower harmonics, a name she shares with a particular growth-pattern her lineage discovered four hundred years ago. She is singing now, a low pulsing tone that barely qualifies as song to an untrained ear. But the wall is responding. A new chamber, beginning its spiral, the cells beneath her palm dividing and differentiating, the architecture emerging from intention. She has been at this for three days. The wall is seventeen centimeters high. By next month it will be a room.

Her hands know things her mind has forgotten. She can feel the Vellum's mood through its surface vibrations, can tell when a deep process is shifting, when the substrate is restless or calm. She remembers when the chord changed, forty years ago—a new harmonic appeared in the dawn register, a high keening note that the settlements had never heard before. Some wrights claimed they could feel it in the tissue before it became audible. She believes them. Her own teacher, a woman whose hands were even stranger, once described the Vellum as feeling like anticipation.

The wright has children. All of them have the hands. The youngest, barely ten years old, is already learning the basic growth-songs, and her fingers are beginning to show the signs—slight thickening at the pads, the first hint of bark-texture at the joints. This is not genetic modification. This is Lamarckian inheritance written across deep time, the body remembering what the body needs.

---

The settlement's residents are not numerous. Call it two thousand, perhaps twenty-five hundred—the largest concentration of human life on the plateau, which means the largest concentration of human life anywhere, because the plateau is everywhere. They are lean. The thin air and the low-calorie extract and the constant subtle vibration of the chord beneath their feet conspire to keep them lean, their bodies compressed and efficient, their metabolism tuned to the available energy. They are not unhealthy. They are adapted. A visitor from a water-world would see them as undernourished; a visitor from a water-world would be wrong.

Their skin is dark—not from sunlight, which is dim and feeble, but from the mineral content of the extract they drink. Generations of hydrocarbon fluid, rich in compounds that have no analog in water-based biology, have stained their skin from within. The color ranges across the settlement: some are deep amber, some almost black, some a strange olive that seems to shift in the bioluminescent light. Children are born pale and darken with age.

Their eyes are large. This is perhaps the most obvious adaptation. The dim sun, the low light, the bioluminescent evenings—their pupils have expanded over generations, their irises contracted, until their eyes look almost like the Vellum's own collecting structures, maximally open, maximally receptive. In darkness they see as well as the flying-things do, by the faintest bioluminescent glow, by starlight alone.

They do not smile the way water-world humans smile. The facial muscles have shifted over generations—the expression they use for greeting involves the eyes and the brow, a raising, an opening, that looks to an outsider like surprise. This is warmth. This is welcome.

---

The extract they drink is the center of their physical lives.

Drawn from deep reservoirs in the Vellum, it is warm when it emerges—body temperature, almost, though no body made it. It has the consistency of light oil, the color of dark amber, and a smell that the residents cannot describe because they have no basis for comparison. It smells like the Vellum's interior, like the substrate's own body chemistry, like the mineral-sweet depths where the real processes run. An Earth nose would find it overwhelming, chemical, strange.

They drink it from vessels grown for the purpose—organic cups sealed with a membrane at the lip that prevents spillage, that can be pressed open against the mouth. The vessels are personal. A resident carries one from childhood, grows into it, grows it into shape. They are never washed because they do not need to be; their inner surfaces are alive, self-cleaning, maintaining a symbiotic bacterial culture that breaks down residue.

The extract provides hydration—though hydration means something different here—and calories, and trace compounds their biology has come to require. They eat other things: Vellum tissue, processed and prepared, with a texture like dense mushroom and a taste like the smell of the extract concentrated and intensified. They eat the runners, occasionally, when a herd can be hunted. The runners' meat is tough and mineral-dense, an acquired taste. They eat the small surface-organisms, the beetle-things, the spider-things, harvested in quantities and processed into a paste that is spread on Vellum-fiber bread.

There is no water. They have forgotten water. Their bodies run on hydrocarbon fluids that circulate in systems parallel to but different from blood—denser, more viscous, carrying different dissolved gases, operating at different pressures. A resident of the Vellum Plains, taken to a water-world, would drown in more ways than one.

---

The substrate-wrights are the most visible specialists, but the settlement supports others.

The drumming-staff: three men and four women who spend their days with their ears pressed to the ground. They track the runners by the vibrations their many feet transmit through the Vellum. This is not a job for the impatient. A skilled drumming-listener can identify a herd's size, speed, direction, and likely grazing path from a single pulse of vibration. They report to the settlement council, and the council decides whether to warn the outlying structures, whether to harvest runners, whether to prepare the perimeter. The runners rarely threaten the settlements, but their grazing can damage outlying growth-chambers, and a herd passing too close to a young building can strip its outer tissue before the growth-songs can harden it.

The song-keepers: the oldest residents, those who hold the growth-songs in memory. The songs are not recorded in any medium—there are no books, no data-stores except the Vellum itself, and the Vellum cannot easily play back sound. The songs are kept in human memory, passed from keeper to keeper in long sessions of call-and-response that can last for days. Each lineage holds a particular set of songs; trades between lineages are formal, negotiated, and the exchange of a new growth-song is an event marked by ceremony. The song-keepers are the closest thing the settlement has to a priesthood.

The boundary-tenders: those who monitor the Vellum's immune responses, who mark the zones where the substrate turns aggressive, who maintain the warning spires. This is dangerous work. The Vellum's defensive tissue is hard, fast-growing, and chemically hostile. A boundary-tender who makes a mistake can be engulfed—encysted, absorbed, converted. This has happened. The settlement has stories. The boundary-tenders wear protective layers grown from the Vellum's own defensive variants, a kind of armor made of the same tissue that threatens them. It is an imperfect solution.

The healers: those who work with the chord's beneficial frequencies, positioning patients at resonance points, using vibration to promote healing. They also work with the Vellum's chemical resources, extracting and refining compounds with medicinal properties. Their medicine is entirely organic, grown from the substrate, adapted to bodies that have themselves adapted to the substrate. An Earth-pharmacist would find their pharmacopeia unrecognizable.

And the children. The settlement's children are everywhere, running between the buildings, playing on the Vellum's surface, pressing their ears to the ground to listen for runners. They learn the growth-songs early, though they do not understand them; the songs are absorbed like language, through immersion, through repetition, through the body's need to make sound that the world responds to.

## The Runners

They are not graceful.

This is the first thing to understand. They run, yes—they run with a speed that seems impossible for their size, their many legs blurring into a single wave of motion—but the running is not elegant. It is desperate. It is urgent. It is the motion of an animal that has evolved to move quickly across a surface that will heal itself behind it, that will close the wound it leaves, that will, if the runner is too slow, close the wound while the runner is still in it.

They are the color of old bone. This is camouflage, or it was once—the Vellum's raw tissue, freshly exposed, is pale, and a runner feeding on it is difficult to see from a distance. Their bodies are elongated, low-slung, the legs emerging from along the flanks in a pattern that suggests neither insect nor vertebrate but something else entirely, something that evolved along a branch of the tree of life that Earth biology never explored. The legs bend backward at the first joint, forward at the second, and the foot is a flat pad that grips the Vellum's surface without piercing it.

Their undersides are their mouths. The abrasive ridges that strip away the Vellum's upper tissue are not teeth but bony plates, evolved from the same structural proteins that form their legs, worn smooth by use. They feed by running, by pressing their undersides against the exposed substrate, by scraping away layers with a motion that is part eating, part locomotion. A runner cannot eat while standing still. It must move. The fastest runners are the most well-fed.

They bark and whistle to one another, sounds produced by forcing air through specialized apertures in their thoraxes. The barks are territorial. The whistles are something else—social, perhaps, or navigational, or both. A herd of runners communicates constantly, and the communication is layered, each individual contributing to a collective soundscape that is, in its own way, as complex as the Vellum's chord.

They sleep in dens at the plateau's edge, in hollows where the Vellum thins and the original mineral crust is close to the surface. The dens are scraped out, maintained, passed down through generations. A den can be ancient—some have been in use for longer than the settlements have existed, the same families of runners returning to the same hollows year after year, century after century. The runners do not build. They do not need to. The Vellum provides. The Vellum is everything.

They have no eyes. This is the strangest thing about them, to a human observer. They navigate by sound and by vibration and by chemical sensitivity—they can smell the Vellum's exposed tissue from kilometers away, and they run toward it with a precision that seems to suggest sight but does not require it. Their heads are smooth, featureless, the bone-color of their bodies, and they run with their heads low, almost touching the surface, as if listening.

A runner herd in motion is a terrifying thing if you are standing in its path. The drumming of their feet through the Vellum arrives before they do, a deep rhythmic pulse that shakes the ground, and then the barking, and then the whistling, and then the silver dust of stripped Vellum-cells rising in their wake like a trail. The settlements have protocols. The protocols work.

## The Small Ones

Between the runners and the Vellum, between the Vellum and the air, between the substrate's surface and its depths, there are ten thousand species. A few of them are visible.

The tenders: creatures like elongated beetles, though they are not beetles, that move slowly between the Vellum's collecting structures, pruning dead tissue, clearing debris, harvesting spores. They are the size of a human finger, segmented, with legs that grip the Vellum's surface with small claws. Their bodies are dark—nearly black—and they move in coordinated groups, dozens of individuals following chemical trails laid down by their leaders. They are the Vellum's maintenance crew, or they are the Vellum's immune system, or they are simply another species that has found a niche in the ecosystem and filled it. The distinction may not matter.

The weavers: things like spiders, though they are not spiders, that spin molecular threads between the Vellum's surface structures, reinforcing them against the wind. The threads are incredibly fine—too fine to see individually, but in aggregate they create a shimmering mesh that catches the dim light and glitters. The weavers are small, the size of a grain of sand, and they work in vast numbers, millions of individuals in a square meter, each one spinning a single thread and moving on. The Vellum tolerates them. Perhaps the Vellum requires them.

The burrowers: things like worms, though they are not worms, that live in the Vellum's upper layers, tunneling through the hyphal mat, aerating it, mixing its layers. They are rarely seen—their tunnels collapse behind them, the Vellum healing itself—but their presence is detectable by the slight depressions they leave on the surface, small dimples in the silver sheen that shift and change over hours.

And deeper, in the layers where the light never reaches, there are organisms that exist at scales that blur the boundary between life and chemistry. These are the Vellum's deepest symbionts, organisms that live in the spaces between hyphae, that process the breakdown products of dead tissue, that cycle nutrients back to the surface. They are too small to see. They are too numerous to count. They are, perhaps, the most important living things on the plateau, because without them the Vellum's cycling would cease, the dead would not become the living, and the great sheet would be a graveyard and nothing more.

## The Flying Things

At night, they emerge.

They do not come from the Vellum. They come from the air itself—or so it seems, materializing from the darkness above the plateau, membranes spread wide, riding the thin air currents with a buoyancy that suggests they are lighter than the atmosphere around them. They are not. They are simply very large and very light, their bodies mostly gas-filled sacs that provide lift, their membranes stretched between structural ribs that are thinner than paper.

They click and sing to navigate by sound. The clicking is rapid, ultrasonic, and the singing is lower, almost musical, a series of tones that overlap and interfere in ways that create a kind of acoustic image of the landscape below. They are, in effect, flying sonar systems, and they hunt the night-active organisms that emerge from the Vellum's surface when the collectors close.

They are not dangerous to the settlements. They are too large to enter the buildings and too fragile to force their way through barriers. But they are startling, when they appear—suddenly, silently, gliding between the spires of a settlement on membranes that span several meters, their bodies trailing behind them like deflated balloons. They glow, faintly, bioluminescent patches on their undersides that pulse in patterns the settlements have never been able to decode. Perhaps they are communicating. Perhaps they are simply breathing.

In the darkest hours, when the Vellum's chord has faded to its lowest murmur, the flying things' songs fill the silence. It is a different kind of music—higher, more variable, more individual—and the residents who are awake at those hours say it is the most beautiful sound the plateau produces. Others say it is lonely. Both are correct.

## The Vellum Itself

Does it live? The question is too simple. The Vellum is alive the way a forest is alive, the way a coral reef is alive—but it is also alive the way a single organism is alive, with integrated systems, with coordinated responses, with a metabolism that functions as a whole. The distinction between ecosystem and organism breaks down at this scale. The Vellum is both. The Vellum is neither. The Vellum is something that Earth biology has no category for.

It thinks. Or it does something that, if observed from a sufficient distance, is indistinguishable from thinking. Information moves through its hyphal networks as chemical pulses, as electrical gradients, as quantum effects in specialized organelles that process data at rates the settlements can measure but not comprehend. The Vellum stores information. It responds to stimuli. It adapts. It predicts.

The chord is the most obvious expression of its processing. Each collecting structure vibrates at a frequency determined by its structure, its angle, its exposure to light—and the sum of these vibrations, across millions of square kilometers, creates a sound that is not random but structured, that changes in response to internal and external conditions, that contains information if you know how to extract it. The settlements have been trying to extract it for generations. They have learned to read the chord's tonal shifts for practical purposes—time, weather, runner-approach—but the deeper patterns, if they exist, remain opaque.

The researchers who believe the Vellum is waking up point to the irregular zones. There, the chord produces harmonics that the settlements cannot predict. There, the growth-songs fail to take hold, as if the Vellum is refusing their input. There, the substrate's behavior suggests something new is happening—new, after billions of years of stability, after an existence so long that change itself has become foreign.

If the Vellum is waking up, what is it waking from? A sleep so deep it has lasted longer than the history of life on Earth? A dormancy that was itself a kind of consciousness, slower than human thought, operating on geological timescales? Is the chord its dream, or its voice, or its alarm clock?

No one knows. The question is the kind that cannot be answered by the entities asking it, because the answer exists at a scale beyond their comprehension. The Vellum may be a mind. The Vellum may be a million minds. The Vellum may be nothing of the kind, and the settlements may be reading significance into patterns that are simply patterns, the way humans always have.

But the chord is beautiful. And it changes. And the Vellum is vast. And the ground beneath your feet is alive. And if you listen, late at night, when the flying-things sing and the runners drum in the distance and the Vellum groans in its thermal contraction, you can almost hear something that sounds like thought.

## What Lives Here, Ultimately

People live here. Strange, adapted people with bark-textured hands and oversized eyes and a biology that would fail on any other world, people who drink hydrocarbon fluid and wear living coats and sing to the ground to make their homes. They have lived here long enough to forget that they ever lived anywhere else. Their stories tell of arrival, but the stories have become myths, and the myths have become irrelevant, because what matters is the next day's work, the next growth-song, the next runner-herd, the next dawn-chord rising through the silvered surface.

Animals live here. The runners, desperate and fast and blind, feeding on the move. The flying-things, enormous and fragile, singing in the dark. The small ones, the tenders and weavers and burrowers, too many to count, too small to see, maintaining the vast living sheet that maintains them.

And the Vellum lives here. The Vellum lives here the way a mountain lives where a mountain is, the way an ocean lives where an ocean is. It is not an inhabitant of the plateau. It is the plateau. It is the ground and the air's chemistry and the sound and the light-processing and the information-flow and the deep slow cycling of death into life into death again. It is the context in which everything else lives. It is the medium, the message, and the meaning.

What lives in the Vellum Plains is the Vellum Plains. Everything else is detail.
