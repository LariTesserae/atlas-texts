# Those Who Dwell in the Weep

The first thing visitors notice about the people of the Weep is their skin. Not the pallor—that's expected, in a place where direct sunlight reaches the ground perhaps two hours each day—but the texture. Living in perpetual moisture does something to a body. The residents' skin has adapted into something almost amphibian: smooth, slightly cool to the touch, possessing a faint natural pliancy that outsiders find unsettling. Their fingertips don't prune in water. They've forgotten that sensation entirely.

The oldest among them—those four or five survivors who remember the flat land, who heard the groaning—have a different quality altogether. Their skin seems to hold moisture differently, as if the water has worked itself deeper over decades. Their wrinkles don't deepen in the damp; they fill, becoming shallow channels on faces that map the settlement's own drainage. When one of the elders dies, they say, the body doesn't dessicate. It continues to weep from within, producing moisture for days, as if something that lived in the skin hasn't yet received the message.

---

Children born in the Weep learn to walk on wet stone before they learn to walk on dry ground. Their first steps happen in the narrow passages where footing shifts with the day's seepage. They develop a distinctive gait—wide-footed, rolling slightly from edge to edge, designed to find purchase on surfaces that would send an outsider sliding. By the time a Weep-born child reaches walking age, their soles have toughened into something water can't soften.

These children play games that visitors find incomprehensible. They stand in the triangular plazras with their eyes closed, feeling for variations in the damp—a game called "tasting the air," though nothing enters their mouths. They compete to identify which channel is running faster today, which passage has grown wetter since morning. The winners are celebrated, pulled aside by the adults, taught to read the water more formally. The Weep has always needed its hydrologists.

Children also learn to read each other's breath. In the hour after dawn, when the cold makes exhalations visible, they trace the shapes their friends produce—steam rising and curling in the still air. They've learned to predict the day's wetness by how quickly the breath dissolves. A slow fade means the seepage will increase. A sharp disappearance means the water is retreating briefly, buying them a few hours of relative dryness. The children don't know why this works. Neither do the adults. But it does.

---

The original founders who remain have become something like vessels. Not leaders—the settlement doesn't have leaders in any formal sense—but carriers of knowledge that doesn't get spoken aloud. They sit in their dwellings with their backs to the walls, their eyes on the floor, and they listen. To the water, yes. But also to something else—something they don't name.

When a founder dies, the community observes a silence that lasts three days. No repairs. No trading. The channels run untended; the mushrooms go unharvested; the passages fill with whoever can fit, standing or sitting, while the body rests on its stone platform in the founder's dwelling. They don't speak during these gatherings. They listen. Trying to hear what the founder heard, perhaps, or trying to determine whether something has been released now that its witness is gone.

One founder—the oldest, a woman named Parrahn who was thirty when the ground sagged and is now somewhere past eighty—has not spoken a word in eleven years. She communicates through gesture and expression, through the placement of stones and the blocking of channels. The others say she's still listening to the sound that made the bowl. They say she's almost heard it. They say she's afraid to hear it completely.

Parrahn's daughter tends her. A woman in her fifties named Deevis, solid and practical, with hands that never stop moving—repairing, clearing, adjusting. Deevis speaks enough for both of them. When someone asks what her mother knows, Deevis says: "She knows that some sounds aren't for telling." Then she returns to her work, and the questioner learns not to ask again.

---

Newcomers arrive with particular kinds of desperation. The Weep's damp air does ease certain breathing afflictions—the continuous moisture keeps mucus thin, keeps passages open, allows those with failing lungs to draw breath with less struggle. Word has spread. People come from the scrubland villages, from the lake settlements, occasionally from as far as the hills three days' walk distant. They arrive coughing, wheezing, dragging themselves down the rim's slope with hope in their eyes.

Most leave within the week. The damp that soothes their lungs also soaks into their clothes, their bedding, their possessions. Their skin prunes, their temperaments sour, their sleep becomes impossible in a place where the sound of water never ceases. They discover that the passages are too narrow, the walls too close, the sky too small a portion of their view. They discover that they cannot learn the water's preferences in a season or a year—and without that knowledge, they'll never belong.

But some stay. Those who stay tend to share a quality that has nothing to do with their illness: they're comfortable being small. Not in size—the Weep has tall residents—but in importance. They're people who've discovered that the world doesn't need them to be large, who've found relief in a place where crowding makes individuality impractical. They come for their lungs, perhaps. But they stay because here, finally, they can stop trying to matter.

The ones who stay undergo a transformation that takes roughly a season. The coughing eases, yes, but more significantly, they begin to blend. Not disappear—they're still present, still visible—but their edges soften. They learn the passages. They learn to step aside at the right moment, to time their movements so they flow through the settlement without interrupting its rhythms. They learn, eventually, to hear the water. Not consciously. Not in a way they could describe. But in a way that lets them wake before their dwelling floods, that lets them know which channel to clear before the blockage becomes visible.

---

The settlement lacks formal structure, but patterns have emerged. Channel-work falls to those with a gift for reading moisture—younger residents, mostly, whose hands remain steady and whose instincts can be trusted. The elders observe, correcting rarely, letting mistakes teach what words cannot. Storage falls to those who can count without losing track in the damp dimness. Cooking falls to those whose sense of taste hasn't dulled from the mushrooms' mineral bite.

Disputes happen but don't last. There's no room for sustained conflict in passages where you brush shoulders with your enemy eight times a day. The closeness forces a kind of truce—not forgiveness, exactly, but a decision to let friction smooth itself through contact. Residents who cannot manage this leave or die. The Weep has no patience for grudges.

The children receive no formal education. They learn by shadowing: following adults through their tasks, imitating until the movements become instinct. A seven-year-old will trail a channel-worker for an entire season, watching how they read the water's speed, how they choose which stone to place where. Then, one day, the adult will hand the child a tool and step back. The child either succeeds or learns from the failure. The adults offer little praise and less criticism. The water teaches; they merely permit it.

---

The Weep's relationship to its water borders on the devotional without crossing into worship. Residents don't pray to the moisture or treat it as divine. But they do treat it as present—as a thing that notices, that prefers, that responds. When they cut a new channel, they apologize to the earth. When they block an old one, they ask permission first. Not aloud. Not with ritual. Just a brief pause, a moment of attention directed downward, before the work proceeds.

Some channels have been abandoned for generations, and no one quite remembers why. When a newcomer asks about reopening one, the answer is always the same: "The water didn't want it." The water's desires are taken seriously, even when they inconvenience the settlement. The residents would rather route around a blocked passage than force moisture through a channel it's rejected. They've seen what happens when someone tries.

Decades ago, before the youngest generation was born, a man named Corthin decided to cut a straight channel through the settlement's eastern section. He'd mapped the water's flow and determined that a direct route would drain faster, would keep the passages drier, would make life easier for everyone. The water, he said, didn't know what it wanted. He would teach it.

Corthin's channel worked for three days. On the fourth morning, residents woke to find their floor-stones shifted, their walls tilting, their doorframes misaligned. The water hadn't just rejected the channel; it had moved around it in the night, finding new paths that undermined the settlement's foundations. The damage took two seasons to repair. Corthin disappeared shortly after—left the Weep, some said, though others noted that no one saw him climb the rim.

His name isn't spoken anymore. But his channel is remembered: a scar in the settlement's geography where water refuses to flow, where the stones remain dry in a way that feels wrong, where even the damp-loving mosses won't grow.

---

At night, the Weep's population dreams. Dreams here carry a particular quality: wetness. Not drowning, not flooding, but the sensation of being submerged without suffocating. Residents dream of breathing water, of opening their mouths and finding the moisture sustains them. They dream of sinking into the earth and continuing downward, past the channels, past the stone, into whatever space exists below.

The dreams don't frighten them. They're too common for fright, too consistent across the population to feel like personal anxieties. Children describe dreams that match their elders'. A newcomer who stays long enough eventually reports the same sensation: the wet, the descent, the strange comfort of going under. The Weep's residents have learned not to discuss these dreams with outsiders. The outsiders wouldn't understand.

Parrahn, the oldest founder, has spent eleven years not speaking. But sometimes, in the deepest part of night, Deevis hears her mother making sounds in her sleep—not words, but something closer. A murmuring that rises and falls like water finding its path. Deevis doesn't wake her. She doesn't write down what she hears. She lies in the dark and listens, and she waits for the day when the sound will become speech.

The whole settlement waits, though most of them don't know they're waiting. They move through their days, clearing channels and tending platforms and raising children who will inherit this damp and strange and patient place. And below them, always, the water rises—slowly, persistently, with a preference that looks from the outside like purpose. It moves through the channels they've cut, follows the paths it recognizes, pools in the places it prefers. And it waits, too.

For the earth to speak again. For the bowl to deepen. For whatever called the hollow into being to call once more.

The rim-stands tremble in their high wind, leaves folding and unfolding, and the Weep's residents go about their work, and the water doesn't tell them what it knows.
