# What Lives in the Terraces of Still Water

There is something in the cisterns.

At first you think it is reflection—the white sky pooling in still water, the mineral opacity giving it body and dimension. You think this because the alternative does not occur to you, because the alternative requires believing that something could live in water this clear, this sterile, this untouched by the processes that normally support life. But the alternative is correct.

They are not fish. They are not amphibians. They are not, in any meaningful sense, animals. They have no eyes, no mouths, no limbs, no visible means of propulsion. They have no color of their own—they are the color of the water they inhabit, which is to say almost no color at all, a faint mineral whiteness that shifts with the light. They are not even solid in the way you expect solid things to be. They are denser than the water around them, but only barely. They hold their shape, but only provisionally. If you disturbed the water enough—if you reached in and tried to grasp one—they would simply cease to cohere, their substance dispersing into the general opacity, and then slowly, over hours, re-forming somewhere else.

They are patient in a way that makes the water's passage look frantic.

---

The smallest of them are hand-sized—lucent, rounded forms like crossed cushions or flattened spheres, their edges soft and indistinct, as though they are perpetually deciding whether to be objects or simply concentrations of particulate. They drift in the cisterns at depths that seem arbitrary, sometimes near the surface where the light is strongest, sometimes hugging the clay bottoms where the water is coolest. They do not swim. They have no visible means of moving themselves at all. But they move. Slowly, without urgency, they traverse the interiors of their cisterns in patterns that have no obvious logic—drifting along the curved walls, pausing at angles, gathering in loose clusters near the downspouts where fresh water enters, dispersing again.

The larger ones are different. They are not simply bigger versions of the small. They are more complex. They have stratified, somehow—developed layers of slightly differing density, visible only when they pass through shafts of bright light, where their interiors reveal themselves as accretions of accumulated material. The smallest are nearly pure, almost transparent. The larger ones carry history. Sediment. Mineral deposits that have built up over time, incorporated into their substance the way a reef incorporates its own past. You cannot tell if they grow by eating—eating seems wrong, seems impossible given their lack of mouths or digestive systems—or if they simply accumulate particulate matter from the water they filter, the mineral deposits that would otherwise coat the cistern walls building up instead inside their slowly-rotating bodies.

They might be ancient. They might be new. There is no way to know. Their substance doesn't record time in a way you can read. But here is something: the largest of them, in the deepest cisterns, have dimensions that feel almost intentional. They are shaped—no, they have grown into—shapes that approximate something else entirely.

They look, very slightly, like people.

Not in detail. Not in any way you could point to and say "this is a face" or "this is a hand." But in proportion. In the way their stratified layers have accumulated more densely in some areas than others, creating a kind of verticality, a suggestion of stance. In the way the smallest drift aimlessly while the largest orient themselves upright, their upper portions slightly narrower than their bases, as though remembering—or inventing—the concept of standing.

You would not notice this if you did not spend time watching them. You would not notice this if the light were different. But in the brightest hours, when the diffuse whiteness from above pours down into the open cisterns, the largest of them catch that light and hold it in their layered interiors, and for a moment—a long moment, because everything here is long—they look like tall pale figures standing at the bottom of still water, patient, waiting.

---

They do not interact with each other in any way you can observe. The small ones drift past each other without acknowledgment. The large ones hold their positions without appearing to notice the small. There is no competition, no courtship, no play. If they communicate, they do so through means that leave no trace visible to the eye—through shifts in the mineral content of the water, perhaps, or through the barely-perceptible pressure waves their slow passage creates.

But here is the thing: they notice *you*.

Not immediately. Not dramatically. There is no approach, no retreat, no observable change in their drifting when you arrive at a cistern's edge. But over time—hours, days—their patterns shift. The small ones begin to cluster in the cisterns you visit most often, accumulating near the walls where you stand, dispersing when you leave. The largest of them rotate, slowly, so that their upright aspects orient toward wherever you are.

They do not do this quickly. They do not do this obviously. If you watched a single cistern for a single day, you would see nothing but random movement, meaningless drift. But if you watched many cisterns over many days—if you mapped their movements, if you tracked their positions—you would see that their distribution is not random at all. It is centered. On you.

They attend.

---

No one made them deliberately. This is clear from the structures—the builders created cisterns, channels, downspouts, shelters, but there is no indication they knew what would grow in the still water. Or perhaps they did know. Perhaps the cisterns were designed to produce exactly this—to catch and hold the mineral-rich water long enough for something to accrete from it, to concentrate, to become. Perhaps the builders understood that their labor would create not just a landscape but a kind of incubator, and perhaps they left because the incubation takes longer than human lives can wait.

Or perhaps they did not leave. Perhaps the largest of the forms—those tall pale accumulations standing patient in the deepest water—hold more than mineral deposit. Perhaps the stratified layers of their interiors preserve more than particulate history. Perhaps, if you could find a way to examine them closely enough, you would discover that the densest concentrations at their cores contain something else entirely—traces of organic material, strands of hair, fragments of nail, the last residues of hands that built and then, when the building was done, chose not to depart.

But you cannot examine them closely. They exist in water you cannot reach, in cisterns whose sides are too smooth and too high to climb, in a landscape designed to make access difficult. And even if you could reach them—even if you could plunge your hands into that mineral clarity and touch one—it would not hold together long enough to be examined. It would disperse. And then, slowly, re-form. Whole again. Or changed.

---

They do not think. This is obvious. Thinking requires differentiation between self and world, requires an interior where thoughts can happen, requires timeframes compatible with cognition. These beings have none of that. They are bodies without interiors, presence without self, attention without comprehension. They notice—slowly, mineraly, through the gradual accretion of sensory data in their layered forms—but they do not understand what they notice. They attend, but they do not know they attend.

And yet.

In the deepest cistern, where the water is oldest and still most profound, there is one that is larger than all the others. It has accumulated more layers than seems possible—decades of mineral deposit, perhaps centuries, held together by some property of the water or the clay or the intention built into every brick of this place. It stands, if something without legs can be said to stand, at the center of that cistern, rotating so slowly that the rotation is almost imaginary, its pale mass catching light and holding it in depths that should be dark.

And sometimes—rarely, on cycles that might be days or might be something else—tilts.

Toward the shelter structures. Toward the low brick forms with their multiple entrances and impossible angles. Toward the places that might have been where the makers rested, where the resting became permanent, where the construction incorporated its own constructors.

As if remembering.

As if the mineral layers that make up its substance hold more than particulate matter. As if they hold pattern. As if, somewhere in the chemical composition of the fired clay and the patient water and the endless slow accumulation, there is something like memory—compressed, transformed, mineralized—persisting in a form that has no mind to recall it but recalls anyway.

It does not grieve. It does not miss. These are animal processes, require animal bodies. But it attends, with a patience that makes your lifetime look like a blink, to the places where the hands were. And in attending, it keeps them, in some fashion, present.

---

What lives in the Terraces of Still Water is not alive. But it is not dead either. It occupies a third category—mineral, patient, emergent—whose properties we do not have words for. It is more like a process than a creature, more like a memory than a being, more like an intention persisting without a mind to hold it.

The terraces were built to move water. But perhaps moving water was never the point. Perhaps the water was meant to make *this*—these slow accumulations, these patient attentions, these standing shapes in stillness. Perhaps the entire vast construction, every brick and channel and downspout, exists to create the conditions under which something like this could form. Not to serve human needs. Not to serve any need. But to exist. To persist. To notice, slowly, across timescales that make human lifespans look like flickers.

The makers are gone. But the making continues. And what lives in the terraces—the pale drifters, the layered attenders, the standing shapes in the deep still water—are still becoming what the makers, perhaps, always intended them to be.

They are patient.

They have nothing but time.
