# What Lives in the Cathedral of Bone

---

The first thing to understand is that no one lives *here*.

Here is a word that implies arrival, implies a door, implies the possibility of departure. The things inside the Cathedral of Bone did not arrive. They *erupted*. They *precipitated*. They condensed out of the chemistry of decay the way minerals condense out of superheated water at a vent — not because they chose to be here but because the conditions demanded that something be here, and they were the something that answered.

They will never leave. There is nowhere to leave to. The abyssal plain extends in every direction like a sentence without punctuation, and at this depth the pressure would kill anything that tried to rise, and the cold would kill anything that tried to walk, and the dark would kill anything that tried to see. They are here because here is where the chemistry works. Here is where the body fell. Here is where the bone became available.

Everything that lives in the Cathedral of Bone lives because a whale died. Everything that lives here is a consequence.

---

## The Builders

They do not have a name. They will never have a name. They are one to two centimeters long, translucent, tubular, the width of a thread, and they do not know they exist in any way that the word *know* requires. If you held one in the water before your face mask — and you would have to hold it very gently, because the pressure difference between the inside of a human hand and the inside of the deep sea is a violence that does not bear thinking about — you would see through it. You would see its alimentary canal as a faint dark line running its length like a vein through marble. You would see the faint pulse of its circulatory fluid, which is not blood, which is blue, which carries something that is not hemoglobin but does the same work. You would see it the way you see a glass eel — as a suggestion of an organism, a sketch, a draft.

But you would not be holding one. There is no *one*. There are ten thousand. There are a hundred thousand. There are so many of them that the water inside the ribcage is denser than the water outside, not by any measurement a thermometer or a salinometer would register, but by feel. The water inside the Cathedral is *thick* with them. They are the medium. They are the atmosphere. They are what a resident of the Cathedral would breathe if a resident of the Cathedral breathed, which the Builders do not, exactly — they absorb dissolved gases through their skins, which are thin enough that the oxygen (such as it is, such trace amounts as persist at this depth) passes directly into their cells.

They do not breathe. They do not think. They secrete.

This is the fact that matters. This is the only fact that matters. Their bodies produce calcium carbonate — the same mineral that forms chalk, that forms limestone, that forms the shells of snails and the exoskeletons of coral polyps — and they deposit it as they move. The deposition is not random. It follows gradients of chemical concentration in the water, gradients that are created by the presence of other deposits, which were created by the presence of other deposits, which were created by the original surface of the bone. The Builders are following a recipe written in chemistry, and the chemistry is recursive, and the result is architecture.

But *architecture* is the wrong word, because architecture implies an architect. There is no architect here. There is no plan. There is no vision. There is a process — a blind, patient, relentless process — and the process produces walls. The process produces corridors. The process produces the mezzanines and the arches and the channels and the things that look, to a human eye desperate for meaning, like rooms.

They are rooms. They are also the residue of a chemical reaction. They are both. They are neither. The Builders do not care.

What the Builders *do* — and this is the verb that matters, the verb that replaces every other verb you might be tempted to use — is *pulse*. Their bodies undergo peristalsis, the wave of muscular contraction that moves food through a gut, and in the Builders this peristalsis is visible, is rhythmic, is *synchronized*. The worms near the frontier, where new construction is happening, pulse faster. The worms in the older, completed sections pulse slower. The worms on the surface of the sphere pulse at a rate that is neither fast nor slow but *regular* — metronomic, steady, the beat of a heart that does not know it is a heart.

And when they pulse, they glow.

The bioluminescence is not decorative. It is not communicative. It is not for the benefit of any eye, because no eye down here benefits from light in any way that evolution has bothered to reward. The glow is a byproduct of the secretion process — the same chemical reaction that produces calcium carbonate produces a photon of blue-white light as a side effect, the way a fire produces smoke. The Builders glow because they are building. They glow *when* they are building. The light is the shadow of the work.

But the light has effects the Builders did not intend and cannot perceive. The amphipods can perceive it. And the amphipods come.

## The Commuters

The amphipods are rice grains with legs.

This is reductive. This is unfair. But it is also, in the dark, in the murk, in the faint blue-white strobe of the Builders' glow, exactly what they look like — grains of rice that have decided, improbably, to walk. They are pale, almost white, segmented, with more legs than seems strictly necessary and two pairs of antennae that wave in front of them like the feelers of someone navigating a dark room. They are small. The largest are four millimeters. The smallest are less than one. They click. The chitin of their exoskeletons clicks against the calcium carbonate of the walls, and the clicking is the only sound in the Cathedral that is not silence — a dry, rapid, intimate sound, the sound of something being typed very quickly on a very small keyboard.

There are thousands of them. They fill the corridors the way blood fills veins — not packed, not compressed, but *moving*, always moving, in a flow that has direction and consistency and the kind of unconscious purpose that traffic has in a city that has existed long enough for its rhythms to become instinct.

The amphipods carry things.

This is the fact that makes you uncomfortable. This is the fact that makes the word *city* stick in your throat and stay there. The amphipods carry things, and they carry them in a direction, and the direction is always the same — inward, deeper, toward the skull, toward the sphere. They carry fragments of bone. They carry the husks of smaller organisms — copepods, nematodes, things too small to have common names and too numerous to need them. They carry the filamentous remnants of the chemosynthetic strands that grow on the walls. They carry, and this is the thing that makes you lean closer to the viewport of your suit until your breath fogs the glass, *feces*. Not their own feces. The feces of other amphipods. The feces of organisms you have not seen and may not see, organisms that live in the deeper chambers and send their waste outward through the corridors, where it is picked up by these amphipods, these carriers, these commuters, and brought back inward.

They are recycling. They are running a metabolism. They are the circulatory system of a body that does not know it is a body, and they are doing it without knowing they are doing it, and they are doing it because the corridors are there and the things are in the corridors and the things are lighter than the walls and so the things move and the walls do not and the path of least resistance is inward.

Or perhaps — and this is the thought you do not want to have, the thought that surfaces anyway, rising through your mind the way methane rises through sediment — perhaps they are doing it because the thing in the sphere needs to be fed.

## The Custodians

On the surface of the sphere, the worms are different.

They are larger — three centimeters, sometimes four, their bodies thick and opaque, the grey-white of old candle wax. They do not build. The sphere is already built. They *maintain*. They move across its surface with a slowness that is geological, that is glacial, that is the pace of something that has nowhere to be and nothing to do except continue to be where it is. They secrete, but their secretion is repair, not expansion — filling a crack, sealing a pit, thickening a wall that has grown thin. They are the janitors, the groundskeepers, the custodians of a monument they did not design and cannot comprehend.

But they pulse. Their peristalsis is slower than the Builders' — a deep, unhurried rhythm, the rhythm of a heart that has been beating for a very long time and has settled into a pace that conserves energy, that conserves purpose, that conserves the faint blue-white glow that coats the sphere in a light that is not quite light.

When a custodian dies — and they do die, their bodies curling and hardening and becoming part of the sphere's surface, a thin organic shell that the next custodian will seal over with a fresh layer of calcium carbonate — when a custodian dies, the others do not stop. They do not pause. They do not acknowledge. The body is material. The body is surface. The body is something to be sealed, to be built over, to be incorporated into the structure, and the custodian nearest to the dead one will, in the course of its slow patrol, pass over the body and lay down a film of mineral that locks it in place, and the body becomes wall, and the wall becomes sphere, and the sphere becomes more of itself.

The custodians have been here the longest. Their walls are centimeters thick. Their layers are legion. If you could cut the sphere open and read its cross-section the way a dendrochronologist reads tree rings, you would find layers of calcium carbonate and layers of worm-body and layers of calcium carbonate and layers of worm-body, a stratigraphy of life and mineral alternating, a record of generations compressed into a shell.

The sphere is a graveyard that is also a building that is also alive. The custodians do not know this. They do not need to know this. They need only to move, to secrete, to pulse. The knowing is not their work.

## The Growers

On the walls, in the patches where the current slows and the dissolved compounds in the water have time to settle, there is rust.

Not rust. But rust is the word that comes, because the color is iron-orange, dark and rich, the color of oxidation, the color of things that have been wet for a very long time. The growers are chemosynthetic bacteria — filamentous, hairlike, forming strands that can reach several centimeters in length, waving in the faint current like the grass of a world that has no wind. They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize. They do not need light. They need what the Cathedral provides in abundance: sulfur compounds, methane, the dissolved remnants of organic matter that the whale's decomposition has been releasing for years, for decades, for however long this has been going on.

The growers are sparse. This is important. The Cathedral is not a garden. It is not a forest. It is a *construction*, and the construction is mineral, and the mineral is white and grey and the blue-white of bioluminescence, and the rust-orange of the growers is an accent, a colonizer, an afterthought. They grow in patches. They grow where the chemistry allows. They grow in the channels where the water flow slows and the nutrients settle, and their strands catch particulate from the current, and the particulate feeds them, and they grow a little more.

The amphipods eat them. This is one of the things the amphipods carry — fragments of the growers, torn from the walls by the mandibles of the commuters and held in their forelegs as they navigate the corridors inward. The growers do not resist. They cannot resist. They grow, and they are eaten, and they grow again. This is not a relationship. This is a chemical equation.

But there is something else.

In the deepest chambers, near the skull, where the water is warmest and the chemistry is thickest, the growers change. They become denser. They become darker — not rust-orange but deep red, almost brown, the color of old blood. They form mats, not strands — thick, fibrous mats that coat the walls of the inner chambers like carpet, like fur, like the lining of an organ. And in these mats, other things live. Things too small to see. Things that exist at the scale of the chemistry itself — bacteria within bacteria, organisms within organisms, a microbiome so dense and so complex that to call it a *micro*biome is almost a joke. It is a world. It is a civilization of the very small, living on the walls of a civilization of the small, living inside the corpse of something that was once very large.

The whale feeds them all. The whale has been feeding them all since it sank. Its body is a battery, a store of chemical energy accumulated over a life of eating krill and singing and breathing air at the surface, and that energy is being discharged now, slowly, in the dark, into the bodies of worms and amphipods and bacteria and the things that eat the worms and the amphipods and the bacteria and the things that eat the things that eat them. The whale is still alive. Not as a whale. As a *reaction*. As an ongoing chemical event. As a fire that burns underwater and has been burning for years and will burn for years more, until the fuel is gone, until the bone is bare, until the city has nothing left to build with and the worms have nothing left to secrete and the amphipods have nothing left to carry and the sphere has nothing left to be fed.

The whale does not know this. The whale has not known anything for a very long time. But the Cathedral knows. The Cathedral is the knowing. The Cathedral is the shape the whale's knowing takes after the whale's knowing has stopped.

## The Eaters

The amphipods carry things inward. The things do not come back.

This is the core mystery of the Cathedral, the thing that the instruments cannot explain and the observation cannot resolve. Material flows inward. Material enters the sphere. The sphere is a meter in diameter, and material has been entering it for what must be years — the amphipod channels are old, worn smooth, the calcium carbonate lining them stained dark with use — and the sphere has not filled. The material goes in and the sphere stays the same size and the material does not come out.

Something is eating.

You do not see it. You cannot see it. The sphere's openings are small — a few millimeters, barely large enough for an amphipod to squeeze through with its burden — and the interior is dark, darker than the surrounding dark, and even the blue-white glow of the custodian worms on the sphere's surface does not penetrate inside. Your suit lights, aimed at the openings, reveal nothing. A faint cloudiness. A movement that might be current or might be something else. The chemical sensors report: sulfur, methane, enzymes. The temperature sensors report: two degrees warmer. The imagination reports: something alive.

It is not a fish. It is not a cephalopod. It is not anything that has a shape you would recognize, because the shapes you recognize are the shapes of things that evolved in light, in gravity, in the three-dimensional freedom of open water. Whatever is inside the sphere evolved in a cave, in a chemical soup, in the dark, and it has been there long enough that its shape has become a response to its circumstances, and its circumstances are: walls, warmth, food arriving through small openings, and nothing else.

It might be a worm. A very large worm. A worm that was once a Builder, once one of the translucent thread-sized creatures that secreted calcium carbonate and moved through the corridors, and that found its way into the skull, into the cavity where the whale's brain once sat, and that found there a concentration of nutrients and a temperature gradient and a chemical environment that triggered — what? Growth? Metamorphosis? A change in the algorithm of its body's chemistry? Something. The worm grew. The worm secreted a sphere around itself, a shell, a home, and the sphere had openings because the worm's body, in its new configuration, required flow — required the movement of water, required the import of material, required the import of food.

It might be many worms. It might be a colony, a collective, a mass of worm-bodies fused together by chemistry and proximity into a single metabolic entity, a organism-by-accretion, a thing that is one and many simultaneously, that has no center and no edge, that eats and grows and eats and grows inside its calcium carbonate shell the way a tumor grows inside a body — not maliciously, not intentionally, but because the chemistry says *more* and the chemistry is all there is.

It might be something else entirely. Something that was never a worm, that was never any of the organisms you have seen, that was already inside the whale when the whale sank — a parasite, a hitchhiker, a cyst that survived the pressure and the cold and the shock of death and that found, inside the whale's skull, a refuge and a food source and a reason to continue. Something that has been growing for as long as the Cathedral has been building, feeding on the influx of detritus the amphipods provide, metabolizing the sulfur and the methane and the bone and the bodies, growing in the dark, becoming whatever it is becoming, and the sphere is its shell, and the custodians are its skin, and the corridors and channels are its gut, and the amphipods are its hands, and the Builders are its architects, and the Cathedral is its body.

You do not know. You cannot know. The sphere keeps its secret. The openings are small and the inside is dark and the water that seeps out is thick with the evidence of metabolism but thin on the evidence of *what* is metabolizing. You could crack the sphere open. You could reach out with the manipulator arm of your suit and break the calcium carbonate shell and look inside. The custodians would not stop you. They would try to repair the damage — their slow, patient bodies would move toward the break and begin to secrete, to seal — but they would not fight you. They do not fight. They do not know how.

But you do not crack the sphere. Something stops you. Not fear — the deep sea has drained you of fear, has replaced fear with a colder, calmer thing, a thing that is closer to respect. Not curiosity — you are curious, but the curiosity has been overtaken by a larger feeling, a feeling that has no name in any language that was invented on the surface. Something stops you. The same something that stops you from pulling a bandage off a wound to see how it is healing. The same something that stops you from opening a chrysalis to see how the caterpillar is coming along. A sense that what is inside is *becoming*, and that becoming should not be interrupted, and that the sphere is not a container but a womb, and what lives inside it is not yet done.

## The City Itself

And then there is the thing that lives in the Cathedral that is not a worm, not an amphipod, not a bacterium, not a creature of any kind. The city itself. The structure. The walls and corridors and chambers and arches and channels and staircases and the sphere and the skull and the ribs and the bone. The whole of it. The system.

It is alive in the way a forest is alive — not as a single organism but as a process, a pattern, a thing that emerges from the interaction of its parts and that has properties none of its parts possess. The walls move. The corridors widen and narrow. The chambers change shape. Not fast. Not in any way that a human lifetime would notice. But on the timescale of the deep sea — the timescale of sediment accumulation, of tectonic creep, of the slow oxidation of bone — the city is dynamic. It is *growing*. It is *responding*. It is adapting to the flow of water and the deposition of material and the movement of organisms and the slow decomposition of the whale's skeleton and the even slower accumulation of sediment on the abyssal plain, which is burying the Cathedral, millimeter by millimeter, century by century, and which the Cathedral is, in its slow chemical way, *resisting*, building upward as the sediment builds upward, adding new layers of structure as the old layers are buried, climbing the rising tide of mud the way a staircase climbs a wall.

The city is alive and the city does not know it is alive and the city does not need to know it is alive and this — this — is the thing that stays with you after you leave, after you ascend, after the pressure decreases and the light returns and the cold recedes and you surface into a world of sun and wind and names. The thing that stays is the not-knowing. The way the city builds without knowing it is building. The way the amphipods carry without knowing they are carrying. The way the custodians maintain without knowing they are maintaining. The way the thing in the sphere eats without knowing it is eating, grows without knowing it is growing, *becomes* without knowing what it is becoming.

They are all of them — every worm, every amphipod, every bacterium, every filament of the growers, every crystal of calcium carbonate, every molecule of sulfur and methane — they are all of them engaged in a project so vast and so slow and so blind that the word *project* is a mockery of what they do. They are building something. They have been building it for longer than you have been alive. They will be building it long after you are dead. They will never see it finished because *finished* is not a concept that exists at this depth, in this cold, in this dark. There is only the process. There is only the next secretion, the next wall, the next chamber, the next corridor, the next delivery, the next layer, the next pulse of blue-white light in the dark.

The city is alive. The whale is dead. These are the same sentence.

---

The deep sea keeps its own time. And the Cathedral of Bone keeps building. And the worms keep secreting. And the amphipods keep carrying. And the custodians keep maintaining. And the growers keep growing. And the thing in the sphere keeps eating. And the city keeps becoming. And none of them know. And none of them need to know. And the cold goes on, and the dark goes on, and the pressure goes on, and the silence goes on, and the building goes on, and the building goes on, and the building goes on.
