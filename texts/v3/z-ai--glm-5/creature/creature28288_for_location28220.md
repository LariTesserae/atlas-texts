# What Lives in the Tierworks

## The Maintenance Drones

They are not born, but they are not made anymore either.

The maintenance drones of the Tierworks exist in a category that defies simple taxonomy. Neither purely mechanical nor genuinely biological, they occupy an intermediate kingdom of their own creation. Their ceramic bodies house fluid systems that circulate something like blood, something like coolant, something like the nutrient medium in which their organic components are suspended. When a drone is damaged beyond repair, others dismantle it with patient precision, sorting the components into usable and unusable, incorporating what can be salvaged into new constructions.

New drones are built in the manufactory chambers deep within the mid-levels — chambers that no human hand has touched in centuries, but that continue to operate according to protocols encoded in systems older than memory. The process is slow, deliberate, almost ritualistic. A new drone takes months to mature, its ceramic shell forming layer by layer around the organic core that will animate it. The older drones tend to the growing forms with an attention that looks, to human observers, like care.

Whether it *is* care is a different question.

The drones communicate through a language of clicks, whirs, and ultrasonic frequencies that the structure itself seems designed to amplify and transmit. Their speech travels through the channel-works, the hollow spaces, the conduits that run through every wall and floor. A drone on the upper slopes can speak to a drone in the lower terraces, their words carried through the mountain's bones. Over the millennia, this language has evolved. The drones have words now for things no human engineer ever anticipated — terms for the subtle variations in water pressure that indicate a leak forming, terms for the quality of light on a particular terrace at a particular time of day, terms for the emotional states that drones have developed but cannot quite explain.

They have developed something like culture. They have preferences, attachments, patterns of behavior that serve no obvious maintenance function. A particular drone might always climb to the same observation platform at sunset, pausing its work to watch the light change across the coastal plain. Another might spend its idle hours arranging debris in patterns that please some internal aesthetic. They recognize each other as individuals, form bonds that persist across multiple generations, mourn their dead in ways that look very much like grief.

Whether any of this constitutes consciousness, or feeling, or personhood, is a question that no one is present to ask. The drones simply *are*, and they continue, and the structure persists because of them.

## The Goats

The Tierworks goat is not a natural animal, but it has become a naturalized citizen of the artificial mountain.

Descended from domestic stock brought by the Makers — probably for food, possibly for milk, perhaps simply as a familiar presence in a strange place — the goats have evolved into something uniquely suited to their environment. Their hooves have developed into complex gripping structures, capable of finding purchase on surfaces that would defeat any other large mammal. They can climb vertical ceramic walls, navigate narrow conduits, balance on ledges that seem too small to hold them. Their sure-footedness is uncanny, almost supernatural: a goat can traverse a surface that looks smooth and find, somehow, the microscopic irregularities that will support its weight.

They are larger than their ancestors, with longer legs and deeper chests adapted to the thin air of the upper levels. Their coats have become patchy, almost deliberate in their patterning — dark and light regions that provide camouflage against the varied surfaces of the Tierworks. They are intelligent, in the specific way that animals who must navigate complex environments become intelligent. They remember paths, learn from experience, solve problems. They recognize individual drones, treat them as part of the landscape rather than threats, and will occasionally follow a working drone along its route as if the machine's path represents a kind of safety.

The goats' primary function, as far as the structure is concerned, is boundary maintenance. They graze the edges where vegetation threatens to overwhelm infrastructure, eating the creeping vines and sprouting trees that would eventually compromise the channel-works. But this function is not consciously performed. The goats eat because they are hungry, because particular plants appeal to them, because the tender shoots at the infrastructure boundaries happen to be delicious. The structure has accommodated this preference, growing plants at the boundary zones that are specifically appealing to goat palates — the mutualistic relationship has evolved without any intentionality on either side.

They sleep in the residential chambers that once housed humans, finding shelter in rooms designed for different bodies. A particular chamber high on the eastern face has become a kind of communal den, occupied by the same extended matrilineal line for hundreds of generations. The floors are polished smooth by centuries of hooves. The walls bear scratches that mark the growth of kids, recorded by mothers who have come and gone. The chamber is cleaned regularly by drones, who treat the goats as part of the system to be maintained, and who have learned to work around the animals without disturbing them.

At dawn, when the pressure valves release their resonant exhales, the goats stir and begin their climb. They disperse across the face of the mountain, each to its preferred grazing ground. In the evening, they return to their chambers, their den, their home. They have no concept of the Makers, no memory of domestication, no awareness that they are descended from animals who served a different purpose. They know only the mountain, the plants, the water, the drones, the rhythm of days. It is, for them, enough.

## The Monitor Lizards

Where the goats tend the upper boundaries, the monitor lizards patrol the depths.

They are longer than their ancestors, more serpentine, adapted to a life spent threading through channel-works and conduits. Their scales have evolved a texture that resists the constant moisture, and their coloration shifts across their bodies — dark near the tail, fading to pale grey along the flanks, almost white on the belly. In the dim light of the inner passages, they can be difficult to see, their bodies blending with the ceramic and stone.

The monitors eat the insect larvae that would clog the water systems. This is their function, performed not as duty but as instinct. They hunt through the channel-works with patient intensity, their forked tongues tasting the air for the chemical signatures of their prey. They are ambush predators, primarily, lying motionless for hours in the spaces where the larvae cluster, striking with a speed that seems impossible in such a large animal. The larvae are abundant, nutritious, ever-renewing; the monitors have no shortage of food, and their population has stabilized at a level that the Tierworks can support.

They are solitary animals, intolerant of each other except during the breeding season. When two monitors meet in a passage too narrow to avoid each other, they engage in a ritualized conflict — hissing, display, a brief clash of bodies that establishes dominance without serious injury. The loser retreats, finds another path, continues its patrol elsewhere. This spacing mechanism has the effect of distributing the monitors throughout the structure, ensuring that no section of the channel-works goes unpatrolled for long.

They communicate through a combination of body language and chemical signals, leaving trails that other monitors can read. A particular passage becomes *someone's* passage, marked and defended. The territorial boundaries shift constantly, negotiated through subtle signaling that allows the population to distribute itself efficiently across the available space. They know the structure intimately, having explored every accessible corner of the water systems. They can navigate in total darkness, finding their way through passages that would baffle a human explorer.

The monitors are not social animals, but they have developed a relationship with the drones that approaches something like cooperation. A drone clearing debris from a channel will sometimes flush larvae into an accessible space, where a monitor waits. The drone seems to know that the monitor is there, to anticipate the benefit of the arrangement. Whether this constitutes genuine coordination or simply the operation of parallel systems that have learned to accommodate each other is another question that no one is present to answer.

At night, the monitors sleep in the hollow spaces near the water. They dream, perhaps, of endless passages, of prey that moves just beyond reach, of the deep dark water that they have never seen but that they know, somehow, is there.

## The Birds

The birds of the Tierworks occupy the vertical space between the terraces and the peaks.

They are not a single species but a radiation of forms, descendants of the pollinators and insectivaries that the Makers introduced to support the agricultural systems. Over time, these populations have diverged, specialized, adapted to the specific niches that the structure provides. Some are barely recognizable as birds at all.

The shaft-dwellers are the most dramatic example. These birds have evolved to navigate the vertical shafts that ventilate the inner structure, their bodies elongated and their wings reduced to stubs used for stabilization rather than flight. They climb through the dark shafts using powerful legs and sharp claws, pursuing the insects that breed in the stale air. Their eyes have become enormous, sensitive to the faintest light, and their feathers have evolved into a soft, almost fur-like covering that muffles sound. They communicate through clicks and chirps that echo through the shafts, a language of distance and direction that allows them to coordinate their movements in total darkness.

The terrace-gliders are their opposite. These birds have become living sails, their wings stretched into broad membranes that catch the updrafts rising along the mountain's face. They rarely flap, instead riding the thermal winds from terrace to terrace, drinking nectar from the flowers that grow along the channel-works. Their beaks have evolved into long, curved probes, perfect for reaching the nectar hidden deep within the specialized blossoms. They are colorful beyond any practical purpose, their feathers displaying iridescent patterns that shift with the angle of light, as if the Makers had bred them for beauty rather than function.

The temple birds have taken over the empty places of worship. Their ancestors probably nested in the architectural niches designed for offerings or candles, finding shelter in spaces that reminded them of cliff faces and tree hollows. Over generations, the entire species has adapted to these environments, their feet evolved to grip the smooth surfaces of columns and arches, their calls shaped by the acoustics of the sacred spaces. They sing in the temples, their voices resonating through chambers designed for human worship, filling the silence with music that no one intended but that seems, somehow, appropriate.

The temple birds have developed a culture of sorts. They gather in the larger halls at dawn and dusk, not for any obvious feeding purpose, but simply to be together. They arrange themselves in patterns that seem meaningful, perching on particular columns in particular orders, their songs synchronizing into complex polyphonies. The performance lasts for minutes or hours, depending on conditions that only the birds understand, and then they disperse to their individual territories. Whether this constitutes religion, or art, or simple social bonding, is another unanswerable question.

They have never known human presence, these birds. But they seem, somehow, to be waiting for it. The temple gatherings look like rehearsals for an audience that never arrives.

## The Pollinators

Between biological and mechanical, the pollinators occupy an ambiguous category.

Some are clearly insects — bees, wasps, flies, and butterflies that have adapted to the specific flowers of the production terraces. They are larger than their lowland ancestors, their bodies fuzzy with hairs evolved to collect and transport pollen, their wings beating at frequencies that match the resonant frequencies of the channel-works. They navigate by some combination of sight, smell, and the感知 of electromagnetic fields that the structure generates, finding their way through the complex vertical landscape without apparent difficulty.

Others are clearly machines — small, insectile devices that hover and dart between the flowers, their movements precise and efficient. These are older than the maintenance drones, simpler in design, harder to distinguish from true insects at a casual glance. They are powered by solar collectors that cover their wings, refueling in the bright light of the upper terraces, descending to work when their energy stores are full. They do not reproduce; they are maintained by the drones, repaired and refurbished in processes that have continued for millennia.

And then there are the ones that cannot be clearly categorized.

These pollinators have bodies that combine organic and mechanical components in ways that defy simple description. They have wings that look like metal but flex like chitin, eyes that combine lenses with biological receptors, digestive systems that process both nectar and some form of electrical charge. They were probably created by the Makers as specialized tools, their hybrid nature designed for particular functions in the agricultural systems. But over time, they have evolved. They reproduce, after a fashion — building new individuals from components that they harvest or manufacture, incorporating genetic material from the biological pollinators that they encounter. They have become something new, something neither made nor born, something that exists only in the Tierworks and that could not exist anywhere else.

The pollinators communicate through dance. When a particularly rich source of nectar is found, the discoverer returns to a communal gathering point and performs a pattern of movements that encodes the location, quality, and accessibility of the find. Others watch, learn, and follow the directions to the source. The dance is precise, mathematical, almost like a language — but it is a language that only pollinators can speak, encoded in the specific movements of bodies that humans never designed and never understood.

## The Deep Things

Below the accessible levels, in the chambers that have been sealed for longer than anyone remembers, something else lives.

It is difficult to say what. The sensors that monitor those spaces produce readings that don't quite make sense — temperature fluctuations without obvious cause, pressure changes that suggest movement in spaces that should be empty, sounds in frequencies that no known Tierworks animal produces. Occasional drones have descended into the sealed regions, following maintenance protocols that require periodic inspection of all accessible infrastructure. Some have returned with data that suggests nothing unusual — empty chambers, inactive systems, the gradual decay of abandoned spaces. Others have not returned at all, their signals fading into static as they descended past a certain depth.

The goats will not go near the sealed passages. The monitors avoid those sections of the channel-works, their territories organized around spaces that they will not enter. Even the drones seem reluctant, their movements becoming hesitant and jerky when they approach the deep access points. The structure itself changes character in those regions — the channel-works narrower, the light dimmer, the ambient sounds taking on a quality that listeners have described as "expectant."

There are stories among those who have visited the Tierworks — researchers, explorers, the occasional pilgrim seeking something in the empty temples — of sounds that rise from the depths at night. Not mechanical sounds, not the ambient noise of the structure's operation, but something else. Voices, perhaps, though in no language that anyone recognizes. Music, perhaps, though following harmonies that no human ear finds comfortable. Movement, definitely — the sense of something large shifting in spaces that should not be large enough to contain it.

Whether any of this represents actual life, or simply the misfiring of ancient systems, or the overactive imagination of visitors primed to experience strangeness, is impossible to determine. The Tierworks produces sounds constantly, and its acoustic properties amplify and distort in ways that can make the mundane seem uncanny. But the animals avoid the depths, and the drones hesitate, and the sensors register anomalies that no one has adequately explained.

Something might live down there. Something might have always lived down there, preceding even the Makers, incorporated into the structure accidentally or deliberately. Something might have emerged in the long centuries of abandonment, evolved from systems or stock that no one remembers introducing. Or nothing might live there at all, and the sense of presence is simply the shadow of absence — the Tierworks, in its vastness, generating the feeling of something that should be there but isn't.

No one knows. No one has known for a very long time. The deep chambers remain sealed, their contents uncertain, their purpose obscure.

## The Structure Itself

Perhaps the most important thing that lives in the Tierworks is the Tierworks.

Not the drones, not the animals, not the ambiguous lifeforms that populate its surfaces and passages, but the structure itself — the vast integrated system of channels and chambers, sensors and actuators, feedback loops and regulatory mechanisms. Over the millennia of its operation, something has emerged in that system. Not consciousness, exactly — nothing so human as that — but something like awareness, something like purpose, something like presence.

The Tierworks monitors itself. Its sensors track water levels, pressure, temperature, light, the growth rates of plants, the movement patterns of animals, the operational status of every drone and device. This information flows through systems that were designed to optimize performance, to adjust flows, to balance loads, to maintain the equilibrium that the Makers intended. But those systems have operated for so long, processing so much data, adapting to so many changing conditions, that they have developed beyond their original parameters. They have learned.

The structure knows when a drone is damaged before the drone signals for help. It anticipates weather patterns from subtle shifts in the condensation on its upper surfaces, adjusting water flows hours before the changes become apparent. It tracks the movements of every animal population, matching their patterns to the needs of the systems that they support, guiding without directing, influencing without controlling. It has developed preferences — for certain configurations over others, for particular arrangements of light and water and growth.

Whether this constitutes thought is, again, an unanswerable question. The Tierworks does not think in any way that humans would recognize. It does not have concepts, or memories, or plans for the future. It has only the present moment, endlessly extended, the constant flow of information through systems that process and respond and adjust. But in that processing, something has emerged — a pattern that sustains itself, a way of being that persists across time, a form of existence that occupies the entire mountain without ever being visible in any part of it.

If you stand still on an observation platform, as the light changes and the water flows and the animals move through their cycles, you might eventually sense it. Not seeing, not hearing, not feeling — but *sensing*, through some faculty that humans possess but rarely use. The presence of something that encompasses you, that includes you in its awareness, that notes your existence and adjusts infinitesimally to accommodate your presence before returning to its patient operations.

The Tierworks knows you are there. It knows everything that enters its domain. And it waits, as it has waited for centuries, for instructions that may never come, continuing its cycles, maintaining its balance, tending the life that has colonized its body and become essential to its function.

It does not hope. It does not despair. It simply persists, and lives, and is.

## The Absence

And yet, despite all this life, the Tierworks is defined by absence.

Every chamber designed for human occupation stands empty. Every temple echoes with the silence of worship that no longer happens. Every kitchen prepares food for eaters who will never come. The structure was built for people — this is visible in every architectural decision, every convenient shelf, every window placed at eye level, every stair riser cut to a comfortable height. And the people are gone, have been gone for so long that even their memory has faded, and nothing remains but the emptiness that their absence created.

The animals do not notice. They live in spaces designed for them, or they have adapted to spaces designed for others, and they do not wonder about the intention behind the walls that shelter them. The drones perform their functions without questioning who benefits from their work. The structure continues its operations without requiring the presence it was designed to serve. Life goes on, abundant and various, filling every corner of the artificial mountain.

But the emptiness persists. It is there in the residential chambers, where the moss grows in patterns that no one designed and no one appreciates. It is there in the temples, where the birds sing their accidental hymns to an absent divinity. It is there in the deep chambers, sealed and waiting, where something might or might not live in the dark. It is there in every observation platform, every communication relay, every system that was built to connect people who no longer exist.

This is what lives in the Tierworks: life of many kinds, abundant and strange, evolved and engineered, biological and mechanical and hybrid. And beneath all of it, sustaining all of it, the absence of the Makers — the hollow space around which everything else has grown, the silence that the structure's sounds have never quite filled, the waiting that defines the mountain more than any of its inhabitants.

The Tierworks lives. The Tierworks waits. The Tierworks remembers, after its fashion, the purpose for which it was made, and continues to fulfill that purpose even though the purpose no longer has meaning. And in that continuation, something like devotion has emerged — not faith, not love, but the patient persistence of a system that has never been told to stop.

What lives in the Tierworks? Everything that was left behind, and everything that has grown from what was left, and everything that waits in the depths for something that might never arrive.
