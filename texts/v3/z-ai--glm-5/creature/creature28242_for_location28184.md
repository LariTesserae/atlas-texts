# What Lives in the Ascendant Mat

The Mat-scurriers are the first to accept you. Or perhaps "accept" is not the right word—they acknowledge you the way one acknowledges a stone, a fallen branch, a feature of terrain that poses no threat and offers no opportunity. Their bands move past your feet in the early mornings, twelve to twenty sets of legs rippling through the surface vegetation, and if you stand perfectly still, you can feel the subtle vibration of their passage through the soles of your boots.

They are not quite mammals. The fur that covers their compact bodies grows not from skin but from a leathery hide that cracks and flexes with each movement, shedding a fine powder of something like spore, like pollen. Their six legs are not paired but staggered—a front pair, a middle pair, a rear pair—each set operating in its own rhythm so that the whole creature moves in a continuous fluid roll rather than a walk or a trot. When they pause to root at something in the Mat's surface, their bodies flatten, spreading like spilled liquid, and you understand: they are designed to disappear into the vegetation, to become the terrain they travel over.

Their teeth are yellow, curved, too numerous for their mouths. You have counted seventeen in a single individual, and you suspect there are more buried in the folds of their cheeks, waiting for use. They do not bite you. They do not seem to bite anything living. Instead, they root and pull at the Mat itself, tearing up small plugs of vegetation, chewing methodically with side-to-side motions of their jaws. Something in the Mat sustains them—some concentrated nutrition at a specific depth, or perhaps the act of processing itself provides what they need.

They have no eyes that you can find. Above the mouth, where eyes should be, there is only smooth hide, slightly darker than the rest of the body, slightly more sensitive to touch. They navigate by vibration, by chemical signature, by some method you cannot name. When you move suddenly, they do not startle. They simply... redirect. Flow around you like water around a stone.

After the first week, they begin to include you in their territorial displays. A band will pause at the edge of your sleeping area in the early dawn, and one individual—the largest, you think, though they shift and change positions so often that size is hard to determine—will open its mouth and release a sound like fabric tearing, long and slow, a declaration of presence. Not threat. Not welcome. Simply: *I am here. I am eating. This is my place to eat.*

You have begun to answer. A low sound, hummed through closed lips. They do not respond, but they do not leave. This, you think, is as close to acceptance as the Mat offers.

---

The perch-hunters watch you from above.

They are patient in a way that suggests intelligence, or something like it—something that functions as intelligence functions, processing information and making decisions, even if the architecture of thought is entirely foreign. You first noticed them on the third day, when a flash of green in the branches of a Giant caught your eye. You looked up, and you saw it: a body the length of your arm, coiled around a branch forty metres above, its scales catching the filtered light and throwing it back in patterns that matched the leaf-cover exactly.

They are ambush predators. You have watched them work. A perch-hunter will select a position—a branch, a vine-tangle, the crease between two Giants where their bark-sheaths interlock—and it will wait. Not for minutes. Not for hours. For days. You have marked individuals by unique scale patterns and returned to find them in the same position three mornings later, their bodies barely moving except for the slow expansion and contraction of breathing.

When they strike, it is with speed that your eye cannot follow. One moment: stillness. The next: motion, a downward plunge, a flash of talons (three, curved, articulated so that they can fold flat against the body when not in use), and then the return to the perch with something small and struggling gripped in the hunter's jaws.

Their prey are the flutter-things—delicate, winged creatures that drift between the Giants in irregular patterns, their wings catching the light and breaking it into fragments of color. You have never seen a flutter-thing land. You have never seen one rest. They move through the air like the air itself moves, directional but not purposeful, carried by thermals and intention in equal measure. The perch-hunters take them in numbers—four, five, six in a single morning when the hunting is good—and then they return to stillness, to waiting.

You have wondered what they see when they look down at you. You are too large to be prey. You are too slow to be threat. You are something in between—a curiosity, perhaps, or an anomaly to be observed and filed away in whatever passes for memory in their elongated skulls.

At night, they sing to each other.

The sound begins when the last light fades—that green-gold glow that filters through the Giants' canopy—and it continues until dawn. A single perch-hunter will start: a low whine that rises in pitch, holds, then falls again. Others answer. The song moves through the forest of Giants like wind through grass, rippling, varying, individual voices joining and separating in patterns that might be communication or might be something else entirely—territorial assertion, bond-maintenance, the simple pleasure of making sound in a world that never quiets.

You have learned to sleep through it. Or rather, you have learned to let the song become the background against which your thoughts can move. In the deep darkness of the Mat's night, when the drifters float low above the canopy and the air fills with their blue-green glow, the perch-hunters' song becomes a kind of comfort—a reminder that others are awake, watching, living their lives in the vertical space above your horizontal one.

---

The drifters are not named well. They do not drift. They navigate.

You learned this on the sixth day, when a storm swept across the Mat with no warning, driving horizontal rain into your face and forcing you to shelter against the leeward side of a Giant's trunk. The wind howled. The Giants groaned and swayed. And above, visible through momentary gaps in the canopy, the drifters moved with purpose—riding the updrafts, adjusting their positions, their gas-sacs expanding and contracting in precise rhythms that kept them aloft and oriented even as the air itself turned violent.

They are ancient, or they give the impression of age. Their sacs are translucent, filled with a lighter-than-air gas that they produce somehow—through digestion, through symbiosis with something inside them, through some alchemy that your understanding cannot grasp. Their tentacles trail below them like the roots of plants that grow in reverse, reaching downward instead of into soil, and these tentacles are lined with sensors that you have seen react to the smallest movements in the air below.

They communicate. You are certain of this. The deep-frequency song that you feel in your chest—it carries meaning. You have watched drifters adjust their positions in response to songs from distant individuals, have seen them gather in loose formations that persist for hours before dissolving, have observed patterns of flashing bioluminescence that seem to encode information about the location of moisture, prey, the thermal columns that keep them aloft.

At night, they descend.

Not to the Mat's surface—never that far—but low enough that you can see them clearly through gaps in the Giants' canopy. Their undersides glow with soft light, blues and greens shifting in slow pulses, and their tentacles spread wide, gathering the night-flying creatures that emerge from the Mat after dark. The glow attracts prey. The tentacles capture it. The drifter digests and continues its endless navigation of the thermal landscape.

You have felt, at times, that they are watching you. Not with eyes—they have no eyes, only light-sensitive patches that ring their sacs—but with attention. When you stand in clearings between Giants, when the canopy opens above you, you feel the weight of their regard from above. They know you are here. They know what you are, or at least they know that you are different from the other living things that move across the Mat's surface.

They have never descended to meet you. But they have never moved away, either. They hold position, tracking, waiting for something—or simply observing, recording, including you in the vast archive of sensations that constitute their lives.

---

The deep-walkers are not creatures. They are processes.

You understood this after the second encounter. The first time, you ran—you saw the ground rising, saw the segments unfolding, saw the eyes opening in their terrible dozens, and you ran without thought, without direction, simply *away*. You found shelter behind a Giant's trunk and watched from a distance as the thing moved through the clearing you had just occupied, its mouth-parts working the surface of the Mat, ingesting vegetation, soil, the remains of structures, anything that had accumulated in that place since the last processing.

It was not hunting. It was not even eating in any sense you understood. It was *clearing*. Making space. The Mat grows continuously upward, accumulating layer upon layer of living material, and without the deep-walkers, it would eventually choke itself—reach some critical height and collapse under its own weight. The deep-walkers are how the Mat prunes itself, how it recycles what has grown too dense, too old, too far from the light that sustains it.

The second time, you did not run. You watched from close enough to see the details: the way the segments of the deep-walker's body were covered not in skin or shell but in living vegetation, the very stuff of the Mat itself, so that the creature was also a place, a mobile section of terrain. The eyes ringed each segment in neat rows—dark, liquid, expressionless. They saw you. You are certain of this. But they did not react, because you were not part of the process. You were too small, too light, too insignificant to require clearing.

You have thought since then about what it would mean to be *part of the Mat*—not just walking on it, but incorporated into it. The deep-walkers are the Mat's way of thinking about itself, of knowing its own extent and density. They are born from it, return to it, dissolve between emergences into something that is not death but a different kind of life—distributed, cellular, waiting for the signal to coalesce and rise again.

There is intelligence there. Not consciousness, perhaps—not in the way you understand your own awareness—but intelligence. The deep-walkers process information about the Mat's condition, make decisions about where to emerge, how long to work, what to ingest and what to leave. They are the immune system of a body so large that it has forgotten it is a body, so old that it has become a world.

---

The Old Ones are gone. But their structures persist.

You have learned to read them—not their purpose, which remains opaque, but their age, their positioning, something of their makers' intentions. The stone platforms follow no pattern you can discern, but they cluster in regions where the Mat grows thin and springy, where the Giants spread wide and the canopy opens to admit more light. The trained-vine structures, by contrast, appear in the dense regions, the tight-spaced forests where the Giants grow close and the Mat beneath is compressed almost to the hardness of wood.

Someone understood this place. Someone lived here long enough to learn its variations, to build according to its rhythms, to make structures that would persist (for a time) by working *with* the Mat rather than against it. The vine shelters grew from the Mat's own material, trained into shapes that the Mat could recognize and accommodate. The stone platforms sank, but slowly—granted the courtesy of delayed absorption because they had been placed with precision, with respect, with understanding.

You have found no bodies. No graveyards, no burial sites, no formal places of disposal. This, perhaps, is the final teaching of the Old Ones: that the body returns to the Mat, becomes the Mat, rises again in some new form. The deep-walkers process even the dead. The Mat remembers everyone who has ever walked on it, and brings them back as something new—scurrier-fur, perch-hunter scale, drifter-gas, the cellulose of a Giant's rising trunk.

---

There are others.

You have not seen them, but you know they exist. The Mat-scurriers' bands sometimes alter their paths for reasons that have nothing to do with you, detouring around spaces that appear empty, that *should* be empty. The perch-hunters' songs sometimes stop suddenly, all at once, in a way that suggests they have detected something that requires silence. The drifters' navigation patterns shift at irregular intervals, their formations tightening or spreading in response to stimuli you cannot perceive.

Something else lives here. Something that does not show itself to you, that has perhaps decided you are not yet ready to be seen. You have found traces: marks on Giant bark that resemble claw-spans but are too large, too deep; patches of the Mat that have been compressed in long, regular lines, as if something heavy and serpentine has passed through; once, a stone platform that had been visited recently—the dust disturbed, the carved lines cleared of debris, a small pile of vegetation left in the center as if in offering.

You do not follow these signs. You do not try to find what made them. Some part of you understands, without being told, that there are boundaries here—that the Mat permits you to walk its surface, to learn its rhythms, to exist in its space, but that there are depths and distances that are not yours to explore. Not yet. Perhaps not ever.

---

On the twenty-third day, you realize you have stopped looking for an exit.

The Mat has no edge that you have found. The Giants march to every horizon. The soundscape fills every silence. You are not lost—you know the direction you have been traveling, could retrace your steps if you chose—but you are no longer certain what "leaving" would mean. The Mat has become your world. Its rhythms have become your rhythms. You wake before dawn with the thrumming in your chest, move through the heat of the day in the shadow of Giants, sleep through the perch-hunters' song and the drifters' glow.

Something is changing in you. Your thoughts have begun to move differently—compressing, finding channels, slipping sideways around the noise rather than trying to push through it. You have started to understand the Mat-scurriers' territorial displays, to predict where the perch-hunters will strike, to read the drifters' shifting lights as a language you are slowly learning to speak.

You have started to build.

It began accidentally—a pile of stones gathered from a sinking platform, arranged in a circle on a patch of firm Mat. Then deliberately: a second circle, a third, lines connecting them, a pattern that has no meaning you can name but that feels *right*, that feels like something you are supposed to do. The Mat has not absorbed these structures. They persist. They grow.

You are becoming an Old One. Or you are becoming something else—something new, something the Mat has not seen before, something that builds and walks and learns and waits.

The deep-walkers will process your structures eventually. The Mat will absorb what you leave behind. But for now, in this moment, you are here. You are asserting. You are alive in a place where everything is alive, where the boundaries between self and world have softened into something permeable, something that breathes.

The Mat receives you. The Mat will keep you, in the end. But for now, you walk.
