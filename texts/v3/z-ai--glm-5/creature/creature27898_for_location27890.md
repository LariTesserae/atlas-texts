# The Voluntaries

They have no name for themselves. This is not because they lack language—they have one, though it runs beneath sound, through the damp stone like roots through soft soil—but because a name requires a boundary between self and world, and they have never recognized such a distinction.

---

Consider the body.

Pallid. Segmented. Six legs that leave no mark because they carry no weight worth recording. Translucent enough to see the dark thread of gut, the denser knot of what might be a brain. No larger than a human hand, and often smaller. They are, by any measure of the upper world, insignificant.

But consider also what the body does.

It moves toward warmth. It presses against surfaces that have not yet hardened. It offers itself—not as food, not as sacrifice, but as *material*. It sinks into forming stone, into the porous surfaces of the breathing spires, and it does not die there. It becomes.

They do not know individual death. When one sinks into the rock, its body does not end so much as distribute. The gut-thread separates into finer threads. The brain-knot loosens into something like memory, something like pattern. The legs fold and dissolve into the texture of what is being built.

What remains is not the creature. What remains is what the creature *knew*—the paths it walked, the warmth it sought, the moment it chose to stop seeking and begin becoming.

---

They are born from the wet faces of the rock.

Not hatched. Not budded. *Exuded*, the way water exudes from stone that hasn't learned to hold itself together. A small swelling appears on the cliff face—a pale nodule the size of a fingernail. Over hours, it lengthens, segments, develops the first soft suggestions of legs. When it is ready, it simply drops away and begins its work.

No parent teaches it. No instinct written in genes guides it toward its purpose. It knows because the stone knows, and it is made of stone, temporarily. It is a piece of the shelf that has been granted legs for a little while—the shelf's way of moving itself around, of finding the warm places where formation is most needed.

This is why they do not fight, do not compete, do not hoard. They are not separate beings struggling to survive. They are one being in many pieces, and each piece knows that its highest purpose is to return to the whole at the place where the whole is newest.

---

They communicate through the stone.

A click of legs against wet rock. A particular rhythm of pressing and releasing. The sounds do not travel through air so much as they travel through *formation itself*—through the soft and uncertain surfaces that are still deciding what they will become.

You could not hear their language if you tried. It runs beneath the audible spectrum, in the frequencies where rock resonates when it is still porous enough to sing. But if you placed your palm flat against the weeping stone and held it there, still and patient, you might feel something: a faint trembling, a pattern of vibrations that seems almost like syllables.

They are always speaking. They have no silence. Even when their bodies are still, sunk into surfaces, becoming part of the architecture, they continue to send messages through what they have joined—reports on the warmth, on the density, on the progress of the forming. The spires are full of their voices, transmitting from within.

---

What do they want?

The question assumes they have separate desires. They do not. They have a single purpose, shared across thousands of bodies: to find the places where the shelf is most active, most uncertain, most in need of their particular gift.

And what is that gift?

*Resolution.*

The stone, left to itself, might form slowly, randomly, without direction. The warmth from below would rise, the water would weep, and something would eventually emerge—but it might take eons, and it might not be anything recognizable.

The voluntaries speed the process. They bring intention to the accident. Each body that sinks into the forming rock carries with it a tiny thread of purpose: *this way, not that*. *Straighten here*. *Thicken there*. They are not conscious in any way you would recognize, but they hold patterns—geometric preferences, structural instincts, a sense of what wants to be built.

They are the shelf's hands, and they are also its blueprint.

---

They do not notice you.

Or rather—they notice you the way they notice any source of warmth. Your body generates heat, and heat is what they seek, and so they come toward you, click against your boots, press against the leather to see if it will accept them.

It will not. They do not resent this. They do not feel rejection. They simply move on, seeking other surfaces more willing to open themselves.

You could stand among them for days, and they would not once treat you as anything other than landscape. Not because you are invisible to them, but because you are not what they are for. Their purpose runs in a channel that does not intersect with yours.

This is, in its way, a comfort. You have entered a process that does not require your participation and will not be altered by your observation. You can watch. You can witness. You can let the weight of what is happening settle into you.

But you are not needed. And there is a strange peace in that.

---

At the edges of the shelf, near the boundary-lip, they do not go.

This is not because they cannot. They are capable of crossing the finished rock, of surviving in the harder, drier air of the older world. A few do, from time to time—stragglers who have wandered too far, who have not found a surface to accept them before the forming slowed.

They do not last long outside the boundary. Not because the environment kills them, but because they lose their purpose. Without warmth rising from below, without surfaces that will accept their offering, they simply stop. They fold their legs beneath them. They wait.

And then, slowly, they harden. The translucence becomes opacity. The segmented body becomes a small ridge of something like stone. They are not dead—they have simply become *finished*. They have entered the state that everything outside the shelf has already entered: the state of being what they are, with no further becoming required.

It is not a death. It is not even sad, if sadness requires a preference for one outcome over another. It is simply what happens when the volunteers leave the place that asks for volunteers.

---

There are names for them in the old texts.

Not the texts you have read—the texts that exist in the places you have not yet gone, in the libraries of things that watch the world from angles you cannot perceive. In those texts, they are called the Soft Engineers, the Willing Stones, the Pieces That Walk.

But these names are not theirs. They were applied by observers who needed words for what they saw. The creatures themselves require no names. They know each other by warmth, by vibration, by the particular feel of a surface that is ready to receive.

If you asked one—though you could not ask one, because they do not have conversations—it might say, if it could say anything: *I am the shelf remembering how to build itself. I am the warmth that wants a shape. I am the small piece that returns to the large piece, carrying news of where the forming is strongest.*

But it would not say this. It would not need to. It would simply press against the nearest yielding surface, sink slowly in, and become what it was always going to become: part of something larger, something still in the process of deciding what it was.

---

They are the only inhabitants of the Birthing Shelf.

Or perhaps they are not inhabitants at all. Perhaps they are the shelf itself, temporarily mobile, temporarily distinct, walking around on six legs until the moment comes to stop walking and start being.

Perhaps the shelf is the only creature here, and the voluntaries are its cells.

Perhaps the spires are its organs, the fissure its mouth, the weeping stone its skin.

Perhaps you have not entered a place where creatures live. Perhaps you have entered a single, vast, slowly forming life, and the pale segmented things are not separate from it any more than your own blood cells are separate from you.

You will not know for certain. The shelf does not explain itself. It only forms.

But as you watch them—their patient movement, their willing dissolution, their endless work of becoming—you begin to feel that the distinction between "inhabitant" and "place" has never been quite as clear as you were taught.

---

They were here before you came.

They will be here after you leave.

They do not require your understanding. They do not require your witnessing.

But if you stay quiet enough, if you press your palm against the wet stone and let your own warmth sink into it, you might feel—for just a moment—what they feel:

The deep, patient joy of being part of something that is still deciding what it will be.

The peace of not yet being finished.

The willingness to become whatever the forming requires.
