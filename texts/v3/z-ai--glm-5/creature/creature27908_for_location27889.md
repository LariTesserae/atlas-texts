# The Keepers of the Weep

They call themselves something that doesn't translate cleanly. The closest approximation might be "those who listen to water learning to be patient," but that carries too many syllables, too much explanation. In their own language—a tongue spoken rarely and always softly—the word is a single breath: *Hesm.*

---

## What They Look Like

The moisture has become them. You see it in the way their skin holds a slight translucence at the temples, the inner arms, the soft skin at the base of the throat. Not diseased. Not frail. *Saturated.* Their bodies have learned to hold water the way the stone does—permeable at the boundaries, steady at the core.

Their hair grows dark and straight, worn long by most, though you see some who have cropped it close to the skull for practical work. In the constant humidity, it rarely dries completely. A slight dampness clings to them always, and they carry no scent of must or mildew—somehow, their bodies have reached an accommodation with the wet. They smell like clean stone after rain, like ferns before they unfurl.

Their hands tell the story of generations. The knuckles have grown slightly broader, the fingers more articulate, capable of the fine adjustments that water-channeling requires. You see palms that can read stone by texture alone, that know whether a surface will shed moisture or drink it, that can feel the difference between a crack that will heal and one that will spread. These are not calloused hands—stone here is smoothed by water, not roughened by wind—but they are hands that know things. Hands that have learned by touching, by correcting, by waiting for the water to show them what it wants.

Their eyes have adapted to the diffuse light. You notice how they can look at the fern thickets, the moss fields, the cliff edges, and see distances accurately where you still struggle. They blink less often. Their pupils have grown comfortable with a world that never sharpens, that always glows from within.

They move with a gait you might initially mistake for hesitation but come to recognize as precision. Each footfall chosen. Each shift of weight deliberate. They do not trip. They do not stumble. They walk as though the path were a conversation they're having with the ground, and they mean to listen as much as speak.

---

## How They Live

They are born into a cradle of wet stone. The birthing rooms sit at the lowest level of the central building, where the cisterns gather the cleanest water, and a child enters the world already damp, already listening to the slow drip that will score their earliest memories. The elders say the water decides when a child is ready to emerge—that the labor begins not when the body demands it but when the moisture in the air shifts, when the channels flow slightly faster, when the stone itself seems to soften its consent.

They have no word for "orphan." When a mother dies in childbirth or after, the child is simply claimed by the household that feels most right—the one whose water-channels run compatible to the infant's disposition, whose fern-tended rooms match the child's emerging temperament. Children are not raised, precisely. They are *accommodated.* The settlement grows around them like moss grows around a stone—patiently, intimately, without hurry or design.

A child spends their first years in the fern thickets, where the older ones teach them the textures of water. Not names, not yet. Textures. This moisture comes from the cliff-face weeping; it carries a mineral tang, a slight heaviness on the tongue. This moisture rose from the air itself, condensed on the moss-beads; it is lighter, sweeter, meant for drinking. This moisture pools in the deep cisterns, aged and mineral-rich, reserved for the sick and the dying. By the time a child can speak, they know water the way most children know their own fingers.

They have no formal education. Instead, they drift toward the work that calls them—the channel-mending, the fiber-weaving, the stone-shaping, the fern-tending, the cistern-keeping. An older practitioner notices the interest and makes space. The child watches. The child tries. The child fails, and the water shows them where they went wrong. Slowly, over years, they learn.

They do not count years. They count channels mended, stones shaped, fern-fountains raised from spore to full height. Time moves differently here—measured in water's slow decisions, in the gradual deepening of a groove worn into stone, in the incremental spread of moss across a pathway. A person's age is not a number. It is a story: "She was born when the eastern channel collapsed," or "He came into the world the year the ferns climbed past waist-height for the first time."

## What They Believe

They do not worship the water. They do not pray to it, do not ask it for favors, do not fear its judgment. To worship implies a distance that simply doesn't exist here. The water is not above them or beyond them. It moves *through* them—literally, constantly—just as it moves through the stone, the ferns, the air itself.

What they have instead is a practice of attention. Every person, every day, spends time watching the water. The child at the cliff edge is not idle. The child is doing the work that all Hesm do: learning to read what the water writes as it descends.

The water tells them things. Not in words, not in messages that translate, but in patterns: which cracks are widening, which channels are flowing true, which ferns need more, which cisterns are full. A skilled Hesm can stand at a cliff-face for an hour and tell you which stones will need mending in the next season, which moss-patches are thinning from too much or too little moisture, where the water wants to go that the current channels aren't allowing.

They believe—though "believe" is too strong a word for something they experience as fact—that the water is learning. The plateau is young. The stone hasn't settled into its final disposition. The water is still figuring out how to move across this new surface, still exploring its options, still tracing paths that may or may not become permanent. The Hesm see themselves as the water's collaborators, not its keepers. They help it find the routes it already wants to take.

They do not interfere. They do not redirect. They do not impose their will on the water's course. They watch, and they wait, and when the water shows them what it needs—a channel deepened here, a crack sealed there, a fern relocated because it's drinking from a flow that could serve a better purpose—they provide it. The water leads. They follow.

This is why they move so softly, speak so rarely. Not from some doctrine of silence, not from prohibition or piety. Simply because the water is always speaking, and if you make too much noise, you'll miss what it's saying.

---

## Their Relationships

They pair, but not in the way you might expect. They have no marriage ceremonies, no formal bonds, no property that needs to be merged or divided. Two people drift together because their work fits—because one shapes stone and the other tends channels, because one watches the cliffs while the other watches the cisterns, because their rhythms complement each other like two streams meeting at a confluence.

They stay together for as long as it makes sense. Sometimes that's a lifetime. Sometimes it's a season. Sometimes they circle back to each other after years apart, finding that their work has grown compatible in new ways. There is no stigma in separating, no virtue in staying. The water changes course; so do they. It would never occur to them to expect permanence from a union that the water itself hasn't sanctified.

They raise children together—not just biological parents, but whoever is present, whoever the child's attention settles on, whoever happens to be working nearby when a young one needs guidance. The child moves through the settlement like water moves through the channels: pausing where there's space, flowing where the way is clear, gathering in the pools that form naturally around certain adults.

They do not have enemies. They do not have conflicts, not in the sense you might think of—no disputes over property, no feuds between families, no competitions for status. The water doesn't compete with itself; neither do they. But they do have disagreements, and these can run deep. One person might see the water wanting one path; another sees it differently. They will stand at a cliff-face for hours, pointing to different trickles, different beads, different stains on the stone, each reading the water's intent in a different way. These disagreements are never resolved by argument. They are resolved by waiting. The water will reveal what it wants, eventually. Both parties know this. Both parties wait.

---

## Their Art and Craft

Everything they make serves the water. Even the objects that seem decorative—the carved stones that edge the pathways, the woven mats that cover the floors, the small sculptures you sometimes find tucked into fern thickets—reveal themselves, on closer inspection, as water-guides. The carving directs moisture away from a root system that needs to stay slightly drier. The woven mat is shaped to collect condensation and funnel it to a particular spot. The sculpture has grooves worn into its surface, almost invisible, that guide the weeping moisture into a slow spiral that feeds a particular fern.

They do not create for beauty. Beauty emerges anyway. A channel mended correctly has an elegance that settles into the eye like satisfaction. A wall built with patience, stone by stone, carries a rightness that has nothing to do with aesthetics and everything to do with balance. They do not make things pretty. They make them true, and truth becomes beautiful over time.

Their fiber work is legendary in the valleys below—though the Hesm themselves would never describe it that way. They harvest the fern-stalks that die back naturally, never cutting living growth, and weave them into cloth that holds moisture without rotting. A Hesm-woven garment will keep you damp but not soaked, cool but not cold, protected from the saturated air by a fabric that learned its properties from the water itself. The weavers say the fibers tell them what pattern to use. They do not design. They follow.

Their stone-work is the same. They do not impose shapes on the rock. They find the shape that already wants to emerge—the stone knows what it is, and they help it become that. A wall that looks like it grew from the cliff-face rather than being built against it. A channel that curves precisely where the water has always wanted to curve. They do not quarry. They do not cut. They split where the stone suggests splitting, and they use what the water has already shaped.

## How They Die

They do not fear death. The water that has moved through them all their lives simply returns to the larger body, rejoins the slow weeping that traces the cliff-faces, becomes part of the flow that will feed the ferns and fill the cisterns and slide down the stone for centuries to come. They have watched the water long enough to know that nothing that enters it ever truly leaves. It changes form, changes speed, changes course—but it continues.

When a Hesm feels their body beginning to release its grip on the world, they simply move to the dying rooms. These sit at the highest point of the plateau, where the air is thinnest and the condensation gathers fastest, where the view extends across the weeping cliffs to the mists below and the valleys beyond. A dying person lies on a stone pallet that has been worn smooth by generations of bodies, and they watch the water. They watch it until they become it.

They are not buried. The plateau has no soil deep enough for graves, and the Hesm would not put their dead somewhere the water couldn't reach. Instead, the body is carried to a particular cliff-face—one of the places where the weeping is heaviest, where the water runs in deliberate threads straight down the stone—and laid in a shallow depression that has been worn over centuries into exactly this shape. The water covers them. The water takes them. Within a season, nothing remains but bone, and within a few more seasons, even that has softened and dispersed, dissolved into the mineral content of the weeping itself.

They do not mourn in the way you might expect. The person has not gone anywhere. They are still here—still in the water, still in the ferns, still in the moisture that beads on the moss and slides down the channels and gathers in the cisterns. You might hear someone mention a name from three generations back: "He would have seen this," or "She knew this current." Not as memory. As presence. The dead are still present. They simply flow differently now.

## What They Fear

They are not fearless. They fear what anyone who has found their proper place fears: the possibility of being displaced.

They fear the water stopping. It has never happened—not in the generations they remember, not in the deeper past that the stone channels record—but they know it could. They have seen the ruins of other settlements, lower in the valleys, where the water changed its mind and stopped coming, where the ferns dried and the moss crisped and the people had to leave or die. They keep watch. They tend the channels with obsessive care. They do not presume that the water will always weep. It weeps now. It has reasons. Those reasons could change.

They fear fire, though they use it rarely and carefully. A single flame, untended, could sweep through the fern thickets and leave the plateau bare, could dry the moss in a single afternoon, could transform the saturated world they know into something ashy and alien. They keep flames small, contained, watched at all times. A Hesm would no more leave a fire untended than they would redirect a water channel without first watching it for a full season.

They fear the rare outsiders who come with iron. Metal has no place here—it rusts in the constant moisture, it tastes wrong in the water, it shapes things that the water hasn't chosen to shape. The few times that traders from the valleys have tried to bring metal tools, the Hesm have simply waited, silent and still, until the visitors grew uncomfortable and left. They do not argue. They do not explain. They simply do not engage, and eventually, the metal goes away.

Most of all—though they would never phrase it this way—they fear meaninglessness. They fear the possibility that all this watching, all this tending, all this soft-spoken careful presence might not matter. That the water might not be learning anything, might not be going anywhere, might simply be falling down stone faces because stone faces are there, because gravity pulls, because water cannot help itself. They do not speak of this fear. They do not need to. It lives in the space behind their patience, the question that their practice holds at bay.

## What They Want

They want nothing you would recognize as desire.

They do not want wealth. They do not want power. They do not want fame, or influence, or love in the desperate sense that makes people chase and clutch and lose themselves. They have what they need: water that speaks to them, stone that holds them, ferns that drink beside them, work that makes sense.

But they do want something.

They want to get the channels right. They want to look at a cliff-face and know, with absolute certainty, where the water wants to go—so they can help it get there, so they can stop being even the slightest obstacle to its intention. They want to be so attuned that the water moves faster when they're nearby, so fluent in its language that they don't need to watch anymore, that they simply *know,* the way you know your own breath.

They want to understand why the plateau weeps. Not the mechanics—they know the mechanics, the saturation, the pressure, the seepage. They want to understand the *why.* What is the water trying to accomplish? Where is it trying to go? The cliffs weep downward, but the water rose first, condensed from air, gathered from mist, collected from every surface. Is it trying to return to something? Is it trying to reach something? They watch. They wait. They believe that if they watch long enough, if they tend faithfully enough, the water will tell them.

And some of them—quietly, privately, only in the deepest hours of the diffuse night—want to see what happens when the stone stops being angry. The plateau is young. The rock faces haven't learned to be round yet. But they will, eventually. The water will wear them down, will soften their edges, will transform the sharp violence of their birth into something gentler. The Hesm want to see that. They want to know what the water will do when it has finished teaching the stone how to surrender.

They will not live long enough. They know this. A human life is nothing against the patience of water and stone. But they want it anyway—want to be part of the process, want to tend the channels that will help the water do its work, want to add their own small moisture to the weeping when they die and become it.

They want, in the end, to belong to something that lasts.

---

## What You See When You Look at Them

If you stay long enough—and you could stay, if you chose—they stop seeming strange.

The way they move becomes the way movement should be: deliberate, considered, in conversation with the ground. The way they speak becomes the way speech should be: rare, meaningful, worth the interruption of silence. The way they work becomes the way work should be: necessary, patient, part of something larger than any single life.

You stop noticing the moisture on their skin. You stop wondering why they don't hurry. You stop waiting for something to happen, because you realize that something *is* happening, constantly—the water is moving, the stone is weeping, the ferns are drinking, the channels are flowing, and all of it means something if you watch long enough to read it.

The child at the cliff edge looks up at you. Their eyes are clear, patient, already older than their years in the way that constant attention ages a person. They look back at the water. They watch it trace its path down the stone. They are learning what the water knows.

You could learn it too. The plateau is waiting. The water is speaking. All you have to do is listen long enough to hear.
