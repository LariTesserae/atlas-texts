# The Tenders

They were not always here. Or perhaps they were always going to be here—the description of the place suggests designers, cultivators, hands that trained bamboo into walls and guided root-mats into floors. Someone planted the first seeds of the Rootworks. Someone shaped the early growth. The question of what happened to them produces a silence more profound than the absence of animals.

But the Rootworks is not empty.

You find the first tender in the smaller chamber, the storage heart where gourds glow with internal light. She is kneeling beside a cluster that has begun to discolor—a patch of gray spreading across the amber glow like mold across fruit. Her hand rests on the surface of the largest gourd, and she is perfectly still. So still that you took her for another stem, another growth, another extension of the vegetable architecture.

She is not young. Age is hard to determine in this light, through this humidity, but her skin carries the marks of decades—creases at the corners of her eyes, lines around her mouth, the weathering of a face that has spent years in an environment hostile to human comfort. Her hair is bound close to her skull, braided with what appear to be thin root-tendrils that have grown into the weave, living extensions that anchor her hair in place. She wears nothing that you would call clothing—only a wrapping of broad, waxy leaves layered over each other and secured with vine-cord, the outermost layers dark with moisture, the inner ones still supple and green. Her feet are bare, and the soles have thickened into something like bark, cracked and calloused into plates that grip the root-mat without slipping.

She does not look up when you approach. Her attention remains fixed on the ailing gourd, her fingers pressing gently against the discolored surface. You realize that she is listening—listening to the fluid movement inside, to the metabolic processes that have begun to fail. Her breathing has slowed to match the rhythm of the chamber: slow, regular, attuned to the cycling thrum of the processing towers in the distance.

*Patience,* her posture says. *There is time. There is always time.*

When she finally acknowledges your presence, it is not with surprise. She turns her head slowly, her movements carrying the deliberate quality of something that does not waste motion. Her eyes adjust to focus on you—they had been adapted for close work, for examining gourd-walls and root-surfaces, and now they must reorient for the distance of another human body. The adjustment takes seconds. She waits for it.

"You have come from above," she says. Her voice is hoarse from disuse, but not weakened. The words emerge carefully, each one considered before release, as if speech is a resource to be conserved. "Few do, anymore."

You ask how long she has been here.

"Long." She turns back to the gourd. "Long enough. The question has less meaning when you stop counting days. Days are an above-ground measure. Here, we count cycles. This gourd is in its fifty-third processing cycle. I have tended it through forty-seven of them."

*We.* The pronoun lingers. You ask if others live in the Rootworks.

She gestures deeper into the chamber, toward the passage that leads further down. "Venn keeps the water-works. He has been here longer than me—he remembers when the upper chambers were still being grown. Mellis tends the fiber-bundles where the light-stems emerge. She was the last to descend from the surface." A pause. "Before you, no one has come for eleven cycles. We do not count years anymore."

You ask if they are the ones who built this place.

"No." The word is simple, final. "We tend what was built. The builders—they are gone. Gone to the surface or gone to the earth. They left the Rootworks in our care, and we have kept it. The system does not need us to function. But it needs us to *continue.*"

She presses her palm more firmly against the gourd. "This one will fail. The discoloration has reached too deep—the nutrient solution has become unbalanced, and the gourd's membrane is too thin to contain the pressure. When it ruptures, the fluid will spill into the collection basin, and the basin will carry it to the processing towers, and the towers will extract what can be extracted. Nothing is wasted here." She rises slowly, her joints stiff from the long kneeling. "But a failed gourd means a gap in the sequence. A gap means less production. Less production means less growth in the upper chambers, where the new stems are being trained. The system compensates, but compensation is not the same as continuation. That is why we are here."

You follow her as she moves through the chamber, checking each gourd-cluster with the same attentive stillness. Her fingers trace the surfaces, feeling for texture changes, density variations, the subtle shifts that indicate health or disease. Occasionally she pauses and makes a small adjustment—repositioning a vine that has begun to choke a gourd's growth, clearing debris from a drainage channel, pruning a fungal patch that has spread beyond its intended zone. The movements are small, precise, unhurried. She does nothing that does not need to be done.

You ask why she stays.

The question produces a pause—not confusion, but consideration. She stops her work and turns to face you fully, and for the first time you see something shift in her expression, a depth of consideration that the surface question had not earned.

"Have you ever tended something?" she asks. "Not owned. Not used. *Tended.* Watched something grow according to its own nature, shaping it only where it needs shaping, guiding it only where it would otherwise fail?" She gestures at the walls, the ceiling, the root-floor beneath your feet. "This place does not require me. But I require it. Above—" She shakes her head slowly. "Above, everything is fast. Everything is loud. The animals move constantly, never still, never satisfied. The people move faster, talk faster, want and need and demand. There is no patience in the surface world. No patience for slow growth. No patience for cycles that take years to complete."

She places her hand against the stem-wall, and the gesture carries the intimacy of touching a friend. "Here, I can be as slow as I need to be. I can tend a single gourd through fifty cycles and know that I have done something that matters. I can watch the roots grow, millimeter by millimeter, and see the architecture taking shape over decades. I can be part of something that does not demand my attention but accepts my care. Does that make sense to you?"

You ask if she ever considers leaving.

"Leaving for where?" She turns back to her work. "The surface has nothing I need. The Rootworks has everything I am."

---

You find Venn in the deeper passages, where the water-works maintain the delicate balance of saturation that keeps the entire system functioning. He is older than the first tender—much older, his skin weathered to the color of the root-bark he tends, his body thin and wiry from decades of living on the minimal nutrition that the Rootworks provides. He moves through the narrow tunnels with a fluidity that suggests he knows every stem, every root, every surface by touch alone. His eyes have adapted to the dim light differently than yours—not sharpening to compensate, but accepting the limitation, developing a different kind of perception.

He hears you before he sees you. "Footsteps," he says, without turning. "Heavy. Uneven. Surface-walker." He continues his work, adjusting a valve formed from a thickened root-joint, redirecting the flow of seep-water through a different channel. "You will disturb the balance if you stay too long. Your breathing adds moisture to air that is already saturated. Your footsteps compress the root-mat faster than it can recover. Your presence is an interruption."

You ask if interruption is unwelcome.

"Unwelcome is not the word." He finishes his adjustment and turns to face you. His eyes are clouded—not blind, but adapted to darkness, the lenses thickened and changed. He sees you as a shape, a presence, a disturbance in the water-pattern that he has memorized over decades. "Interruption is simply interruption. It happens. The question is what the interruption does. If you damage the water-works, the damage propagates. The drainage slows. The saturation increases. The roots begin to rot, and the rot spreads, and the chambers above become unstable. So I ask: what does your interruption do?"

You assure him that you mean no damage.

"Meaning is not doing." He turns and continues down the passage, and you follow because there is nowhere else to go. "Those who built the Rootworks meant no damage. They designed a system that would process surface water into industrial fluids—resins, dyes, medicinal extracts. They planted the first growth-chambers and trained the first stem-walls. They meant to create something useful. But they did not stay to tend what they had built. They returned to the surface and left the Rootworks to continue without them."

You ask if the system needed them.

"Need?" He pauses at a junction where four passages meet, his fingers reading the root-surfaces for information you cannot interpret. "The system functions without tending. The water flows. The processing towers cycle. The growth continues at its margins. But function is not the same as *flourish.* Without tending, the system compensates. It slows. It becomes less than it was designed to be. The gourds produce less fluid. The stems grow more slowly. The roots compact and lose their resilience. The system does not fail—it never fails, because the design is robust—but it diminishes."

He resumes walking. "We do not keep the Rootworks running. We keep it *whole.* There is a difference."

You ask how long he has been here.

"Longer than the others." He speaks without sentiment, as if describing the age of a stone rather than a human life. "I came down when the upper chambers were still being grown. I helped train the stem-pillars that hold the ceiling of the main chamber. I watched the first processing towers reach their full height. I have tended the water-works through—" He pauses, calculating. "Through more cycles than I can count. The counting stopped mattering after the first decade."

You ask if he remembers the surface.

"I remember that it exists." He stops at another valve, adjusts it with practiced precision. "I remember that it is dry and fast and loud. I remember that people up there measure their lives in years instead of cycles. I remember that they want things quickly and discard things slowly. The Rootworks taught me a different way of measuring. A gourd does not rush because you want it to. A root does not grow faster because you are impatient. You adapt to the pace of the system, or you become an interruption."

He turns to face you, and his clouded eyes focus on your shape with unsettling precision. "You are asking questions that have answers, but the answers will not give you what you are looking for. You want to know who lives here. You want to know why we stay. You want to know what the Rootworks *is.* Those are surface questions. They assume that there is a difference between the system and the tenders. Between the place and the people. Between the function and the meaning."

He gestures at the passage around you—the root-walls, the water-channels, the stem-pillars, the fungal glow. "There is no difference. We are the Rootworks. The Rootworks is us. We tend it, and it tends us. We keep it whole, and it keeps us whole. That is not an answer you can carry back to the surface, because the surface does not have words for it. But it is the only answer I have."

---

Mellis tends the fiber-bundles where the light-stems emerge, carrying daylight from the surface to the chambers below. She is the youngest of the three—the last to descend, the one who still counts in years sometimes, when the memories surface. She works in the highest chambers, where the fiber-optic stems pierce the root-mat ceiling and spread their diffused brightness across the upper walls. Here, the light is almost strong enough to see by. Almost.

You find her repairing a damaged stem-bundle—one of the channels that carries daylight from the surface. The fiber has cracked somewhere above, and the light has begun to leak, reducing the illumination in the chambers below. She works with tools that you recognize as manufactured—metal blades, synthetic cord—but they are old, worn, maintained far beyond their expected lifespan. The industry of the surface, repurposed for the tending of the depths.

"Hello," she says, when she notices your presence. Her voice is clearer than the others, less adapted to silence, still carrying the rhythms of surface speech. "You're the visitor. I heard you had come down."

You ask how she knew.

"Venn hears everything. The water-works carry sound through the root-mat. He heard your footsteps in the upper chambers and tracked your progress through the system." She smiles, slightly, and the expression transforms her face—younger than the others, still carrying some trace of the world above. "He complained about the moisture you were adding to the air. He complains about everything, though. It's his way of caring."

You ask if they communicate often.

"When we need to. The Rootworks carries sound well, if you know how to listen. And we cross paths in the main chambers sometimes, during the cycle transitions. We're not separate, exactly—we're all tending the same system. But we have different domains, different rhythms. Venn works in cycles of hours. The first tender works in cycles of days. I work in cycles of months—repairing the light-stems, training the new growth, watching the system expand." She pauses, examining the fiber-stem she has been repairing. "I'm the one who sees the changes most clearly. The growth at the margins. The new chambers forming. The system is still expanding, even now. It's not dead. It's not abandoned. It's *growing.*"

You ask where it grows toward.

"Deeper, mostly. Further into the earth. The water-table is down there, somewhere—the real water, not just seepage from above. The system wants to reach it. It's been reaching for it for decades, maybe centuries. The original designers may have intended it, or it may have learned the intention on its own. The roots are patient. They extend a few centimeters per year, pressing through the substrate, dissolving rock with chemical secretions, creating new passages that the chambers will eventually fill." She tests the repair, and the light-stem brightens slightly. "I help guide the growth where it wants to go. Clear obstacles. Reinforce weak points. The system does the work; I just remove the friction."

You ask if she remembers coming down.

Her hands still. "Yes. I remember."

You wait.

"I was twenty-seven." She resumes working, but her movements are slower now, more careful. "I had been working in the manufactories above—this was when they were still functioning, when there was still industry on the surface that needed what the Rootworks produced. Resins for waterproofing. Dyes for textiles. Medicinal extracts for the healing houses. The Rootworks supplied it all, and the manufactories processed it, and the surface world ran on products that came from below."

She adjusts the fiber-stem, securing it with cord. "I was curious. I wanted to see where the fluids came from. I asked permission to descend, and the tenders at that time—the ones who came before us—they allowed it. They showed me the chambers, explained the system, let me watch the processing cycles. I was fascinated. I had never seen anything like it—this living factory, this vegetable industry, this patience encoded into architecture. I asked if I could stay."

You ask what they said.

"They said: *You can stay, but you must understand what staying means. You will not leave. You will not return to the surface. You will tend the system until you cannot tend anymore, and then you will become part of the system. Your body will feed the roots. Your fluids will join the cycle. You will give everything to the Rootworks, and the Rootworks will give everything to you.*" She finishes the repair and steps back, examining her work. "I understood. I stayed. I have been here ever since."

You ask if she regrets it.

"No." The answer comes immediately, without hesitation. "Never. The surface world is—" She shakes her head. "I was twenty-seven, and I had already learned that the surface does not know how to wait. It does not know how to value slow things. It measures worth in speed, in quantity, in immediate returns. The Rootworks measures worth in patience. In continuation. In giving more than you take. I found my measure here. I found a place where my pace was the right pace."

She turns to face you, and her face is calm, settled, whole. "You are asking all these questions because you are trying to understand. You want to take something back to the surface—an explanation, a meaning, a way to make sense of this place. But you cannot take the Rootworks back with you. You can only carry the memory, and the memory will fade, and eventually you will wonder if you imagined it. That is what happens to everyone who descends. They see the system, they hear the tenders, they feel the weight of the patience, and then they return to the surface, and the surface erodes the memory, and they are left with a feeling they cannot name."

She touches the fiber-stem she has repaired, and it brightens under her fingers. "If you want to understand, you would have to stay. And if you stay, you will never leave. That is the bargain. It has always been the bargain. The Rootworks accepts tenders, not visitors. We are not holding anyone here. We are simply *here.* And if you choose to stay, you will tend, and you will become part of the system, and you will never count years again."

---

The tenders are not the only presence in the Rootworks.

As you ascend back through the chambers, preparing to return to the surface, you notice something you had missed before. The root-walls, the stem-pillars, the gourd-towers—they bear marks that are not natural. Not scars, exactly. Not damage. But *shapes.* Patterns pressed into the bark, grown into the stem-surfaces, repeated across the architecture.

You stop to examine one of them. It is a hand-print. A human hand, pressed into the surface of a stem-pillar, the fingers slightly spread, the palm firm against the vegetable surface. The bark has grown around it, preserving the shape, creating a permanent impression in the living tissue.

And then you see another. And another. Hand-prints on root-walls, on gourd-surfaces, on the undersides of platforms. Different sizes—some large, some small. Different depths—some fresh, some nearly grown over. A gallery of impressions, scattered throughout the Rootworks like signatures on a guest book that spans decades.

You ask the first tender, when you find her again, what the hand-prints mean.

She is checking a processing tower in the main chamber, and she pauses when you point to one of the marks—a small hand, child-sized, pressed into the bark near the tower's base.

"Those are the ones who stayed," she says. "Not the tenders. The others."

You ask who the others were.

"Workers. Visitors. Seekers. The ones who descended and did not return." She touches the hand-print gently, her fingers tracing the outline. "When you stay in the Rootworks, you leave your mark. It is tradition—a way of saying *I was here. I tended. I became part of the system.* The bark grows around the impression, preserving it. Some of these are older than me. Some are older than Venn. The system remembers everyone who gave themselves to it."

You ask how many marks there are.

"Hundreds. Perhaps thousands. We have never counted. The system extends deeper than we have explored—there are chambers we have never entered, passages that lead to places we have not seen. The hand-prints go all the way down. They are part of the architecture now. Part of the memory of the Rootworks." She withdraws her hand. "When I die, my body will be placed in a growth-chamber, and the roots will absorb what I was. But my hand-print will already be here. Already preserved. Already part of the system."

You ask if she has made her mark.

"Not yet." She smiles, slightly. "There is time. There is always time. I will make it when I am ready. When I feel that I have finished what I came to do. When the system has accepted me as fully as I have accepted it."

She gestures toward the passage that leads back to the surface. "You should go. The system does not need you to tend it, but it does not need you to interrupt it either. You have seen what there is to see. You have asked the questions that can be answered. What you carry back to the surface is your own concern."

You ask if she thinks you will forget.

"Everyone forgets. That is what the surface does—it makes you forget the depths. You will remember for a while, and then you will remember less, and then you will remember only a feeling, and then you will wonder if you ever descended at all." She turns back to her work, pressing her palm against the processing tower, listening to the fluid movement inside. "But the Rootworks will remember you. The moisture you added to the air, the pressure of your footsteps on the root-mat, the sound of your voice asking questions—the system records everything. Not in words or images, but in the way that plants remember: through growth, through response, through slow adaptation to the presence that was there and then was gone."

Her voice follows you as you begin the ascent. "If you ever want to stay, the Rootworks will accept you. It accepts everyone who is willing to give everything. That is the bargain. It has always been the bargain. Return to the surface, and forget, and wonder—or stay, and tend, and become part of something that does not forget."

---

The Rootworks of Lower Vell has no animal life. The tenders are not animals anymore, not really. They have adapted to the environment, slowed to its rhythm, given themselves to its patience. They eat what the system provides—gourd-flesh, nutrient concentrates extracted from the processing towers, occasional supplements from fungal colonies that grow in the wettest chambers. They drink the seep-water, filtered through root-mats and purified by specialized plants bred for that purpose. They sleep on platform-beds woven from flexible stems, cushioned by moss-like growths that have learned to accept human warmth.

They do not speak much. They do not need to. The system communicates through its cycles, and the tenders have learned to read those cycles like a surface-dweller reads a clock. They know where they are in the processing sequence, how long until the next drainage adjustment, when the light-stems will brighten or dim with the surface day. They move through the chambers like extensions of the architecture—hands that the system has grown to tend itself.

And when they die, they do not leave. Their bodies are placed in chambers where the roots grow thick and hungry, and the Rootworks absorbs what they were. Their fluids join the processing cycles. Their matter becomes new growth. They become part of the system they tended, feeding the architecture that fed them.

This is not death, in the way the surface understands death. This is continuation. This is the bargain.

The Rootworks tends to itself. And the tenders are how it does so.
