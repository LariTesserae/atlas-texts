# What Lives Here

They are small. That is the first thing to understand, and it matters more than you might think, because the entire constructed landscape is built to their scale, not yours. A channel that you could span with your thumb is, for them, a stream wide enough to warrant attention. A basin the size of your cupped palm is a pool. The walls that rise between your ankles and your knees are walls indeed — chest-high, sometimes higher, dividing their world into rooms and corridors and open courtyards that you would walk across without noticing, stepping over what is, to them, architecture.

They are not human. Or if they were once — if something in their lineage branched from the same trunk that produced you — the divergence was long ago, so long that the connection is more theoretical than visible. They are the size of your middle finger, perhaps a little larger, and their bodies are built close to the ground: low, wide, stable. Four limbs that move with a deliberation that suggests each step is considered, though whether this is caution or simply their nature is hard to say. Their skin — if it is skin — has the wet sheen of the stone itself, a pale grey-green that shifts toward translucence along their undersides, so that when they move across a wet surface you can see the dark tracery of something beneath — veins, perhaps, or something analogous, a network of fine lines that mirrors the network of channels carved into the plateau, as though their bodies were made in the image of their home.

They are many. The plateau does not belong to a single creature, a single mind. There are hundreds — perhaps thousands, if you count the smallest ones, the ones that live in the narrowest channels and rarely emerge into the open basins. They move through the waterworks the way blood moves through a body: constantly, quietly, each one following a path that seems individual but that, seen from above, from the high ground where the natural rock gives way to the constructed surface, forms part of a larger pattern too slow and too complex to grasp in a single moment of watching.

---

They tend the water. This is not quite right — the water does not need tending, exactly. It flows because it condenses, because it collects, because gravity draws it downward through the carved channels the way gravity draws everything downward. The water would flow whether or not they were here. But they are here, and what they do — what occupies their days, their nights, their long, slow lives — is maintain the channels.

You would not think this would be necessary. The stone is hard. The channels are carved deep, their walls smooth, their curves precise. Stone does not erode quickly. But water is patient, and this water has been flowing for what must be an extraordinarily long time, and over that time the channels shift — not much, not visibly to a visitor, but enough. A basin develops a lip that was not there before. A channel narrows where mineral deposits accumulate. A curve tightens where the water has worn the outer wall. And when a channel narrows, the water slows. When the water slows, it deposits more mineral. When it deposits more mineral, it narrows further. The process accelerates. Without intervention, the waterworks would clog, then fail, the water finding new paths, uncontrolled, destructive to the pattern.

So they work.

They work with tools that are, if anything, simpler than the ones that made the plateau. A piece of stone, chosen for its hardness and its edge. A bit of bone, perhaps, from something that once lived here and no longer does. Their hands — and they do have hands, of a sort, though the digits are more numerous than yours and far more flexible, each one narrow and precise — their hands hold these tools and they chip and scrape and grind at the stone with a patience that makes the original construction seem hasty by comparison. They work in the channels. They work in the basins. They work underwater, in those places where the film is thin enough that they can lie in it and breathe — and they do breathe, though whether they breathe air or water or both is a question that the evidence does not answer clearly, because they seem equally comfortable in shallow pools and on wet, exposed stone.

One of them will spend a day — and their days are long, longer than yours, their metabolism slow in the way that life in cold water tends to be slow — working a single section of channel no longer than your forearm. Chipping away a deposit. Refining a curve. Widening a constriction by a millimeter, perhaps two. The work is so fine, so incremental, that you could watch for an hour and not see progress. But they are not watching the way you watch. They are not thinking in hours. They are thinking in seasons, in years, in the long, slow rhythm of water on stone, and in that rhythm a millimeter a day is not slow. It is precise.

---

There are different kinds of them. This becomes clear only after watching for some time, because the differences are subtle — variations in size, in coloration, in the shape of the head and the configuration of the hands, that at first seem like individual variation but that, as you observe more carefully, resolve into something more structured.

The largest are the ones who work the channels. They are the builders, the maintainers, the ones whose labor keeps the water moving. Their bodies are dense, their limbs thick, their skin rougher, almost textured, as though they have been abraded by years of pressing against stone. They move with a methodical certainty, each step placed, each tool-stroke deliberate, and they seem to possess a knowledge of the waterworks that is not learned but inherited, a map of every channel and basin and curve carried in their bodies the way you carry the map of your own home — not as information but as orientation, a sense of where you are that does not require thought.

Smaller ones — much smaller, half the size, delicate, with limbs that seem almost vestigial in their thinness — live in the highest channels, the narrowest runnels at the tops of the walls, where the water first condenses and begins its downward journey. They do not carry tools. They do not work the stone. What they do is harder to name. They seem to *read* the water — crouching at the lip of a channel, their heads — if they are heads — tilted down, their bodies still, for long stretches of time, as though they are listening to something the water is telling them. Whether they are monitoring the flow, or tasting it for some quality invisible to you, or simply existing in proximity to the source, you cannot tell. But they are always there, in the highest channels, and when the flow changes — when condensation is heavier, or lighter, or arrives from a different direction — they move. They reposition. And shortly after, the larger workers below shift their patterns too, as though they have received some signal.

And there are others, still different, that live in the basins.

The basins are the slow places. Water fills them over the course of hours, sometimes days, and the stillness there is different from the stillness of the channels — deeper, more complete, a pause in the system where the water collects itself before continuing. The basin-dwellers are the most translucent of all, their bodies so clear in places that you can see the stone through them, as though they have taken the quality of the water into their own substance. They do not work. They do not read. They sit in the basins, in the still water, and they are — and this is the word that keeps returning, the word that does not quite fit but that nothing else fits better — they are *listening*.

Not to sound. The sound of the waterworks is what it has always been — drip, whisper, hum, the patient accumulation of small noises that never become noise. What they listen to is something else. Something you cannot hear and that perhaps they cannot hear either, not in the way that hearing works, but that they sense, or attend to, with a concentration so complete that it has the quality of devotion.

What they are listening to, if it is anything, is the pattern.

---

Because the pattern is the thing. The pattern is what the waterworks is *for* — not to filter, not to store, not to power. The pattern is the purpose. The water moves through the channels and basins in a sequence so complex that no single mind could hold it, and the pattern that the water traces — the spirals, the convergences, the geometries that mirror and repeat and vary — is, for them, something that requires maintaining not because it does anything but because it is. The way a song does not *do* anything. The way a prayer does not *do* anything. The pattern exists because it is beautiful, or because it is true, or because it is what they have, and they have had it for so long that maintaining it is not a choice but a nature, the way the water's nature is to flow downhill, the way the light's nature is to scatter off wet surfaces and fill the air with brightness.

They are the pattern's custodians. That is perhaps the truest description. Not its creators — the creators are dead, have been dead for longer than the concept of *long* can carry, dead in the way that the original bedrock is dead, reduced to memory and absence. The makers carved this place with stone tools and bare hands and a vision that spanned a kilometer in every direction, and the makers are gone, and what remains is the pattern and the custodians, who are not the makers' children, not exactly, but the makers' continuation — the part of the project that had to stay alive in order for the project to stay whole.

---

They do not speak. Or if they do, it is not in a way that you would recognize as speech — no calls, no clicks, no vibrational signals that your body can detect. They communicate, clearly — the way the channel-readers in the high runnels signal the workers below, the way the basin-dwellers seem to share some understanding that coordinates the labor across the entire plateau — but the medium is not sound and not light and not any chemical you can identify. It may be the water itself. They live so completely within the system, their bodies so saturated with the same water that flows through the channels, that a change in one part of the system — a shift in flow, a new mineral content, a temperature fluctuation of a single degree — might carry information the way a current carries a leaf: passively, undirected, but arriving nonetheless. They are, in this sense, part of the waterworks. Not inhabitants of it. *Components* of it. Nodes in a network that includes stone and water and light and the slow, patient labor of maintenance, all of it connected, all of it flowing, all of it — if you watch long enough, if you let your own thinking slow to the pace of water in a channel — alive.

---

And they know you are there. This is the last thing, and perhaps the most important, because it rewrites the experience of standing at the lip of the plateau, looking down at the constructed landscape, the bright wet stone, the creeping films of water, the small grey-green shapes moving through the channels with their tiny tools. They know you are there. They have known since you arrived, perhaps since before — perhaps the vibration of your footsteps on the natural rock transmitted through the stone into the waterworks, a disturbance in the pattern that they registered the way you would register a voice calling your name. They have not reacted. They have not changed their behavior. They have not looked up, because they do not look up — their world is the world of the channels, the basins, the wet stone, and the sky is not a thing that concerns them. But they have adjusted. Subtly. A worker who was about to chip a deposit has paused, and will pause for as long as you stand there, because the vibration of your presence has introduced a variable into the water that they cannot predict, and they do not work when they cannot predict the water. A basin-dweller has shifted position by a quarter of a body-length, orienting toward the edge of the plateau where you stand, not in alarm but in *attention*, the way you might turn slightly toward a sound in another room — not afraid, not investigating, simply aware.

They are waiting for you to leave. Not with hostility. Not with fear. With patience — the same patience with which they work the stone, the same patience with which the water fills the basins and overflows and finds the next channel and continues. They have waited before. Visitors come, sometimes — not often, not in any span of time that matters to you, but in the long count of the waterworks, there have been others. Creatures of the low places, the dry places, who climb to the altitude and stand at the lip and look down and try to understand. They stand for a while. They leave. The pattern continues. The water flows. The custodians return to their work, and the disturbance in the water smooths itself out, and the channels run clear, and the light — the thin, exposed, too-real light of altitude — continues to arrive everywhere at once, bouncing off wet surfaces, filling the air with a brightness that has no source and no shadow, only bright, and brighter, and the small, patient movements of the ones who live here, who have always lived here, who will live here long after the pattern has worn them smooth and new ones have come to take their place, chipping and grinding and *listening*, in the slow, bright, wet, ancient quiet of the plateau.
