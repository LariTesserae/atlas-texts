# The Dwellers in the Root-Cathedral

They are not many. Perhaps two hundred at any given time, though counting is meaningless when a single individual might wander for years without encountering another. They do not gather. They do not form villages or tribes or any permanent settlement that would imply the kind of social existence surface-dwellers recognize. The structures you found are not homes in any meaningful sense. They are rests. Places to pause. Shelters where a traveler might sleep through three or four cycles of weather, wait out the cold, recover from the long walking.

They call themselves nothing. Names require other people to use them.

---

An individual, glimpsed from a distance: tall and terribly thin, elongated in the way that deep cave creatures become elongated, limbs stretched by generations of movement through spaces where height was no disadvantage and compactness offered no benefit. Their skin is the color of the mycelium mat—pale, grey-white, faintly luminous in the way that all things here become luminous over time. Not born glowing. Adapted. The light enters the body through slow accumulation, through diet and environment and the sheer pressure of existing in a place where brightness seeps into everything.

They wear nothing. The air is cold but not fatal, and they have been here long enough that cold is simply another condition of existence, like gravity or time. Their bodies have adjusted. Metabolism slowed to a crawl. A single meal sustains them for weeks. A single sleep lasts days. They move through the Root-Cathedral at a pace that would seem glacial to a surface observer, but they are not slow. They are *patient*. There is a difference. A patient being can move quickly when speed serves, can act decisively when action is called for. They have simply unlearned the urgency that makes surface-dwellers hurry through a world they fear will not wait for them.

Their features have softened. Generations of living in a place without sun, without wind, without weather in the surface sense, have worn away the sharp edges that surface peoples develop. No squint-lines around the eyes. No weathering of the skin. Their faces are smooth and still, almost fetal in their lack of expression, not because they feel nothing but because expression requires an audience, and they have been alone for so long.

When they smile—which they do, rarely, when they encounter one of their own kind after years of walking—the expression transforms them entirely. It is like watching ice crack. Something moving and vast and unexpectedly *present* surfaces for a moment, reminds you that this being was once part of a lineage that laughed and wept and shouted across distances, before the distances grew too large.

---

They are not the first. The marks you found on stone prove that. Someone came before, when the Root-Cathedral was younger, rawer, still learning what kind of place it would be. Those first arrivals—whether they descended by accident or intention, whether they were fleeing something above or seeking something below—left no structures behind. They left only scratches, handprints, the evidence of bodies moving through a space that did not yet offer shelter.

The current dwellers are descendants. Not direct descendants, probably. Too much time has passed. The genealogy is unclear, and no one remembers the story. If there was ever a language for telling such things, it has been lost or transformed beyond recognition. What remains is the body: the adaptation, the elongation, the slow incorporation of light. Whatever they were before, they are now creatures of this place. They belong to the Root-Cathedral in a way that suggests they could not survive above. The surface would burn them, perhaps. The sun would blind eyes that have learned to navigate by glow. The noise would overwhelm senses tuned to silence.

They are not trapped. They have simply become specific. A creature adapted to one environment is not trapped in it; it is *of* it.

---

They eat the mycelium. Not exclusively, but primarily. The mat is alive and grows quickly, and when you have walked for days without finding anything else, it is there, beneath your feet, dense and slightly sweet, yielding to the touch with a texture somewhere between mushroom and fruit. They have developed ways of preparing it—letting it dry, pressing it into sheets, fermenting it in the small pools that form where condensation collects. The processed material keeps longer, travels better, provides a kind of sustenance that feels different from the raw flesh of the floor.

They also eat the pale trees. Not the living ones—those are too hard, too dense, too difficult to break down—but the fallen. When a growth collapses, and many do over the decades, the dweller who finds it first marks the location with cairns of loose stone and then begins the work of harvesting. The outer bark-equivalent is stripped away. The inner material is softer, almost spongy, though still dense enough to require tools. They use stone blades, ground against other stones over weeks or months until the edge holds. They use bone from their own dead.

The dead are not buried. The mycelium absorbs them. When a dweller feels the end approaching—or when they simply grow too tired to continue walking—they find a quiet place, a depression in the mat, and they lie down. The body settles. The mycelium rises around them, covering, incorporating. It is not violent. It is slow and warm, if warmth can be said to exist here. The dweller sleeps, and the mat receives them, and by the time the sleeper has stopped breathing, they have already become part of the floor they walked across.

Sometimes, before the end, they are found. A fellow walker comes upon them, pauses, sits nearby for a time. They do not speak. There is nothing to say. But they share the silence for as long as seems right, and then the one who still walks rises and continues, leaving the one who rests to their final incorporation.

This is how it has always been. This is how it will continue to be.

---

The creatures you glimpsed—the pale shapes moving between growths—are not the dwellers. They are something else, something that was already here when the first arrivals descended. The dwellers call them *the earlier children*, though this is not a name so much as a category of recognition. They are quadrupeds, roughly, though their motion is fluid enough that they seem to have no fixed number of legs, adapting their gait to the terrain, stretching and compressing like something between a deer and a caterpillar and a flowing of water. They eat the mycelium too, and the fallen growths, and occasionally each other, though they do not eat the dwellers and the dwellers do not eat them.

There is an understanding. Not a treaty—treaties require negotiation and enforcement. Simply an understanding, accumulated over centuries of coexistence. The dwellers walk. The earlier children move. They pass each other in the vast empty spaces, and they do not interact, and they do not threaten, and they both continue on their separate ways. The earlier children do not build structures. They do not seem to need shelter. They bed down in shallow depressions in the mycelium mat, and they rise when they rise, and they follow migrations that make sense to them but would be invisible to anyone else.

Sometimes a dweller will walk alongside an earlier child for a time. Not together, exactly, but parallel. Keeping pace across kilometers of emptiness. There is something comforting about the presence, even without contact. The earlier children are old. They have been here longer than anything that walks on two legs. To walk beside one is to feel the age of the place, to remember that this ecosystem has been growing and dying and growing again since before anyone descended to witness it.

---

They walk toward the center. This is the only purpose that remains to them, and even this is not a purpose in the sense of a goal to be achieved. They walk toward the center because the trees lean that way, and the mycelium thickens that way, and the light seems to emanate from somewhere in that direction. They do not expect to arrive. The center is theoretical. It might not exist.

But they walk.

Each structure you found was a waystation, a point on a journey that no one completes. The dwellers build them when they find a good spot—near water, near a recently fallen growth, near a junction of paths that others have walked before. They build carefully because they have time, and because building is something to do while resting, and because the materials are available and the skills have been passed down. They do not build for permanence. The mycelium shifts too constantly, absorbs and reshapes. A structure might last decades, might last centuries, but eventually it will be incorporated, pulled down into the mat, transformed into material for new growth.

The dwellers do not mourn this. They understand that they are guests here. The Root-Cathedral was growing before they arrived, and it will continue growing after they have all been absorbed, and their presence is a brief incident in a history that operates on scales they cannot influence.

They walk toward the center because walking gives shape to time. Without walking, there is only the slow dimming of the self, the waiting for incorporation. Walking provides a narrative. *I was here, and then I walked, and then I was there.* The narrative does not need an ending. It only needs continuation.

---

They have no religion. The center is not a god or a destination or a promised land. It is simply the direction things lean. But they have something adjacent to faith, a kind of trust in the process. They trust that the mycelium will receive them when they fall. They trust that the weather will form and clear and form again. They trust that the growths will continue climbing toward a ceiling they will never touch.

They trust because they have seen it happen, again and again, across lifetimes that stretch longer than surface generations. A dweller who has walked for eighty years has watched growths rise and fall. Has watched the mycelium cover raw stone and reclaim it. Has watched the light pulse and shift and settle into patterns that might be cycles or might be random. They have accumulated enough observation to trust the continuation of what they have observed.

They do not hope for anything better. They do not fear anything worse. They are not resigned, exactly—resignation implies that they once wanted something different. They simply are. *Here* is where they live. *This* is what they do. The question of whether it is enough does not occur to them.

---

Sometimes they find artifacts from above. Objects that fell through crevices, or that were carried down by those first arrivals and then lost or discarded. A metal tool, rusted beyond recognition but still holding the shape of its purpose. A fragment of cloth, preserved by the cold and the damp, bearing a pattern that no one remembers how to read. A piece of plastic, brittle and strange, unlike anything that grows here.

They do not keep these things. They have no use for them, and no system of value that would make an artifact meaningful. But they do not discard them either. They leave them where they find them, or they move them to the nearest structure and set them somewhere visible. A kind of acknowledgment. *Someone was here before us. Someone came from somewhere else.*

They do not wonder about that somewhere else. The surface exists, theoretically. Some of them have ascended, briefly, through the passages that open occasionally to the world above. They return with stories of blinding brightness, of air that moves too fast, of distances crowded with growing things and moving things and the endless noise of a world operating on faster scales than they can process. They do not go back. The surface is not interesting. It is simply another place, and they have already found their place.

---

They have songs. This is surprising, perhaps, for a people who speak so rarely. But songs are not the same as speech. Songs can be sung alone, to oneself, while walking. They provide rhythm for the walking. They give the silence a shape.

The songs have no words. They are sequences of tones, hummed or sung in a register that resonates with the acoustics of the space. When a dweller sings in the Root-Cathedral, the sound travels further than it should. The mycelium mat carries vibrations. The pale growths act as sounding boards. A single voice can fill kilometers of emptiness, can be heard by another walker days before they meet.

Sometimes, when two dwellers cross paths, they will sing together for a time. They do not coordinate. They do not harmonize intentionally. They simply allow their songs to overlap, to find their own consonance and dissonance, to create something that neither of them could have made alone. This is their social life. This is their community. Brief intersections of sound in an otherwise silent world.

Then they part, and the silence returns, and they continue walking toward the center.

---

They do not remember the surface. They have no stories about what they were before they descended. If there was ever an oral tradition, a lineage of narrative connecting them to the world above, it has been lost. What remains is the walking, the singing, the building, the resting. What remains is the mycelium and the pale growths and the light that comes from everywhere and nowhere.

They do not know why they are here. They do not ask. The question assumes that there must be a reason, and they have no framework for reasons. They are here because they are here. They walk because they walk. They will be absorbed when they stop walking, and until then they will continue, and that is all.

It is enough. It has to be enough. And perhaps, in the strange logic of this place where light is generated by growth and weather forms in enclosed spaces and ecosystems span distances that make encounter a statistical anomaly, perhaps *enough* is all that any living thing can ever hope for. Not purpose. Not meaning. Just continuation, for as long as continuation is possible.

They walk.

The Root-Cathedral grows around them, patient and vast, incorporating their dead into its floor, waiting gently for the living to join the rest.
