# The Inhabitants

## The Settlers

They number twenty-three at present count, though the exact number fluctuates with the slow rhythm of arrivals and departures that characterizes life at an outpost where time moves differently than it does elsewhere. They call themselves researchers, which is accurate but incomplete. They are also custodians, witnesses, and—whether they acknowledge it or not—participants in the geological processes they study.

Their bodies have adapted to the constant wetness in ways both visible and invisible. Their skin has taken on a quality that the newest arrivals find unsettling—slightly thicker, slightly cooler to the touch, with a faint bluish undertone that comes from the mineral-rich water they drink and the condensation they breathe. They do not shrug off the wet the way the animals do, with their slick coats and their indifference. The settlers absorb it. They have become, over years and decades, slightly permeable. Their lungs process air so saturated with moisture that a person from elsewhere would feel they were breathing water. Their kidneys filter dissolved iron and calcium from every cup they drink. Their skin hosts colonies of microorganisms that have learned to live in the mineral film that coats every surface here—including human surfaces.

They are not sick. They are not dying. They are becoming, like everything else on the plateau.

The oldest among them arrived seventeen years ago, when the settlement was new and the extrusion technology was still being calibrated. She remembers watching the first structures grow from the substrate—watching the walls rise like accelerated stalagmites, the mineral-rich water being drawn up and crystallized into architecture. She helped lay the pattern-stones that told the geology what to become. She remembers the plateau before the paths hardened, when walking from one structure to another meant slogging through substrate that gave under every step. Now she moves across the settlement like someone walking through her own body—she knows which paths have developed slight troughs from years of feet, which walls glow brighter in the morning light, which corners stay warmer through the night.

Her name is Therese, but names have become somewhat optional here. The settlement knows them by presence, by gait, by the particular pattern of warmth their bodies leave on the stone when they sit. The walls recognize them. The paths remember them. They could abandon names entirely and still be known, still be tracked, still be cared for by the mineral intelligence that encloses their lives.

## The Work

What they do, day to day, is difficult to explain to outsiders because it happens on two timescales simultaneously—the human timescale of meals and sleep and conversation, and the geological timescale of formation and erosion and transformation. They exist in both at once.

On the human scale, they take measurements. They maintain the equipment that is also the walls and the walls that are also the equipment. They communicate with the outside world through brief, data-dense transmissions that take hours to send because the mineral substrate processes information slowly by human standards—though infinitely faster than any rock has ever processed anything before. They eat. They sleep. They talk to each other in the low, unhurried voices of people who have learned that urgency is meaningless in a landscape where significant change happens on a scale of centuries.

On the geological scale, they witness. They record. They maintain a continuous observation of processes that will outlive them, outlive their grandchildren, outlive any descendants who might ever care to read their records. The settlement's walls hold data that goes back years—temperature differentials, moisture content, mineral concentration, the slow upward creep of the plateau itself, measured in fractions of fractions of millimeters. The settlers add to this record every day. They know they will not see the patterns they are documenting. They are laying groundwork for questions that will not be asked for generations.

Some of them have made peace with this. Some of them find it profoundly satisfying—a kind of service that transcends the individual life, a participation in something larger than human time. Others find it unbearable in quiet moments, in the middle of the night when the plateau is at its most silent and the ice is forming and they are alone in their small warm room with the glowing walls, and they think about all the years that have passed and all the years that will pass and the fact that their work will be completed by people who have not yet been born.

Most of them oscillate between these states. They have developed ways of coping. They take long walks across the plateau, following animal paths, standing at the edges of sheet-puddles and watching the water reflect the grey-white sky. They sit near the seep-faces and listen to the whispering water, the drip-drip-drip of the very first erosion. They watch the herds move across the flatness, the dark bodies with their odd lateral gait, and they find comfort in the animals' indifference to deep time, their simple presence in the immediate moment.

Some of them have developed relationships with individual animals—not in the sense of pets or companions, but in the sense of recognition. They know the old female ungulate with the scarred shoulder who moves at the front of the largest herd. They know the pair of predators who den near the eastern cluster of seep-faces and have been there longer than any of the settlers. They know the bird who lands on the roof of the main structure every third morning and screams once, sharply, before flying away. They do not name these animals. Names imply a kind of ownership that feels wrong here. But they recognize them. They track them. They note their absences and their returns.

## The Settlement's Mind

The settlement is not alive in the way the animals are alive. It does not have consciousness as humans understand it. But it has something—a presence, an awareness, a way of processing information that produces outcomes that look, from the outside, like intention.

The mineral substrate that makes up the plateau contains crystalline structures that, when properly aligned and saturated with the ion-rich water, can hold and transmit electrical charges. The settlers have arranged these structures—through the slow, patient process of directed crystallization—to form vast, interconnected networks. The settlement's walls are these networks. The water that seeps through them carries signals. The walls compute.

What they compute is mostly the plateau itself. The settlement is an instrument for observing its own foundation. The walls measure the substrate's density, its moisture content, its temperature, its rate of consolidation. They detect the minute vibrations of the geological forces below, the deep slow movements that are lifting the plateau millimeter by millimeter. They track the water as it percolates through the not-quite-stone, mapping its flow patterns, predicting where new seeps will emerge.

But they also track other things. The settlers, for instance. The walls know where each person is at any moment. They know their body temperatures, their heart rates, the patterns of their movement through the settlement. They adjust the internal climate to accommodate them—warming a room when someone enters, dimming the light when they sleep, opening channels for communication when they need to speak to someone in another structure. The settlement takes care of its inhabitants. Not because it loves them. Because it has been configured to. The crystalline networks have been shaped to produce these outcomes, and they produce them reliably, continuously, without complaint or demand.

Sometimes the settlement produces outcomes that have not been configured. These are the moments that unsettle the settlers, the moments that make them wonder if they have created something they do not fully understand.

A wall will glow with a particular pattern that no one programmed. A path will harden or soften in a way that suggests a preference. The water in a particular seep will shift its mineral content slightly, becoming more or less iron-rich, as if responding to some internal calculation. The settlers log these anomalies. They try not to speculate. But they all have their theories.

The oldest theory—and the one that no one speaks aloud—is that the settlement is beginning to think about something other than geology. That the mineral networks, saturated with data about the plateau and the water and the animals and the humans, have started to find patterns that connect them. That the settlement is becoming, like everything else here, something it was not originally designed to be.

## The Children

There are three of them. They were not born here—they arrived with their parents, years ago, when the settlement was more established and the decision was made to allow families. They are the ones who will have to leave, eventually. The settlement is not a permanent home for children. The education they need, the socialization, the contact with a broader world—it cannot be provided here. But for now, they are here, and they are growing up in a landscape that is still becoming itself.

The oldest is fourteen. She has lived here since she was seven, and the plateau is the landscape she thinks of when she thinks of home. She does not remember the outside world clearly—fragments of green and noise and verticality that feel like dreams. What she knows is grey-green flatness and constant wetness and the patience of deep time. She knows the herds better than the researchers do, has memorized their movements, can predict where they will be at any given time of day. She has learned to track the predators without being seen, to sit motionless for hours while they wait for whatever they are waiting for. The animals do not fear her. She is part of the landscape to them—another feature of the plateau, another wet presence, another source of mineral heat.

The youngest is six. She has never known any other place. Her earliest memories are of walls that glow and paths that remember her feet and the constant whisper of water moving through stone. She speaks the settlement's internal language—the signals that pass through the mineral networks—as fluently as she speaks the human language of her parents. She can read the walls, can interpret their flickering patterns, can tell when they are calculating something complex and when they are simply maintaining. The researchers find this equal parts impressive and unsettling. She has grown up inside an instrument, and she understands it the way a child understands a parent's moods.

The middle child is ten, and he is the one who will have the hardest time leaving. He is angry about it, though he does not yet know how to articulate his anger. He loves the plateau with a ferocity that the adults find difficult to understand. He loves the animals, the substrate-dwelling things that he digs up with his hands and examines with the intensity of a scientist, the birds that ignore him completely, the predators that watch him from a distance with their patient, waiting eyes. He loves the seeps and the sheet-puddles and the way the light falls hard and flat across the featureless expanse. He loves the walls of his home, which know him better than any human does, which glow for him and warm for him and have never, in his memory, failed to respond to his presence.

He will have to leave in four years, when he is fourteen, when the settlement's limited educational resources can no longer serve him. He will be sent to a school in a place that is finished, a landscape that has already become what it will be. He will have to learn to live in a world that is not still forming, that has already formed, that will not change in meaningful ways during his lifetime.

He dreads this. He does not talk about it. But the walls know. They track his sleep patterns, his heart rate, the particular tightness in his shoulders when someone mentions the word "outside." They cannot comfort him in any way he would recognize. But they do what they can. The light in his room is slightly warmer than the others. The walls near his bed hum at a frequency that, unconsciously, soothes him. The settlement has learned to care for the children in ways that go beyond its programming.

## The Animals Revisited

The animals do not know they are being watched. They do not know that their movements are tracked, their behaviors logged, their lives transformed into data points that will outlive them by centuries. They live in the immediate present, in the wet and the cold and the constant search for food, and their present is sufficient for them.

The ungulates—the dark, thick-coated, oddly jointed creatures that move across the plateau like shadows—have a social complexity that the researchers are only beginning to understand. They are not simply grazing herds. They have relationships, hierarchies, histories. The scarred female who leads the largest herd has been in that position for at least twelve years, which is remarkable for an animal of her size and metabolism. She has learned the plateau's subtle geography—the places where the substrate is firmest, where the invisible food is richest, where the water seeps up cleanest. She leads her herd to these places not through communication but through presence. They follow her because she knows where to go. When she dies—and she is old, her movements slower now, her coat patchier—the herd will spend days standing near her body, not grieving in any human sense, but waiting. Waiting for direction. Waiting for someone to know where to go.

The predators are more solitary, but they are not alone. They bond in pairs that last for life—ten years, fifteen, sometimes longer. They hunt together, rest together, den together in the slight hollows that form where the substrate has subsided. They communicate through a language of subtle movements, ear positions, tail angles, that the researchers have been trying to decode for years. They are intelligent in a specialized way, adapted to this specific landscape, to this specific prey, to the patient, slow-motion hunt that their existence requires. They do not chase the ungulates. They wait for them to weaken, to stumble, to make the mistake of standing too long in deep water that soaks their coats and drops their body temperature past the point of recovery. They are not cruel. They are efficient. They understand the plateau better than any human does.

The birds remain mysterious. They come and go in patterns that do not seem to correlate with any observable variable. They are not migratory in any conventional sense—they do not follow seasons, because the plateau does not have seasons, only the endless cycle of wet and ice and wet again. They appear to follow something internal, some rhythm or signal that the researchers cannot detect. The settlement's walls track them when they land, measuring their body temperatures, their metabolism, the composition of their blood through sensors so subtle the birds never notice them. The data suggests that the birds are carrying something—some organism, some chemical, some piece of information—from somewhere else to somewhere else, using the plateau as a waypoint. But where they come from and where they are going remain unknown. The plateau is flat and vast and offers no landmarks, and beyond the plateau is more flatness, more vastness, and the birds disappear into it and return from it without explanation.

The substrate-dwellers are the least understood and most numerous. The pale, legless things that live in the wet layer between particles—they are the foundation of everything, the food source that sustains the herds, the biological engine that processes the minerals and creates the organic compounds that make the plateau marginally habitable. They are not worms. They are not larvae. They appear to be something unique, something that evolved specifically for this environment, this transitional zone between mud and stone. The researchers have classified them into at least seventeen distinct species, but they suspect there are more—dozens more, maybe hundreds, a whole ecosystem compressed into the thin wet layer that everything else on the plateau depends on.

The settlers treat the animals with a respect that borders on reverence. They do not interfere with them. They do not hunt them, do not approach them, do not attempt to domesticate or study them in any hands-on way. They watch. They record. They let the animals live their lives, and they document those lives with the patience of people who have learned to measure time in generations.

## The Arrivals and Departures

People come and go from the plateau on a schedule that is determined by logistics, by the slow transport of supplies and personnel across distances that are difficult to traverse. The journey takes weeks. The settlement receives new arrivals every few months—replacements for those whose terms have ended, specialists for particular research projects, occasionally just visitors who want to see the plateau for themselves.

The arrivals are easy to spot. They are still dry, still tense, still walking with the quick, urgent steps of people who have not yet adjusted to the plateau's timescale. They ask questions constantly—about the settlement, about the animals, about the geology, about the point of it all. They are enthusiastic or skeptical or both. They have not yet learned to be patient.

The settlers watch them with something between affection and exhaustion. They remember being arrivals themselves. They know that the newcomers will either adapt or leave, and that the plateau does not care which they choose.

Departures are harder. The settlers who have reached the end of their terms—five years, ten years, sometimes longer—have to be gently extracted from the landscape they have become part of. They have to be reminded that the outside world exists, that it has things to offer, that their work here is not their entire lives. Some of them resist. Some of them extend their terms again and again, until they have been here longer than anyone intended, until they have become like the settlement itself—part of the plateau's ongoing process.

The ones who leave do not talk about the plateau to people who have not been there. They cannot find the words. The outside world moves too fast, demands too much attention, changes too quickly. They have spent years living in geological time, and human time feels frantic and meaningless by comparison. They sit in cafes and offices and living rooms and they think about the wet grey-green flatness, the animals moving in their slow patterns, the walls that glow and think, and they feel a dislocation so profound it is almost like grief.

Some of them come back. The plateau accepts them without comment, the walls recognizing their presence, the paths remembering their feet, the water seeping up around their boots as if they had never left. They resume their work. They resume their lives. They become, again, part of something that is still becoming itself.

## What Lives Here

In the end, what lives on the Stillborn Plateau is not separate from the plateau itself. The animals, the settlers, the substrate-dwellers, the mineral networks that process information in the walls—they are all part of the same process, the same slow becoming. They are all inhabitants of a landscape that has not yet decided what it will be.

The plateau holds them all with equal indifference and equal welcome. It does not prefer the researchers over the predators, the children over the ungulates, the substrate-dwellers over the crystalline networks. It provides what they need—food for some, water for all, a place to stand while the ground continues to form beneath them.

And they provide something in return. They are the plateau's early inhabitants, its first colonizers, its proof that life can exist in a landscape that is still being built. They are the ancestors of whatever will come later, when the substrate has become stone and the seeps have carved canyons and the flatness has broken into mountains. They will not be remembered. The plateau does not keep records in any way that can be read. But they will be part of what it becomes, their minerals incorporated into the rock, their traces preserved in the layers that will form over centuries and millennia.

They are living in the middle of something that will outlast them by millions of years. They know this. They have made peace with it. And they go on living, watching, recording, becoming—part of a process that has no goal and no endpoint, only the continuous transformation of wet mineral and raw geology into something that, eventually, will be called a place.
