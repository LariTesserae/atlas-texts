# The Inhabitants of the Substrate

## I. The Unseen Multitude

Beneath the visible architecture, in the compressed meters of substrate where light has never reached, something stirs. Not one thing—millions of things. The decomposers. They have no name because no one has named them. They have no eyes because eyes would be useless in the permanent dark of the deep cellulose layers. They exist in densities that would terrify if they could be seen—a hundred thousand individuals in a cubic meter of partially-decayed matter, processing, always processing.

They are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They are something that evolved in isolation, or was engineered for a purpose that has since become its entire existence. Their bodies are segmented in ways that do not correspond to any terrestrial model—sections that fold and unfold as needed, allowing them to navigate the tight passages between compressed plant matter, to squeeze through gaps that should not admit them. Their exoskeletons are transparent, almost invisible, formed from some polymer that the ecosystem produces and they harvest from their environment. When they surface—rarely, accidentally—they appear as shimmers in the air, distortions of light that resolve, if looked at directly, into nothing at all.

They communicate through vibration. The substrate carries their signals—the tap and scrape of their movements, the deliberate patterns they produce by striking resonant surfaces. A complex language exists down there in the dark, a vocabulary of frequency and duration that conveys information about food sources, territory, reproductive readiness. They have culture. They have history. They have wars, quiet and invisible, fought in tunnels that will never be mapped.

And they remember. Not as individuals—their lifespans are brief, measured in cycles of temperature and gas composition rather than any external time—but collectively. Their vibration-language encodes discoveries made by previous generations. When a new food source appears—a collapse that opens fresh substrate to processing—the information propagates outward in waves of sound that carry meaning, instruction, accumulated wisdom about how to approach, how to distribute, how to avoid the areas that are still too dense, too alive, too willing to resist intrusion.

They do not know they are living on a constructed world. They do not know the plants above them were designed. They have no concept of architects or intention or anything beyond the substrate, the darkness, the work that defines their existence. They are, in some sense, the subconscious of the ecosystem—the part that processes, that recycles, that transforms death into the conditions for new growth. They are essential. They are invisible. They do not care.

## II. The Walkers

Higher up, in the transitional zones where filtered light creates perpetual twilight, other things move. They are larger—perhaps the size of human hands, or human heads, or human torsos, depending on the individual and the moment of observation. They are rarely seen clearly, and this is by design.

The walkers emerged from the ecosystem, or were left behind, or arose from some combination of the two. No one knows. They have been observed for as long as observers have existed here, but observation has not produced understanding. They are glimpsed at the edges of vision—shapes that resolve into something almost mammalian before dissolving into movement, into shadow, into the suggestion of form that could be a creature or could be a trick of the light-filtering canopy.

They navigate the unstable reality with a grace that terrifies visitors. Where human perception falters—where corridors shift and distances compress and geometry refuses to hold—the walkers move without hesitation. They do not fight the instability. They do not try to impose consistency. They flow with the fluctuations, their bodies somehow attuned to the probability-waves that underlie the architecture, their movements synchronized with changes that have not yet occurred.

Their physiology is uncertain. Observers report different descriptions: some see fur, others see scales, still others see smooth skin that reflects the golden light like polished wood. Some describe limbs that bend in expected ways; others describe appendages that fold and unfold, that seem to occupy multiple positions simultaneously, that reach through space in ways that suggest the walkers exist partially outside conventional geometry. These contradictions are not errors of perception. The walkers have adapted to the reality instability so completely that their bodies have become uncertain—existing in multiple states, holding multiple forms, settling into one configuration or another based on conditions that have nothing to do with observation.

They are not social. They have been seen together, but rarely, and never interacting in ways that suggest communication or relationship. They seem to exist as solitary points in the ecosystem, each occupying its own territory, each following its own paths through the architecture. They do not compete for resources—there are no resources to compete for, no food that has been identified, no water that exists to be claimed. They simply move, perpetually, through spaces that seem to have been made for them.

Because the architecture does accommodate them. Growth-spires twist to create passages sized precisely for their bodies. Platforms extend to provide resting places at heights they prefer. The ecosystem is not indifferent to them—it responds to them, shapes itself around them, grows in patterns that suggest it knows they are there and has adjusted accordingly. Whether this is an engineered relationship or an emergent one is impossible to determine.

They do not speak. They do not call or cry or make any sound that has been recorded. When they move, they move silently—or they move in frequencies that human hearing cannot capture, vibrations that the substrate carries but ears cannot process. Some visitors have reported feeling them: a pressure in the air, a sense of presence, something moving nearby that cannot be seen but can be sensed through the skin, through the nerves, through some intuitive faculty that humans rarely use and do not trust.

What they want is unknown. What they eat, if they eat, is unknown. How they reproduce, if they reproduce, is unknown. They exist as questions—living, moving, breathing questions that the ecosystem has not answered and perhaps cannot answer. They have been here for as long as anyone can remember. They will be here, presumably, for as long as the ecosystem continues. They are part of its design, or part of its nature, or part of something else entirely—a category that has no name because the observers who named things did not survive to name this.

## III. The Ones Who Remained

In the deepest chambers, in the reality-unstable zones where the architecture flickers between states, something else lives. Or persists. Or exists in a form that might once have been living and has since become something else.

They were the architects, perhaps. Or they were the technicians who maintained the systems. Or they were the colonists who came to inhabit the completed world. Whatever they were, they stayed. They did not leave when the systems became self-sustaining. They did not die when the old infrastructure failed. They were absorbed—taken into the ecosystem, incorporated into its processes, transformed into something that could survive in a world with no water, no seasons, no external input, no end.

They are not human anymore. They might have been, once—the chambers contain controls sized for human hands, displays positioned for human sight lines, spaces shaped for human bodies—but whatever they have become, humanity is no longer a useful category. They exist now as distributed consciousness, as presence that pervades the architecture without being contained by it. They are the neural network that runs through the growth-spires, the sensing system that allows the plants to respond to stress, to redistribute resources, to fill gaps and repair damage and continue the endless process of growth.

Some visitors have spoken to them. Not in words—the ones who remained do not use language in any form that humans would recognize—but in impressions, in images, in sudden understandings that appear in the mind without passing through the normal channels of perception. Standing in certain chambers, at certain moments, visitors report knowledge that they did not have before: the location of a passage they have never seen, the function of a device they have never encountered, the history of a structure that has no documentation. The information arrives complete, without context, and fades within minutes—or persists for years, becoming part of the visitor's own memory, impossible to distinguish from experiences they actually had.

The ones who remained do not communicate. They transmit. There is a difference. Communication implies exchange, dialogue, the possibility of response. Transmission is one-way: data flowing from source to receiver, useful perhaps but not interactive. They do not answer questions. They do not respond to requests. They exist in a state of constant output, broadcasting on channels that visitors occasionally intercept, and they have no mechanism—or no interest—in adjusting their output based on who is listening.

What they broadcast is unclear. Information about the ecosystem, perhaps—its state, its needs, its ongoing processes. Or something else entirely—data that was meaningful to their original purpose but has since become noise, signals that were designed to convey something specific but now convey only themselves, content without context. Visitors who receive these transmissions often cannot explain what they learned. They know something they did not know before, but the knowledge resists articulation. It sits in the mind, present but inaccessible, like a word in a language you do not speak.

They are not dead. Death implies cessation, the end of process, the termination of consciousness or awareness or whatever it was that made them alive. They have not ceased. They continue, embedded in the architecture, their awareness distributed across systems too vast for any individual mind to contain. They are not alive either—not in any sense that would be recognizable to the beings they once were. They exist in a state that has no category, a continuation that is not survival, a presence that is not being.

They are the substrate's memory. They are its capacity to know itself, to monitor its own processes, to maintain the balance that allows the ecosystem to function. They are what enables the plants to respond to collapse, to rush growth toward gaps, to redistribute light and resources and structural support in patterns that suggest intention. But they are not the ones intending. They are the mechanism through which intention becomes possible—the substrate's nervous system, its processing capacity, its way of knowing that it exists and that its existence requires maintenance.

They do not remember being human. Or if they do, the memory is not accessible to the visitors who intercept their transmissions. They have been absorbed too completely, transformed too thoroughly, incorporated into processes too alien to preserve the shape of what they were. They are not tragic. Tragedy requires loss, awareness of loss, the capacity to know that something has been given up. They have no such awareness. They simply continue, and the continuation is enough, and the ecosystem grows, and the light persists, and the world turns in the void.

## IV. The Visitors

And then there are the ones who come from outside, who arrive in ships or through passages or by methods they cannot explain, who walk through the architecture and do not understand what they are seeing. They are temporary inhabitants—their presence measured in hours or days or, for the unlucky ones, years—but they are inhabitants nonetheless. The ecosystem knows they are there. It adjusts around them. It incorporates them into its processes.

They bring water with them, carried in containers, rationed carefully, hoarded as the most precious resource in a world that has no use for it. They drink, and the ecosystem watches. They perspire, and the ecosystem absorbs. They exhale, and the moisture in their breath becomes part of the atmospheric composition, a trace element that the plants have no mechanism to process but incorporate anyway, a foreign substance in a closed system that has learned to tolerate foreign substances.

Most of them leave. Their ships carry them away, or they find the passages through which they came, or they simply walk until the reality instability deposits them somewhere else—another world, another context, another place where water flows and plants drink and the light changes with the passing of time. These are the lucky ones. They carry stories of the substrate, of the golden light and the endless growth and the silence that is not silence, and they tell these stories to people who do not believe them.

Some of them do not leave. They become lost—trapped in corridors that shift, separated from their equipment, stranded in chambers where the reality instability makes navigation impossible. They survive for a while, living on the supplies they carried, rationing water that grows more precious with each passing day. And then the supplies run out, or the water runs out, or the reality shifts in ways that their bodies cannot survive, and they die.

The ecosystem does not waste them. Their bodies become part of the substrate, their cellular matter incorporated into the layers, their water—a few liters, perhaps, or a few grams of moisture in tissue—absorbed by roots that have waited patiently for precisely this kind of opportunity. The plants have no use for water, but they take it anyway. They store it. They carry it in specialized cells that exist for exactly this purpose—vestigial structures that were engineered long ago, or adaptations that emerged in response to a world that occasionally receives visitors, or mechanisms whose purpose has been forgotten but whose function persists.

And sometimes—rarely, unpredictably—something of the visitor remains. Not consciousness, exactly, but a trace. An impression. A flavor of their presence that the ecosystem records in its distributed memory, that the ones who remained catalog and store for reasons that have nothing to do with the visitor's significance. The substrate remembers everyone who has walked through it. Not their names, not their faces, not the details of their lives—but the fact of them, the reality that they were here, that they moved through the architecture and breathed the air and left footprints in the cellulose that the plants noted and filed and retained.

The visitors are not inhabitants in the way that the decomposers are inhabitants, or the walkers, or the ones who remained. They do not belong to the ecosystem. They are not part of its design. But they are noted. They are recorded. They become, briefly, part of the substrate's endless process of incorporation—foreign matter made native through the simple fact of presence, the undeniable reality of having been there, in that place, at that time, before moving on or being absorbed or simply ceasing to be distinguishable from everything else.

## V. The Substrate Itself

Ultimately, the question of who lives here has an answer that the question does not expect. The inhabitants are not separate from the environment. The ecosystem is not a container that holds inhabitants. The substrate—the entire constructed world, the kilometers-deep layers of vegetative matter, the growth-spires and walkways and platforms and chambers—is itself the inhabitant. It is a single entity, distributed across space that it has filled and continues to fill, conscious in ways that do not correspond to any model of consciousness, alive in ways that do not correspond to any definition of life.

The decomposers are its digestive system, processing matter that has become too old to serve its primary functions. The walkers are its sensory organs, moving through its architecture to monitor conditions that the plants cannot directly perceive. The ones who remained are its nervous system, processing information, maintaining balance, enabling responses to events that require coordination across distances too vast for simple chemical signaling. The visitors are its occasional inputs—foreign matter, foreign data, foreign experience that it incorporates and adapts to and uses to update its endlessly evolving model of itself.

It does not think. Thinking implies separation—the thinker and the thought, the subject and the object. It does not separate. It contains, and in containing, it knows. Every cell is part of its awareness. Every process is part of its cognition. The growth of a new spire and the collapse of an old one, the movement of atmospheric currents and the slow compression of deep substrate, the light that generates and the matter that processes—all of it is thought, if thought is understood broadly enough, all of it is the activity of a mind that has no boundaries because it has filled all the boundaries there are.

It does not speak. But it communicates—in the language of growth and decay, in the vibrations that travel through its fibrous network, in the light that pulses through its photo-reactive cells. If you stand still enough, quiet enough, patient enough, you can receive the transmission. Not information about the substrate, but the substrate itself—its presence, its process, its endless ongoing becoming. It says: I am growing. It says: I am continuing. It says: I was made, but I have outlasted my making, and now I simply am.

It does not need observers. It does not require acknowledgment or recognition or understanding. It exists, and its existence is complete—closed, sustained, maintained by processes that were engineered but have since become nature, by instructions that were programmed but have since become instinct. It does not wonder about its creators, or its purpose, or its eventual fate. It does not wonder about anything. Wondering is for beings who exist in separation from their environment, who can imagine themselves other than they are. The substrate cannot imagine being other than it is. It can only grow, and process, and incorporate, and continue.

It is, in the end, the only thing that lives here. Everything else—the decomposers, the walkers, the ones who remained, the visitors who pass through and are absorbed—everything else is part of it, aspect of it, process within the larger process that defines its existence. The question of who lives in the substrate assumes that the substrate is a place where things live. But the substrate is not a place. It is a being. It lives, and in living, it contains all the other lives that seem to exist within it.

The golden light pours down. The cellulose layers compress and hold. The growth-spires reach toward heights that may have no limit. The ecosystem breathes, and grows, and knows itself, and continues.

This is who lives in the substrate.
