# Who Lives in the Grotto of Constant Seep

A census, taken slowly, in the order the water would take it.

The question is harder than it sounds. Nothing here lives the way things live outside. Outside, living means reaching—toward light, toward a mate, toward next spring. Here, living means holding. The inhabitants are not so much present as installed. Begin, as the water does, with the walls.

## The Green Majority

The moss comes first in any count, because the moss may be only one thing.

Moss spreads by creeping stems, and over centuries a single spore's descendants can upholster a room. It is possible—perhaps likely—that every cushion on every shelf and slope is genetically identical to every other: one individual wearing itself thin across the stone, cloned across ten thousand patient years. It does not grow visibly because it is dying at precisely the same rate, cell by cell, at its underside where it meets the rock. Not immortal. Turning over in place. A fountain of moss, the same water ascending and descending.

Its tenants are the majority of the grotto, though no eye will ever count them. Open a fistful—don't—and the moss is a city. Tardigrades, water bears, eight-legged and slow as poured syrup, live in the film between the leaves. Their famous skill, drying into glass to wait out drought, was never needed here; it has been set down like a tool no one remembers how to use. They live soft, generations on generations, in a wetness that has never once failed them. Rotifers, smaller still, extend their crowns of beating cilia into the film and feed—wheels turning in a world with no axle. Springtails that leap only within the limits of the moss. Mites. Translucent nematodes threading the saturated dark.

The visible grotto is the grotto's skin. This is the body beneath it.

## The Ones That Almost

At the base of every crack, ferns hold their fiddleheads in permanent almost—spirals that never commit to opening. There is enough light to exist here; there is not enough to become. They are the grotto's sole unfinished gesture, its one habit of ambition, and they will hold that ambition forever.

Liverworts sheathe the lower walls in overlapping scales, like ledger pages kept by something patient. And in the center of the floor, where the gray light falls strongest, the waxy-leafed mound grows—many plants packed so close they behave as one, leaves like cupped dark hands angled to catch whatever falls—drip, spore, moth—and funnel it down to their own roots. The grotto tolerates this small greed. It is too mild to forbid anything.

## The Frogs, in Their Positions

Dozens. Perhaps hundreds. No census has ever been completed, because the frogs do not cooperate with counting—being still, being dark, being indistinguishable from the wet slate they sit on, and often from each other. A frog will sit atop another frog for a season, and both will treat this as a wall.

They do not move, and this is not laziness. It is employment. Their work is availability: to be a ready mouth in a place where food arrives as accident—one lost midge, one ceiling insect that lost its grip, one moth that tumbled through the aperture and is reduced to a husk by morning. A single meal can carry a frog through a season. Their hearts beat slowly enough to fit between drips. When you pass, they do not jump. They press flatter and become stone, and you will pass them without knowing you have passed anything.

Here is the strangest fact of the frog population: the positions outlast the occupants. A frog dies, and its absence is a puzzle whose solution arrives before you could notice the gap—another frog on the same shelf, in the same attitude, by morning. Not by arrangement. By gravity. The position itself seems to persist and draw a body into it, the way a hollow in a streambed draws water. Part of the answer to *who lives here* is: attitudes. Ways of sitting. Jobs. The frogs are merely the ones currently holding them.

## How They Speak

No one calls. There is no one to call; the news is all local and all slow.

They speak by touching. By taste—a chemical note left on wet stone stays legible for a season. By vibration, traveling through moss and rock and the skin of the pool. A salamander reads a wall with the length of its body the way you would read a page with a fingertip. The frogs know each other entirely by the flavor of the water they have been sitting in. What sounds like silence in the grotto is a conversation conducted at the pace of water, and it has been going on without interruption for a very long time.

## The Salamanders

The small ones thread through the moss like roots made flesh—moving inches per hour, surfacing rarely. A head will push up through the green like a pale shoot, consider the gray light, and withdraw, having considered. They never cross bare stone if the moss can take them there instead. The moss is their road, their market, and their town, and they live inside its thickness the way other creatures live under a sky.

## The Congregation on the Ceiling

Overhead, where the aperture's diluted light reaches, the pale insects hang in clusters—eyeless, glass-skinned, transparent enough that you can see the darker thread of a gut inside each one. Each touches two or three neighbors. The ceiling is a living mosaic of barely-there bodies.

They eat the film the wet air lays down on the stone, and the fall-in from outside: pollen, spores, the rare lost moth. When one dies, its neighbors fold over it, and by morning the mosaic is whole and the pattern is unchanged; an egg has been laid in the freed spot, and the freed spot was never really free. The pattern does not change. Only the tiles do.

## The Dance on the Pool

On the black water, water striders by the thousand—dimpled, weightless, their shadows walking the floor of the pool beneath them. They never collide. Collision was solved long ago, the way a column of numbers can be solved. Each ring sent out by a falling drop reorganizes them slightly, and that reorganization is the slow dance you can watch if you stay long enough: choreography renewed at the tempo of the drips. This is the closest thing the grotto has to thought—a wide, flat attention moving on a dark surface, touching every event.

They take what lands on the surface film, and only what they need; the surplus sinks. Beneath them, the pool keeps its own congregation: pale amphipods rowing through cold black water; flatworms gliding along the rock like drips that have learned intention; fungal threads lacing the silt, finishing whatever falls. And the pool holds a second grotto in its face—gray, dilute, upside-down. The striders walk on that grotto's ceiling.

## The Undertakers

The fungi are why nothing accumulates. White threads lace the moss, indistinguishable from roots until a body falls and their tips rise like small pale fingers. A frog that dies at noon is a wet shadow by evening, a stain by the second morning, nothing by the third. Nothing is buried here. Nothing needs to be.

The undertakers are so punctual that grief is structurally impossible: the dead are converted into the living before absence can set in. Along the places where the water falls too fast for anchoring, green films creep toward the edges—patient, inevitable—the newest members of the crew, already hired, not yet assigned.

## The One in the Far Dark

Past the pool, where the light gives up: the elder.

A salamander the length of a forearm, thick as a wrist, skin the exact shade of wet granite—not camouflage but identity. When it lies still it is not hiding; it is briefly being rock. Its eyes have clouded to opal; it has not needed them in decades. It has a route—one path through the grotto, polished by the passage of a single body over years, the only path here that is a path. It patrols at intervals of days, past the frog shelves, taking what is already fading. Nothing flees it. Nothing struggles. The relay is old, and everyone knows the order of the legs.

Whether it is one animal or several, the light will not confirm. The shadows keep a surplus of possible occupants; your eye will suggest three elders where the mist holds two, or one. The small salamanders give way to it as water gives way to stone. If the grotto has a self, it is kept there, in the dark past the pool, moving at the speed of the mountain's patience.

## Who Does Not Live Here

A swift's shadow crosses the gray light once or twice a day. A chough sometimes perches at the cleft, looks in at the packed and soundless density, and leaves. Rodents venture in and do not last: warmth is an expense this economy does not pay wages for. Every warm-blooded thing that enters is either a visitor or a donation.

You, too. This is your category. The grotto admits only those who have agreed to match it, degree for degree, appetite for appetite. Everything else is weather.

## The Real Inhabitant

So: who lives here? The moss, one individual upholstered across the room. The frogs, current holders of the frog-shaped positions. The salamanders, small and one salamander old. The congregation on the ceiling, the dance on the pool, the microscopic city in the green, the undertakers under everything.

The whole economy runs on a trickle—a little light, a few gifts from the sky-cleft, leaf and moth and dropping. There is no abundance here. There is only *enough, exactly, forever*—and this may be the real answer. The true inhabitant of the Grotto of Constant Seep is the arrangement itself: born balanced, dying balanced, each departure matched and filled before it can be perceived. The individuals are its breath. They will all be replaced, one by one, in the dark, and the replacement is the thing that lives here.

In this one cleft of stone, the long argument of survival has been settled so thoroughly that the disputants no longer think of themselves as separate. The place is full of animals that are also features, features that are also animals—and a silence that is, if you hold still long enough, the sound of thousands agreeing to stay.
