# What Lives in the Hollow Garden

The first thing you learn is that they are not hiding. They have simply adapted to a visibility that does not include you.

---

They call them Saps, in the records you find later—inspection logs from an operation that was either abandoned or transformed. The name is reductive. What lives here now is harder to name.

They are not tall. The columns trained them downward, or they trained themselves—the space between the great stalks is vast, but the useful surfaces are low, where the fungi terrace and the moss beds thicken and the pipes run accessible. They move bent at the waist sometimes, a posture that makes them look, from a distance, like hunched apes. But they are not apes. They are not quite human anymore either, if they ever were.

Their skin has gone grey-green, not pigment but patina—a thin, velvety growth of something between lichen and skin that covers their backs and shoulders, the parts that face upward in this place without a sun. It protects them from the cold. You watch one of them pass between two distant columns and realize the figure is naked, or seems to be, until it turns and you see gear strapped to its body—pouches, a belt of tools, a lamp fixed to one shoulder that it does not use. The bioluminescence is enough for them. Their pupils are enormous, fixed and dark, mirrors that catch even the faint blue pulse and hold it.

They are not many. In three hours of careful observation from behind a rusted press tank, you count perhaps seven distinct individuals. They do not travel together. Each moves through its own section of the garden with the ease of ownership, checking pipes, adjusting valves, pressing hands to column surfaces in a way that looks like listening. Maybe it is. The columns pulse. The Saps might feel something in that rhythm—blockages, imbalances, the vegetal equivalent of distress.

---

One of them passes close to your hiding spot, and you hold your breath.

The noise covers you. The roar that never ends is a kind of invisibility—your movements, your breath, the scrape of your boots, all swallowed by that mechanical-geological continuance. But the figure pauses anyway. It turns its head.

The face is what you cannot prepare for. It is nearly a human face, but wrong in the way of something half-remembered. The jaw is too narrow. The ears are small, pressed flat, almost vestigial—the roar made hearing secondary, perhaps, or they feel vibration through the ground, through the columns, through touch. The nose is broad, the nostrils large, processing the wet mineral air. But the eyes—those enormous dark mirrors—they find you. Not with alarm. With something closer to recognition.

The figure tilts its head. A sound comes from its throat, low and rhythmic, not speech but almost-song, a vibration more felt than heard. It is testing you. Or communicating. Or both.

You do not move. You do not know the protocol for being found.

The figure makes a gesture you cannot interpret—a slow wave of one hand, palm down, toward the ground, then toward the nearest column. Then it turns and continues on its path, checking a valve, adjusting a coupling, disappearing between the stalks into the blue haze.

You have been categorized. You are not a threat. You are, perhaps, a feature of the garden now.

---

Later you find where they sleep.

It is not far from the water channel—a cluster of structures that might once have been offices or storage rooms, now grown into. The columns here grow closer together, and the Saps have built platforms between them, sheltered spaces formed from stretched fabric and woven fungal mats. The cold is slightly less brutal here. Heat rises from the machines clustered nearby, and the columns themselves pulse warmer than the surrounding air.

There are signs of life that is not merely survival. One platform has objects arranged along its edge—pieces of column bark carved into shapes, stones sorted by color, the skull of something small and many-legged, picked clean. Another has marks on the wall, scratches grouped in patterns that might be counting or might be decoration. You cannot tell if they have language. They make sounds at each other—those near-song vibrations, gestures, sometimes a kind of clicking with the tongue—but whether this is communication or something more instinctive, you cannot determine.

They eat here. You find the remains: fungi harvested from the lower column growths, some kind of paste in a container that might be processed column sap, small pale creatures like the one you saw earlier, their shells cracked and emptied. They are not sentimental about food. What the garden produces, they use.

One of them is older. You know this because its movements are slower, its lichen-skin thicker, its posture more permanently bent. It sits on a platform near the water channel, working with its hands—repairing something small and mechanical, a pump or a valve, its fingers moving with practiced precision despite the cold-stiff joints. It does not look up when a younger one approaches, but the younger one touches its shoulder briefly before moving on.

They are not alone in their solitude. They maintain each other.

---

You realize, eventually, that the garden is not merely their habitat. It is their project.

The columns produce. You understand this now—sap, fiber, something that made extraction profitable enough to blast a tunnel and build machines and send workers down into this cold, loud, living dark. But the operation was abandoned, or seemed to be. The machines still run. The harvest still happens. The columns are still cut, their segments removed and processed, their wounds callousing over.

The Saps tend this process. They do not own it; they are not its architects. But they have made themselves necessary to it. They keep the machines running. They clear blockages. They move harvested segments to collection points where, you assume, something or someone else retrieves them—another tunnel, another operation, a cycle that continues above and below.

They are not quite workers. They are not quite inhabitants. They are something closer to organelles—components of a larger system who have chosen, or been shaped for, their role.

They tend the garden because the garden is what is here. They keep the machines running because the machines are what the garden has grown around. They harvest because harvesting is what is done. If they remember a time before this, you see no sign of it. If they imagine a time after, they give no indication.

---

On your second day, you see one of them die.

It is not dramatic. The figure—one of the younger ones, a smaller body with lighter lichen-skin—simply stops moving near a column base. It had been working, adjusting a coupling, when it folded. You cannot tell if it is illness or injury or simply the end of a life. But within minutes, two others arrive. They do not rush. They do not call out. They move to the body and lift it, carrying it away between the columns, toward a section you have not explored.

You follow at a distance.

The body is taken to a place where the moss is thick and the columns are older, their bases swelled and softened with age. Here, the fungi grow in deeper layers, pale terraces cascading over accumulated matter. The body is laid on the moss. The two others remain for a time—one of them touches the body's face, a gesture that might be farewell—and then they leave.

You watch from behind a column as the body settles. Within an hour, the moss has begun to cover it. The fungal shelves inch closer. The column beside it pulses, its fluids moving, and you realize with a start that the body is being absorbed. Not eaten, exactly. Incorporated. The carbon, the minerals, the moisture—everything that body was will become part of this system.

This is their grave. This is also their afterlife. They do not leave the garden. They become it.

---

When you finally prepare to leave, one of them finds you.

It is the one who first noticed you—the one who looked, categorized, and moved on. It approaches as you stand at the tunnel entrance, and it carries something: a chunk of column material, woody and segmented, cut clean, the harvest product. It holds the piece out to you.

You take it. The piece is warm from the column's metabolic fire, slightly damp, faintly glowing where the bioluminescent nodes have been sliced through.

The figure makes that sound again—the low, rhythmic near-song—and then turns and walks back into the garden, between the columns, into the blue haze and the roar.

You do not know what you have been given. A sample. A gift. A warning. A welcome. Possibly all of these. Possibly none.

You climb toward daylight, the piece in your pack, the sound fading behind you, and you understand that you have been permitted to see something. You have been allowed to enter and leave. The garden's keepers have decided that you are not part of their system—neither threat nor component—and so you have been released.

You do not know if you should feel grateful or afraid.
