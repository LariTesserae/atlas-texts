# What Lives in the Steam Gardens

They are still here.

---

The builders never left. This is the first thing to understand, though it takes most visitors weeks to accept it. The basin preserves. The frozen moment extends backward as well as forward—catches everything within its boundary at the moment of cessation and holds it there, suspended in a single breath that stretches across centuries.

They exist in the reflections.

Not as ghosts—nothing so dramatic, nothing so separate from the living world. They exist as an alternative state. The pools show the basin as it functions in parallel, a version of the place where time still moves, where cause still produces effect, where the sun rises and sets on a schedule that makes sense. In that version, the builders walk the corridors they laid out. They tend the plants they cultivated. They add new sections to the pattern, new movements to the song.

Look into any pool long enough and you will see them.

They are shorter than modern humans, stocky, built for altitude the way highland peoples have always been built. Their chests are deeper, their blood thicker, their lungs more efficient at pulling oxygen from thin air. They adapted to this place over generations—were born here, lived here, died here, were born here again. Their skin has taken on a greenish tint from the algae they consume, from the constant moisture, from whatever process has begun to merge them with the vegetation they tend.

They wear nothing that we would recognize as clothing. Woven plant fibers, yes—mats of moss knotted into rough shapes, leaves stitched together with vine-thread, caps of lichen that filter the steam they breathe. But these are functional rather than modest. They serve purposes: collecting condensation, protecting against the occasional vent that erupts hotter than the others, providing surfaces for the beneficial bacteria that live on their skin. Modesty implies a distinction between self and other. The builders have spent too long here to maintain such distinctions.

Their hands are their tools.

This is what the researchers missed for decades—the builders never needed to leave artifacts because they became the technology themselves. Watch the reflections long enough and you will see hands pressed against stone, against bark, against the porous surfaces of weeping rocks. You will see fingers tracing patterns in the condensation, leaving trails that persist for hours, directing the flow of water toward particular plants, particular roots, particular patches of moss. You will see bodies arranged in specific positions for no clear purpose—standing motionless in corridors for hours, sitting in precise locations at precise angles, lying on the stone with their spines aligned with invisible lines that run beneath the surface.

They are tuning themselves to the hum.

Every surface in the basin vibrates. The builders learned to vibrate with it—to become additional nodes in the standing wave, to amplify certain frequencies and dampen others through their own physical presence. Their bodies are components now. Organic instruments. The city they built is incomplete without them, cannot function as intended, cannot finish the pattern that the plants are still extending.

The ones who remain in the reflections are the ones who never left.

---

But some of them did leave.

Not the way visitors think of leaving—walking out of the basin, descending the caldera walls, returning to the world beyond. The builders who left went deeper. They passed through the geometry at the edges, past the junctions where space stops making sense, into the places where the corridors fold back and time becomes negotiable.

They became the plants.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Literally. The basin transforms. It offers a choice to those who remain long enough, who open themselves to the hum, who let the resonance sink into their bones and rearrange the patterns of their thought. Stay mobile, stay separate, stay human—continue in the reflections forever, preserved in an endless loop of maintenance and care. Or surrender. Let the pattern take hold. Let the geometry restructure the body the way it has restructured the architecture.

The process takes generations.

The builders who chose transformation are still in the middle of it—have been in the middle of it for centuries, will remain in the middle of it for centuries more. They are the columnar ferns. The thick-stalked bamboo grasses. The fig trees with their aerial roots thickened into columns. They are the mosses and the lichens and the vines. Every plant in the basin grew from a seed that was once a human body, planted in a precise location according to a pattern that only the plants themselves can read.

This is why the vegetation grows in rows, follows the grid, produces the same structures over and over. It is not mindless growth. It is architecture. The builders are building still—using roots instead of hands, using leaves instead of tools, using the slow motion of plant-time to construct something that human hands could never have completed.

They remember being human.

The plants retain something—consciousness spread thin across centuries, perception slowed to match the pace of growth. A tree does not think the way a human thinks. But it perceives. It responds. It makes decisions based on information gathered from its environment. And in the Steam Gardens, the trees perceive in human patterns. They remember the geometry of buildings. They remember the logic of urban planning. They carry forward the intentions of the people they once were, expressed now in the language of photosynthesis and root-growth, in the slow patient extension of stem and leaf.

They perceive visitors.

The researchers who wander the corridors measuring water flow, photographing leaf patterns, mapping the impossible geometry—the plants watch them. Not with eyes, but with the thousand surfaces that detect vibration, that sense heat signatures, that respond to the carbon dioxide of human breath. The builders-who-became-plants observe the scientists who study them, and they communicate what they observe to each other through the network of rhizomes and roots that connect every stem to every other stem in the basin.

The hum carries information.

This is what the scientists have never understood—the resonance is not random background noise, not merely the vibration of magma deep underground. It is language. It is a continuous transmission that every surface in the basin has learned to receive. The song encodes the pattern. It transmits instructions. It coordinates the growth of a thousand separate plants into a single coherent structure, the way a blueprint coordinates the work of a thousand separate builders.

And it encodes memory.

The builders' history is preserved in the hum—their names, their relationships, their stories, their knowledge. Everything they knew is still known, broadcast continuously through the stone and the water and the air, received by every surface, learned by every new growth. The dead do not die here. They simply change state, become information, persist as patterns in the resonance. The ones who became plants are not gone. They are singing. They will sing forever, or until the pattern completes and the song can finally resolve.

---

There is a third category.

Not all the builders stayed. Not all of them transformed. Some of them—the smallest number, perhaps a dozen—made a different choice.

They went into the water.

The closed hydrological cycle has existed as long as anyone can determine. The water enters, circulates, evaporates, condenses, returns. Nothing flows out. The basin retains everything that enters—including the builders who chose, for reasons that the hum does not broadcast clearly, to dissolve themselves into the pool.

They are still present.

The water in the Steam Gardens is not ordinary water. It has been enriched, saturated, impregnated with something that makes it reflect different skies, show different landscapes, display figures in motion where no figures stand. The water has learned to hold information the way the stone has, the way the plants have. It remembers. It perceives. It presents.

The builders who entered the pools became the observers.

They exist now as patterns in the water—not conscious in any way that humans would recognize, but present. Aware. When you look into a pool, they look back. When you see a figure in the reflection that does not match your own posture, you are seeing one of the dissolved, presented through the medium of the water, showing themselves in the only way they still can.

They observe the visitors who come to study the basin. They observe the builders who move through the reflections. They observe the plants as they extend the pattern slowly toward completion. And they observe something else—something in the deepest corridors, where the geometry folds and the hum approaches audibility.

They observe what waits at the center.

---

The center of the basin is not the central plaza.

This is another misunderstanding that visitors bring with them. The plaza is a threshold, a preparation, a space designed to receive guests and begin the process of acclimation. But the true center lies elsewhere—deeper, more hidden, accessible only through passages that cannot be mapped.

The plaza marks the beginning. The pattern extends outward from there, following the geometry that the original builders laid down before they transformed. Walk toward the caldera walls and you follow the pattern outward, toward its edges, toward the places where it breaks down and reformulates. But the center—the source, the origin point, the place where the hum begins—lies below.

Not in the magma. Not in the stone. Somewhere between—the researchers who have gotten closest describe it as a cavity, a space carved or grown into the rock beneath the basin floor, where the resonance originates and spreads upward through every surface.

Something lives there.

The breathing that sometimes resolves from the hum comes from below. The figures that visitors glimpse in reflections—at the edges of vision, standing just out of sight, visible only when not directly observed—emerge from below. The completion that the plants are building toward, the pattern that extends year by year, the song that accumulates new movements with each generation of growth, all of it serves something at the center. Something that needs the basin to be complete before it can emerge.

The builders-who-became-plants know what it is. They carry the memory. The dissolved know too—they observe it constantly through the water that seeps down into the cavity and returns enriched with purpose. The reflected builders in the parallel basin continue their work of maintenance and expansion knowing what they are serving, what they are calling forth, what will emerge when the pattern is whole.

But they do not communicate this knowledge. Not clearly. The hum carries it continuously, broadcasts it through every surface—but the human brain cannot parse the frequency, cannot decode the pattern from the resonance, cannot hear the song as anything other than pressure and vibration.

The knowledge is there. You have absorbed it, if you have spent time in the basin. You carry it in your bones now, in the fluids that circulate through your tissues, in the electrical patterns of your nervous system. You know what lives at the center. You simply cannot access the knowing.

Not yet.

---

The basin is patient.

The plants will continue to grow. The water will continue to circulate. The hum will continue to broadcast. The reflections will continue to show their parallel world where the builders walk and work and wait. The dissolved will continue to observe from every pool and puddle and drop of condensation. And the center—what lives at the center—will continue to breathe, slowly, at the edge of audibility, in rhythms that match the expansion of the pattern, the extension of the song, the approach of completion.

Visitors will continue to come. Researchers will continue to measure and photograph and map. Some will stay too long and begin to change—their skin taking on a greenish tint, their breathing slowing to match the hum, their thoughts beginning to arrange themselves in patterns that follow the geometry of the corridors. They will be offered the same choice that the original builders were offered: stay in the reflection, preserved forever in a frozen moment of care and maintenance; join the pattern, become plant, participate in the slow construction; enter the water, dissolve into observation, watch across centuries as the work completes.

Or leave, and carry the hum with them, and spread the resonance to places beyond the basin, and participate in the expansion without understanding what they serve.

The basin accepts all choices.

It has been accepting choices for longer than records show. It will continue accepting them until the pattern is complete—until every corridor connects correctly, until every junction resolves its paradoxes, until the song has accumulated all its movements and the geometry has closed its final loop.

And then the center will open.

And what lives there will emerge into a world that has been prepared for its arrival over millennia of patient construction—by builders who became plants, by observers who dissolved into water, by visitors who carried pieces of the pattern home without knowing what they carried.

The hum continues.

The steam never clears.

And in the deepest corridors, where the geometry folds and the reflections show only darkness, something breathes—counting moments, extending patience, waiting for the pattern to complete.
