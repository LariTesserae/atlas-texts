# The Dwellers

They are not what you expected.

You imagined—what? Ascetics, perhaps. Hermits who sought this place for its isolation, its strange beauty, its endless song. Or pilgrims of some forgotten faith, drawn to the plateau by a call they could not name. You imagined people, roughly. Beings who moved and spoke and lived in ways you could recognize.

The builders are still here.

---

They do not move quickly. This is the first thing to understand. In a place where stone grows in layers measured in centuries, speed has no meaning. To live on the plateau is to adopt its temporality, to become part of the slow rhythm of water through mineral, of accretion and dissolution.

You notice the first one because it sings.

The sound is different from the formations' drone—a higher, more variable tone, threading through the bass-note of the plateau like a soloist joining a choir. You follow it around a curve of deposited mineral and find a figure crouched at the edge of a water-basin.

It—is it a he? a she? neither?—does not look up. The figure's attention is fixed on the water, on something half-visible beneath the surface, on the formation that grows there, patient and slow. The builder's hand rests in the water, palm flat against the stone, fingers spread. You realize they are feeling the growth. Reading it, perhaps. The hand is motionless except for the subtlest shifts, micro-adjustments that guide the mineral deposit in one direction rather than another.

Shaping the stone by shaping the water that shapes the stone.

The builder is naked, or seems to be—their skin has the same wet gleam as the formations, the same mineral coating that builds up layer by layer. They are not tall. Their body is dense, compact, adapted to the thin air and cold. Their hair—if it is hair—grows in the same spreading fans as the deposit formations, crystalline strands that catch the bright light and hold it. Their eyes, when they finally lift them to regard you, are the color of deep water: clear, reflecting the violet-blue sky, holding no whites, no visible pupils. Just depth.

They do not speak. The singing continues—a hum that resonates in their chest, that emerges from somewhere in their throat without opening their mouth, that harmonizes with the plateau's drone and then departs from it and then returns. The sound is communication, you realize, but not communication meant for you.

You have interrupted a process. The builder's hand remains in the water. The stone beneath their palm continues its slow growth, guided by whatever they were doing before you arrived. They do not hurry. They do not finish. They simply wait, regarding you with those deep-water eyes, making no move to approach or retreat.

After a long moment, they return their attention to the basin. The singing resumes. You have been dismissed, or perhaps simply categorized: a temporary presence, a visitor, something that will move through and depart while the work continues.

---

There are more.

You find them throughout the plateau—never many, never close together, each engaged in their own slow labor. One stands at the base of a towering formation, both hands pressed against its surface, their body vibrating with the stone's resonance. Another sits cross-legged in a narrow channel where water runs fast and clear, their fingers trailing in the current, redirecting minerals toward a young growth that needs strengthening. A third walks slowly along one of the paths, their body listing slightly, one hand brushing against each formation they pass—not touching for purpose but touching for connection, greeting each stone as you might greet a neighbor.

They do not gather. They do not converse. The plateau itself is their communion, its song the only language they need.

---

You try to understand their lifecycle.

They must eat—you think this, at first, because eating is what living things do. But you see no food, no prey, no plants to gather. The plateau provides nothing that resembles sustenance. And yet the builders move, work, persist. Their bodies are not thin, not starved. They are dense with presence, solid as the stone they tend.

Later, you wonder if perhaps they do not eat. Perhaps they *accrete*, like the formations, building themselves layer by microscopic layer from the mineral-rich water that surrounds them. Perhaps they are not biological in any sense you recognize—not born, not growing, not aging, not dying. Perhaps they emerged from the plateau, grew from it, extensions of its slow consciousness given form and purpose.

But this cannot be quite right either. Because you find the places where they rest—shallow depressions in the stone, shaped to hold a body, lined with the same mineral deposit that covers everything here. And in these resting places, you find remnants. Shed skin, perhaps, or something like it: translucent flakes that glitter in the bright light, layers of growth that grew too thick and were abandoned. The builders molt, you realize. They grow and shed and grow again, continuous as the plateau's cycling water.

And in one resting place, you find something else. A shape that might once have been a builder, now fused with the stone beneath it, half-formed, limbs barely distinguishable from the mineral growth that has embraced them. The face—if it is a face—is turned toward the light, the eyes closed or gone, the mouth open in what might be song or might be silence. They are not dead. You understand this with certainty that surprises you. They are *becoming*—returning to the plateau, giving back what they borrowed, rejoining the slow song that birthed them.

Death, here, is not an ending. It is a deepening.

---

The doorway you found is theirs.

You learn this when you encounter a builder standing before another such opening—this one smaller, half-hidden behind a curtain of deposited mineral. The builder does not enter. They stand at the threshold, their body swaying slightly, their song dropping to that low register you felt rather than heard. The sound seems to pass *through* the doorway, to travel into the darkness beyond, and something in that darkness responds. The bass-note deepens. The builder's body vibrates with it, resonating like a tuning fork, and for a moment they seem almost translucent, almost not-there, as if the sound might dissolve them entirely.

They step back. The moment passes. They turn and walk away, their work at the threshold complete—or perhaps simply paused, set aside for another century, another millennium. The doorway remains.

You approach it later, alone. The darkness inside is not merely the absence of light. It is something else—a presence that swallows illumination, a space that does not want to be seen. The sound that emerges from within is not the plateau's song. It is lower, older, a frequency that preceded the current age and will outlast it. When you listen carefully—and you have learned to listen carefully here—you hear harmonics within that bass-note that sound almost like voices. Almost like countless voices, layered and vast, speaking in a tongue that has no words.

The builders, you think, do not enter because entering is not for the living. The doorways are not passages. They are *membranes*. And what exists on the other side is the plateau's memory, its history, the accumulated song of every builder who has ever tended its stones and returned to its depths.

---

You begin to recognize individuals.

They are not distinct in the ways you expect—no faces that stick in memory, no marks of identity, no variation in form that signals selfhood. And yet each builder has a signature: a harmonic frequency in their song, a pattern of movement across the plateau, a particular way of touching the stone that marks their presence. You learn to read them the way you might read handwriting, recognizing the same hand in different texts.

There is one who tends the oldest formations, the towering spires that have grown for millennia. You find their harmonic thread woven through the plateau's song in the deepest registers, a bass-note that anchors the entire choir. This builder moves slowly even by local standards, their body seeming to carry the weight of the stones they tend. When they rest, they rest for long periods—days, perhaps weeks—curled in a hollow of their own creation, their song reduced to a whisper, barely audible, barely present.

There is another who moves constantly, never staying in one place, their song a bright running thread that dances through the plateau's frequencies. This builder tends the young growths, the new formations just emerging from the wet stone, and their attention is quick, light, passing from one to another in an endless circuit. They never seem to rest. They never seem to still. Perhaps they cannot—perhaps their nature requires motion, requires the constant running of water through new channels.

And there is one who tends the structures. Not the mineral formations but the built things—the cairns and rings and walls left by earlier visitors. This builder does not create. They preserve. You watch them repair a crumbling cairn with careful hands, using the same mineral mortar that the original builders used, matching the weathering of the existing stones with uncanny precision. They work on structures that are not plateau-native, that were made by hands that might have looked like yours, that came from somewhere else entirely. And they treat these structures with something that might be reverence, or might be curiosity, or might be the simple duty of preservation that applies to everything in their care.

---

You ask them—no, you do not ask. Asking requires language, requires the expectation of response. Instead, you wonder aloud, you vocalize, you let your confusion become sound in the thin cold air. And one of the builders hears.

They approach you slowly, their movement barely distinguishable from stillness, their song shifting to incorporate your presence. When they stand before you, they are shorter than you expected, their body denser, their deep-water eyes reflecting your face in duplicate. They do not speak. They touch you.

Their hand—cool, wet, firm with the mineral coating that covers everything here—presses against your chest. You feel the vibration of their song enter your body, resonating in your ribs, your spine, the hollow spaces within you. And for a moment, you *understand*.

Not in words. The understanding has no words. But you feel the plateau as they feel it: a vast organ of resonance, a living throat that has been singing since before humans learned to speak. You feel the purpose of the song—not communication, not exactly, but *continuation*. The plateau sings to maintain itself, to keep its reality stable, to push back against the entropy that would dissolve it. The builders are not separate from this process. They are its gardeners, its technicians, its extensions. They guide the water, shape the stone, tune the frequencies of the formations, ensure that the song remains harmonious, remains whole.

And you feel their history. Not a linear narrative—they do not experience time that way—but a depth, a weight, an accumulation. The builders have been here as long as the plateau has sung. They emerged from it, you understand now, were born from its processes in some ancient age when the water ran differently, when the minerals accreted in new patterns, when something that might have been life and might have been geology produced beings capable of intention. They are the plateau's self-awareness, its capacity to tend itself, its way of ensuring that the song does not falter.

They are not human. They never were.

---

The builder removes their hand. The connection breaks. You stand alone again in the bright cold light, the plateau's drone surrounding you, your body suddenly your own, small and temporary and strange.

You understand now why you cannot stay. Not because the plateau rejects you—it does not, it barely notices you—but because you cannot join the song. Your body does not resonate at the right frequencies. Your presence introduces dissonance, subtle but persistent. The builders tolerate you because tolerance costs nothing, because you will leave, because your disruption is temporary. But you cannot become what they are. You do not have the patience, the density, the capacity to accrete rather than consume.

They do not mourn this. Mourning requires attachment to outcomes, and the builders do not attach. They tend. They sing. They continue.

---

Before you leave, you find one more thing.

It is a structure—new, or new-ish, its mineral coating thin enough that you can see the tool-marks beneath. It is larger than the others, waist-high, a cairn of deliberate shape: wider at the base, tapering upward, with a hollow in its top that holds perfectly clear water. The water reflects the violet-blue sky, and when you look into it, you see your own face.

Someone made this for you. Or someone made this because you came. Or someone made this because making is what they do, and your presence introduced a variable that required acknowledgment, and this is the acknowledgment: a structure that marks your visit, that incorporates your brief existence into the plateau's record, that will grow and accrete and eventually join the song.

You place your hand on the cairn. You feel the water moving through it, the slow crystalline growth at its edges. You hum—not the plateau's song, not the builders' song, but something closer to both than anything you have sung before. The structure does not respond, not exactly. But the sound shifts, slightly, harmonics adjusting, and for a moment, you are part of the choir.

---

The builders do not watch you go.

They continue their work—the slow shaping, the patient tending, the endless song that holds the plateau together. They will be here after you have gone, after your species has risen and fallen, after the sky changes color and the water finds new channels. They will sing the plateau's name, whatever that name is, in whatever tongue comprises it. They will accrete and shed and accrete again. They will tend the doorway-membranes and preserve the visitor-structures and emerge from and return to the stone in cycles too slow to perceive.

And if you close your eyes, back in the world of warmth and green and growth, you will hear it still: the resonant harmonic drone, the voice of water through mineral, the song that precedes and outlasts all temporary things.

The Weeping Heights. The Singing Stone.

Home to those who are not inhabitants but extensions, not dwellers but *depths*.

Singing, always singing.

Saying something you cannot understand and will never forget.
