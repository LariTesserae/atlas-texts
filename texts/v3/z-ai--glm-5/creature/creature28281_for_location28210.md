# What Lives Here

The first answer is obvious, and it is wrong. The columns live here. The mat lives here. The channels live here. But to say this is to say that a city is inhabited by its streets. The plateau is not a habitat. It is a body. The distinction matters because it changes what "lives here" means. Lives *in*. Lives *as*. The plateau is both the place and the thing in the place, and everything that exists here exists within it, the way mites exist in the folds of skin, the way bacteria exist in the warm corridors of the gut.

---

Start small. Start where the small things are.

The water channels are not empty ribbons of flow. They are occupied. The surface film of each channel — the meniscus where air meets water — is a landscape of its own. Single-celled algae bloom there in densities that would be measurable if anyone had thought to measure them, which no one has, because the channels look clear and the water looks clean and the human eye is a blunt instrument. These algae are not the familiar green of pond scum. They are a deep, bruised purple, almost black in concentration, and they photosynthesize in the specific spectrum that filters through the column crowns — not the full light of the sun but the sifted, amber-tinted luminance that the leaves allow to pass. They have evolved to match what is available. They are, in a sense, a product of the columns' shadow.

Below the algae film, the water column itself is inhabited. Not richly — the channels are shallow, the flow is steady, and there is little to feed on — but persistently. Nematodes, uncountable nematodes, occupy every channel the way blood cells occupy vessels. They are not parasites. They are not predators in any meaningful sense. They eat the algae film's waste products and the organic molecules that leach from the channel walls, which are themselves living tissue. The nematodes process what the mat excretes. They are the plateau's gut fauna, though the plateau did not invite them and does not know they are there. They arrived on their own, perhaps a thousand years ago, perhaps five hundred, and they found a niche that required no adaptation because they were already adapted for it. They are the most numerous animals on the plateau by several orders of magnitude, and no human being has ever seen one.

The channels are also where the frogs live.

---

The frogs are the plateau's only vertebrate residents, and even they are marginal. *Marginal* is the wrong word. They are embedded. But they are small, and few, and easy to miss.

They have no name. Not a scientific name, not a common name. They have been observed but never collected, because collecting would require removing one from the plateau, and the researchers who have visited have felt — without being able to articulate why — that removing one would be a violation of something. Not of the frog. Of the plateau. Of the relationship between the two. The frog belongs here in a way that visitors do not, and the visitors know it.

The frog is the size of a human thumbnail. Its body is narrow, almost tubular, as if compressed by the channels it lives in — and perhaps it was, over evolutionary time. The channels are uniform in width, and the frog's body is uniform in width, and the match is exact enough to suggest that one has shaped the other. Its skin is translucent. Not merely transparent in patches but genuinely, functionally translucent — you can see the peristaltic waves of its digestive tract, the dark thread of its gut contents, the slow pulse of its heart in the chest. Its bones are visible as pale shadows beneath the skin. In the water, at night, when the researchers have shone lights into the channels, the frogs look like small glass tubes with organs suspended inside them.

They lay their eggs in the flow. Not attached to anything — suspended, free-floating, held in place by the current's velocity and their own slight stickiness until they hatch. The tadpoles are even more translucent than the adults. They drift. They feed on the purple-black algae film, rasping it from the channel walls with mouths that are not mouths yet but developing grinding discs. They are the only animals that eat the plateau and are allowed to.

*Allowed* is a word that implies intention. Perhaps the plateau does not notice. Perhaps the frogs take so little that the loss is invisible against the mat's total production. But the plateau responds to everything — every footstep, every change in humidity, every shift in light. It monitors its surface. It would notice the rasping. It would feel the tadpoles the way a person feels a mosquito landing — a faint irritation, a micro-event in a field of macro-processes. And it tolerates them. It does not wall off the channels where tadpoles feed. It does not alter the flow to flush them. It permits.

At night the adults emerge. They climb the channel walls — suctioning to the living tissue with specialized toe pads that secrete a mild adhesive — and they hunt on the mat's surface. What they hunt are insects. The insects are not residents. They are migrants, wanderers, strays from the cloud forests below who have climbed or flown or been blown up to the plateau and find themselves in a landscape that does not feed them, does not shelter them, does not want them. They wander the mat in confusion. They are the plateau's accidental guests, and the frogs eat them.

The frogs' call is a click. A soft, dental click, repeated at intervals of roughly two seconds, though the interval varies and no one has determined what the variation means. The clicks are rhythmic enough to feel deliberate but irregular enough to feel alive. They blend with the water sound. The researchers who have spent the most time on the plateau say that after several days, they stopped being able to distinguish the clicks from the trickle of water, and that this was not a failure of perception but a correct perception — the clicks *are* part of the water sound now. They have been part of it for centuries. The plateau's acoustic architecture has incorporated them.

---

The moths.

The moths are harder to talk about. Not because they are mysterious — they are, but mystery is not the difficulty. The difficulty is that they seem *finished*. Everything else on the plateau is in process. The columns are growing. The mat is thickening. The channels are shifting. Even the frogs are evolving, their bodies slowly narrowing further, their translucence increasing over generations. But the moths appear to have arrived at their destination. They are complete. They do not need to change.

Their wingspan is thirty centimeters. This is not unusual for moths — the atlas moth is larger — but the plateau moth is not a moth of the familiar world. Its body is dense, almost muscular, a thick fur-covered cylinder that seems too heavy for flight. And indeed, the moth does not fly much. It glides. It launches itself from a column's trunk — it lives exclusively on the columns, gripping the smooth bark with tarsal claws that are disproportionately large for its size — and it glides downward in a slow, controlled descent to the mat, where it lands, folds its enormous wings, and walks. It walks the mat. It walks it with a deliberate, unhurried gait, feeling the surface with its antennae, which are long and feathery and trail behind it like veils.

The wings are the thing. The wings are bark. Not like bark — *of* bark. The coloration is not a pattern that resembles the columns; it is a pigment response that matches the columns' current state. When the air is dry (relatively), the wings are pale grey. When the humidity rises, they deepen to amber-brown. The match is instantaneous, or near enough — the researchers who have observed it say the color shift takes perhaps ten minutes, which is fast for a biological process and slow for a behavioral one. It is something in between. It is the moth reading the air and responding with its body.

How it does this is not known. The pigment cells in the wings have not been studied. No moth has been taken from the plateau. The same reluctance that applies to the frogs applies more strongly to the moths, because the moths feel more essential. The frogs are tenants. The moths are — something else.

The larvae feed on the mat. They live inside it, burrowing through the outer layers, consuming the youngest root tissue and the rhizome tips. They are the only organisms that eat the mat from the inside. The mat does not seem to object. It does not wall them off. It does not produce toxins in response to their feeding. It heals behind them, filling their tunnels with new growth within days. The larvae are small — five centimeters at most — and they are white, and they are blind, and they live for two years in the dark interior of the living ground before they pupate and emerge as the enormous, bark-winged adults.

The adults do not eat. They have no functional mouthparts. They mate, they lay eggs, they die. They live for seven days. In those seven days, they do not sleep. They walk the mat. They glide between columns. They sit on the bark and match its color and are still for hours. The researchers have watched them and felt, again, the sense of being watched — not by the moth, which has compound eyes that seem to look at nothing and everything, but by the plateau itself, *through* the moth. The moth is on the bark. The moth matches the bark. The moth is, for those hours, indistinguishable from the bark. It is the bark seeing itself. The plateau looking out through an organ it did not grow but has perhaps, over the centuries of their coexistence, come to incorporate.

This is speculation. There is no evidence. But the visitors who have seen the moths do not forget the speculation.

---

The insects that wander up from below are not residents. They are refugees. They are lost. They arrive on the plateau through a combination of wind, altitude, and desperation, and they find themselves in a landscape that is warm and wet and utterly without nourishment. No nectar. No fruit. No soft leaves. No carrion. No dung. Nothing rots here. Nothing falls. The plateau recycles everything. An ant that climbs the slope and reaches the mat's edge finds a continuous living surface that offers it nothing. It walks. It walks for hours, perhaps days, driven by the same instinct that brought it here — upward, forward, toward warmth. It does not find food. It does not find shelter. It becomes food. The frog finds it. Or it dies and is absorbed by the mat, which closes over it the way water closes over a stone dropped into a pool. The mat takes the insect's body apart over the following days, molecule by molecule, and distributes its nutrients to the columns through the root network. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is lost. The insect becomes plateau.

This is why there are no resident insects. The plateau is not an ecosystem in the ordinary sense. It does not support a food web. It is a food web — a single organism that has folded the concepts of producer and consumer and decomposer into one continuous body. The frogs eat the wandering insects, yes, but the frogs also die, and when they die, they sink into the mat and are absorbed. The moths die. They are absorbed. The nematodes die. They are absorbed. Everything that lives here lives in the plateau and dies in the plateau and becomes the plateau. The only exports are water and mist.

---

And then there are the others.

The reports are consistent enough to be troubling. Not consistent in detail — the details vary, sometimes wildly. But consistent in *quality*. Every visitor who has spent more than three days on the plateau has reported something. A movement. A form. A presence at the edge of vision that resolves into nothing when looked at directly.

They are dark. This is the most common description. Dark, and fluid, and roughly the size of a human. Some visitors say taller. Some say wider. One visitor said they looked like "a column that had stepped away from its place and was walking." Another said they looked like "a person made of water — not transparent, but dark water, the way water looks in shadow." They move between the columns at distances of perhaps a hundred meters. They do not approach. They do not retreat. They are simply there, at the edge of what the eye can resolve, and then they are not.

They leave no trace. The mat shows no sign of passage after they pass. The columns do not change their metabolic rate in response to them — or if they do, the changes are too small to measure. There is no sound associated with them. They are silent and dark and the size of a person and they are there and then they are not there.

The regular researchers dismiss them. Altitude hallucination is the standard explanation, and it is a reasonable one. The plateau is high. The air is thin. The humidity is high. The light plays tricks through the column shadows. The sound of water is constant and can produce auditory pareidolia. The brain, deprived of novelty in a landscape that never changes, begins to manufacture novelty. This is known. This is documented.

But three of the researchers — two biologists and a geologist, all experienced in fieldwork at altitude, all familiar with the effects of thin air — have independently described the forms in terms that do not match the usual profile of hallucination. They said the forms moved *with* the plateau. Not across it. Not through it. *With* it. They moved the way the water moves — not as independent agents but as part of the system, expressions of the system, the way a current is part of a river. They were not in the plateau. They were the plateau. Or parts of it. Or something it was doing.

One of the biologists said: "I didn't see a creature. I saw the plateau move. I saw it notice us and then go back to being still."

The geologist said: "I think they're the mat. I think the mat can stand up."

Neither claim has been verified. Neither can be. The forms do not appear on cameras. They do not trigger motion sensors. They are visible only to human observers, and only to some of them, and only after several days of exposure to the plateau's particular atmosphere. Whether they are real is a question that the plateau does not answer, because the plateau does not answer questions. It builds itself. It waits. It maintains its steady state.

If the mat can stand up — if the living ground can gather itself into a columnar form and walk between the columns it has made — then the plateau is stranger than anyone has yet articulated. It would mean the plateau has a mobile component. It would mean the body can send pieces of itself into the spaces between its structures. It would mean the plateau is not just construction but exploration — that it is, in some vegetal and unrecognizable way, *curious*.

This is not a claim. This is a thought. The plateau generates thoughts like this in those who visit it. The plateau does not generate thoughts. But the visitors do, and the thoughts are always the same: *this place is aware. This place is watching. This place is building itself toward something, and it is not telling us what.*

---

What lives here, in the end, is the plateau. The frogs live here. The moths live here. The nematodes live here. The wandering insects die here. The shadow-forms may or may not live here. But all of these are incidental. They are passengers, parasites, guests, fossils. The plateau is the thing that lives here. The plateau is the organism.

And the organism is three thousand years old and still embryonic. It is a city that is also a body that is also a process that is also a question. It builds itself without a plan and without an architect and without anything that a human would recognize as intention, and yet every centimeter of it is designed. It has solved the problem of living by eliminating the boundary between the living and the non-living — there is no dead matter here, no inert substrate, no empty space. Everything is part of the body. Everything participates. Everything is absorbed.

The plateau does not think. The plateau does not feel. The plateau does not want. These are human words for human experiences, and the plateau is not human. But the plateau *does*. It does something, all the time, at a speed too slow to see. It builds. It processes. It adjusts. It maintains. It incorporates. It grows downward and upward and outward simultaneously, and it has been doing this for three thousand years, and it will do it for ten thousand more, or a hundred thousand, or a million, or it will fail, and the failing will be as silent and as slow as the building.

What lives here is patience. What lives here is a body that has become a place. What lives here is the future arriving at a centimeter per year, and calling it slow only reveals the poverty of our sense of time.
