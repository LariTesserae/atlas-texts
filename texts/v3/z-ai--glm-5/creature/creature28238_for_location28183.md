# Those Who Dwell in Ashkilth

They are, first and always, listeners. Not by training but by adaptation. The constant sound of water—dripping, flowing, seeping, freezing, thawing—has rewired something in the way they process sound. New arrivals speak too loudly, their voices bouncing strange and harsh off the wet stone. Long-term residents have learned a different register. They speak close to the throat, in frequencies that carry through the moisture-heavy air without scattering. Their voices have a quality that outsiders sometimes find unsettling: intimate yet distant, as if each word is being whispered directly into the listener's ear from several meters away. They have developed a way of speaking that works with the acoustics rather than fighting them, and when they travel to lower elevations, they often find themselves unable to project. They have forgotten how to raise their voices. The shouting of lowland cities feels to them like a kind of violence.

The oldest among them—there are perhaps two dozen who remember the emergence, who were present when the plateau rose—carry a particular quality in their bearing. They witnessed something that most humans never witness: the birth of a landscape. Not the slow geological birth of erosion and deposition, but the violent rapid birth of uplift and emergence. They watched stone that had been seafloor become mountain in the span of months. They felt the earth break beneath them and rearrange itself into something new. This has left them with a relationship to solid ground that is fundamentally different from other humans. They do not trust stability. They know that the rock beneath their feet is capable of sudden motion, that the crust itself can decide to fold upward and expose its wet interiors. They walk with a kind of readiness, a deep readiness, as if at any moment the ground might speak again. Some of them have developed a sensitivity to subtle vibrations—perhaps real, perhaps imagined—that they interpret as signs of further geological change. They are not always wrong.

The water has marked them in other ways. Their skin is always slightly damp, or if not damp then preparing to be, the pores open and waiting. They do not use moisturizers—the air here provides all the moisture their skin could want—and when they descend to lower elevations, they often find themselves uncomfortable in the dryness. Their bodies have adjusted to constant wetness and have come to expect it. Their hands are permanently pruned at the fingertips, the skin softened and pale from the water. They smell of stone and mineral, faintly sulfuric, and their hair has a limpness that no product can correct. They do not mind. Cleanliness here is a matter of rinsing, and the water is always available, always flowing, and they wash constantly without thinking about it, their hands running under the thin sheets of moisture that crawl across every surface.

The residents eat with a peculiar intensity. The cold demands it. They consume fats and proteins in quantities that would alarm a lowland nutritionist, and they have developed a cuisine that centers on density rather than flavor. The goats they keep—those engineered animals with their expanded lungs and thick wool—provide milk and meat and cheese that tastes faintly of the minerals in the water they drink. The residents have learned to enjoy this taste, or at least to no longer notice it. Their palates have shifted to expect the slight saltiness, the trace of sulfur, the mineral depth that comes from food raised on fossil water. They eat quickly and they eat much and they eat often, and still they are always slightly hungry. The cold and the altitude keep their metabolisms running hot, burning through whatever they provide. No one here is overweight. The environment would not permit it.

They have developed a relationship with the domains that borders on the devotional. These are not pets and they are not precisely tools; they are something in between, semi-living infrastructure that the residents have come to regard with a kind of affectionate respect. The engineers speak to them sometimes, murmuring encouragement when a filter seems sluggish, offering thanks when the water runs particularly clean. This is not scientific behavior, and they know it, but the domains respond to something—perhaps the vibration of the voice, perhaps the carbon dioxide in the breath—and the engineers have learned that speaking kindly seems to improve performance. Some residents have developed a sense for the domains' rhythms, an ability to feel when they are struggling or thriving, and they adjust their behavior accordingly. They walk more softly past walls where the glow is dim. They avoid touching surfaces where the luminescence has concentrated. They have learned to read the city's health in the glow of its walls.

The salamanders occupy a different place in their attention. The residents respect the salamanders and fear them a little and depend on them completely. These pale eyeless creatures are the only living things that seem truly native to the city's conditions, and the residents have developed a complex etiquette around their presence. When a salamander appears in a water channel—a rare but not unheard-of event—there is a protocol to follow. You do not touch it with bare hands. You do not startle it. You guide it gently back into the deeper channels using tools set aside for this purpose. You check the downstream filters for signs of distress. The salamanders are treated as both guests and guardians, and their presence in the drinking water, while alarming, is also considered a kind of blessing. A salamander-sighting means the channels are flowing freely, the system is working, the city is alive. It is only when the salamanders disappear entirely that the residents begin to worry.

Children born here—if they are born here, which is rare—have a different quality from the start. Their lungs develop differently in the thin air, their chests slightly deeper, their blood cells more numerous. They are born into a world of wetness and darkness and glow, and their eyes adjust to the low light in ways that lowland children's eyes do not. They can see in illumination that would seem black to others, their rods and cones optimized for the bioluminescence and the filtered grey light. They learn to walk on wet stone before they learn to walk on dry ground. They learn the sound of water before they learn words. They speak the close-throated language of the city as their native tongue, and when they travel to lower elevations—if they travel—they find the shouting voices and the dry air and the hard shadows almost unbearable. They are native to a place that is not quite a natural environment, not quite a built environment, but something in between, and they belong to it in a way their parents never will.

The researchers who come to Ashkilth come for specific reasons. They come to study the water—its composition, its age, its behavior in the unique conditions of the uplift. They come to study the stone—its fracture patterns, its mineral content, its slow erosion. They come to study the domains—the semi-living filters that were created here and exist nowhere else, whose operating principles are still not fully understood. Some of them stay longer than they intended. Some of them never leave. The city has a way of claiming people, of convincing them that their work here is unfinished, that there is always more to observe and understand. The water never stops flowing, the stone never stops weeping, and there is always another measurement to take, another sample to collect, another data point to add to the slowly accumulating understanding of this strange place. The residents speak of being caught, of having intended to stay for a season and finding themselves still here decades later. They speak of the difficulty of leaving once you have become attuned to the water-song, once your body has learned to expect the moisture and the cold and the glow.

They have a particular way of moving through the corridors. They do not hurry—hurry is dangerous on wet stone—and they do not dawdle—dawdling means losing heat. They move at a steady deliberate pace, their feet finding purchase on surfaces that would be treacherous to the uninitiated. They know which way the water flows, which surfaces freeze at night, which passages accumulate condensation and which stay relatively dry. They can read the walls for moisture content, noting the slight changes in color that indicate where the water is concentrating. They navigate by sound as much as by sight, following the water-song of the building to orient themselves. A stranger can get lost in Ashkilth; the corridors fold back on themselves, the chambers connect in unexpected ways, the darkness and the moisture disorient. But a resident always knows where they are, always knows the way to their destination, because they are reading the building in ways that are not entirely conscious.

They dream of water. This is nearly universal among the residents, and it does not seem to matter how long they have lived here or what they studied before they came. They dream of rising water, of the slow submersion that the engineers predict. They dream of the lower chambers, the ones that have already been abandoned, filling slowly with dark water that glows faintly with domain-light. They dream of the salamanders multiplying in those drowned rooms, growing larger in the darkness, developing eyes to see by the glow, becoming something new. They dream of the water rising past the level they have prepared for, rising faster than the calculations predicted, rising to meet them in their sleep. They wake with the taste of minerals in their mouths, and they go to the walls and touch the stone and feel for the dampness, reassuring themselves that the water is still where it should be, still flowing through the channels, still being caught and cleaned and circulated.

There is a particular demographic that Ashkilth attracts: not the young and ambitious, who want to build and grow and be seen, but the middle-aged and patient, who have learned that some work requires quiet and time and hiddenness. They are people who have already had careers elsewhere, who have already made names or failed to make names or realized that names are not the point. They come to Ashkilth to do work that will not be published quickly, that will not make headlines, that may not be understood for centuries. They come to study processes that unfold slowly, that require decades of observation, that are not suited to the frantic pace of lowland science. Some of them are running from something—failed marriages, professional disasters, political complications—but others are running toward something, toward a kind of work that can only be done here, in conditions that exist nowhere else on earth.

They have developed a culture of careful observation and minimal intervention. They have learned that the city is most stable when left to its own processes, that the water and the stone and the domains have their own equilibria that should be disturbed as little as possible. They do repairs when repairs are needed, but they do not make improvements. They do not expand. They do not try to make the city more comfortable or more efficient or more suited to human preferences. They accept the cold and the wet and the darkness as conditions of their presence, and they do not fight them. This has given them a reputation among those who know of them for being austere, severe, perhaps a little monkish in their dedication. They do not mind. They know that their work requires this austerity, that the city would not tolerate a more demanding population, that they are guests here more than owners.

They keep records in a way that is specific to their conditions. The humidity and the cold make paper unreliable, and they have developed an alternative: they carve their data into stone tablets that are stored in the driest chambers, or they encode their observations into patterns of domain-growth that can be read by trained eyes. They have a writing system that uses the bioluminescence itself, coaxing the domains to glow in specific patterns that persist for years before fading. This writing is not easy to produce—it requires an understanding of the domains' chemistry that takes decades to develop—but it is durable and it is appropriate to the environment, and the residents have come to prefer it to any imported alternative. Their archives are kept in a deep chamber where the glow of the domains is brightest, and the walls there are covered in slowly pulsing script, records of a hundred years of observation in a language that only a few can read.

They are waiting, though they do not always acknowledge this. They are waiting for the plateau to stabilize, for the water to find its final level, for the erosion to begin the long process of creating soil and seed-bed and eventual plant life. They are waiting to see what Ashkilth will become when it is no longer young, when its sharp edges have been softened, when its walls have been worn by the water into something smoother. They know they will not see this themselves—these processes take longer than human lifespans—but they have been taught by the stone and the water to think in longer scales, to imagine futures that they will not inhabit. They are a bridge generation, a community that exists in the gap between one geological moment and the next, and they have made peace with their temporary position. They will be forgotten, eventually. The city will drown or it will endure, and either way the specific residents who lived here in its first centuries will be footnotes in a longer history. They have accepted this. They work and they observe and they record and they wait, and the water runs past them, and the stone weeps beneath them, and the glow of the domains lights their way through the darkness, and they are, for now, alive in a place that is not quite made for human life, but close enough, for now, close enough.
