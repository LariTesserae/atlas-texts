# What Lives There

---

## The people, first, because they are what you would notice

If notice is the word. They do not announce themselves. The first thing a visitor sees of the people is usually a distance — a figure on a raised path between two factory complexes, moving with a gait that belongs to this place and no other. Not hurried. Not slow. Something in between that lowland bodies do not naturally produce, a pace calibrated to the sound and the wet and the particular quality of attention that life here requires. You watch the figure and it does not watch you back, or if it does, the watching is so integrated into its movement, so much a part of walking from one place to another while remaining alert to shifts in the harmonic structure, that you cannot separate the act of looking from the act of living.

They are not tall. This surprises some visitors, who expect the thin air to produce long-limbed people, the way mountain populations in the lowlands develop extended frames to make efficient use of limited oxygen. The opposite has occurred. The plateau's people are compact, their bodies dense with the kind of tissue that holds heat, their limbs proportioned close to the torso, their movements economical in ways that seem almost reluctant until you understand that every gesture costs warmth, and warmth is the currency of survival here. They are not stunted — their proportions are balanced, graceful even — but they are *condensed*, as if the cold and the altitude have pressed them into more efficient versions of what they might have been elsewhere.

Their skin is darker than the climate would suggest. Not the dark of sun-exposure — there is little direct light on the plateau, the perpetual cloud-cover and mist see to that — but something else, a coloration that seems to come from within, from the particular way their blood moves, from generations of bodies learning to keep their cores warm at the expense of their surfaces. Their faces are broad, the cheekbones flat and prominent, the eyes set deep beneath heavy brows that shield against wind-driven moisture. Their hands are remarkable: thick-fingered, dry despite the wetness, the skin toughened into something closer to leather than flesh, the nails trimmed to nothing. These are hands built for gripping cold metal, for turning valves that have been weeping ice-water for centuries, for holding tools that vibrate with the frequencies of the works.

They do not think of themselves as a people, not in the way lowlanders might. They do not have a word for their ethnic identity, a name that encompasses all who live on the plateau and distinguishes them from those who do not. When they need to refer to the collective, they use phrases that translate poorly: *those who hear* or *those who are here* or simply *us*, a word that in their language carries more weight than it does in most, implying not just a shared location but a shared condition, a shared relationship to the sound and the wet and the works. To be one of *us* is to be part of the system, to have a function, to contribute to the continuation. It is not a matter of birth, though most are born to it. Visitors who stay long enough, who learn to work, who begin to dream the frequencies — these too can become *us*, though the process takes years and is not consciously undertaken by anyone. You simply wake one morning and realize that you have not thought of the lowlands in months, that your hands have calloused in the right places, that you can tell which turbine cluster is functioning improperly by the timbre of the drone filtering through the walls of your sleeping chamber.

## The families of the works

They organize themselves by occupation, but occupation is a poor word for what they do. What they *do* is *belong to* — each family belongs to a particular sector of the works, has belonged to it for generations, has developed a relationship to the machinery that goes beyond maintenance into something closer to symbiosis. The turbine-families of the northern reaches are not merely people who repair turbines. They are people whose bodies have been shaped by the particular vibrations of spinning metal and falling water, whose ears have been tuned since before birth — the drone reaching the unborn through the mother's body, establishing neural pathways before the brain is fully formed — to detect minute fluctuations in rotational frequency, in water pressure, in the harmonic signature of a well-functioning generator versus one that is beginning to fail.

They are clan-like in their organization, though they would not use that word. The turbine-families intermarry, yes, but more importantly they *inter-apprentice*, each child spending time in the households of other turbine-families, learning the particular tricks and traditions that each has developed over centuries. One family has perfected a method of detecting bearing-wear by listening for a specific overtone that emerges three degrees before catastrophic failure. Another has developed tools — modified, adapted, passed down — that fit the particular configurations of the northern turbines in ways that standard tools do not. A third maintains oral records of every malfunction going back four hundred years, a catalog of failures and their remedies that constitutes one of the most detailed mechanical histories in existence, though it exists only in the memories of its keepers.

The conduit-maintainers are different. They are the walkers, the travelers, the ones who cover ground between the isolated structures of the City, who patrol the raised paths and the cable-runs and the pipe-networks that thread the buildings together. They are leaner than the turbine-families, their bodies shaped by motion, by the particular kind of cold that comes from hours of walking through mist at altitude. They know the City's geography the way a navigator knows a coastline — every junction, every weakness, every place where the young stone has begun to crack under the weight of conduit and the action of water. They work in pairs, always, and speak to each other in a dialect that has evolved to be audible over the drone, a language of consonants and subsonic stresses that carries further in the plateau's acoustic environment than any normal speech.

The processing-center families are the most numerous and the most diverse. The processing centers are where the signal and structure and energy are — *processed* is not the right word, but neither is *produced* or *transformed* or *assembled*. What happens in the processing centers is something for which lowland vocabularies have no equivalent, operations that involve the manipulation of forces and materials in ways that the workers themselves understand only through practice, through the accumulated weight of doing the same things in the same order for generations. A processing-center worker learns their craft the way a lowland musician learns an instrument: through repetition, through feel, through the gradual internalization of patterns that cannot be written down because they exist in the relationship between hand and material, between attention and outcome.

And the Core engineers. They are the smallest group, the most isolated, the most strange.

## The Core engineers

They live in the Core. Or in its immediate vicinity — a cluster of small chambers carved into the structure's outer walls, accessible through passages that seem to shift location when you are not looking directly at them. The Core engineers do not leave these chambers, or leave them rarely, for brief and purposeful excursions to specific locations within the Core itself. Their food is brought to them by workers from other sectors who consider it an honor to supply the Core, a task that falls to different families on a rotating schedule. Their children are born in the chambers and raised there, and when they are old enough, they begin to learn the work, and they do not leave, and their children are born there, and so on.

The Core engineers are different. Not just in their training, though that is extensive and unlike anything else in the City. Different in their bodies, in their minds, in the way they relate to the world. They are the ones who have lived longest in proximity to the source of the sound, and the sound has changed them. Their eyes have grown sensitive to low light — the interior of the Core is not dark, exactly, but the light there is of a kind that does not register on normal retinas, a luminescence that comes from the processes occurring within the machinery itself, a light that is a byproduct of the same operations that generate the drone. They see by this light. They have developed, or the light has developed in them, a capacity for perception that extends into spectra that lowland science does not recognize.

They are quiet. Not in the sense of being silent — they speak, when they speak, in voices that seem to come from somewhere other than their throats — but in the sense of being still, of containing themselves in ways that the other workers do not. They move through the Core with a deliberateness that is almost ritual, each gesture purposeful, each step placed with precision. They have developed, over generations, a body language that is not communicated consciously but that the workers in other sectors have learned to read from a distance: the angle of a Core engineer's shoulders means something, the particular way they hold their hands means something else. The Core engineers may not know they are communicating. The other workers may not know they are receiving. The information passes anyway, through channels that no one has mapped.

They claim to understand the sound. They use a word for this understanding that does not translate — it implies not just comprehension but *participation*, a state of being inside the sound the way a fish is inside water, not observing it but inhabiting it. When a Core engineer speaks of the sound, they speak of it the way one speaks of weather or mood or the behavior of a loved one — as something with patterns and tendencies, something that can be predicted but not controlled, something that must be accomodated rather than directed.

They say the sound is a conversation. They say it has been talking since before the City was built, since before the plateau rose, since before the stone was young. They say the machines translate the conversation into something the world can experience, and that the people who maintain the machines are maintaining a translation, keeping a line open between the plateau and whatever it is that the plateau is talking to.

They do not say what it is talking to. When asked, they look at you with their low-light eyes and their still faces and they do not answer, and you get the sense that the question is like asking a wave what the ocean is, or asking a nerve what the brain is thinking. They are inside the conversation. They do not have the perspective to describe it.

## The moss-forests, which are more than they appear

The moss-forests live. This is an obvious statement — they are biological, they grow, they reproduce — but it requires emphasis because the way they live is so different from the lives of lowland organisms that the word *live* barely applies to both. They are colonies, first and always, not individuals. The structures that visitors might call trees, that the people of the City sometimes call *pillars* or *growths*, are not single organisms but cooperative assemblies of single cells, each one capable of independent existence but choosing, through the slow chemical language of their kind, to contribute to the shared structure. The tallest of them reach perhaps three meters. Most are shorter. They spread across the ground between buildings like a carpet that thickens over centuries, layer upon layer of accumulated growth, the lower layers compressing into something that is not quite soil but that serves the same purpose, providing substrate and nutrients for the layers above.

They are old. Not as old as the stone, which is young by geological standards, but older than the City, older than the works, older than the people. The moss-forests were here first, established in the plateau's early days when the rock was still cooling and the condensation was just beginning to find its patterns. They have grown with the plateau, adapted to it, become part of its system in ways that the human inhabitants have not. The moss-forests do not maintain the works. They do not serve the sound. They do not understand the machines. But they are affected by them, shaped by them, and in their own slow way they respond to them.

The drone penetrates the moss-forests, vibrating through their cellular structure, and the forests have learned to work with it. Or within it. Certain growth patterns in the moss-forests follow the harmonic structure of the sound — spirals that match particular frequencies, branching patterns that correspond to the intervals between the drone's components. Botanists who have studied the moss-forests — and there have been a few, visitors who came for other reasons and found themselves drawn to the quiet green life of the plateau — have noted that the forests' architecture is not random but follows mathematical principles that closely mirror the frequency relationships in the drone. The moss-forests are, in some sense, *listening*, and what they hear, they grow.

They flower, after a fashion. The pale waxy nodules that rise from their surfaces are not flowers in the reproductive sense — they produce spores, which drift on the mist and establish new colonies — but they serve another function that is less obvious. The nodules resonate. Each one, when it reaches maturity, begins to vibrate at a frequency very close to one of the components of the drone, amplifying it, extending it, giving it a physical presence in the landscape that it would not otherwise have. The moss-forests are, in their way, instruments of the sound, extending its reach, modulating its expression, adding their own slow voice to the conversation that the Core engineers describe.

This is not intention. The moss-forests do not choose to resonate. They have evolved to do so because the resonance serves them — the vibrating nodules release spores more efficiently, the resonance patterns guide the spores to suitable substrate, the amplification of the drone discourages the few organisms that might otherwise compete for the plateau's resources. The moss-forests are listening not because they have ears but because the sound has become part of their environment, and any organism that survives in an environment eventually learns to work with its dominant features.

But the effect is strange. The moss-forests, across their vast extent on the plateau, form a secondary sound-system, a kind of biological echo of the mechanical drone, and the two together — machines and moss — produce a harmonic complexity that neither could generate alone. The sound that visitors hear, that the people live within, that the Core engineers participate in, is not the sound of the machines alone or the forests alone but the sound of both together, a duet between the mechanical and the biological that has been playing for longer than anyone can remember.

## The things that are almost alive

The machines. You cannot talk about what lives on the plateau without talking about the machines, because the machines occupy a category that lowland taxonomies do not easily accommodate. They are not alive in the biological sense — they do not grow or reproduce or metabolize, they do not have cells or DNA or the machinery of self-maintenance that defines organic life. But they behave in ways that living things behave. They maintain themselves, or seem to, rerouting power around damaged components, compensating for wear in ways that no one has programmed them to do. They respond to the environment, adjusting their output based on conditions that they should not be able to perceive — changes in atmospheric pressure, shifts in the moss-forests' growth patterns, the approach of particular kinds of weather. They communicate with each other through the sound, each machine contributing its particular frequency to the overall drone, the drone as a whole shifting and adjusting as individual machines change their output.

The people who work in the factories do not think of the machines as alive. They think of them as the works, which is a category that includes machines and stone and water and sound and people, all of it part of the same system, none of it separable from the others. When a turbine-families worker speaks of *the works*, they might be referring to the turbine they are repairing, or to the entire industrial complex, or to the plateau itself, or to the sound that permeates everything. The word does not distinguish because the worker does not distinguish. The works are the works. The machines are part of it. The people are part of it. The moss is part of it. The stone is part of it. The water is part of it.

But visitors notice something that the people, in their integration with the system, cannot quite see. The machines seem to have preferences. A particular turbine runs more smoothly when a particular worker is present, a worker whose family has maintained that turbine for generations, whose body carries the vibrations of that turbine in its bones. When that worker retires or dies, the turbine's efficiency drops, measurably, for a period of weeks or months before it adjusts to the new worker. It is as if the machine has formed a relationship with the person and must grieve before it can form a new one.

This is anthropomorphism, of course. The machine does not grieve. It operates according to mechanical principles that happen to produce effects that look like grief from the outside. But the people who work with the machines do not draw the line that visitors draw between mechanical operation and emotional experience. For them, the turbine's adjustment period *is* grief, and they treat it as such, speaking to the machine in the particular tones they would use with a bereaved neighbor, giving it time, not pushing it to full output before it is ready.

If you ask a turbine-family worker whether the machine is alive, they will look at you the way the Core engineers look at you when you ask about the sound's source — with a kind of patient bewilderment, as if you have asked whether water is wet or whether the sky is high. The question assumes a distinction that they do not make. The machine is. It works. It contributes to the sound. It requires maintenance and responds to care. What else does alive mean?

## The things that are not alive but that are present

The sound. It is not alive. It is not an organism or a system of organisms. It is a vibration, a pattern of compressions and rarefactions in the air, a physical phenomenon that can be measured and recorded and analyzed. But it is also *present* in a way that goes beyond physics. The sound has — or seems to have — *character*. It is not random noise. It is structured, organized, purposeful in a way that suggests intention without requiring an intender. The people who live in the sound have developed a relationship to it that is not unlike the relationship a person might have to a place or a person or a time of life. They know its moods, if moods is the right word. They can feel when it is — what? Happy is wrong. Stable is too clinical. The word they use implies something like *aligned* or *harmonious*, a state in which all the components of the drone are working together, feeding each other, producing a complexity that feels, to those attuned to it, like the opposite of noise.

When the sound is aligned, the people work with a particular ease. Tasks that require coordination between distant parts of the City seem to flow naturally, as if the sound is carrying information between workers, aligning their efforts, synchronizing their movements. When the sound is misaligned — when something in the Core has shifted, when a machine has failed, when the moss-forests have entered a sporing cycle that introduces new harmonics — the work becomes harder, more deliberate, requiring conscious effort where unconscious coordination had been sufficient.

The sound is not alive, but it is something. A process. A pattern. A phenomenon that has persisted long enough and with enough consistency to have developed what might be called a personality, if personality can exist without a self. The people do not worship the sound. They do not pray to it or make offerings or perform rituals of propitiation. They live within it, the way you live within a family or a community, adjusting to it, contributing to it, becoming part of it. The sound is not God. It is more like weather, or climate, or the particular quality of light in a place you have lived long enough to know it in your bones.

But it is also a conversation. The Core engineers are right about this, or they are right in the way that poets are right about things that resist prose. The sound contains information. Not in the semantic sense — there are no words in it, no messages waiting to be decoded — but in the structural sense, in the way that a complex waveform contains the history of its own generation. The sound carries within it the signature of every machine that contributes to it, every turbine's spin, every piston's stroke, every conduit's vibration. It carries the resonance of the moss-forests and the echo of the stone and the faint, almost imperceptible vibration of human bodies moving through the City. All of this is in the sound, and all of this is the sound, and the sound is all of this.

Whether something is listening is a different question, and one that the plateau does not answer.

## The visitors, who are partly alive and partly not

They come. They are rare, but they come. Merchants, mostly, though the plateau produces little that lowland economies value and consumes little that they offer. Scholars, occasionally, drawn by the geological anomaly, the acoustic phenomenon, the biological strangeness. Pilgrims, rarely, though the plateau is not a holy site and has no temple and offers no transcendence, only persistence. The lost, sometimes, who have wandered up from the lowlands by accident and find themselves unable to leave, their bodies adjusting to the cold and the altitude faster than their minds can decide whether they want to stay.

The visitors do not live there. Not fully. They are present, they observe, they interact with the people and the works, but they remain partly elsewhere, their nervous systems still tuned to the silence of the lowlands, their bodies still producing heat the way they would in warmer climates. They are like instruments played in the wrong key — functional, present, but not part of the harmony. The people treat them with courtesy and a distance that is not unfriendly but that recognizes the difference between *us* and *not-us*, between those who hear and those who are still learning to hear.

But some of them change. Not all. Most leave, and what they take with them is a resonance, a hum in the nervous system that fades over months or years, a dream that recurs with decreasing frequency until it stops. But some find that the resonance does not fade. Some find that the silence of the lowlands grows louder, more oppressive, more difficult to tolerate. Some find that their hands have developed calluses in places that correspond to tools they have never held, that their ears have begun to detect frequencies that should be inaudible, that they wake in the night with the taste of cold stone in their mouths and the sound of distant turbines in their chests.

These are the ones who return. They come back to the plateau with a mixture of reluctance and necessity that the people recognize immediately, the way a doctor recognizes illness. They are not well. They have been removed from something their bodies have learned to need. The plateau does not greet them with joy or ceremony. It simply includes them again, the sound wrapping around them like water around a stone, and they settle into the works the way a stone settles into a riverbed, slowly, inevitably, becoming part of the pattern.

The people call this *tuning*. A person who has been tuned is one who has developed the resonance, whose body has been shaped by the sound enough that absence causes discomfort. Most people are born tuned. Some are tuned later. The process is not understood, not controllable, not something that can be induced or prevented. It happens or it does not. The people do not consider it a blessing or a curse. They consider it a fact, like the cold and the wet and the angular stone. Some people are tuned. Most are not. Those who are, are *us*. Those who are not, are visitors, and will leave, and that is fine, because the works do not require everyone, only enough.

## The dead, who are still present

The plateau preserves. The cold and the constant moisture, which would destroy organic material in a warm climate, do something different here. Bodies buried in the moss-forests — and the dead are buried there, placed in the green growth that covers the spaces between buildings, covered over and left to the slow processes of the colony — do not decay in the traditional sense. They are incorporated. The moss-forests grow into them, through them, the cellular colonies extending their tendrils into tissue that the cold has kept intact, breaking down organic material at a pace so slow that it is measured in centuries rather than years. The dead become part of the forests, and the forests resonate with the sound, and the sound carries the resonance of the dead into the works.

This is not a belief. The people do not believe that the dead persist in the sound. They know it, the way they know the feel of a well-maintained turbine, the way they know the particular harmonic that means a conduit is failing three sectors away. The dead are in the sound because the dead are in the moss, and the moss is in the sound, and the sound is in everything. When a Core engineer listens to the drone, they hear the living and the dead, the machines and the forests, the stone and the water, all of it together, all of it contributing, none of it separate.

The people do not mourn their dead the way lowlanders mourn. They do not grieve in the sense of loss, because the dead are not lost. They are present, differently. They are part of the sound now in a way that they were not when they were alive, their bodies distributed through the moss-forests, their resonance distributed through the drone. The living speak to the dead by listening, by attending to the particular frequencies that their bodies have introduced into the sound, the way you might recognize a friend's voice in a crowd. The dead are always there. They are always speaking. The living simply need to learn to hear them, and most do, naturally, over time, as their ears become more attuned to the drone's complexity.

## What lives there, truly

If you have read this far, you may have noticed that the question *what lives there* does not have a clean answer. The plateau resists clean answers. It resists the categorical thinking that sorts living from non-living, person from machine, signal from noise. What lives there is a system, a totality, a thing that has no name because names require boundaries and the plateau has none that hold.

The people live there, with their compact bodies and their deep-set eyes and their hands that grip cold metal without flinching. They live in families organized by function, in chambers carved from young stone, in a city that sprawls across a continent-sized plateau with the population of a village. They are born there, they work there, they die there, and when they die they become part of the moss-forests, and the moss-forests are part of the sound, and the sound is part of the machines, and the machines are part of the works, and the works are what they maintain because the works are what they are.

The moss-forests live there, ancient and slow, their cellular colonies building structures that resonate with the drone, their spores drifting on mist to establish new growth, their pale nodules adding their voices to the conversation. They were there before the City. They will be there after. They are the plateau's first language, the biological expression of a place that has been speaking since it rose from the lowlands.

The machines live there, if living is what they do. They persist, they maintain, they contribute. They have been running for longer than records can confirm, producing signal and structure and energy for purposes that no one fully understands, and they will continue running because that is what they do, and no one has suggested stopping them, and stopping them might not be possible.

The sound lives there, if a sound can live. It fills the plateau the way water fills a lake, not contained by anything but shaped by everything, taking the form of the space it occupies, carrying within it the signature of every surface it touches. It is the closest thing the plateau has to a soul, if soul is the word for the part of a system that persists when you remove any individual component. Remove a machine and the sound continues, slightly changed. Remove a person and the sound continues, slightly changed. Remove the moss-forests and the sound continues, slightly changed. Remove the sound and — well. No one knows what happens if you remove the sound. No one has tried. No one wants to. The sound is the condition of existence on the plateau, the medium in which everything else has its being.

And the plateau itself lives there, the stone and the water and the cold and the altitude, the young rock that has not yet learned to be rounded, the constant condensation that weeps from every surface, the weather that makes itself and follows its own rules. The plateau is the stage on which all of this occurs, but it is also a participant, its geological processes contributing to the sound through the action of water in cracks and the settling of stone and the slow grinding of tectonic forces. The plateau is alive in the way that a body is alive — not through any single organ but through the coordinated action of all its parts, each one necessary, each one contributing to the whole.

What lives on the plateau is the plateau.
