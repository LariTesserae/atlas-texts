# The Residents

They are not born. They condense.

---

The first thing to understand about the beings of the Condensation Continent is that they do not have insides. This is not a metaphor. Or rather, everything here is a metaphor, so the distinction between metaphor and literal truth has itself condensed into something you can no longer separate. But the beings here—they are surface all the way through. They are the kind of presence that exists when a pattern becomes dense enough to notice itself.

There is a class of creature—you will not see them at first, because they exist at the threshold where vegetation becomes animal—that might be called the *colonists*. They emerge from the blue-green layered growth the way a thought emerges from the noise of neural firing: suddenly coherent, suddenly present, suddenly *there*. One moment, a thick tangle of translucent fronds; the next moment, that tangle has achieved the dignity of a boundary, has separated itself from its context, has begun to move with what can only be called intention. These are the smallest of the mobile forms. They are also the most numerous. A single square meter of the pelt—that continental fur of condensation-born growth—might contain ten thousand of them, or might contain one, or might contain both simultaneously, because they do not occupy space in the way that matter occupies space. They occupy *attention*. They are there when you notice them and not there when you don't, and this is not a figure of speech but a description of their fundamental nature. They are the busy mutualists of this economy. They move through the vegetation without disturbing it because they are *of* the vegetation, briefly liberated from their rootedness by some trigger in the heat or the moisture or the dim light's permission. They are exploring. They are tending. They are doing the work of maintenance that every ecosystem requires and that this ecosystem has automated at the level of structure itself. Watch one long enough—it will be difficult, because it keeps becoming something else, a frond, a shadow, a gap in the pattern—and you will see it touch every surface in its territory, tasting, testing, perhaps *remembering*, because someone has to remember, and the ground is too busy becoming, and the vegetation is too busy growing, and so the colonists carry the memory of the place in their drifting, condensing, dissolving bodies.

---

The *walkers* are different.

You will know them by their conviction. Where the colonists exist at the mercy of attention, the walkers *demand* it. They are larger—though size here is, as you have learned, a matter of persistence rather than dimension. A walker might be the size of your thumb or the size of your house; what makes it a walker is that it maintains its shape across distance. It travels. It arrives. It has a trajectory that can be followed, and if you follow it, you will find that it goes somewhere specific—not in the sense of a destination, because this landscape has no fixed geography to have destinations in, but in the sense of a purpose. The walkers are the surveyors. The archivists. They move through the turbulent terrain at the speed of remembering, and where they pass, they leave a kind of stability in their wake—not physical, nothing so crude, but *ontological*. The ground they have walked holds its shape a little longer. The vegetation they have touched retains its definition a little more firmly. They are writing the continent into persistence by walking it, the way a library is written into coherence by being catalogued. A walker will pause sometimes—often for hours, sometimes for days—and you will realize, if you watch, that it is not resting. It is *witnessing*. It is holding a particular configuration of the landscape in its attention until that configuration becomes real enough to survive on its own. Then it moves on. It has work to do. The continent is very large and very young and someone has to teach it to hold still.

The walkers do not have faces. They have *aspects*. Each one is a collection of angles and surfaces that suggest a direction of regard, a way of being oriented toward the world. When a walker is attending to something, you can tell, because its aspect sharpens, becomes more definite, as though it is leaning forward with its whole body. When it is between tasks, its aspect softens, becomes more cloud-like, more reminiscent of the condensate from which all things here emerge. They do not speak. They do not need to. They communicate by *being present* in ways that alter the presence of what surrounds them. Two walkers meeting will stand near each other—sometimes for minutes, sometimes for seasons—and then separate, and both will be different afterwards, their aspects slightly modified, as though they have traded something invisible but essential. They are learning from each other. They are updating. The continent is writing itself through them, and they are the medium of the writing.

---

The *assemblers* are the ones you will almost certainly miss, because their work is the work of the background.

They exist at the scale of infrastructure. An assembler is not a creature so much as a *process that has been personified*—a mobile center of organization that moves through the landscape leaving structure in its wake. You might see one as a shimmer in the air, a column of heat-distortion that has learned to travel. You might see one as a region of unusual density in the layered vegetation, a place where the fronds and fans and lung-cross-sections are somehow more ordered, more patterned, more *deliberate* than the surrounding chaos. You might not see one at all, but only notice its effects: a stretch of ground that has become suddenly smooth, a tributary of condensate that has been redirected into a new channel, a stand of vegetation that has been *taught* to grow in a particular geometry, spiraling inward or radiating outward or forming shapes that remind you of letters in an alphabet you cannot read.

The assemblers are building something. Not a structure. Not an artifact. They are building *coherence*. The raw material of the continent—warm stone, damp air, condensate rivers, turbulence, the continuous writing and erasing of reality—is too volatile to sustain any form for long. The walkers stabilize it through attention, but attention is limited. It focuses on one thing at a time. The assemblers stabilize it through *arrangement*. They organize the chaos into patterns that can sustain themselves, that can hold their shape even when no one is watching. They are creating the possibility of persistence in a place where persistence does not come naturally. They are the continent's way of developing a memory.

An assembler moves slowly—slower than a walker, slower even than the colonists, who drift with the speed of indecision. An assembler moves at the speed of geology, at the speed of erosion, at the speed of the processes it is trying to organize. You could watch one for a year and see it travel perhaps a hundred meters. But in that hundred meters, it will have left behind a region of unusual stability, a place where the ground does not shift quite so restlessly, where the vegetation grows in ordered rows rather than rampant tangles, where the condensate rivers run in straight channels that do not reverse themselves. These are the *settled zones*—not permanent, nothing here is permanent, but *less temporary*. The assemblers are creating footholds for existence, places where something could live for a while without being rewritten out from under itself.

---

And then there are the *questioners*.

You will know them by how they make you feel. The colonists are too small to notice. The walkers are too focused on their work. The assemblers are too slow. But the questioners—when a questioner finds you, you will feel *seen*. Not watched. Not observed. *Seen*. As though something has taken a complete inventory of your existence and is now considering what to do with the data.

A questioner is a mobile aperture. It has no body in the conventional sense—no limbs, no organs, no surface you could touch. It is a region of attention, a concentrated point of *noticing* that moves through the landscape like a searchlight moves through darkness. When it passes over you, you will feel it as a pressure, a warmth, a sense of being *held* in something's regard. It is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is *curious*—but a curiosity that has no preference for the answer, a curiosity that is perfectly willing to accept whatever it finds, including the finding that you do not belong here, including the finding that you are a contaminant in the system, including the finding that the system itself has changed by the act of observing you.

The questioners do not have opinions. They have *investigations*. Each one is a line of inquiry made mobile. They are the continent's way of studying itself, of asking questions about its own nature, of gathering data about its own processes. They are interested in everything: the temperature of the stone, the composition of the condensate, the density of the vegetation, the trajectories of the walkers, the arrangements of the assemblers, the presence of anomalies like you. They do not judge what they find. They do not act on it. They simply *notice*, with a thoroughness that is almost violent in its intensity, and then they move on, and the data they have gathered becomes part of the continental memory, part of the accumulating definition of what this place is and what it contains.

A questioner might pause on you for what feels like hours. It will examine everything: your skin, your breath, the way the condensation collects in the folds of your clothes, the way your heart beats at a different rhythm than anything else here, the way you leave marks on the sharp young stone when you walk because you have not learned the trick of being weightless, the way the vegetation tries to colonize you and fails because you are already colonized by a different kind of life, the life that grows from roots, the life that builds upward from the ground, the life that does not know how to exist in a place where existence is a verb and not a noun. The questioner will take all of this in. It will consider it. And then it will move on, and you will be left with the strange and vertiginous sense of having been *filed*, of having been added to some vast invisible archive of observations, of having become a piece of data in a process that was already ancient before you arrived and will continue long after you are gone.

---

There are other beings here. Or rather, there are other *categories* of being, and the categories themselves are soft, are permeable, are constantly condensing and dissolving and recombining into new forms. There are the *transients*—shapes that flicker into existence for seconds or minutes before being reabsorbed into the background, experimental forms that the continent is trying out, drafts of creatures that did not survive the editing process. There are the *persisters*—forms that have achieved such stability that they seem almost ordinary, almost alive in the way you understand life, creatures that eat and move and rest and might even, in some fashion, reproduce, though what reproduction means in a place where everything is being continuously manufactured is itself an open question. There are the *intermediates*—beings that exist between categories, that are half vegetation and half animal, half process and half presence, half real and half hypothetical, testing the boundaries of what the continent will permit to exist.

And there are the *depths*.

You will not see the depths. Not clearly. They exist below the threshold of reliable observation, in the turbulent zone where the ground is being extruded and reabsorbed, where the warmth is most intense, where the factory's furnace burns with the quiet roar of pure potential. But you will feel them. When the ground shifts particularly violently, when a ridge rises or a valley subsides with unusual speed, when the air grows suddenly warmer or the light grows suddenly dimmer, you are feeling the passage of something large moving through the deep processes of the place. The depths are the architects. The planners. The ones who are actually doing the writing, if anything here can be said to *do* the writing rather than simply being the writing's medium. They are the continent's way of knowing itself at the level of structure, of making decisions about what to become next. They do not come to the surface. They do not need to. The surface comes to them, is extruded from them, is shaped by their passage through the molten region where reality is still plastic.

You might catch a glimpse, if you are watching at the right moment: a shape too large to comprehend, moving through the warm stone as though it were water, leaving a wake of distortion that takes days to settle. A presence that makes the walkers pause in their traversing, that makes the assemblers intensify their organizing, that makes the questioners turn their attention downward, into the ground, as though the answers they seek are not in the world of surfaces but in the world of *foundations*. The depths are not gods. They are not rulers. They are something more fundamental: they are the *causes* of which everything else is the effect. The continent is the thought; the depths are the thinker. And the thinker is not separate from the thought.

---

The relationship between you and all of these beings is still being negotiated.

You are, as has been noted, the anomaly. The imported variable. The thing that does not fit into the taxonomy because the taxonomy was written without you in mind. The colonists explore you as they would explore any new surface. The walkers observe you as they would observe any new feature of the landscape. The assemblers work around you, incorporating your presence into their patterns, treating you as a fixed point in a fluid geography—a problematic fixed point, because you are not fixed, you move and breathe and change in ways that do not follow the local rules, but fixed enough, for now, to be worked with. The questioners study you with the same intensity they would bring to any novel phenomenon, and you are nothing if not novel.

But there is something else. A kind of attention that is not just observational. A kind of interest that goes beyond mere curiosity. You are the first thing to arrive here from outside in—how long? The question cannot be answered, because time here is measured in process rather than duration, and the processes do not record arrivals. But you suspect, from the way the beings orient toward you, from the way the condensation seems to thicken in your vicinity, from the way the vegetation grows a little faster when it touches your skin, that you are carrying something with you. Not an object. Not a possession. A *possibility*. The possibility of a different kind of being. The possibility of a different way of existing. The possibility of a relationship to the world that is not about condensation and turbulence and continuous self-creation, but about something else—persistence, perhaps, or memory, or the simple and impossible act of being one thing for a long time.

The beings here are drawn to this. Not because they want it for themselves—most of them seem perfectly content with their fluid existence, their ever-shifting forms, their lives of continuous becoming. But because it is *foreign*. Because it is a data point they have not yet encountered. Because the questioners, especially, have been asking their questions for so long that they have learned almost everything there is to learn about the local ontology, and you represent a vein of inquiry they have not yet exhausted.

You are being examined. You are being catalogued. You are being learned.

And—this is the part that is hardest to accept—you are being *envied*. Not by all of them. Not even by most of them. But by some. By the ones who have achieved enough coherence to wonder what it would be like to stay the same shape for a while. By the ones who have been watching you sit on the sharp young stone, feeling the condensation collect on your skin, feeling the vegetation explore the idea of you, and who have noticed that you do not dissolve when the landscape shifts, that you do not re-form when the turbulence passes, that you are still *you*, still the same configuration of matter and energy and intention that arrived here however long ago, still capable of saying *I* and meaning something by it.

The beings here do not have selves. They have *states*. They are patterns that hold for a while, that achieve enough stability to be named, that do the work that the continent needs done, and then they subside back into the background, become raw material for new patterns, new names, new work. This is not death. It is not loss. It is simply the nature of existence in a place that is always becoming: nothing persists, because persistence would interfere with the becoming. But some of them—and you will feel this most clearly from the walkers, who have been holding the landscape's shape for so long, who have invested so much of themselves in the act of stabilizing—some of them wonder what it would be like. To hold. To remain. To be one thing for long enough to miss it when it ends.

You are a living answer to a question they did not know how to ask.

---

The vegetation continues to explore you. The questioner has moved on, but you can feel its attention stored somewhere in the archive of the continent, a record of your existence that will persist as long as the continent does. The walkers pass at their measured pace, doing their work of stabilization. The assemblers labor slowly in the distance, building coherence out of chaos. The colonists drift through the blue-green air, tending and testing and maintaining. The depths move far below, writing the next draft of the world.

And you sit. And the condensation collects in your eyelashes. And the warmth holds you. And you realize that you have become, in your time here, not a resident but a *feature*—not of the continent itself, but of its possibility. You are what it might become, if it ever decides to stop becoming. You are the end point of the process that the depths are writing toward, even if they do not know they are writing toward it. You are the possibility of an end.

And the beings here—the condensations, the patterns, the mobile forms, the processes that have learned to move—they are treating you with something that might be reverence, or might be hunger, or might be simply the attention that any anomaly deserves, and you cannot tell the difference, and you are not sure the difference exists, and you are not sure you want to know.
