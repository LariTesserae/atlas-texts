# Those Who Live in the Veldtmere

## The People of the Weft

They call themselves nothing. Names require boundaries, and boundaries do not exist here. Outsiders—who encounter them rarely, and never by accident—have called them the Weft, a word that captures something essential: they are not separate from the fabric of this place but threads within it, running parallel to the grain, woven into the whole.

Their number is impossible to determine. A traveler might walk for weeks without encountering another soul, then stumble upon a family of five emerging from a culled clearing where their dwelling grows. Another might find signs of recent passage—a deliberately broken stem, a section of canopy thinned to admit light—and never see the person who left them. The plateau is large enough to hold thousands in scattered isolation, small enough that a determined walker might cross it entirely in a long season. No census has ever been attempted. No census would be possible.

They have been here long enough to change. Not dramatically—four limbs, recognizable faces, hands that grasp and voices that speak—but subtly, thoroughly, in ways that mark them as belonging to this place more than any place belongs to them. Their skin has darkened to a deep blue-green, not from melanin alone but from something symbiotic living in the upper layers of their epidermis. In the dim light, they appear almost black; in the rare moments when direct sun breaks through the canopy, their skin reveals an iridescent quality, shifting through shades that match the surrounding vegetation.

Their eyes have changed most visibly. The whites are gone—or rather, transformed. The entire visible surface has darkened to a deep amber, the iris nearly indistinguishable from the surrounding tissue until light catches it and reveals a slit pupil, horizontal like a goat's, capable of closing almost entirely in the mist-thick air. They see in wavelengths that newcomers cannot, the blue-green spectrum amplified, movement detected where a foreign eye would see only stillness. A Weft can look into a wall of vegetation and pick out the creatures hiding within, the ripeness of fruit, the health of a growth by its color-shift. They do not see better, exactly. They see *here* better.

Their bodies have grown slight. Not from scarcity—the plateau provides abundantly—but from economy. Bulk requires fuel. Mass requires maintenance. In a world where movement must be negotiated through dense growth, where every step requires parting the vegetation and sliding through gaps, a smaller body moves more efficiently. They are rarely taller than a child of the lowlands, but proportioned as adults, their limbs lean and ropy with efficient muscle. A Weft can fold themselves into spaces that seem impossible, compressing their ribcage, turning their shoulders sideways, slipping through gaps in the root-mat that a hand could barely fit through.

They do not walk so much as flow. A lifetime of navigating this terrain has given them a gait that newcomers find unsettling to watch—a continuous forward motion that incorporates obstacle-avoidance as a dance incorporates steps, the body reading the path ahead and adjusting before the conscious mind registers the barrier. They move through the vegetation as water moves through reeds, parting it, sliding past, leaving it to close behind them with barely a ripple.

Their hair—if it can be called that—grows in dense mats that trail behind them when loose, spreading across their shoulders and back like a second skin. Each strand is thicker than human hair, almost wire-like, and it serves a purpose: the surface grows small filamentous structures that trap moisture from the air, directing it toward the scalp where it can be absorbed. A Weft can go days without drinking from a pool or plant-cup, their hair harvesting water from the perpetual mist. The mats also provide habitat for beneficial mites that keep parasites at bay, a symbiosis so ancient that neither party remembers its origin.

They dress in what they grow. Not in the sense of cultivation—though that too—but in the sense that their clothing is itself alive. A garment begins as a cutting taken from a particular vine, one of several species domesticated so long ago that its wild form no longer exists. The cutting is trained around the body, guided across shoulders and around limbs, encouraged to form sleeves and panels and hems through a process of patient binding and pruning. Within a season, the wearer has a garment that photosynthesizes, that draws moisture from the air and delivers it to the skin, that grows thicker in response to cold and thinner in response to warmth. It protects. It nourishes. It is never removed because it is never separate.

This is their way. They do not make things. They grow them, tend them, enter into relationships with them. The distinction between the made and the found does not exist. A tool is a plant that has been asked to serve a particular purpose. A dwelling is a growth that has been shaped over years into chambers and passages. A path is not cleared but cultivated, a strip of ground where particular growths have been encouraged and others discouraged, maintained by regular passage as much as deliberate tending.

## Their Dwellings

A visitor from elsewhere would see nothing.

The Weft do not build structures. They grow them. A dwelling begins when a particular type of framework-plant—a species related to the canopy-supports but smaller, more flexible—is induced to send out multiple trunks in a circular pattern. These are guided inward as they grow, woven together at the top to form a dome, then allowed to thicken and harden over years. The walls fill in with smaller growths, epiphytes and mosses and trailing vines that the residents encourage to fill particular gaps, creating chambers within the dome, passages between them, window-openings that can be closed by rolling leaves into tubes.

The floor is not floor but root-mass, a woven mat of particular cultivars that provide a surface slightly firmer than the surrounding terrain. These roots also store water drawn from below, accessible by pressing a hand against the mat and drawing upward—a slow process, but the Weft are patient. Storage-containers are grown from gourds the size of a torso, cultivated on vines that hang from the dome's interior, their flesh thick and waterproof, their skins hard enough to resist puncture.

A dwelling takes seven years to become habitable, twenty to reach full size. It continues growing slowly throughout its existence, rooms expanding, passages shifting, the dome rising and spreading. A family might live in the same structure for generations, watching it change around them, guiding its development like a gardener guiding a tree. When the original inhabitants die, their descendants continue tending it, or it is abandoned to be reclaimed by the surrounding vegetation, or—rarely—it is given a new purpose: a waystation for travelers, a meeting-place for the scattered families, a repository for preserved knowledge.

Inside, the light is almost non-existent. The Weft do not need it. They navigate by touch, by smell, by the subtle sounds of their dwelling's living processes. They sleep on hammocks grown from trailing vines, slung between anchor-points that have been cultivated into the walls. They cook—when they cook at all—using heat generated by composting masses of particular plants that produce warmth through their decomposition, a slow steady heat that never flares but can be channeled into enclosed chambers for steaming food.

Their food is the plateau itself. They eat new growth, selected from hundreds of edible species, harvested in quantities that do not damage the parent plants. They eat the insects that process the compost-layers, cultivated in colonies within the dwelling's walls, harvested by inserting a hand into a breeding-chamber and letting the creatures crawl onto it. They eat the eggs of ground-dwelling birds and the young of small mammals that they do not hunt but rather manage, maintaining populations in particular areas, harvesting sustainably, never taking enough to threaten the breeding stock. They eat the fruiting bodies of fungi that grow in the damp lower layers, some of them luminous, some of them medicinal, some of them so potent they can induce visions that last for days.

A dwelling is never alone. The Weft live in clusters—three to five families, usually related, each with their own dome connected to the others by cultivated passages at ground level. These clusters are spaced far enough apart to give each family privacy, close enough to share resources and child-rearing and the work of maintaining the paths that connect them to the wider network. A cluster might total twenty individuals, spanning four generations. Larger clusters exist but are rare; smaller ones are vulnerable to the ordinary dangers of plateau life—illness, accident, predation by the few creatures large enough to threaten a human.

The clusters themselves are connected. Paths—cultivated strips of easier passage—link them to their nearest neighbors, who are linked in turn to their neighbors, forming a web that spans the entire plateau. A message can travel this network in days if urgent, carried by runners who have memorized the routes, who can move at speeds newcomers find impossible, their bodies reading the terrain ahead and flowing around obstacles without breaking stride. Most messages travel slower, passed along by ordinary movement, a visitor carrying news from one cluster to the next, accumulating new details with each telling.

## The Way They Speak

They have no word for the plateau. It is simply *here*, or *the world*, or a gesture that encompasses everything—the hand opening, fingers spreading, indicating the canopy above, the ground below, the growth in every direction. A child learning to speak learns words for specific things first: this plant, that creature, the action of harvesting, the action of tending. The concept of the whole comes later, and it comes not as a word but as a recognition, a drawing-together of everything the child has learned into a single understanding that never requires articulation.

Their language is difficult for outsiders. The sounds are not the problem—any human throat can make them, and the vocabulary is not large. The problem is reference. Weft nouns do not point to objects but to relationships. The word that an outsider would translate as *vine* actually means *the thing that climbs on the thing that supports*, and the word for *support* means *the thing that is climbed on by the thing that climbs*. Every term implies its context, its connections, its place in the larger web. A sentence is not a string of self-contained meanings but a set of coordinates in a conceptual space that both speaker and listener must already share.

They speak little. The humidity dampens sound, the vegetation absorbs it, and the close quarters of their dwellings make speech unnecessary—everyone already knows what everyone else is doing. They communicate instead through touch, through gesture, through the subtle movements of bodies that have learned to read each other. A hand on a shoulder can convey reassurance, warning, affection, or a complex combination that would require a paragraph to explain. They have developed a system of signed language used when silence is necessary—hunting, hiding, moving through territory where something dangerous might be listening—but even this is sparse, efficient, more suggestion than statement.

They sing, though. Long cascading phrases that weave through the canopy like birds, rising and falling with the mist-cycle, telling stories of ancestors and migrations and the slow work of growing. The songs are not performed so much as lived, arising naturally during communal work, fading when the work is done. They serve a practical purpose—the vibrations encourage certain plants to release their seeds, repel certain insects from the dwelling, and seem to stimulate the compost-heaps to greater productivity—but their primary function is memory. The history of the Weft is stored in song, in verses passed down through generations, modified and expanded as new events require, the melody remaining recognizable even as the lyrics shift.

Outsiders who have heard these songs describe them as beautiful, though few can explain why. The tonal system is unfamiliar, based on intervals that do not exist in the scales of the lowlands. The rhythm follows the breathing of the plateau itself, the slow in-and-out of the mist-cycle, the pulse of water through vascular systems. A song might continue for hours, the singers taking turns, their voices rising and falling in patterns that seem random until the structure reveals itself, a vast architecture of sound that mirrors the vast architecture of the world that produced it.

## The Children

They are born in the dwellings, delivered by the eldest woman of the family, a role passed down through generations. The infant is small—smaller even than the slight frames of their parents would suggest—and arrives with a fine coating of the same blue-green film that covers adult skin, though fainter, incomplete. Within days, the symbiosis begins to establish itself, the microscopic organisms that will live in the skin finding their new host, spreading slowly across the surface. A newborn Weft looks strange to foreign eyes, almost unfinished, the skin patchy, the eyes still light-sensitive, the hair-mat barely formed.

The infant is carried constantly for the first year, strapped to the mother's chest in a sling grown from the same living fabric as adult garments, absorbing warmth and moisture from her body. The child learns the world through contact—the texture of leaves, the vibration of footsteps on the root-mat, the smell of particular growths, the sound of the dwelling breathing. This is the foundation of all later knowledge: the world is not something observed from a distance but something felt, continuous with the body, extending outward in every direction.

They learn to walk by navigating the dwelling. The floor is soft and uneven, the passages narrow and twisting, and a toddler learns to move through them by trial and error, by following the paths that the adults take, by absorbing the subtle cues that indicate which way to turn. By the time a child is old enough to leave the dwelling, they have already internalized the basic grammar of movement—the lean, the slide, the turn, the fold. They emerge into the larger world already fluent in the terrain.

Their education is not formal. There are no lessons, no teachers in the conventional sense. A child follows adults as they work, watching, imitating, gradually taking on tasks of increasing complexity. They learn to identify plants by touch and smell, to recognize the signs of animal passage, to find water in the root-mat, to navigate by the sun-filter through the canopy. They learn the songs that contain the history of the people, first as melody, then as meaning. They learn the paths that connect the clusters, traveling with adults to neighboring dwellings, building a mental map that expands with each journey.

They learn to read the land itself. The plateau communicates its condition through a thousand subtle signals: the humidity in the air, the color of particular leaves, the movement of insects, the sound of water moving through the vascular systems of large growths. A child learns to synthesize these signals into an understanding, to know when rain is coming even in a world where rain is constant, to sense the movement of large animals through vibrations in the root-mat, to find particular resources by following trails of chemical signature too faint for an outsider to detect.

They are not children for long. By ten years, a Weft is contributing meaningfully to the work of the dwelling. By fifteen, they are recognized as adults, capable of traveling alone, of maintaining their own garments, of taking a partner if they choose. The transition is not marked by ceremony—ceremonies require the kind of formal structure that the Weft avoid—but by a gradual shift in the way others treat them, a recognition that has accumulated slowly and settled into permanence.

They choose partners from neighboring clusters, traveling the paths to meet potential mates in gatherings that happen irregularly, timed to the fruiting-cycles of particular plants. These gatherings are the closest thing the Weft have to social events: clusters sending representatives to a central point, dwelling together for days in temporary shelters grown for the occasion, exchanging news and gifts and genetic material. The young people pair off according to mutual attraction, encouraged but not arranged by their elders, forming bonds that will last for life.

A bonded pair returns to the dwelling of one partner's family—the choice depends on practical considerations, the needs of each cluster, the state of each dwelling. They are given a section of the dwelling to tend, a place that will become their own as they age, that they will shape and guide and eventually pass to their own children. The cycle continues.

## The Old Ones

They do not grow old in the way of outsiders. The plateau preserves. The constant moisture, the abundant food, the lack of temperature extremes, the slow rhythms of life—these extend what the lowlands would consider a normal span. A Weft who survives childhood illness and the ordinary dangers of the plateau can expect to see eighty years, ninety, some have reached a hundred and more. But their aging is strange.

They do not weaken. The body remains lean and capable, the mind sharp, the senses if anything more acute as the decades pass. What changes instead is speed—the old ones move more slowly, not from infirmity but from deliberation. They have seen more, know more, understand the consequences of actions more thoroughly, and they pause to consider, to weigh, to consult the knowledge accumulated over a lifetime. An elder making a decision about a dwelling's growth might spend days observing before acting, checking patterns of sunlight and water-flow, predicting the consequences for generations ahead.

Their skin grows darker, the symbiosis deepening, until the oldest among them appear almost purely black in the dim light, their forms visible only as silhouettes, as absences in the green. Their hair-mats grow longer, trailing behind them, collecting moisture that their bodies need less of but have learned to store against future need. They become in a real sense part of the vegetation—they photosynthesize significantly, can go weeks without food if necessary, drawing sustenance from light and air and the water their hair harvests.

They accumulate knowledge in quantities that the young cannot match. A Weft elder knows every path within weeks of travel, every plant within the region, every creature's habits, every seasonal pattern over decades. They have watched the canopy shift and the paths change and the dwellings grow and die. They remember ancestors who died before the youngest generation was born, have inherited songs that stretch back to times when the plateau was different—cooler, perhaps, or wetter, or traversed by peoples who have since vanished.

They are respected but not revered. The Weft do not hierarchy. An elder's opinion carries weight because of experience, not because of position, and a young person with a good argument can overrule an elder's objection. The oldest are consulted on matters of long consequence—where to plant a new dwelling, how to respond to the appearance of a new creature, whether a particular path should be maintained or abandoned. On matters of immediate practicality, the young decide for themselves.

When they die, it is often without warning. The body simply stops, some accumulated small failure reaching a threshold, some symbiosis that could not be maintained finally collapsing. They sit down to rest and do not rise. They walk a familiar path and fall. The end comes without drama, without prolonged dying, without the long decline that lowlanders often experience.

Their bodies are given to the dwelling. The family wraps them in their living garments and places them in a particular chamber, a hollow in the root-mat that has been prepared over years, lined with mosses and fungi and the seeds of particular plants. Within weeks, the body has been absorbed—not rotting, but incorporated, the flesh becoming vegetation, the bones becoming structure, the hair-mat continuing to grow on its own, spreading through the dwelling, carrying the elder's last moisture into walls that will shelter generations to come.

There is no mourning in the way outsiders understand it. The dead are not gone; they have simply changed form. They are present in the dwelling they helped grow, in the songs they taught, in the paths they maintained, in the children they raised who are now raising children of their own. A Weft does not fear death because a Weft has never conceived of the self as separate from the world that will absorb it.

## The Creatures They Know

The plateau's animals are not separate from the Weft's lives. They are neighbors, sometimes dangerous, sometimes useful, always present. The Weft have names for them in their relational language, terms that describe not what the creatures are but how they relate to the people: the thing that eats the growing-floors, the thing that hunts in the mist-thick, the thing that lives in the canopy and drops its waste, the thing that burrows and must be discouraged from burrowing under dwellings.

The largest of them—the flat-bodied grazers that move through the dense growth like islands moving through sea—are called by a term that means approximately *the ones we do not disturb*. These creatures are immense, six meters long and nearly as wide, their bodies flat and armored with a substance like bark but more flexible, like the carapace of an insect but grown rather than secreted. They move with a patience that matches the plateau itself, each step deliberate, the vegetation parting around them and closing behind, their passage visible only as a trail of compressed growth that slowly rises again over weeks.

They are herbivores, grazing on the lower layers of growth, their mouths wide and flat, their teeth designed for grinding rather than tearing. They take enormous amounts of plant-matter each day, but their impact is spread across territories so vast that no area suffers from their attention. They migrate in patterns the Weft have learned to predict, following the fruiting-cycles of particular plants, following the moisture-gradients, following ancient routes that their ancestors walked before them, remembered not in maps but in the bodies of the creatures themselves.

The Weft do not hunt them. The effort required would be immense, the risk catastrophic—a wounded grazer can thrash with enough force to destroy a dwelling, and the noise of a kill would attract every scavenger within kilometers. But the Weft do use them. The paths the grazers create are easier to travel than the unbroken vegetation, and a careful walker can follow a grazer's trail for hours, making good time in the wake of the creature's passage. The waste they leave behind—enormous piles of partially-digested plant matter—are collected by the Weft for compost, rich in nutrients that particular plants require. And when a grazer dies, of age or illness or accident, the news travels fast, and every cluster within reach sends representatives to harvest the body for materials: the bark-like skin, the immense stores of fat, the bones that can be shaped into tools.

The hunters are called by terms that mean *the ones that wait* and *the ones that follow*. They are smaller than the grazers but still formidable—a large one might reach two meters in length, low-slung, many-legged, their bodies so perfectly camouflaged that they are invisible until they move. They do not chase prey; they wait for it, blended into the vegetation, their bodies indistinguishable from the surrounding growth until the moment they strike. Some have evolved to mimic particular plants so precisely that they carry on photosynthesis through their skin, deriving energy from light while they wait for something edible to pass within reach.

The Weft avoid them by knowing where they are. The hunters establish territories and stay within them for seasons at a time, and their presence can be detected by subtle signs: a particular smell, an absence of the smaller creatures that usually populate an area, a wrongness in the texture of the vegetation that an experienced eye can read. A cluster that discovers a hunter in its territory will not confront it but will avoid the area, redirecting paths, warning visitors, waiting for the creature to move on. Only if a hunter takes up residence too close to a dwelling will the Weft take action—and then they do not attack but discourage, creating disturbances in the vegetation that make the area unattractive, making noise at intervals that prevent the creature from hunting effectively, subtly poisoning the local prey until the hunter decides to seek better ground.

The smallest creatures are the most integrated into Weft life. The insect-like things that process the compost-layers are cultivated in colonies, their populations managed, their harvest scheduled so that the colony is never depleted. The canopy-gliders—small mammals with membranes of skin that let them sail from branch to branch—are encouraged to live near dwellings, where they eat the insects that would otherwise infest the growing-floors. The pale eyeless things that live in the deepest layers are left alone; they do not interact with the surface and seem to serve a function in the root-mat that the Weft do not fully understand but respect.

Birds exist here, though they are not like the birds of the lowlands. They have no need for the long wings that open-country fliers possess; their wings are short and broad, designed for maneuvering through dense growth, for short bursts of flight between perches. Their calls are low and resonant, carrying through the humidity without dissipating, and the Weft use them as signals—particular birds respond to particular whistles, their calls alerting others to conditions beyond sight, the approach of travelers, the presence of unusual activity in the canopy.

There are creatures the Weft speak of but that few have seen. Things that live in the deepest compost-layers and are rarely seen—immense pale shapes that move through the darkness below, their purposes unknown. Things that appear only during the densest mist-periods, moving through the white soup, their forms never clearly visible, their existence inferred from tracks and sounds and the occasional disappearance of smaller animals. Things that might be legends, stories told to children, warnings about the dangers of wandering too far, of sleeping in the wrong places, of ignoring the signs that an area is not safe.

The Weft do not dominate the creatures of the plateau. They coexist. They manage, when management serves. They avoid, when avoidance serves. They harvest, when harvest serves and the population can sustain it. The relationship is not adversarial but collaborative, each party aware of the others, each adjusting to the others' presence, a dynamic equilibrium that has lasted as long as anyone can remember.

## The Things They Fear

They are not afraid of death. Death is change, and change is constant, and the form that a body takes after death is simply a different form from the one it took before. What they fear is disruption—the breaking of patterns, the failure of systems, the arrival of something that does not fit.

They fear fire. Not because fire is common—it is not, the humidity makes combustion almost impossible—but because the possibility exists. A sufficiently hot fire, started by lightning or by human carelessness, could burn through the dried layers beneath the surface, the compressed plant-matter of ages, the peat-like strata that go down kilometers. Such a fire would not burn quickly; it would smolder, spreading underground, emerging in unexpected places, impossible to extinguish, lasting for years, decades, slowly consuming the foundation on which everything lives. The Weft have songs about such a fire, ancient songs, passed down from a time when it may actually have happened, verses that describe the sky going dark with smoke, the water turning bitter, the creatures dying or fleeing, the plateau becoming a graveyard. They do not speak of it casually.

They fear the sky. The canopy has always been there; to imagine it gone is to imagine the end of the world. But the old songs tell of times when the sky was visible, when a great clearing spread across a region of the plateau, when the full sun beat down and the creatures that lived there adapted or died. The clearing was made by the ancestors, the songs say—the people who came before the Weft, who built the structures whose ruins still remain, who did something that changed the plateau forever. What they did, and why, and whether it was worth doing, the songs do not say. But the clearing spread, and the sky opened, and the plateau burned, and only after many generations did the growth return, the canopy close, the world become again what it is now. The Weft do not clear. They do not cut. They do not make openings larger than a dwelling's footprint. They remember what happens when the balance is broken.

They fear the outsiders. Not the rare travelers who find their way to the plateau—these are welcomed, fed, given shelter, sent on their way with gifts—but the possibility of more. The lowlands hold people in numbers the Weft cannot imagine, cities, nations, peoples who might one day decide to climb to the roof of the world and take what they find there. The plateau holds nothing they need—it is too difficult to reach, too strange to farm, too wet for conventional building—but the Weft know that need is not the only reason people do things. They know that curiosity exists, and greed, and the simple desire to possess what has not been possessed. They watch the approaches to the plateau—the few routes that can be climbed, the passes that lead up from the lowlands—and they have ways of discouraging those who approach. They do not kill, not if they can avoid it. But they have been known to lead travelers astray, to confuse their direction, to appear and disappear in ways that make the plateau seem haunted, to create the impression that nothing of value exists here, only danger and difficulty and a strangeness not worth investigating.

They fear the thing that has no name. It appears in the oldest songs, in fragments that the Weft themselves do not fully understand. Something that lives in the plateau without being part of it. Something that was here before the people, before the creatures, perhaps before the plants themselves. Something that sleeps, or waits, or simply exists, deep in the compressed layers, in the darkness below the root-mat, in the geological time that has become the ground on which they walk. The songs say it is not dangerous, not actively, not in the way that a hunter is dangerous. But they say also that it should not be disturbed, that its sleep should not be broken, that the plateau above it is a kind of blanket, a kind of seal, a kind of protection—not for the people, but from what lies beneath. The Weft do not dig. They do not sink wells. They take their water from the surface, from the root-mat, from the plants that hold moisture in their tissues. They do not ask what lies below.

## The Way They Think

A Weft does not ask whether a thing is true. Truth is a concept that assumes a separation between the knower and the known, a distance that allows verification. The Weft do not experience distance. They experience connection. A thing is not true or false; it is present or absent, useful or useless, familiar or strange. The question is not *is this so* but *what does this connect to*, *what follows from this*, *what does this require of me*.

They do not count. Not precisely. They recognize quantities—this group is larger than that group, this dwelling has been here longer than that dwelling—but they do not enumerate. The concept of a discrete number requires separating items from their context, and the Weft do not separate. A cluster is not five people; it is a family, a unit, a whole that contains particular relationships. To ask how many is to ask a question that has no meaning.

They do not plan in the way of lowlanders. Planning assumes a future that can be predicted, a trajectory that can be shaped by present action. The Weft understand that the future is a continuation of the present, that the patterns of now will extend into the patterns of then, but they do not try to control those patterns. They tend. They guide. They create conditions that will produce desired outcomes, but they do not expect those outcomes to arrive in a particular form at a particular time. A dwelling is not built; it is grown, and growth takes the time it takes.

They do not believe in progress. The plateau is not progressing toward anything. It has arrived at a state that could not be improved upon, a terminal equilibrium, a closed loop that sustains itself indefinitely. The Weft are part of this loop. They do not seek to move forward, because forward does not exist. They seek to maintain, to participate, to remain within the pattern that has always been.

They do not have gods. Or rather, they do not have beings separate from the world who control the world's workings. The plateau itself is what lowlanders might call divine—not a person, not a will, but a presence, an immensity, a system so vast and complex that it contains the people who live within it, includes them, sustains them, will eventually absorb them. They do not worship it. Worship requires distance. They simply live within it, aware that it is larger than they are, older than they are, will be here after they are gone.

They do not tell stories about themselves. The songs contain history, but it is the history of the people, not of individuals. A Weft does not say *I did this*; they say *this was done*, or *the dwelling was shaped*, or *the path was found*. The self is not a protagonist. The self is a point of view, a location in the web, a place where the plateau experiences itself.

They do not feel alone. Even when traveling by themselves, even when separated from their cluster by days of travel, a Weft is surrounded by the presence of the plateau. The vegetation is alive. The creatures are present. The root-mat beneath their feet connects them to every other living thing. They walk within a community that includes every plant and animal and fungus, a community that does not require presence or speech or recognition, only the shared condition of being here, of being part of the web.

## What They Are Becoming

The plateau changes slowly, and the Weft change with it. Not in ways that any generation notices—each generation perceives itself as essentially identical to the generation before—but in ways that become visible across the long spans that the songs remember.

The skin has darkened. The songs describe the ancestors as lighter, their skin still carrying something of the lowland tones from which they came. Over generations, the symbiosis has deepened, the organisms living in the skin have become more numerous, more integrated, more essential. A Weft can no longer survive in the lowlands; the brighter light would burn their photosensitive skin, the different atmosphere would disrupt the symbiosis, the lower humidity would desiccate them in days. They are no longer human in the sense that their ancestors were. They are something new, something that could exist only here.

The eyes continue to adapt. The horizontal pupils are a recent development, appearing in the songs only a few hundred years ago, spreading through the population until now every child is born with them. The ability to see in the blue-green spectrum, to detect movement invisible to outsiders, to read the health of plants by their color—these are skills that the songs describe as learned, once, but that now seem innate, present in children who have not yet been taught.

The hair-mats have grown longer, more complex. The symbiosis that keeps parasites at bay has expanded to include other functions: the hair now seems to communicate with the root-mat, picking up vibrations that the body can feel, allowing a Weft to sense the movement of creatures or people at distances that hearing cannot reach. A Weft can tell, by the feel of their hair, whether something large is moving nearby, whether the ground ahead is solid or hollow, whether a particular plant is stressed or healthy.

The lifespan continues to extend. The oldest members of the oldest clusters remember their great-grandparents living to ages that would have been remarkable then but are common now. The plateau preserves, and the Weft have learned to be preserved by it, to accept its processes into their bodies, to become more like the plants that surround them—slower, more patient, more capable of existing on light and air and water, less dependent on food, less vulnerable to time.

They are becoming the plateau. Not in the sense of losing themselves, of dissolving into the vegetation—but in the sense of deepening their integration, of becoming more fully what the plateau requires of its inhabitants. The process is slow. It will continue. The songs imagine a time when the Weft will be indistinguishable from the growths around them, when they will root and photosynthesize and live for centuries, when the boundary between person and plant will have ceased to exist.

That time is not yet. The Weft are still recognizable, still what a lowlander would call human, still possessed of hands and voices and minds that think and sing and remember. They walk the paths they have always walked, tend the dwellings they have always tended, live within the pattern that has always held them. They do not know what they are becoming.
