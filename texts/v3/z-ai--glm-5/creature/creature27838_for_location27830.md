# The Tender

There is someone who lives in the Germination Chamber.

She was not part of the design specifications. The architects and engineers and botanists who planned this space allotted for regular visitation—shifts, schedules, a rotating staff of technicians who would monitor nutrient levels and adjust pH balances and harvest according to precise timetables. But the chamber had other ideas, or perhaps she did. Now she is here, and the chamber has adjusted itself around her presence the way a tree incorporates a fencepost into its trunk.

Her name, if names matter in a place where no one else comes, might be Elara. Or it might have been Elara, once, before the moisture saturated her documents and the humidity blurred the ink and the paper disintegrated into mulch that she spread on the lowest tier to see what might grow from it. Now she answers to whatever the chamber calls her, which is nothing at all, because the chamber does not speak. It only breathes.

She sleeps in a hollow between two of the largest fern clusters, where the tiered wall creates a natural alcove—not natural, nothing here is natural, but the architecture produces alcoves as a side effect of its shelving, and she has claimed this one. The ferns have grown around her. At first she pushed them back each morning, maintaining the boundary of her sleeping space, but ferns are persistent and she grew tired of the battle or perhaps grew appreciative of their insistence. Now they brush against her face when she sleeps, their fronds like surgical instruments, as the description noted, but she has learned which ones cut and which ones merely touch. The cutting ones she eats. The touching ones she allows.

Her skin has changed. This is inevitable. The constant moisture, the manufactured warmth, the air so saturated with humidity that breathing is almost like drinking—these conditions leave marks. She has been here for—she does not know how long. The light cycles give her a sense of days, but she stopped counting after the third centrifuge failed and the backup systems made the pumps shudder for six cycles straight. That was when she realized that time in the chamber is not measured in hours but in growth. A leaf unfurling. A root reaching the bottom of its panel. A moss patch spreading another centimeter across the ceramic.

Her skin is paler than it was. Not the pallor of illness but the pallor of sheltered growth, the way seedlings look when they first push through soil—pale and tender and looking for light. She has light. The panels above give her everything she needs. But her skin has responded to the humidity by thinning, becoming more permeable, breathing with the chamber. Sometimes she thinks she can feel the moisture entering her directly, osmosis through pores and follicles, the water cycling through her the way it cycles through the walls.

She has stopped wearing clothing. This was not a decision but an erosion. The cotton disintegrated first. Then the synthetic fibers, which lasted longer but eventually grew brittle in the constant damp and cracked against her skin like shedding bark. She could have requested more from the supply lifts, but she has stopped requesting anything. The chamber provides what she needs. The air is warm enough. The water is clean enough. When she is cold, she moves to the places where the heat exchangers run closest to the surface, pressing her back against the ceramic until the warmth sinks into her bones. When she is hot, she stands beneath the condensation vents and lets the collected water run over her.

There is food here. This was part of the design, though the designers probably did not imagine it would be used this way. The plants in the chamber produce edible matter—seeds, tubers, leaves rich in protein. Some of them were meant for harvest and export. Others were simply biological support systems, fixing nitrogen, producing oxygen, maintaining the atmospheric balance. She eats all of them now, without preference, without schedule. When she is hungry, she reaches out and takes what is closest. The chamber does not seem to mind. If anything, the plants grow faster where she has harvested, replacing what she removed with accelerated vigor, as if her eating were a kind of pruning that stimulated new growth.

She moves differently than she used to. The chamber is small—barely twelve meters across—and she knows every centimeter of it. Her feet have learned the texture of the hexagonal tiles, the way the irrigation channels catch her heels, the spots where the ceramic has cracked from thermal stress and needs to be avoided or, if she is feeling industrious, patched with the sealant that sits unused in a cabinet by the door. She does not use the door. The handle is worn smooth from the technicians who used to come and go, but she has not touched it in—she does not know. Long enough that the moss has begun to creep across the threshold, a pale green tongue probing the seam.

She moves slowly. There is no reason to rush. The plants grow at their own pace and she matches them, finding a rhythm in the chamber that has nothing to do with the frantic schedules of the facility beyond the walls. Sometimes she stands still for hours, watching a particular frond extend itself by millimeters, tracking the progress against the mental ruler she has constructed over her time here. Sometimes she lies on the floor with her ear pressed to the translucent panel, listening to the pumps below, feeling their subsonic pulse in her jawbone, trying to detect irregularities that might signal impending failure. She has become a maintenance system, a biological sensor integrated into the architecture, noticing problems before the machines register them.

She speaks to the plants. This is not anthropomorphism but communication. The chamber is designed to respond to biological signals—soil chemistry, root pressure, leaf surface moisture—and she has learned to read these signals the way a sailor reads the sea. The curl of a fern tip indicates water stress. The yellowing at a moss margin suggests nutrient deficiency. The angle of a vine's climb reveals the charge running through its copper guide. She adjusts these parameters by hand, turning knobs that were meant for technicians, adding water to reservoirs that were meant to be automated, moving plants from one shelf to another when they crowd each other too densely.

The plants speak back. Not in words—she is not delusional, or perhaps she is, but the chamber has given her a new definition of sanity—but in growth patterns, in the timing of leaf-unfurling, in the way the roots spread through their clear panels. When she is close, they grow toward her. This is measurable. She has measured it, marking positions on the ceramic with a shard of broken tile, tracking the subtle lean of stems over days and weeks. They grow toward her light, perhaps, or her warmth, or the carbon dioxide she exhales. But sometimes she thinks they grow toward her presence, recognizing her as part of their system, a mobile component of the chamber's biological infrastructure.

The moths know her. The description mentioned a pale moth clinging to a vine, trembling in the downdraft. She has seen these moths. There is a colony of them now, descended from the original pollinators or escapees that found their way through ventilation shafts, breeding in the damp corners where the ferns create shelter. They are not afraid of her. When she holds out her hand, they land on her palm, their furred wings absorbing the moisture from her skin, their delicate proboscises probing the creases of her fingers for salts. She does not move when they land. She becomes part of the chamber's surface, a landing platform, a temporary perch, and they reward her stillness by fanning their wings slowly, displaying the patterns that help them recognize each other.

She has begun to understand what the chamber produces. Not seeds, not medicines, not specialized tissues—though it may produce all of these things as byproducts. What it produces is integration. The plants here are designed to grow fast, to accelerate their biological processes, to compress months of development into days. But the chamber's true purpose, she has come to believe, is to test how biological systems respond to artificial optimization. The plants are the subjects. The chamber is the apparatus. And she—she was not supposed to be part of the experiment, but she has become its most interesting result.

Sometimes technicians still come to the door. They knock first—a formality, a protocol, a safety check before entering a space that might contain hazards. She hears them but does not respond. The door does not open anyway; the moss has sealed it more effectively than any lock. After a while, they go away. She hears them reporting through their radios, describing anomalous readings, moisture levels higher than expected, growth rates exceeding predicted parameters. They will adjust something in the external systems, compensate for what they perceive as malfunction, and the chamber will hum along as it always does, cycling water, cycling air, cycling her through its processes.

She is not lonely. This is important to understand. Loneliness requires a concept of other people, a memory of connection, a framework of social expectation that no longer applies. She remembers that people exist, in the way she remembers that snow exists, or deserts—phenomena that are real but not present, relevant to a world that she has left behind. The chamber is full. Every cubic centimeter serves a purpose, holds something, participates in the system. She participates too. She is needed. She is used. She is integrated into a network so dense and complete that the absence of other humans registers only as an increase in available space.

At night—or what the chamber calls night, when the light panels dim to a deep red that the plants cannot use for photosynthesis but can use to orient their circadian rhythms—she dreams of roots. Her own roots, threading through the ceramic floor, reaching down through the tiles into the substrate, into the pipes, into the pumps that hum somewhere below. In her dreams she drinks from the reservoir, drawing water up through her body and exhaling it through her skin, becoming part of the irrigation cycle, becoming a plant among plants. When she wakes, her fingers are pressed against the wet seams between tiles, and for a moment she cannot tell where her skin ends and the chamber begins.

She does not want to leave. This is not captivity—she could break the moss seal on the door if she chose, could turn the handle and walk out into the facility's corridors, could request reassignment or evacuation or medical attention for whatever the chamber has done to her skin, her hair, her way of being. But she does not choose this. The chamber has given her a purpose, and the purpose is to be here. To tend. To observe. To grow alongside the growing things, accelerated and optimized, a biological component in a biological system, producing whatever it is that she was designed to produce.

The designers did not design her. But she is designed now. The chamber has made her so. And when the light panels cycle back to their blue-white daytime intensity, she rises from her hollow between the ferns, shakes the moisture from her skin, and begins her rounds—checking the moss, testing the soil, adjusting the wires, measuring the millimeters of new growth. The pumps hum below her feet. The water runs down the walls. The air sits close on her skin, heavy with the smell of wet soil and green growing things and something else underneath—something mineral, chemical, electric.
