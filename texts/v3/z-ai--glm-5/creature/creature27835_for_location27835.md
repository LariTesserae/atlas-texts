# What Lives in the Root-Cavity of Iyesh

---

## First, the majority

Before the people—before any shape that might recognize itself in a mirror—there is the substrate itself, and the substrate is alive.

The Old Growth came first and has never stopped coming. It is not a plant in the way surface-dwellers use the word. It does not reach for light. It has no interest in light. It grows downward and inward, through soil, through stone, through the remains of things that died before it was born, digesting as it goes. Its tissue is dark, almost black, braided like muscle fiber, dense enough to resist a blade but yielding enough to deform under sustained pressure. It forms the walls, the floor, the ceiling, the passages. It is the architecture and the architect both, though "architect" implies planning, and the Old Growth does not plan. It *does*. It grows toward what it needs and away from what it doesn't, and over centuries the consequence of those impulses is a cavity—a hollow in its own body, a space it does not fill.

Why does it not fill the cavity? This question has been asked. The inhabitants do not ask it. The Old Growth does what it does. The cavity exists because the Old Growth's growth patterns left a gap, and the gap persists because the conditions that created it have not changed. If they change, the cavity will close. The Old Growth will grow inward, the walls will meet, and Iyesh will cease. This is not a threat. It is simply what could happen. The inhabitants know this. They live with it the way surface-dwellers live with the possibility of earthquake: present in awareness, absent from daily panic.

The Old Growth breathes. The membrane that lines the cavity—thinner than the structural mass behind it, paler, more responsive—expands and contracts on a cycle of roughly forty seconds. It is not breathing air. It is moving moisture and dissolved compounds through capillary channels, processing the atmosphere of the cavity the way a lung processes the atmosphere of a chest. The effect is that the air in the cavity is turned over, slowly, continuously, kept fresh enough to sustain life and stale enough to remind you that you are inside something. The breath is warm at the peak of expansion and cooler at the trough of contraction. The inhabitants do not notice this. Visitors always do.

Above the Old Growth, or rather above the structural layer of it, hangs the New Skin. This is the living carpet: mosses, lichens, thread-fungi, and organisms that fit no surface-world category, forming a mat that renews itself every few weeks. The New Skin is pale—grey, green, faintly blue, sometimes iridescent in the fungal patches where bioluminescence concentrates. It grows on the Old Growth the way moss grows on stone, except that the Old Growth is not stone; it is alive, and the New Skin is not moss; it is a complex of organisms in symbiosis with the substrate and with each other. The New Skin regulates moisture. It absorbs waste—human waste, insect waste, the particulate remains of anything that dies on it—and converts it into nutrients that feed back into the Old Growth. It is the cavity's kidney, its skin, its digestive lining. It is also food. The inhabitants harvest it, cutting strips of the thick, curling tissue that the New Skin produces as part of its growth cycle, leaving the deeper layers intact so it regrows. The taste is mushroom and mineral. The texture is slightly rubbery. It is the staple. It is eaten raw, dried, ground into paste, or pressed into sheets that keep for weeks. The inhabitants do not consider it remarkable. It is what is here.

And from the ceiling, the Hangers descend. Dark cords, some thumb-thick, some wrist-thick, some longer than a person is tall. They are not vines. They are not roots. They are not animals. They do not fit. They hang motionless in air currents that have no visible source, swaying with a slowness that suggests inertia rather than intent. They do not respond to touch. They respond to vibration—footsteps, voices, the particular resonance of a child's run—by curling upward, retracting toward the ceiling, holding for a time, then descending again. The inhabitants move beneath them without concern. The Hangers have never harmed anyone. But the inhabitants move differently when they are directly beneath them. A slight quickening of pace. A subtle angling of the body. Not fear. Respect, perhaps. Acknowledgment that something is present whose purpose is not understood.

The Hangers are the cavity's question mark. The one element that resists assimilation into the inhabitants' understanding. The Old Growth is understood: it is the structure, the body. The New Skin is understood: it is the food, the filter. The insects are understood: they are the neighbors. The Hangers are not understood. They simply are, and their presence changes the character of the space below in ways that no one has articulated but everyone has internalized. You do not stand under a Hanger for longer than you must. You do not speak loudly when Hangers are near. You do not run beneath them. These are not rules. No one has made them rules. They are habits, and the habits are consistent across the community, which means they come from somewhere—experience, or story, or the kind of collective memory that does not require a single origin point.

## The smaller lives

The dust-crickets are the most visible non-human inhabitants. Thumb-length, pale, nearly transparent—their carapaces so thin that the dark mass of their digestive organs shows through like a stain inside glass. They navigate the root-walls in constant motion, their legs producing a dry scratching sound like paper against paper, a sound so pervasive it becomes the cavity's resting noise, the baseline against which all other sounds are measured. They shed their exoskeletons regularly, leaving behind fine particulate—the "dust" that gives them their name—which settles on every surface and is in turn consumed by the New Skin and by the glisten-mites.

They swarm to organic matter. Food left unattended for more than a few hours will be found by dust-crickets, and what they find, they reduce. The inhabitants have learned to store food in sealed cells, woven-grass containers, recesses carved high in the root-walls where the crickets are less dense. But the crickets are also useful. They are the cavity's first-line composters. They process waste. They clean. The inhabitants tolerate them the way a city tolerates pigeons: with a mixture of annoyance and acceptance, recognizing that they are part of the system, that removing them would create problems larger than the ones they cause.

The glisten-mites are smaller—pinhead-sized, barely visible as individual organisms, noticeable only in aggregate. They cluster in the bioluminescent zones, feeding on the fungi, their bodies catching and reflecting the faint light until patches of wall seem to shimmer with movement that is not the fungus's own. The effect is beautiful, if beauty is a word that applies here. The inhabitants do not use that word. They say the walls are *busy* when the mites are thick, and they say it with the same tone one might use to describe a day with many tasks. The mites migrate. At rest—in the communal sleeping piles, where bodies lie still for hours—the mites drift across skin, seeking warmth, seeking moisture, or simply moving because movement is what they do. The inhabitants do not brush them off. They have tried. The mites return. They are a condition of existence, not a problem to be solved. The inhabitants' skin has adapted—thicker in certain areas, producing oils that discourage the mites from settling in pores or orifices—but the adaptation is partial. The mites are always present. They are part of the sleeper, the way the dampness is part of the air.

The weave-spiders are not spiders. Taxonomically, they are something else—the inhabitants do not know what, and taxonomy is not their concern. They fill the spider's niche. They spin, producing a sticky excretion that hardens into thread, building structures in corners, in angles, in any gap wider than a finger. But their webs are not traps. They do not eat what they catch. They filter. Particulate—dust, spores, dead mites, debris—collects in their webs, and the spiders move through their constructions with methodical patience, bundling the catch into neat packets that they deposit at the edges of their territories. The packets are eventually collected by the inhabitants, who use the filtered material as fertilizer for the fungus gardens. The relationship is symbiotic, though neither party has agreed to it. The spiders clean the air. The inhabitants harvest the cleaning. Neither acknowledges the other. The spiders do not acknowledge anything. They build, they bundle, they deposit. They are the cavity's most industrious residents, and they are the least interested in being observed.

The damp-singers live in the water-channels. They are small—finger-length, frog-like, with bodies so translucent that their internal organs are visible, pulsing with the slow rhythm of their circulation. They produce sound. Not a call, not a song—a hum, low, almost subsonic, resonating through the water and through the root-mesh into the cavity's air. The hum vibrates the floor. The inhabitants feel it in their feet, their spines, their chests. During mating season—which corresponds to the high-sweat, when the water-table rises and the channels deepen—the singers' humming becomes constant, a baseline vibration that the inhabitants navigate by. The singers go silent when something large enters their channels. This silence is the cavity's alarm system. A sudden quiet from the water-direction means something has moved in, something big enough to disturb the singers, something that might be a threat. The inhabitants have learned to read these silences the way a sailor reads the sudden absence of birds. It is not infallible. It is reliable enough.

And then there are the deep-crawlers, and here the description must slow down, because the deep-crawlers resist the summary.

They are centipede-like, but they are not centipedes. They are longer than a forearm, segmented, with flexible exoskeletons that allow them to move through spaces narrower than their bodies should permit. They are dark—not black, but the deep brown of old wood, the color of something that has never seen light. They have many legs. They move with a fluidity that is wrong for an arthropod, too smooth, too deliberate. Insects react. Deep-crawlers *assess*. You can see them pause at the mouth of a passage, antennae—or what serves as antennae—sweeping the air, the floor, the walls, before they commit to a direction. They are not hunting. They are reading.

They live in the spaces between the root-mesh—the gaps beneath the floor, the channels inside the walls, the passages that are too small for humans but too large for mites. They emerge when they detect significant movement above, which means they emerge often, because in a community of sixty people, significant movement is constant. They do not attack. They do not threaten. They cross rooms—living rooms, working rooms, sleeping rooms—with the unhurried confidence of something that belongs there more than the inhabitants do. They have crawled across sleeping people. Legs on skin. The sensation, according to those who have experienced it, is not painful. It is cold, and it is intentional. The deep-crawler is not lost. It is going somewhere, and you happen to be in the way.

The inhabitants do not kill them. They do not trap them. They mark their passages—scratches on the root-walls, a system of notation that identifies which gaps are active, which are dormant, which are likely to reopen. They avoid blocking exits. A deep-crawler that cannot return to its passage is a problem. It will not attack, but it will not leave, and a deep-crawler that refuses to leave is a presence that reshapes the room around it. People stop using that room. The room becomes the deep-crawler's. The inhabitants accept this. They have lost rooms before. They will lose rooms again. The cavity has more rooms than it needs, for now.

There is something else about the deep-crawlers that the inhabitants discuss in low voices, in stories, in the careful language of people who are not certain whether what they have observed is real. The deep-crawlers avoid the sick. When someone in the community falls ill—which is rare, given the ferocity of their immune systems, but does happen—the deep-crawlers reroute. Their passages shift. They emerge from different gaps, avoiding the chambers where the sick person lies. It is as if they can detect illness and choose not to risk exposure. This has been observed enough times that it is not dismissed as coincidence. It is incorporated into the community's understanding. If the deep-crawlers are avoiding someone, that person is sick, even if the symptoms have not appeared yet. The inhabitants have learned to trust the deep-crawlers' assessment before their own.

This is, perhaps, the strangest relationship in the cavity. The humans and the deep-crawlers do not communicate. They do not interact, except in the incidental crossings that happen when spaces overlap. But the deep-crawlers' behavior provides information that the inhabitants use. The deep-crawlers are, in a functional sense, part of the community's early-warning system, alongside the damp-singers and the breathing walls. The cavity is full of sentinels, and none of them are human.

## The people

Now: the sixty.

They are not a tribe. "Tribe" implies a unity of identity, a shared self-concept that extends beyond the immediate. The inhabitants of Iyesh do not have a shared identity in that sense. They have a shared *situation*. They live in the same place, under the same conditions, subject to the same pressures, and this has produced a community that functions more like an organism than a society—interdependent, specialized, adaptive, each part responding to the needs of the whole without requiring instruction or consensus.
