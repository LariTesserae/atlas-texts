# The People of the Cold Cities

## The First Form

They are not tall. This is the first thing you notice, or rather, the first thing you understand after you have been among them long enough to see past the layers. Height is a liability at this altitude — longer limbs mean more surface area for heat to escape, longer necks mean more vital tissue exposed to the cold. The people here have evolved compactness, or perhaps the cold has simply culled the tall from the gene pool over the generations too numerous to count. Their bodies are dense, proportioned for retention: shorter limbs, deeper chests, heads that sit close to the shoulders as if perpeturally braced against wind that may or may not come.

The chest depth matters. The air is thin, and the people here have lungs that dominate their thoracic cavities, hearts that pump blood thick with the cells that carry oxygen. Their ribcages flare wider than lowlanders', creating torsos that appear almost barrel-shaped when stripped of their coverings. This is not deformity; it is adaptation, the body learning to extract what it can from air that gives reluctantly. Their breathing is different — slower, deeper, each breath held longer as if reluctant to surrender what has been gained.

Their skin tells stories of the cold. Not pale in the manner of those who hide from sun, but weathered, the complexion of people who have faced the elements for too many generations to count. The wind leaves its marks — fine lines that appear earlier than they would in softer climates, a permanent flush across cheekbones where the capillaries have learned to dilate and constrict with the rapid shifts between warmth and cold. Their hands show the most: thickened skin across palms and fingers, not from softness but from use, from the constant gripping of stone and tool and rope. The nails are kept short, practical, often stained by minerals and dyes extracted from the earth.

Their hair is darkness itself. Black dominates, occasionally brown, almost never lighter shades. It grows thick and straight, worn long by most — another layer of insulation, another barrier against the cold that seeps through every aperture. Men and women alike bind it back when working, release it when sleeping, the loose hair spreading like dark water across pillows of woven fiber. Gray appears early, by the thirtieth year in many cases, but this is not seen as decline. Gray hair, white hair, is valued for its softness, harvested when someone chooses to cut theirs, woven into ceremonial items or traded as a gift between families.

## The Face of the Cold

The people here have faces that seem carved from the same stone they inhabit. High cheekbones create natural windbreaks for the eyes below. The eyes themselves are often dark, brown or near-black, though green and amber appear in certain lineages with concentrations of iris pigment that seems to intensify rather than fade with age. The palpebral folds are pronounced — an adaptation, perhaps, to wind that carries ice crystals, to glare that reflects from snow and stone surfaces with equal intensity. Their eyes are not narrow, but protected, set slightly deeper than those of lowlanders, shaded by bone and brow.

The nose varies by region, but in the highest cities, it tends toward the aquiline — a prominent bridge that warms air before it reaches the lungs, nostrils that can narrow or flare depending on the cold. In the deepest chambers, where warmth permits, the nose appears less pronounced, the need for modification less urgent. But across the plateau, the face is a survival instrument, shaped by the air itself.

They do not smile as readily as peoples from warmer climes. This is not unhappiness — it is conservation. Facial expression requires muscle movement, requires energy, and in a place where every calorie matters, expression becomes a language of economy. When they do smile, it is meaningful, a deliberate signal rather than a reflex. Laughter exists, but it tends toward the quiet, the held-in, the sound that does not require great exhalation of warm air. They have humor, dry and sharp, but it is expressed through words more than through the face.

The voice is deep, resonant. Thin air carries sound poorly, and the people have learned to speak from the chest rather than the throat, producing tones that travel. In the carved chambers, this creates a natural amplification, the stone itself becoming an instrument. Some among them can sing in ways that seem impossible — sustained notes that harmonize with the acoustic properties of specific rooms, creating chords from single voices. This is learned young, the ability to hear the space and adjust accordingly.

## The Body Beneath

Stripped of their layers, the people reveal bodies of surprising strength. Not the defined musculature of those who train for display — the muscles here are functional, embedded in a framework of dense bone and considerable body fat. Fat is not stigmatized; it is revered as survival. To be thin is to be vulnerable, to be soft is to be protected. The ideal form is solid, heavy, the kind of body that retains warmth through mass alone.

Their hands are remarkable. The fingers are not long — length loses heat — but they are thick, strong, the grip powerful from generations of climbing and carving. The palms are wide, providing surface area for holding. They can maintain grip on ice-slicked stone, on frozen rope, on tools that become cold enough to burn. Their skin produces oils that help, a natural reaction to constant cold exposure, and they supplement this with rendered fats from animals, rubbing their hands and faces before going into the open air.

The feet are equally adapted. Wide, stable, callused in patterns that reflect the terrain of each city. In the high settlements, where stone floors are perpetual cold, the feet develop layers of protective skin that would seem extraordinary to lowlanders — not injuries but adaptations, the body creating its own footwear. Children born in these cities develop these calluses as they grow, the soles of their feet thickening with each year until they can walk across stone that would freeze the feet of the unadapted.

They do not walk so much as they flow across terrain. The gait of the plateau peoples is distinctive: a rolling motion that conserves energy, a center of gravity kept low and stable, each step placed with precision that becomes unconscious. To watch them move is to see something between walking and dancing, a continuous negotiation with ground that may shift, slope, or hide unexpected drops. They do not hurry — hurry burns energy without purpose. They move steadily, consistently, the same pace maintained across hours of travel.

## The Layers of Being

To see them dressed is to see architecture wrapped around flesh. The clothing here is not fashion but survival, and it reflects the same principles as the cities: layers upon layers, depth creating insulation, every surface considered for its thermal properties.

The first layer against the skin is soft, woven from the fibers of underground fungi or from the fine hair of certain burrowing animals. This layer is never removed in the cold season — it becomes a second skin, worn until it wears through, patched and mended until it cannot hold together any longer. The fibers are chosen not just for warmth but for their ability to wick moisture away from the body. Sweat is dangerous in the cold; dampness against skin becomes ice, and ice becomes death.

Above this, layers of woven material, each slightly looser than the one beneath, creating air pockets that hold warmth. The outer layers are treated with fats and oils, rendered waterproof against the pervasive ice crystals that drift through the air. The colors are the colors of the earth and its inhabitants: brown, black, gray, white, occasionally the rusty red of certain dyes extracted from mineral deposits. Patterns exist but are subtle — checks and stripes that identify family or clan, visible only to those who know how to read them.

The hands are covered in gloves where possible, though this makes fine work difficult. Many have learned to work with bare hands for short periods, warming them periodically against the body or near heat sources. The face is covered partially — hoods that frame the features, scarves that can be pulled up against wind. Only the eyes remain exposed in the coldest weather, and these are protected by narrow slits in headwear, the wearers looking through their own clothing at a world that would freeze them.

Footwear is perhaps the most sophisticated element. Boots constructed from multiple layers of leather and woven material, soles thick enough to insulate against frozen stone, uppers that extend above the ankle to prevent the entry of snow or ice. In deep winter, additional layers are added — outer shells of treated hide, inner liners of soft fur, the entire assembly creating a warmth that can be maintained for hours in the coldest conditions. The boots are removed only in heated spaces, and even then, they are kept near warmth sources to ensure they do not freeze into unusable shapes.

## The Texture of Life

A day in the life of these people follows the rhythms of warmth rather than light. In the deep chambers, time becomes abstract — the sun is a presence known through shafts and reflections, a distant god whose movements matter less than the immediate needs of body and community.

Waking is gradual. The body emerges from sleep slowly, reluctantly, the warmth of the sleeping space difficult to leave for the cooler air of the surrounding chamber. Families sleep together in shared warmth, bodies pressed against bodies, the youngest in the center, the oldest at the edges where cold is most present. To wake is to shift position, to feel the movement of others, to determine by the temperature of the air and the angle of light shafts how much time has passed.

Work begins with the necessary tasks. Water must be drawn from cisterns where the moisture has collected overnight. Food must be retrieved from cold storage, prepared in the warming chambers where geothermal heat or stored thermal mass makes cooking possible. The management of these spaces is the work of many hands — children carry water, adults tend the fires where they exist, the elderly direct the placement of items in the thermal gradients that maximize preservation.

Beyond these necessities, work varies by location and skill. The carvers — those who shape the stone that shapes the lives of the people — work in shifts, their tools striking against rock in rhythms that echo through the chambers. Some work expanding existing passages, others creating new ones, still others repairing the damage that time and cold inflict on the carved surfaces. This is slow work, measured in generations. A single room may take decades to complete, its shape evolving as different hands work different sections, the original plan modified by circumstances that could not be foreseen when the first stroke was made.

Weavers work with fibers that require delicate handling, their fingers moving in patterns memorized over years. The looms are simple, the techniques complex, the resulting fabrics varying in quality from the rough work of outer layers to the fine threads of undergarments worn by those with status or wealth. Weaving is often done in the warmer chambers, where fingers remain flexible, where the materials do not stiffen with cold.

Hunters and trappers move between the cities and the surface, following the paths that connect the carved entrances to the open plateau. Their work requires different clothing, different tools, different bodies. They tend to be leaner than those who live entirely in the depths, their bodies adapted to the cold they face daily. They carry the snares and nets that capture the burrowing animals, the spears and throwing sticks that can bring down the larger grazers. They are respected, feared slightly, seen as necessary intermediaries between the safe world of the chambers and the dangerous world of the open air.

Tenders work in the fungal gardens, managing the decay that produces food. These are quiet people, observant, skilled at reading the subtle signs of growth and rot. Their chambers are warmer than most, heated by the processes they oversee, and they tend to be more comfortable than their peers, their bodies less compressed by cold, their mannerisms more relaxed. The gardens themselves are not gardens in the conventional sense — they are managed ecosystems of decay, the careful encouragement of organisms that transform organic matter into edible forms. Substrate is prepared, inoculated, monitored, harvested. The work requires patience and attention, but it yields reliable returns.

Children move through all these spaces, learning by watching, helping where they can. Their education is not formal but osmotic — they absorb the knowledge of their elders through proximity, through the stories told in the evening gathering times, through the tasks they are assigned that gradually increase in complexity. By adolescence, most know where they fit in the work of the city, where their skills and inclinations will lead them.

## The Social Web

The density of living creates social structures unlike those in more dispersed populations. Privacy exists as a concept but rarely as a reality — most chambers are shared by family groups, most activities visible to neighbors and kin. This creates a society where everyone knows everyone, where secrets are difficult to keep and deception is quickly detected.

The family is the fundamental unit, but the family extends beyond blood. Those who live together are family, regardless of parentage. Adoption is common, formalized through rituals that bind children to new parents, parents to new children. The orphan is never truly orphaned — there is always someone willing to add another body to the warmth of a sleeping cluster, another set of hands to the work of survival.

Beyond the family is the clan, a broader grouping that traces descent from shared ancestors, real or mythological. Clans control sections of each city, their territories defined by tradition and maintained through constant presence. To enter another clan's space is not forbidden, but it is noticed, monitored, potentially questioned. The clans compete for resources, for prestige, for the best chambers and the warmest locations, but this competition is ritualized, constrained by traditions that prevent open conflict.

Beyond the clan is the city itself, unified by geography and history. Each city has its own identity, its own dialect variations, its own customs and ceremonies. The cities are connected by the roads and tunnels that span the plateau, but travel is slow, communication limited. What unifies them is more than what divides them — the shared experience of cold, of stone, of a world where survival requires cooperation.

Governance happens through councils, gatherings of representatives from each major clan. These councils meet regularly, their decisions recorded in carved symbols and oral tradition. The councils manage disputes, allocate resources, plan the long-term expansion of chambers and passages. Leadership is not inherited but earned, through skill, through wisdom, through the demonstrated ability to make decisions that benefit the community. The leaders are often old, their experience valued, but young people with demonstrated ability can and do rise to positions of influence.

Conflict exists, of course — humans remain human. But the forms it takes are shaped by circumstance. Violence is rare, not because the people are peaceful by nature but because violence is dangerous in a world where injury can mean death, where every body is needed for survival. Disputes are resolved through mediation, through compensation, through the careful application of social pressure. Those who cannot or will not resolve their conflicts face the ultimate sanction: exile, removal from the warmth and community that make life possible. This sentence is rarely imposed, and when it is, it is often a sentence of death.

## The Shape of Thought

The people here think differently, shaped by the land that shapes their bodies. Time is long — the stone they carve has been stone for millions of years, the chambers they create will outlast them by generations. They do not think in days or months but in decades and centuries. A project begun by an ancestor is a project continued, not a burden but a continuation, the living participating in work that the dead began and the unborn will complete.

This temporal perspective creates a relationship with the present that seems detached to outsiders. Urgency is rare — what cannot be done today can be done tomorrow, or next year, or by someone else when the time is right. The present is important, but it is not paramount. The future matters more, the past matters as much. The self is seen as a link in a chain, not the endpoint of existence.

Their relationship with the land is not ownership but stewardship. They do not possess the stone; they inhabit it. The chambers they carve are not property but inheritance, held in trust for those who will come after. This creates a pattern of care that extends across generations — nothing is built cheaply, nothing is done temporarily. Everything is made to last, to serve not just the maker but the maker's descendants.

Faith, as noted, centers on warmth and depth. The gods are not distant creators but presences in the earth itself, the heat that rises from below, the life that persists in cold. The deepest chambers, those nearest the geothermal warmth, are the most sacred, access to them restricted to those who have earned it through age or contribution. To be permitted into these spaces is to experience a foretaste of what comes after death — not an afterlife in some distant heaven but a return to the warmth that birthed all life.

The concept of the soul is thermal. The soul is heat, the spark that differentiates living flesh from dead meat. When a person dies, their heat departs — this is visible, measurable, the body cooling as the soul leaves. Where the soul goes is uncertain — some believe it returns to the earth's warmth, merging with the geothermal forces that sustain the cities. Others believe it joins a collective warmth, the accumulated heat of all the ancestors, available to be called upon by the living when need arises. Still others believe it simply dissipates, spreading outward like the warmth of a fire long extinguished.

Rituals mark the transitions of life. Birth is celebrated not when the child emerges but when it takes its first breath, when the air of the plateau enters its lungs and the body demonstrates its ability to survive. This moment is called the First Welcoming, and it involves the entire family, gathering to witness the child's first cry, its first proof of life. The child is then wrapped in layers, introduced to the chambers that will be its world, presented to the clan that will be its community.

Death is marked by the Final Cooling. The body is wrapped in the finest layers available, placed in a chamber that grows progressively colder as one moves outward from the center of the city. The family keeps vigil, watching as the warmth departs, noting the moment when the body becomes indistinguishable from the stone around it. The dead are not buried — they are returned. Bodies are placed in natural cavities, in abandoned chambers, in the spaces between stone and air that exist throughout the plateau. The cold preserves them, mummification occurring naturally in the dry air and freezing temperatures. In some cities, the dead remain visible, their forms creating a physical connection between generations, the ancestors present in the stone that houses the living.

## The Relations Between Cities

No city exists in isolation. The network that spans the plateau connects them all, and this connection creates relationships as complex as those within any single settlement. Trade moves along the carved roads and tunnels — materials that are abundant in one place and scarce in another, crafted goods that reflect local skills, foodstuffs that vary by region and altitude.

Marriage and partnership cross city boundaries. Young adults often travel, spending time in distant cities, forming connections that may lead to permanent bonds. This movement creates ties of kinship that span the plateau, networks of relatives who can be called upon for shelter, for assistance, for information. The resulting web of connection helps maintain peace — to war with another city is to war with one's own extended family.

But tensions exist. Resources are not evenly distributed, and cities with access to better geothermal warmth, better hunting grounds, better trade routes can become wealthy in ways that others cannot. This wealth is not displayed but it is known — the quality of clothing, the fineness of carved decorations, the abundance of stored food. Jealousy is rare, but desire is not, and cities may negotiate for access, for rights, for the opportunity to share in advantages they did not inherit.

Language varies across the plateau, dialects emerging from isolation and evolving from contact. The core vocabulary is shared — words for stone, for cold, for warmth, for family — but accents differ, idioms reflect local circumstances, and visitors from distant cities may find themselves struggling to understand the precise meaning of unfamiliar phrasing. This is not a barrier but a marker of identity, a way of knowing where someone belongs before they have spoken more than a few words.

The relationship with outsiders is complex. Visitors are rare, the journey to the plateau difficult and dangerous. Those who arrive are treated with caution but not hostility — curiosity outweighs suspicion, the desire to learn outweighs fear of the unknown. The people recognize that they are not alone in the world, that vast populations exist in the warmer lowlands. Some of these populations send traders, seekers, the occasional lost traveler. The plateau peoples trade with them, welcome them, learn what they can from their presence. But the visitors rarely stay long — the cold defeats them, the thin air exhausts them, the food seems strange and insufficient. They leave, eventually, and the plateau returns to its equilibrium, its population of adapted humans continuing the slow work of survival.

## The Shape of Love

Intimacy in the cold cities is shaped by necessity and privacy. Bodies that warm each other are valued, partnerships that produce children are celebrated, but these practical considerations coexist with emotional bonds as deep as any in warmer climates.

The concept of romance is different. Courtship is not display but negotiation, the determination of compatibility between families and individuals. But within this framework, affection develops — the shared warmth of sleeping spaces, the quiet conversations during evening work, the small kindnesses that mark the passage of time. Love exists here, expressed through presence rather than presents, through the giving of warmth rather than the giving of things.

Sexual relationships are private by necessity — the shared chambers offer little opportunity for concealment, but social conventions create boundaries that are respected. The children of these unions grow up surrounded by family, learning early that they belong to a group larger than any individual pairing. Jealousy is discouraged, possessiveness seen as a threat to the cooperation that survival requires. This does not mean that pain is absent, only that it is managed, channeled into forms that do not disrupt the community.

Partnerships persist across time, sometimes formalized through ceremonies that bind families together, sometimes informal arrangements that continue until they no longer serve. Dissolution is possible, recognized, accepted — the individuals simply find new arrangements, new sleeping clusters, new family configurations. What matters is not the specific bond but the ongoing connection, the continuation of relationship in some form.

## The End of the Description

The people of the cold cities are not exotic. They are human, shaped by circumstances that would defeat most and sustained by adaptations that have accumulated over countless generations. They laugh, they love, they work, they die. Their faces carry the marks of the cold, their bodies tell the story of the stone, but their eyes hold the same light that illuminates human eyes everywhere — curiosity, determination, the will to continue.

If you were to visit, you would notice the cold first. Then the thinness of the air. Then the silence. But if you stayed long enough to acclimate, to enter the deep chambers, to experience the warmth that lives at the heart of each mountain-city, you would notice something else. You would see people who have found a way to live in a world that seems hostile, who have carved comfort from stone, who have built warmth in the midst of cold. You would see children playing in carved passages, their laughter echoing off walls that have heard laughter for generations. You would see elders teaching skills that their elders taught them, knowledge passing down through hands and voices, through demonstration and practice. You would see, in short, a civilization — not one defined by the monuments it builds above ground, but by the spaces it carves below, by the warmth it creates and maintains, by the persistence of human life in a place that would prefer to be empty.

This is who lives here. This is what they are.
