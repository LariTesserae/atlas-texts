# The Inhabitants of the Veldt-Works

## The Drifters

They do not know they are maintaining anything.

This is the first thing to understand about the hexagonal filter-feeders, the ones you see moving along the columns in their slow, rhythmic way. They experience their lives as a sequence of hungers and satisfactions, not as a contribution to a system. They do not think "I am keeping the electrolysis membrane clean." They think nothing, or something adjacent to nothing—a diffuse awareness of gradient, of the boundary between food and not-food, of the faint pleasure of contraction and release.

Their bodies are not complex. A hexagonal frame of cartilage-like material, thin enough to flex, thick enough to hold shape. Between the struts, a membrane—not gelatinous, but structured, layered with channels that move water through and past and out. At each corner of the hexagon, a cluster of simple sensory cells: light-dark, chemical presence, electrical field. They navigate by reading the columns' bioelectric signature, following the faint charge differential that exists around every trunk. Where the charge is strongest, the film is thickest. They have learned this, or rather, they have been shaped to it, over time so long that learning and instinct have become indistinguishable.

Their movement is beautiful, if you watch it long enough. Each contraction is a pulse, a heartbeat that propels them forward, then a relaxation, a gliding, then another pulse. They move like jellyfish, but the rhythm is different—slower, more deliberate, with a rest between each effort that makes the effort seem considered. They are never in a hurry. There is nothing to hurry toward. The film grows at a constant rate; the drifters eat at a constant rate; the equilibrium maintains itself.

They reproduce, though you are unlikely to see it. A drifter, after enough cycles of feeding, begins to split—not violently, not dramatically, but along a seam that forms at the center of the hexagon, a line of weakness that deepens over weeks until the organism is two half-hexagons, each of which then grows the missing segments, filling in the gap, becoming whole again. The process takes months. It happens in the quieter zones, between the columns, where the filaments are sparse and the electrical discharge is minimal. The two new drifters do not acknowledge each other. They separate. They begin to feed. They have no memory of having been one thing. They are not the type of organism that would benefit from memory.

They do not live long, by the standards of this place. A decade, perhaps two, before the membrane begins to degrade, before the sensory clusters lose their sensitivity, before the drift becomes less purposeful and more passive, until the current carries them down into the dark zones and they become food for something else. But "long" is not a meaningful concept here. There is always another drifter. There have always been drifters. The columns are always clean. The film is always eaten. The function continues.

## The Thread-Swimmers

The small, silver-green creatures that navigate the filament networks have a different relationship to the electrical discharge than you might expect.

They are attracted to it.

Not to the discharge itself—the spark is dangerous, can damage their delicate lateral lines, can stun or kill if they are too close—but to the field that precedes it. They can sense the potential building between two filaments, can read the charge differential, can predict within a few seconds when the discharge will occur. And they gather. In the moments before a spark, the thread-swimmers cluster near the crossing point, waiting, feeding rapidly on the biofilm that accumulates there—the film grows faster at the electrical nodes, for reasons that have to do with chemistry, not with intention—and then, in the instant before the discharge, they scatter. Away. Outward. In all directions. A small explosion of silver-green bodies, visible for a half-second as a cloud, then gone, dissolved back into the distances between the filaments.

It is not fear that makes them scatter. It is something closer to strategy. The discharge stuns the smaller organisms that live in the biofilm—microscopic things, barely visible, the thread-swimmers' primary food source. When the spark passes, these microorganisms are momentarily immobilized, their membranes disrupted, their defenses down. The thread-swimmers return, quickly, before the microorganisms recover, and they feed. The spark is a tool. The thread-swimmers do not know they are using it. They only know that if they gather at the crossing points, and scatter at the right moment, and return quickly, there is more food than if they feed elsewhere.

This is not intelligence. It is optimization. The same process that shaped the columns, that shaped the drifters, has shaped the thread-swimmers—selection acting over such deep time that the result appears designed, even though no designer ever existed. The thread-swimmers are shaped to use the electrical fields. The electrical fields are produced by the filaments. The filaments are produced by the columns. The columns are maintained by the drifters. The drifters are fed by the film produced by the columns. Everything feeds into everything else. The system is closed. The system runs.

The thread-swimmers have a social structure, of a kind. They move in loose schools—not tight formations, not coordinated movements, but aggregations, clusters of individuals who happen to be moving in the same direction, toward the same concentration of resources. They communicate through electrical pulses of their own, faint signals generated by specialized cells along their flanks. The signals are simple: *here food*, *here danger*, *here others*. The meaning is not precise. It does not need to be. The thread-swimmers do not have complex lives. They feed, they avoid the stronger discharges, they breed, they die. Their lifespan is shorter than the drifters'. Their numbers are greater. The system requires more of them, because their function—keeping the filament network functional, preventing the biofilm from choking the electrical pathways—requires more.

They do not sleep. They slow, at times, their movements becoming sluggish, their responses delayed, but they do not fully stop. There is no safe place to stop, in the filament network. The discharges are unpredictable. The drifters are always moving. The columns pump and pulse. The thread-swimmers exist in a state of constant low-level alertness, their sensory systems always sampling, always testing, always ready to scatter.

It sounds exhausting. It is not. Exhaustion requires a memory of rest. The thread-swimmers have never rested. They do not know that rest exists.

## The Deep Ones

You will not see them. But you will know them, eventually, by their effects.

The deep zones—below the light, below the active columns, where the water pressure compresses and the temperature rises and the chemistry changes—have their own inhabitants. They live in the root zones, among the bacterial mats and the mineral deposits and the slow decomposition of everything that drifts down from above. They are not like the drifters. They are not like the thread-swimmers. They are something else entirely.

They are larger. Much larger. You know this because of the way the light dims when they pass below you—shadows that are not the shadows of columns, shadows that move with purpose, shadows that have shape and mass and duration. You cannot see their outlines. They are too deep, too far, too obscured by the layers of water and growth between you and them. But you can feel their presence. The water shifts when they move. The sound changes, subtly, a new frequency entering the chord, a deeper note that was not there before and then is gone again.

They are not predators, in the sense that they hunt. There is nothing to hunt, in the deep zones. The food comes to them—drifting down, falling, settling, accumulating. Dead drifters. Dead thread-swimmers. The constant rain of organic particulate from the working world above. The bacterial mats that grow on the mineral deposits, processing, decomposing, releasing. The deep ones move through this resource like gardeners moving through a field, gathering, processing, digesting. Their metabolic rate is slow. Their movements are slower. A deep one can drift for months without feeding, sustaining itself on stored reserves, waiting for the right concentration of resources to accumulate.

They do not come up. They have no reason to come up. The world above is brighter, more turbulent, more electrical. Their sensory systems are adapted to the deep—the pressure, the warmth, the particular chemistry of decomposition. They detect their environment through chemical gradients, through the faint vibrations of things moving far above, through the subsonic rumble of the columns' root systems pumping and processing. They do not see, in any meaningful sense. They do not need to. The deep is dark. The deep has always been dark. The deep ones have found ways to live in the dark that do not require light.

They are not solitary, but they are not social either. They exist in proximity to one another, their ranges overlapping, their paths occasionally crossing. When they cross, there is a recognition—not friendship, not bonding, but an acknowledgment. A chemical signal, passed through the water, that says *I am here, I am alive, I am of your kind*. Then they separate. They continue their slow drift. They do not communicate further. There is nothing to communicate. Their lives are the same life, repeated across individuals, across generations, across the deep time of the Veldt-Works.

They reproduce in a way that connects them to the world above. When a deep one has accumulated enough resources, when its reserves are sufficient, it begins to rise. Slowly. Over weeks, or months, or years—no one has observed the full process, because the deep time is measured differently than the surface time, because the distance is too great, because the observation has never been made. But they rise. They move up through the columnar forest, passing through the zones of light, past the drifters and the thread-swimmers, past the electrical discharges and the hydraulic pumping, until they reach a zone where the conditions are right. Then they release. Eggs, or spores, or something in between—particles that drift on the currents, that catch in the filaments, that settle on the columns. The particles grow. They become larvae. The larvae become something else. The something else sinks, slowly, over time, back down into the deep zones, where it becomes, eventually, a deep one.

The cycle is closed. The cycle has always been closed. The deep ones do not know they are part of a cycle. They do not know anything. They simply do what they do, because doing anything else has never been selected for, because the system has no room for alternatives, because the equilibrium is total.

## The Microbial Mat

You cannot see them, but they are what makes everything else possible.

The bacterial cultures that live in the root zones, in the deep substrate, on the surfaces of the columns, in the biofilm that accumulates on the filaments, in the digestive tracts of the drifters and the thread-swimmers and the deep ones—these are the true inhabitants of the Veldt-Works. They outnumber the visible organisms by factors that cannot be calculated. Their total biomass exceeds the biomass of everything you can see. They are the workers, the processors, the ones who do the fundamental labor of the system.

They fix nitrogen. They break down mineral compounds. They process the waste products of the larger organisms and transform them into substances the plants can use. They consume the hydrogen that the columns produce, in certain zones, and convert it into energy, sustaining themselves in the dark places where no light reaches. They consume the oxygen that the columns release, in other zones, driving the decomposition that returns nutrients to the substrate. They live in gradients—on the boundaries between conditions, between chemical states, between the aerobic and the anaerobic, the light and the dark, the living and the dead.

They do not have individual lives. A single bacterium in the Veldt-Works is not meaningfully separate from the bacteria around it. They exchange genetic material constantly. They share metabolic functions. They are, in a meaningful sense, a single distributed organism, spread across thousands of kilometers, existing in every zone where the conditions permit, performing every function that the system requires. When a drifter dies and sinks into the deep zones, the bacterial mat receives it. When a column releases oxygen into the root system, the bacterial mat processes it. When the water chemistry shifts, for whatever reason, the bacterial mat adapts, shifting its composition, adjusting its metabolism, maintaining the equilibrium.

The bacterial mat is the oldest inhabitant of the Veldt-Works. It was here before the columns. It was here before the fish, before the drifters, before any of the visible life. It will be here after all of them are gone, if they ever go—processing, transforming, maintaining. It does not know that it is maintaining anything. It does not know anything. It is a process, not a being. It is the process that makes all other processes possible.

## The Absent

There is something that does not live here, and its absence is the most important fact about the Veldt-Works.

There are no builders. No architects. No designers. No one who sat down—or swam, or drifted, or existed in whatever form—and decided that there should be columns, that there should be filaments, that there should be drifters and thread-swimmers and deep ones. No one who drew up plans, who calculated outputs, who designed the system for efficiency or for profit or for any reason at all.

There are no witnesses. No observers who watch the system and record what happens. No historians who document its changes. No scientists who study its processes. No one who experiences the Veldt-Works as a thing to be understood. The drifters do not understand. The thread-swimmers do not understand. The deep ones do not understand. The bacterial mat does not have the capacity to understand, even if it had the inclination, which it does not, because inclination requires a self, and the bacterial mat has no self.

There are no visitors. The Veldt-Works is not a place that receives guests. It is not a place that anyone comes to, except you, now, in this moment of observation that is outside the normal operation of the system. You are the first being to see the columns and know them as columns. You are the first being to hear the sound and recognize it as industrial. You are the first being to move through this world and experience it as a world, rather than as the background conditions of existence.

This is why the system feels so strange. Not because of its scale, not because of its complexity, not because of its industrial character—but because it has existed for so long without anyone knowing it exists. It has no relationship to a consciousness. It has no relationship to a perspective. It simply is, in a way that most things are not, because most things are observed, most things are named, most things exist in relation to beings who experience them.

The Veldt-Works exists outside of experience. It is not for anyone. It is not about anyone. It is not from anyone or to anyone. It is a process that has continued so long that the process has become the reality, and the reality has no need of acknowledgment.

This is what it means to be a world in completion.
