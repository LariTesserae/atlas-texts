# Those Who Dwell in the Root

## The First Glimpse

You see them before you understand them.

A figure emerges from a bark-walled passage, walking toward you across the root-mat. The first thing you notice is the gait — a slow, rolling motion, weight settling fully onto each foot before lifting the next. Not labored. Not elderly. Simply... deliberate. The way someone walks when they have nowhere to be and all the time to get there, or when the ground beneath them requires attention.

As the figure approaches, you see more. The clothes are layers of the pale cloth — three, four, maybe five distinct garments, loose and overlapping, the inner layers darker with absorbed sap-oil, the outer layers weathered to a soft cream. The fabric moves independently, each layer shifting against the others. The effect is oddly fluid, like watching sediment settle through water.

The face is what surprises you. You had expected — what? Some sign of deprivation, perhaps. The pallor of those who live without sun. The gauntness of those who subsist on strange food. But the face is round, calm, unremarkable. The skin carries a faint amber undertone, not from pigment but from something in the sap-air, a staining that builds over years. The eyes are clear. The expression is neutral, curious, waiting. A face that has never needed to guard itself against bright light or hard wind.

This is when you understand that you have entered a place that takes care of its own.

---

## The Body

They are not tall. This is the second thing you notice, after the gait. The average height is perhaps a hand's breadth shorter than surface-dwellers, and the frame is broader, denser. The low gravity of expectation — the absence of urgency — has not made them soft. The root-mat demands balance. The work demands patience. The body adapts.

Their hands are distinctive. The fingers are thick, the palms wide, the skin calloused in specific patterns — pads at the first joints of the fingers, ridges along the edges of the palms, a toughened shield across the heel of the hand. These are the marks of grafting, of shaping bark, of working root. The nails are kept short, practical, and the nail-beds are stained dark from the sap. You see older workers whose hands have become almost bark-like themselves, the skin tough and ridged, the joints swollen but not painful. The hands of someone who has spent decades negotiating with plants.

Their hair is worn long, usually braided or bound, and it carries the amber stain more visibly than the skin. Some of them have hair that has become almost the color of cured tobacco — not dyed, just accumulated. The young have darker hair. The elders have hair that matches the root-mat.

They do not smell like surface people. The air here is thick with resin and sap, and the bodies absorb it. A worker who has lived here for years carries a scent that is sweet, slightly vegetal, like dried fruit or cured tobacco. It is not unpleasant. It is simply *present* — a signature of the place, worn in the skin.

## The Pace

You watch them work, and you begin to understand that the machinery has taught them.

In the oil-refinery, a woman stands before a vat of heating sap, stirring with a long paddle of lignified root. She stirs once every thirty seconds — a slow, sweeping motion, bottom to top, top to bottom. Between stirs, she is motionless. She watches. She waits. She does not fidget. She does not check anything else. Her attention is singular, patient, complete. The sap will tell her when it is ready. She knows the signs — the viscosity, the color, the way the surface tension catches the light. She does not rush. Rushing would not make the sap ready faster. Rushing would only make her tired.

This is the lesson of the root-mat: things take the time they take. The root grows a centimeter a day. The thread spins at forty revolutions per minute. The oil separates in its own time. The inhabitants have learned to match their rhythm to the place. Not out of philosophy. Out of necessity. Out of decades of living inside a system that does not hurry.

A child runs past you once, chasing a beetle-like creature across the root-mat, and the contrast is striking. The child moves at a surface pace — quick, urgent, burning energy. The adults watch with expressions that are hard to read. Not disapproval. Not amusement. Something closer to recognition. They remember moving that way. They will watch the child slow down, over the years, the way a stream slows down when it reaches a flat plain. It is not a loss. It is an arrival.

## The Social Texture

There is no government here.

This is not the same as saying there is no order. There is order everywhere — in the layout of the structures, in the rhythm of the factories, in the unspoken agreements that let two hundred people share a valley-sized space without collision. But there is no council, no mayor, no written constitution. When you ask about this, the question is met with puzzled silence. "Who decides?" they repeat back to you. "Decides *what*?"

The question does not seem to make sense to them. The root-mat grows where it grows. The factories produce what they produce. The sap flows where it flows. There is no decision to be made about these things; they simply *are*. And the human arrangements — who works where, who lives in which structure, who takes responsibility for which section of the root-mat — emerge through a process you cannot quite see. Conversation, perhaps. Habit. Long-established patterns that predate anyone living. "My grandmother trained the roots on the eastern edge," one worker tells you. "Her grandmother built the refinery. We have always worked with oil." This is not a hierarchy. It is a memory.

There are disputes, of course. Humans are humans. But the disputes are resolved through a process that seems to involve everyone sitting very still and waiting until the situation becomes clear. This can take hours. It can take days. No one seems to find this unreasonable. When you ask how they know when a dispute is resolved, the answer is: "You know."

They are not wrong. You watch one such situation — a disagreement about who has the right to harvest from a particular sap-pool. The two parties stand in the plaza, facing each other. Others gather, not to take sides, but simply to be present. No one speaks for a long time. The silence holds the disagreement, turns it over, examines it from different angles. Then one of the disputing parties says, quietly, "It was my mother's pool." The other nods, slowly. "I did not know." The gathering disperses. The dispute is over.

This would not work on the surface. You know this. On the surface, silence is a weapon, a void to be filled with persuasion, rhetoric, force. Here, silence is a medium. It is how the place speaks.

## The Young

There are children. Not many — perhaps thirty in total, scattered across the settlement — but enough to remind you that this is a population, not a workforce.

They are raised collectively, though not institutionally. A child's primary caregivers are usually their birth parents, but the definition of "parent" is loose. The woman who runs the thread-factory is called "aunt" by at least six children who are not related to her by blood. The old man who tends the lichen-mats on the ceiling is "grandfather" to everyone under twenty. When you ask about this, you are told: "Everyone is related. Some are just closer than others."

The children do not go to school. They *go to places*. They follow adults. They watch. They ask questions. They are given small tasks — sorting seed-pods, sweeping bark-dust, feeding the beetle-things. The tasks are not educational in any formal sense. They are simply work, scaled to small hands. The children learn by proximity, by absorption, by being present while things happen.

They also play. The root-mat is a playground — soft enough to fall on, complex enough to explore, full of tunnels and passages and hidden corners. You see children invent games that involve climbing the root-structures, balancing on the sap-conduits, hiding in the spaces between dwellings. They know which areas are safe and which are not. A child who ventures too close to the growing edge is called back by whoever is nearest, not with alarm, but with a calm, "Not yet. The roots aren't ready." The child accepts this. The roots will be ready eventually. There is no rush.

Literacy exists here, but it is not universal. Those who need to read — the workers who receive shipments from the surface, the few who maintain the written records of graft-patterns and sap-flows — learn to read. The others do not. It is not considered a fundamental skill. Information travels by voice, by memory, by the living text of the root-mat itself. A worker can look at a wall of grafted bark and read its history — when it was grown, who trained it, what pressures it has endured. This is a kind of literacy, too.

## The Old

You meet an elder on your third day. Her name is not given to you; names are not casually exchanged here. She sits outside a dwelling near the refinery, her hands folded in her lap, her face turned toward the pale lichen-light above. She is, you are told, over a hundred years old.

She does not look it. Her skin is lined, yes, but her posture is upright, her eyes clear, her attention present. She is not waiting to die. She is simply sitting. When you approach, she acknowledges you with a slight inclination of her head. When you speak, she listens. When you finish, she is silent for a long time.

Then she says: "You are very fast."

It is not an insult. It is an observation. She is describing what she sees — a person whose rhythm is mismatched with the place. "The young are fast," she continues. "Then they slow. Then they see. You have not slowed yet."

You ask what she sees, now that she has slowed.

She considers. "The roots growing. The sap moving. The stone giving way. The same things I saw when I was young. But now I see that they are the same thing."

She does not explain further. Perhaps she cannot. Perhaps understanding is not something that can be transferred, only arrived at.

You ask how long she has lived here.

"All my life," she says. "My mother's life. Her mother's life. We have always been here."

You ask if she has ever wanted to leave. To see the surface.

She looks at you with something that might be pity. "The surface is bright," she says. "And dry. And the plants there do not know you." She touches the root-mat beside her. "This knows me. I have walked on this ground for a hundred years. It has grown around my steps. When I die, it will take me. And I will stay."

## The Dead

They do not bury their dead.

This is explained to you in the same tone used to describe the machinery: factual, unhurried, without drama. When someone dies, their body is taken to the edge of the root-mat — the active edge, where the roots are still advancing, still hungry. The body is laid on the raw stone, just ahead of the root-line. The roots reach it within hours. They cover it within days. They absorb it within weeks.

There are no markers. No monuments. The dead become part of the substrate, literally. Their substance feeds the root-mat, becomes the floor that the living walk on, becomes the walls of future structures. It is not considered morbid. It is considered correct.

"The root-mat is made of ancestors," one worker tells you. He does not say this with reverence. He says it the way you might say "the river flows downstream." It is a fact of the place.

You ask if this is painful to think about.

He considers the question seriously. "It would be painful to lose someone and have them go *away*. To a hole in the ground. To a box. To nowhere. Here, they stay. They are still the floor. They are still the walls. When I walk to the factory, I walk on my grandmother. When I sleep, my grandfather holds up the roof." He pauses. "How is that painful? That is just staying."

## The Surface

No one here has been to the surface.

This is the fact that sits in your chest, heavy and strange. They know it exists. They know things come down the shafts and go up the shafts. They trade with the surface — the cloth goes up, the tools and books and occasional people come down. But the shafts are not for them. They are for cargo. No one climbs them. No one asks to.

You ask a young worker if she is curious. She considers. "I am curious about what it looks like," she says. "The light, the plants that do not know us, the people who move fast. But I would not go there."

Why not?

"Because I would have to come back," she says. "And when I came back, I would not fit. I would have seen the wrong things. I would move at the wrong speed. The root-mat would know."

You ask what the root-mat would do.

She does not know. No one has ever tested it. But the fear is there — not a sharp fear, not a panic, but a deep, cellular caution. To leave is to become foreign. To become foreign is to lose something essential. The surface is a trading partner, not a destination. This is not a prison. It is a home that requires presence.

## The Strangers

Sometimes people come down the shafts.

They are rare — once or twice a year, perhaps — and they never stay long. They are traders, mostly, bringing goods that cannot be grown here: metal tools, ceramic vessels, books, paper. Sometimes they are travelers, curious about the legendary root-town in the deep earth. Sometimes they are lost.

The inhabitants treat strangers with a consistent, careful courtesy. They are housed, fed, given sap to drink and cloth to sleep under. They are shown the factories, the root-mat, the growing edges. They are answered when they ask questions. They are left alone when they do not.

But there is always a distance. The strangers move too fast, speak too loudly, look around with eyes that do not know how to read the place. The inhabitants watch them the way you might watch a guest who does not know the customs — not with judgment, but with an awareness that something is mismatched. The guest will leave. The root-mat will remain. There is no reason to invest too deeply in what will not stay.

One worker tells you, quietly, that strangers often ask the same question: "How can you live here?" The worker does not understand the question. "Where else would we live? This is where we are. This is what the ground is."

The surface, to them, is the strange place. Bright, dry, exposed, temporary. Plants that grow and die in a single season. Stone that sits on top of the earth instead of underneath it. People who do not know where they will be buried because they do not consider the ground beneath them. The surface is a place without roots. The thought makes them uneasy.

## The Faith

You ask if they worship the root-mat.

The question confuses them. Worship implies distance, hierarchy, supplication. They do not pray to the root-mat. They do not ask it for favors. They do not fear its judgment. It is not a god. It is a context.

"We are the root-mat's creature," one elder says, trying to explain. "Not its servant. Not its child. Just... the thing that lives in it. Like the beetle-things. Like the ceiling-grazers. We are more complicated than the beetle-things, but not different in kind. The root-mat does not care about us. But it holds us. It feeds us. It takes us when we die. That is enough."

There are no rituals here. No shrines. No sacred spaces. The entire chamber is sacred, or none of it is, and the distinction does not occur to them. They do not set aside time for reverence because reverence is not a separate activity. Grafting a wall is reverence. Feeding the beetle-things is reverence. Walking slowly across the root-mat, feeling the ground adjust beneath your feet, is reverence.

When you press, they admit that there are stories. Old stories, passed down through generations, about the root-mat's origin. But the stories are vague, mythic, more atmosphere than narrative. The root-mat came from somewhere. Someone planted it, or something happened, or the earth decided to grow inward. The details are not important. The root-mat is here. It is growing. It will continue to grow after everyone living is gone. That is the story that matters.

## The Shape of a Life

You piece together the pattern, watching them.

A child is born in a bark-walled dwelling, delivered by a mother and an aunt and a grandmother, onto a floor of living root that will remember the shape of their body. They grow slowly, learning to move at the pace of sap, to speak in the space between sounds. They are given small tasks, then larger ones. They find a place in the factories or the graft-yards or the root-training crews. They form attachments, loose and patient. They may take a partner, or not. They may have children, or not. There is no pressure. The population is stable. The root-mat does not demand growth.

They age. Their hands toughen. Their hair amber-stains. Their steps slow further, settling into the rhythm of the place. They become an elder, sitting in the lichen-light, watching the young move too fast, waiting for them to slow.

They die. Their body is taken to the edge, laid on the stone, absorbed by the advancing roots. They become floor. They become wall. They stay.

The entire cycle happens without urgency. There is no race to accomplish things, no window of opportunity closing, no fear of missing out. The root-mat will be here. The factories will hum. The sap will flow. A life is not a project to be completed; it is a span of time to be inhabited.

You find yourself envying them, and then catching yourself, unsure whether the envy is valid. Is this peace, or is it stagnation? Is this meaning, or is it mere persistence? You cannot tell. You are too fast to judge.

---

## The Departure

When you leave — climbing back up the shaft, the cellulose walls pressing close, the resin-iron taste fading from your mouth — you look back once.

The chamber is a pale haze of light and shadow. The figures moving across the root-mat are small, slow, patient. The hum of the factories follows you up the shaft, growing fainter. The smell lingers in your clothes, your hair, your skin.

You emerge onto the surface, and the light is wrong. Too bright. Too directional. The ground beneath your feet is hard stone and packed earth, and it does not adjust when you step. The air smells of nothing — or rather, of the absence of resin, of cold iron, of amber sap.

You walk away, but part of you stays in the root-mat. Part of you is still there, walking slowly across the living floor, feeling the warmth seep up through your soles.

You understand, suddenly, why they do not leave.

You also understand that you cannot stay.
