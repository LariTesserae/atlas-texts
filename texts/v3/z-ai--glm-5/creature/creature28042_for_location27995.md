# The Tenders

They were not always here. Or perhaps they were—the question is difficult because they do not remember arrival, and the basin does not record history in a way that preserves events. They exist in the present tense, mostly. The past is a smooth surface, like the ground before moisture rises.

---

There are three of them. Or four. The number varies because they are not quite separate, not quite unified. They move through the basin at distances that would take days to cross on foot, and yet they are aware of each other—aware in the way that parts of a single body are aware, not through communication but through a shared sensation of orientation. When one turns toward the moisture channel that is beginning to darken, the others feel the turning, adjust their own positions accordingly. Not coordination. Convergence.

They have no names. They have locations, sometimes, when location matters—near the eastern drainage, where the moisture rises first; in the deep center, where the heat accumulates most intensely; at the basin's edge, where the constructed ground meets whatever lies beyond. But these locations are not identities. They are positions in a process, and the tenders move between them as the process requires.

Their forms are difficult to describe because they have adapted to a place that did not exist before they adapted to it. They are bipedal in the sense that they move on two legs, but the legs are not entirely leg-like—too long, too patient, capable of standing motionless for durations that would cramp human muscle into uselessness. They do not pace. They do not fidget. They stand and they walk and they kneel, and each posture is held for as long as the posture is needed. Their skin has the quality of the ground itself: warm, slightly yielding, absorbing heat without suffering from it. They do not sweat because the heat is not a problem to be solved. It is a condition to be inhabited.

They do not eat, or not in any way that resembles eating. They kneel at the moisture channels when the dampness rises, and they drink—not through mouths, exactly, but through a kind of absorption that happens wherever their skin meets water. The moisture enters them and becomes part of them, and they carry it across the basin to places where the moisture does not rise, and they release it through their palms onto the roots of plants that have been placed too far from the channels. This is their primary function: redistribution. The drainage system is good but not perfect. Some plants need more than the channels provide. The tenders complete the pattern, filling gaps in a design that was made vast before it was made complete.

They do not speak because there is no one to speak to, and because speech would be inefficient for the information they share. When one notices that a plant near coordinate 447-82 is struggling—its leaves curling slightly, its orientation shifting away from the light—it does not tell the others. It simply knows, and the others know, and one of them will adjust its trajectory to pass near that plant on its next circuit, will kneel and touch the soil and release moisture and perhaps make some small adjustment to the surrounding ground, and the plant will stabilize. This is not communication. This is a shared attention that moves between them like weather moves across the basin.

They have no memory of learning these tasks. They have no memory of a time before the tasks. They know the basin because the basin is what they are in, and they know the plants because the plants are what they tend, and they know the moisture cycles because the cycles are when they drink and when they walk and when they rest. But the knowledge is not memory. It is pattern, held in the body rather than the mind. They do not think *now the moisture will rise*; they simply begin to move toward the channels when the moisture is about to rise. The knowing happens before the thought, or instead of the thought, or in a place where thought is not needed.

They do not sleep. They rest, sometimes, standing motionless in the heat while the light moves across the sky, but this is not sleep in the sense of unconsciousness. It is a different kind of presence—a narrowing of attention to a single point, a deep watching that excludes everything else. They watch the plants grow. They can feel the growth if they stand close enough, feel the slow expansion of cells, the gradual turning toward light, the infinitesimal spread of roots through the made ground. They watch the ground settle. They watch the channels deepen by increments too small to measure but conceptually real. They watch the basin become more itself, and in watching, they participate in the becoming.

They have opinions, though they would not call them that. They prefer certain plants—the ones near the eastern channel, which grow more vigorously and seem to orient toward them when they approach. They find certain areas of the basin more comfortable—the places where the heat is slightly less intense, where the ground has a texture that pleases whatever serves as their sense of touch. They avoid certain coordinates, though they do not know why—the ground there feels incomplete, as if something is meant to be added that hasn't arrived yet, and they experience this absence as a kind of warning. These preferences do not guide their actions—actions are guided by the needs of the basin—but they color the experience of action, make some circuits more satisfying than others.

They do not reproduce. They do not age. They do not die. They have always been three or four, and they will always be three or four, or the number will change for reasons that are not biological, and they will continue to tend the basin until the basin no longer needs tending. They have no concept of an end to the tending, no vision of a future where their function is complete. They are part of a process that has no stated conclusion, and this does not bother them because bother requires a comparison between what is and what should be, and they have no access to what should be. They only have what is, and what is is sufficient.

They noticed the visitor. They always notice visitors—the basin is so sparse, so still, that any anomaly registers immediately, a disruption in the pattern of heat and moisture and growth. They did not approach, did not interact. They watched from their separate positions, felt the visitor's footsteps disrupting the ground surface, felt the ground recording the passage and then slowly erasing it. They felt the visitor's confusion, their attempts to understand the place, their eventual departure. This happens sometimes. The basin is visited by things that are not part of its design—observers, witnesses, anomalies. The tenders do not engage with these presences because engagement is not their function. Their function is to tend. The visitors come and go, and the basin remains, and the tenders continue their circuits, their redistributions, their patient watching.

They do not wonder what they are. They do not wonder why they were made, or who made them, or whether they were made at all. They do not experience themselves as objects in need of explanation. They experience themselves as movements—movements toward moisture, movements toward plants, movements toward the next position in a pattern that extends beyond their perception. They are what the basin uses to complete itself. They are the small adjustments to a vast design. They are the hands of a process that has no other hands.

They do not have language, but if they did, they might say: *we are what lives here because something needed to live here. We are the living that the basin required in order to become what it will be. We are not the purpose of this place. We are one of its mechanisms.*

But they do not have language, and so they say nothing, and the silence of the basin continues, and the moisture rises in its channels, and the three or four or sometimes five tenders move across the vast made ground, kneeling to drink, walking to redistribute, standing to watch the slow growth of a landscape that is still deciding what it wants to be.

They are patient. They have no other choice. Patience is not a virtue for them; it is a condition of existence, as natural as heat, as regular as moisture. They will be patient until the basin no longer needs them, and then they will stop, or change, or become something else. The question of what happens after does not occur to them. There is no after. There is only the tending, the watching, the slow becoming.

In the deepest part of the basin, where the heat accumulates most intensely and the ground is warmest, one of them—not the first, not the second, but one of them, the one who happens to be there at this moment—kneels beside a plant that has grown slightly larger than its neighbors. It does not know why this plant has grown larger. It does not need to know. It touches the soil near the roots, feels the moisture that has gathered there, feels the plant's slow consumption of that moisture. It does nothing. The plant does not need adjustment. It is growing as it was designed to grow, and the tender's only task is to witness the growing, to include it in the pattern of attention that the tenders hold across the vast expanse of the basin.

The tender stands. Walks toward the next coordinate. The light shifts, the moisture cycle approaches, and somewhere across the continental expanse of the made ground, the others feel the movement and adjust their own trajectories accordingly. They are three, or four, or five. They are what lives here. They are tending.
