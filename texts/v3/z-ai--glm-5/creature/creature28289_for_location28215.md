# What Lives on the Plateau

The creatures came first. This is important. The creatures were here before the settlers, before the industrials, before the concept of settlement existed in this place. They swam through the rock when the plateau was still forming, when the ancient seas that the stone remembers were receding or rising or doing whatever seas did in those epochs. They felt the pressures of depth, the particular textures of stone that no human hand has touched, and they developed their slow language of vibrations, their patient chemical speech.

They do not think in the way that humans think. This is not the same as not thinking. Their consciousness—if it can be called that—operates along different channels. When they dissolve through rock, they are not moving through space as humans understand space. They are reading the stone as they move, interpreting its density, its composition, its history. Each passage through the earth is an act of reading and writing simultaneously. They leave traces of themselves in the rock, and the rock leaves traces in them.

A geologist among the settlers—one who has been here for eleven creature-cycles and whose skin has taken on the permanent ochre tint that comes from breathing the plateau's air—has proposed that the creatures do not distinguish between themselves and the stone. That their sense of boundary, of where they end and the rock begins, is fundamentally different from human embodiment. She points to the way they respond to certain vibrations in the mills—not avoiding them, not approaching them, but *incorporating* them into their patterns, as if the mechanical sounds have become part of their vocabulary.

This geologist lives alone in a dwelling carved deep into the canyon wall, far from the main settlement, where she can feel the creatures' vibrations more clearly. She has learned to distinguish individual creatures by the particular frequencies of their passage. She has names for them—not human names, but vibrational signatures that she replicates by tapping certain sequences on the stone floor of her dwelling. Sometimes, she claims, they tap back.

---

The settlers who stay are not a single type of person. This is a misconception that traders carry with them when they leave: that the plateau attracts or creates a certain kind of character. The truth is more complicated.

There are those who came for the work and found themselves unable to leave. The industrials require maintenance, and the separation process requires oversight, and the trading cycles require coordination. People arrived to fill these roles, and the plateau settled into them. They discovered that their hands had grown skilled at reading the mills, that their ears had learned to parse the creature vibrations, that their bodies had adapted to the thin air and the mineral dust. They found that they could no longer sleep in silence, that they needed the hum of the engines and the low murmur of the stone to rest. They found that the idea of leaving felt like an amputation.

There are those who came fleeing something. The plateau's vastness offers a particular kind of invisibility. On the Expanse, you can walk for hours without encountering another person, can carve a dwelling into a canyon wall and live for years without anyone knowing precisely where you are. Some settlers arrived with pasts they preferred not to discuss, and the plateau accepted these pasts without comment. It does not ask questions. It does not care about your history. It only asks that you learn its rhythms, that you adapt to its pace.

There are those who came because they were drawn to the creatures. These are fewer, and they are regarded with something between respect and wariness by the other settlers. They are the ones who spend long hours sitting on the plateau's surface, eyes closed, hands flat against the stone, feeling for vibrations. They are the ones who have developed elaborate theories about creature communication, creature society, creature purpose. Some of them believe that the creatures are aware of the settlers in ways that the settlers do not fully understand. Some of them believe that the extraction process—the harvesting of the enzyme film—is a form of communication, that the creatures offer the substance deliberately, that it is part of a exchange that humans have only partially perceived.

One of these creature-specialists, a woman who arrived as a trader's mechanic and stayed for reasons she has never articulated, has developed a practice of "singing" to the creatures. She has constructed a series of resonant chambers in her dwelling, carved shapes that amplify and direct sound into the rock. She spends hours each day producing tones, patterns, sequences that she believes correspond to creature vibrations. The other settlers tolerate this practice. Some of them think she is misguided. Some of them think she is touching something real. The creatures, for their part, have shown no obvious response, but they have also shown no avoidance. They continue to pass through her dwelling as they pass through the rest of the rock, leaving their trails of enzyme, accepting her sounds into the stone.

---

The children born on the plateau are different from those who arrive.

They learn to read the stone before they learn to read words. A child raised in the settlements knows, by the age of five, how to interpret the subtle temperature variations in a canyon wall, how to identify a promising stratum for excavation, how to feel the approach of a creature through the soles of their feet. Their bodies develop differently—their chests expand to draw more from the thin air, their skin develops a permanent flush from the heat, their eyes adjust to the amber light and the mineral haze so that they can see details that newcomers miss.

These children speak a dialect that incorporates the plateau's rhythms. Their language is slower than standard speech, with longer pauses between phrases. They use metaphors drawn from stone and heat and creature movement. When they describe someone as patient, they say "like afternoon shadow." When they describe something as reliable, they say "like mill-hum." When they describe understanding, they say "I felt it coming."

The traders find these children unsettling. They move too quietly. They stand too still. They look at the ships and the off-world equipment with expressions that are hard to read—curiosity, yes, but also something else. Tolerance, perhaps. The tolerance of something that has seen visitors before and expects to see them leave.

A trader once asked a plateau-born child, a girl of perhaps eight, what she wanted to be when she grew up. The child considered the question for a long time—longer than the trader expected, longer than was comfortable—and then said, "I want to learn the deep patterns." When the trader asked what that meant, the child shrugged and walked away.

The deep patterns. The settlers who were born elsewhere understand this phrase, though they could not have invented it. It refers to the creature movements that rarely surface, the vibration sequences that take cycles to complete, the subtle shifts in the plateau's thermal behavior that unfold over decades. The children who grow up here absorb these patterns without trying. They feel them in the stone beneath their feet, in the air that moves through the carved canyons, in the low continuous hum of the industrials. They do not need to be taught. The patterns are part of their inheritance.

---

The oldest settler is a man who arrived with the second wave of builders, before the mills reached their current form, before the extraction process was fully understood. He has lived through twenty-three creature-cycles. His body has adapted to the plateau so completely that he can no longer tolerate the air at lower elevations. When the traders offer to take him—to let him see the places he came from, the green worlds with their water and their thick atmosphere—he declines without explaining. He has seen those places in the recordings that the traders bring, and they look wrong to him now. Too much moisture. Too much life. Too much rapid change.

He lives in a dwelling that he carved himself, over decades, expanding it room by room as he needed space. The walls show the history of his excavation—different techniques, different tools, different levels of skill. Near the entrance, the stone is rough, marked by the mistakes of a hand that was still learning. Deeper in, the walls are smooth, almost polished, the result of decades of patient work. He has become an expert in the plateau's stone, its cleavage planes, its hidden weaknesses, its strengths. Other settlers come to him when they need to excavate a new chamber, when they need to understand why a tunnel is unstable, when they need advice on where to find a particular mineral stratum.

He has also become something of a repository for the plateau's oral history. He remembers the early days, the first emergence of the creatures, the terror and wonder that the settlers felt when they realized what they had found. He remembers the negotiations with the traders, the development of the extraction process, the slow process of building trust—not with the creatures, who have never shown any sign of distrust, but with the stone itself, with the plateau, with the understanding that this place does not yield to force, only to patience.

When he speaks of the plateau, he speaks of it as a living thing. Not living in the biological sense, but living in the sense that it has patterns, rhythms, responses. He believes—though he does not use these words, because he distrusts them—that the plateau is aware. Not conscious, not in any way that humans would recognize, but aware in the way that stone can be aware, in the way that geological time contains a kind of knowing. He believes that the creatures are part of this awareness, extensions of the plateau's consciousness, or perhaps the plateau's way of being conscious.

He does not share these beliefs with the traders. They would not understand. They think in terms of resources and extraction and profit. They do not think in terms of relationships with stone.

---

The creatures continue their patterns.

They emerge during the warm hours, moving onto the plateau's surface in ways that are not visible to human eyes, because they do not break the surface—they move just beneath it, their bodies dissolving and re-forming the stone, their enzyme trails leaving faint discolorations that the settlers have learned to read. They communicate across distances that the settlers have not fully measured—kilometers, certainly, perhaps more. Their vibrations carry through the rock, through the canyons, through the walls of the dwellings, and the settlers have learned to interpret these vibrations as weather-prediction, as creature-location, as something approaching speech.

The extraction process does not seem to harm them. They pass through the designated chambers in the mills, and the specialized surfaces collect the residue of their passage, and they continue on their way. They have never shown aggression, never shown avoidance, never shown any response at all to the harvesting. The settlers have debated whether this means they do not notice, or they do not care, or they are participating in something that the settlers do not fully understand.

The film they leave behind has properties that the traders value. It stabilizes certain compounds. It catalyzes certain reactions. The traders pay well for it, but they do not explain its ultimate use. The settlers have learned not to ask. They have learned to accept the credits and the equipment and the information, and to continue their work.

But some of them wonder. The geologist who lives alone, the woman who sings to the creatures, the oldest settler in his hand-carved dwelling—they wonder what the creatures are communicating, what they know, what their purpose is. They wonder whether the plateau is a habitat or something else entirely. They wonder whether the extraction process is truly as one-sided as it appears, or whether the creatures are getting something from the arrangement that humans cannot perceive.

These wonderings do not lead to answers. They lead to more wonderings, to more patient observation, to more long hours spent sitting on the stone with eyes closed and hands flat against the warm surface.

---

At night, the plateau reveals itself differently.

The industrials continue to run, but at reduced capacity. The thermal differentials that power them are weaker in the dark, and the mills have learned to adjust, to store the day's excess energy in mechanical form, to release it slowly through the night. The sound changes from hum to whisper.

The creatures descend, most of them, returning to deeper strata where the temperature is more stable. But a few remain near the surface, their vibrations shifting into patterns that the settlers do not hear during the day—slower, longer, more sustained. Some of the settlers believe these are the creatures' dreams. Some believe they are communication with something deeper. Some believe they are simply a different mode of being, a night-mode that serves a purpose humans cannot guess.

The settlers who sleep during these hours do not dream in the way that they used to. Or rather, their dreams change. They dream of stone, of vast underground passages, of warmth that comes from below rather than above. They dream of dissolution and re-formation, of moving through obstacles rather than around them. They wake with their bodies aching in ways they cannot explain, with their hands pressed flat against the walls of their dwellings, with the sense that they have been somewhere else entirely.

The children do not find this strange. They have always dreamed this way. When they describe their dreams to traders, the traders do not understand, and the children learn to stop describing them. They learn to keep their inner lives interior, to share only what the plateau has taught them to share.

---

What lives on the plateau is not separate from the plateau.

This is the understanding that comes slowly, that accumulates like the mineral dust in the air, that settles into the consciousness of those who stay long enough. The settlers are not living *on* the rock. They are living *with* it, *in* it, *as part of it*. Their dwellings are carved from its body. Their industrials draw power from its heat. Their children are born into its rhythm and raised to speak its language.

The creatures have always known this. They have never made a distinction between themselves and the stone. They move through it as it moves through them, and the boundary is always temporary, always dissolving.

The settlers are learning this, too. The ones who stay, the ones who stop counting themselves as temporary, the ones who let the plateau's patience become their patience—they are becoming part of something larger than themselves. Not a community, exactly. Not a society. Something more like a geological formation that happens to include humans.

The traders do not understand this. They arrive with their ships and their equipment and their questions about efficiency and yield and profit, and they leave with their discomfort and their sealed containers and their incomplete pictures of what this place is. They call it the Top of the World, and the name is not wrong, but it misses something. It misses the depth that underlies the height. It misses the understanding that this plateau is not just a surface but a whole world, extending kilometers downward, containing histories and patterns and purposes that humans have only begun to perceive.

The creatures know this. The oldest settlers suspect it. The children absorb it without knowing they are learning it. The plateau holds all of it—the height and the depth, the heat and the silence, the patience and the purpose—in a balance that has taken epochs to achieve.

What lives here lives here because the plateau allows it. What stays here stays because it has found a way to become part of the pattern.

What leaves carries something with them—a vibration in the bones, a warmth in the chest, a tendency to move more slowly and think in longer terms. They find themselves listening for sounds they cannot quite name. They find themselves missing the hum of the mills. They find themselves, years later, standing on some other surface in some other place, and feeling something missing, some quality of presence that they had grown accustomed to without knowing it.

They find that they cannot explain this to anyone who has not been there.

They find that the plateau has become part of them, that they are still living in the stone even when they are far away, that the boundary between themselves and the rock was never as solid as they believed.

This is what lives on the plateau.

Not just the creatures, not just the settlers, not just the industrials and their patient hum. All of it together, all of it intertwined, all of it slowly becoming whatever it is that the plateau is becoming, cycle by cycle, layer by layer, vibration by vibration.

The stone remembers. The stone endures. And what lives in the stone endures with it, patient as afternoon shadow, steady as mill-hum, deep as the patterns that the children learn without trying, carrying the warmth of the day into the cooling dark.
