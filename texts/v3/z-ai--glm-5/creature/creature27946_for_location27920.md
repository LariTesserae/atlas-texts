# What Lives Here

---

The frogs.

Start with the frogs because they are what you notice last, which means they are what the chamber has been most successful at keeping. There are not many of them. Seven, perhaps eight — it is hard to count because they do not move enough to be tracked as individuals, and because their coloring, adapted over generations to the pale-and-dark mottle of flowstone and shadow, makes them difficult to separate from the rock they sit on. They are not large. The biggest would fit in a closed fist, if you were the kind of person who closes a fist around a frog, which you should not be, especially here.

Their skin is the defining thing. It is wet — everything here is wet — but it is wet in a particular way, a way that suggests the moisture is not a condition imposed on them but a property they maintain. The skin is thick, glossy, with a sheen that in the dim light reads as almost metallic. Not green. Not the green of the frogs in the outside world, the ones that sit on lily pads and sing in ditches. These frogs are the color of the stone they live on: cream and grey-brown and umber, with mottling that follows the mineral staining of the flowstone so precisely that you begin to wonder whether the coloring is a response to the environment or whether the environment has colored them so long that the distinction no longer matters. Their undersides are lighter — almost white, almost translucent — and when one of them shifts its weight on its ledge and you catch a flash of belly, it looks like a gap in the rock, a momentary opening into something brighter underneath.

They are warm. You can feel this if you are close enough, though you probably will not get that close because they watch. Their eyes are the one part of them that breaks the camouflage. They are large, proportionally, and they are dark, but not uniformly dark — there is a depth to them, a layered quality, like looking into the pools they live near. The eyes do not shine the way frog eyes do in external light. There is no reflective tapetum because there has been no need for one. These eyes have adapted to a world where the brightest light is a filtered gray-brown leak through a crack in stone, and they have become deep and still and patient in response. A frog watching you from its ledge is not afraid of you. It is not curious about you. It is registering you the way the chamber registers you — as a thermal event, a disturbance in the moisture gradient, a slight increase in the ambient noise. You are data. You are not a predator, because predators are not a category that exists in this frog's world.

They eat the insects. This is the relationship that defines the chamber's animal layer — the frogs sit in their stations and the insects move along their trails and the frogs take the insects that pass within range. The taking is not dramatic. No tongue-flip, no strike, no sudden motion. The frog simply opens its mouth and closes it around the insect and continues sitting, and the only evidence that anything happened is the small bulge that works down the throat and disappears into the body. It is the most casual predation you have ever witnessed. The frog does not seem to be hunting. It seems to be eating the way the moss drinks the condensation — passively, as a function of being in the right place with the right apparatus.

There are also tadpoles. This means the frogs breed here, which means there must be eggs, which means there must be a reproductive cycle, which means there must be a social element to their lives that you cannot see because it happens in the deeper recesses of the chamber or in the small hours of the geological night (which is the same as the geological day, down here, but there must be some rhythm they follow, some internal clock that says *now*). The tadpoles are in the deepest pool, the one two meters across and knee-deep at its center. They are dark and large-headed and fat, grazing the algae from the submerged rock with a steady, methodical movement that is almost soothing to watch. They are not afraid of you either. When you put your hand near the water, they do not scatter. They continue grazing, and one of them bumps against your fingertip and continues past it, and your fingertip is warm from the water and you realize this is the warmest you have been since you entered the chamber.

The frogs do not vocalize. Not that you can hear, not while you are here. There is no call, no croak, no territorial declaration. Whether they make sounds at frequencies below your hearing, or in rhythms of time longer than your visit, or in ways that do not involve sound at all — chemical signals, skin-to-skin communication in the dark recesses where they gather — you cannot know. You only know that the chamber, for all its life, makes almost no sound that an animal produces. The frogs are silent citizens. They exist in the visual register, the thermal register, and almost not at all in the auditory one.

---

The insects are the working class of this ecosystem, and they are the thing you notice first if you look at the moss carefully enough.

They are not a single species. That is the assumption you make initially — dark, small, wingless things moving through the moss — but when you crouch and let your eyes adjust, the diversity becomes apparent. There are at least three distinct forms. The largest is about the length of your thumbnail, flattened, dark brown-black, with a glossy carapace that catches the dim light in the same brief flashes the liverworts produce when the air moves. These move with purpose, following trails through the moss that are invisible to you but clearly established — they do not hesitate, do not wander, but move along fixed routes between the pools and the fungal clusters and the deeper organic accumulations. They are the decomposers, you think. The ones doing the primary work of breaking down the dead plant matter. Their legs are short and thick and they push through the moss the way a plow pushes through soil, and behind them the moss is slightly disturbed, slightly aerated, a small tunnel of worked material.

The second form is smaller — half the size of the first, paler, with a more elongated body and longer legs. These move differently: faster, less directed, climbing over the moss surfaces and along the stalagmites and even across the surface of the shallow pools, walking on the microbial film that coats the water. They are the foragers, you think, the ones that range widely and take whatever is available. They do not follow trails. They follow opportunity.

The third form is the smallest and the most difficult to see. It is nearly transparent — not translucent like the crustaceans in the pools, but genuinely transparent, visible mainly as a distortion in the air above the moss, the way heat shimmer is visible. These live in the air spaces between the moss cushions, in the humid layer just above the stone, and they are so small and so numerous that the moss surface, when you watch it carefully, appears to have a faint quality of motion that is not the moss itself but these creatures moving within it. You cannot count them. They are the substrate of the animal life here, the bottom of the visible food chain, and they are everywhere.

None of them have wings. This is the detail that tells you the most about how long this ecosystem has been sealed. Wings are expensive — they require energy, they require complexity, they require a reason. In a chamber where the food is below you and the food is always below you and the distance between where you are and where the food is never exceeds thirty meters, wings are not a luxury. They are a liability. And so, over the time this chamber has been what it is, the insects here have lost them. Not all at once. Generation by generation, the winged individuals dispersed (through the fissure, maybe, or died trying), and the wingless ones stayed, and stayed, and stayed, until staying was all any of them knew how to do.

They do not react to you. You are not food. You are not a threat. You are a large warm object that displaces air and blocks light, and when you step near a moss cushion, the insects in that cushion pause for the duration of your disturbance and then resume. They do not flee. They do not swarm. They do not investigate. They simply continue, around your boot, over your boot, past your boot, as if your boot is a new stalagmite that has appeared in their landscape, and they will route around it as they route around everything else.

---

The crustaceans in the pools are the creatures that make you stay and watch longest, because they are the ones whose lives are most invisible to you.

They are small — a centimeter, maybe two — and they are pale, and they are translucent, and they live on the bottom of the pools, not the surface. You see them only when you are close enough to the water to look down into it, and even then you see them only when they move, because when they are still they are the color of the mineral floor they sit on, and when they move, the light catches the faint opacity of their internal organs — a shadowy mass in the center of the body that is the digestive tract, and a smaller dense point that is something else, something neural, something that is running the show.

There are four or five in the large pool. There are one or two in the mid-sized pools. There are none in the smallest pools, the ones the size of a cupped palm, because there is not enough water to support them, or because they cannot get there, or because they have no reason to be there. They move by walking, not swimming — their legs are visible, small and jointed and deliberate, and they pick their way across the crystalline mineral formations on the pool floors with a care that suggests the crystals are part of their landscape, features they know and navigate around. They are eating the microbial film, you think — the iridescent surface layer that breaks into tiny rainbows when the air moves. They graze it the way the tadpoles graze the algae, steadily, methodically, in small bites that you cannot see but can infer from the pattern of cleared patches on the pool floor behind them.

They have been here a long time. This is evident from their adaptation — the pallor, the translucence, the absence of any pigment that would matter in a world without light to absorb. But it is also evident from their behavior. They are not anxious. They do not flee from your shadow. They do not flee from the tadpoles, which are larger than them and which share their pools. There is an arrangement between them — the crustaceans eat the microbial film, the tadpoles eat the algae, and neither eats the other, and this arrangement has held for long enough that neither party questions it.

When a root drip falls into a pool, the crustaceans react. They stop. They sink lower against the mineral floor. They wait for the ripple to pass. Then they resume. This is the extent of their response to external stimuli. A drip is the most violent thing that happens in their world, and they have a protocol for it, and the protocol is to wait.

---

There are other things, too. Things you see once and do not see again, or things you almost see and cannot confirm.

Something moves in the roots near the ceiling. A small, quick motion — not an insect, larger than an insect, perhaps the size of a mouse but moving differently, with a fluidity that suggests it is at home in the three-dimensional tangle of the root systems. You see it once, a pale shape shifting among the hanging roots where they enter the chamber from above, and then it is gone, and you do not see it again. It could be a bat — bats use caves, bats adapt to darkness — but it did not move like a bat. It moved like something that climbs. It might be a rodent of some kind, something that has found the chamber through the root systems and uses it as a warm refuge, coming and going through the fissures in the ceiling that you cannot see from below. It might be many things. You saw it once. The chamber does not owe you a second sighting.

Something lives in the organic accumulation on the floor, in the layer of decomposing moss and fungal matter beneath the living surface. You know this because when you step carefully in one spot, the ground gives slightly more than expected, and a small cloud of something — spores, perhaps, or microorganisms, or some other biological particulate — rises from the compressed layer and hangs in the air for a moment before settling. The something that lives there is below the threshold of your vision, but the chamber is full of life at that scale. The fungal mycelium that threads through the organic layer is itself alive, itself a being, itself a resident. The bacterial communities in the warm pools are residents. The microbial film on the pool surfaces is a resident. The chamber is not a place with a few animals in it. It is a place that is full, end to end, of living things occupying every niche that warmth and moisture and the faintest light can support.

---

And the one who left the marks.

You do not know if they still live here. You do not know if they were human. You do not know if "they" is the right pronoun, because you do not know if there was more than one. The marks on the wall near the ceiling fissure suggest a single individual, or at least a single set of hands, coming to the same spot repeatedly over a long enough period to wear grooves into limestone. The bone on the shelf suggests a death, or at least a leaving-behind.

But you wonder about them. You wonder whether they lived in the chamber or visited it. You wonder whether they sat in the warmth the way you are sitting in the warmth and felt the moisture on their skin and watched the frogs on the ledges and listened to the dripping. You wonder whether they touched the wall because it was a ritual or because it was a habit or because the texture of the stone was satisfying to the fingers in some way that the texture of other stone was not. You wonder whether they knew the frogs were there. You wonder whether the frogs knew they were there.

The marks are old. The bone is old. The mineral crust on the bone suggests it has been sitting on that shelf for at least centuries, possibly much longer. Whoever left them is not coming back. But the chamber has kept what they left — has held it the way it holds the warmth, the way it holds the water, the way it holds the light — and the keeping is a kind of relationship, too. The chamber remembers its inhabitants. Not in a cognitive sense, not with intention, but in the way that geology remembers: by incorporating what was into what is. The bone is becoming stone. The marks are becoming features of the rock. The one who made them is becoming part of the chamber's identity the way the minerals in the water are becoming part of the crystalline formations on the pool floors.

This is who lives here: the moss and the insects and the crustaceans and the tadpoles and the frogs and the fungi and the roots and the microbes and the something in the ceiling and the something in the floor and the something that was, once, and is now part of the stone, and will be, and will be, and will be until the hillside shifts and the fissure closes and the warmth finds another way up and the chamber becomes something else, which it will, eventually, in the way that all things that are complete and balanced and settled become something else.

But not yet. Not now. Now it is what it is. Full. Warm. Quiet.

Waiting for nothing.
