# What Lives Here

---

## The Clickers (provisional name — you will outgrow it)

They are not one species. You understand this by the fourth or fifth basin, though it takes longer to articulate it even to yourself. "Clickers" is what you called the first sound you heard, and the name stuck to the makers of that sound, and then it stuck to everything small that moved in the mineral deposits, and now you are carrying a word that has become too large for what it contains.

The ones that clicked first — the ones that found you in that first basin, that crossed your knuckles and investigated your thumb — are the surface foragers. Their bodies are low and flat, wider than they are tall, shaped the way a stone is shaped when water has been running over it for a very long time. That is not a coincidence. Their dorsal surfaces are smooth and mineralized, wearing the same dark patina as the basin floor, and when they are still — which they often are, for long stretches, between bursts of rapid movement — you cannot distinguish them from the substrate by touch alone. You learn to find them by temperature. They run warmer than the deposit around them, a degree, maybe two, as though they carry a small internal reservoir of the engine's heat and release it slowly through their shells.

The clicking is their navigation. You are confident of this. The sound is produced by — you think — the rapid striking of a hardened mouthpart against the substrate, or possibly the striking of two body segments against each other. The clicks are short, precise, and they return. Each clicker produces a click, then waits, then produces another. The intervals vary. When you first arrived in the basin, the intervals were long — cautious, or merely observational. As you sat still and the clickers grew accustomed to your presence (or catalogued you and dismissed you), the intervals shortened, and the clicking became continuous, overlapping, a dense acoustic mesh that you eventually realized was not random. The clickers were listening to each other. Each click produces a return — not an echo, but a response, slightly delayed, slightly altered in pitch. They are building a map of the basin through sound the way you built a map of the plateau through your feet. The map tells them where the deposit is richest, where the water is deepest, where the edges are, where the large things are not.

There are other kinds.

In the deeper parts of the larger basins, where the standing water covers the substrate, the clickers are different. They are not flat. They are tall — relatively, a few centimeters at most — and narrow, and they move through the warm water with a motion you can only describe as sculling, a rapid oscillation of paired appendages along their flanks that produces not a click but a low buzz, barely felt through the water. You catch one between your fingers, very gently. It is rigid, almost crystalline. Its surface is faceted, not smooth, and the facets catch against your skin with a faint abrasive quality, like holding a raw crystal. It buzzes against your thumb and forefinger — a physical vibration, not an audible one — and you set it down and it sculls away into the dark water without lingering.

These are not the same animals that clicked across your knuckles in the dry basin. The body plan is different. The locomotion is different. The sound is different. You begin to suspect that what you are calling "clickers" is a grade, not a group — a level of organization, a way of being small and numerous and warm in the engine's output, and that dozens — hundreds — of lineages have arrived at this level independently, the way Earth's arthropods and annelids and mollusks all arrived at small-and-segmented without sharing a recent ancestor. The clicking ones and the buzzing ones are as distant from each other as a beetle is from a snail. They share a place, not a history.

And there are others.

In the driest basins, the ones where the substrate is powder-fine and barely warm, you find clickers that are tiny — barely larger than the grains they live among — and almost immobile. They do not click. They do not buzz. They sit in the deposit and process it, passing substrate through their bodies and depositing it behind them, moving in slow straight lines that you can trace with your fingertip: a shallow furrow in the fine grain, beginning somewhere, ending somewhere, the organism at the head of the furrow so small you lose it against your skin. These are the ones that have given up almost everything — movement, sound, individual identity perhaps — in exchange for the simple business of feeding. They are the earthworms of this place, if the earthworms had never seen earth and had no interest in it. They are everywhere. Every basin has them. They are the base of something, though you cannot map the something from above.

You stop using the word "clickers" internally. You start calling them the surface fauna, which is uglier but more honest. Then you stop calling them anything, because the naming has become an obstacle to the seeing, and the seeing is difficult enough in the dark.

## The Thread-Growth

It is not a plant. You are almost certain.

The first time you find it — a pale mat of fibers spread across the floor of a damp basin, thin as spider silk, soft to the touch, warm — you think it might be the engine's product, a mineral precipitate, something crystalline and fibrous that forms when the right chemical conditions meet the right temperature gradient. It looks like asbestos. It feels like hair. It is neither.

It is alive because it grows. You confirm this over what you believe is the span of two days in the same basin, returning to the same spot by navigational reckoning (the damp streaks on the stone, the shape of the basin's rim under your boots). The mat has expanded. New fibers bridge gaps that were open before. The edge has advanced by a handspan, maybe two, into an area of bare substrate. The new growth is paler than the old, almost translucent, and softer, and when you touch it, it contracts. Slowly. Not a flinch — a settling, the way a sponge contracts when you press it, but over minutes, not seconds. It does not want to be touched, or it does not care, or it responds to pressure as a matter of physics rather than behavior. You cannot tell.

It is chemosynthetic. It must be. There is no light here, and it has no structure that suggests light-seeking — no broad leaves, no orientation, no tendency to grow toward anything. It grows along the chemical gradients in the substrate, following the richest deposits, spreading in the direction where the mineral food is thickest. When you sample the substrate beneath the mat and compare it to the substrate beyond the mat's edge, the difference is clear: beneath the mat, the deposit is depleted, its fine grain reduced to something even finer, almost colloidal. The thread-growth is eating the basin floor.

It is the closest thing this place has to vegetation, and it is not vegetation. It is not a plant and it is not a fungus and it is not a lichen and it is not a biofilm, though it shares properties with all of these. It is what happens when the engine's chemical output meets the engine's moisture output and the two combine in the presence of a surface and enough time — enough time being a span you cannot imagine, because the thread-growth may have been in this basin for a thousand years, or ten thousand, or a hundred thousand, growing and dying and growing again in the same spot, the same chemical niche, the same warm dark water.

The surface fauna live in it. When you part the fibers with your fingers, small bodies scatter — not away from you, but away from the disturbance, and then back, and then the mat is full of them again. The thread-growth is their habitat, their cover, their grazing ground. Some of them are eating it. Some of them are eating the substrate it grows on. Some of them are eating each other. The mat is a world. It is a forest of one species, if species applies, and the fauna are its understory, its canopy, its everything.

You find the thread-growth in most of the larger basins. It is never on the bare stone between basins. It requires the substrate, the warmth, the water — all three, together, in the same place. It is not adventurous. It does not colonize. It stays where the conditions are right and dies where they are not, and the engine's rebalancing kills it and creates it in new places on a schedule that has nothing to do with its preferences.

## The Large Things

You cannot describe them. You have never seen them. You have only felt them — through the stone, through the water, through the vibrations they produce as they move through the engine's deep channels — and the feeling is enough to tell you that they are there and nothing else.

The first one passed beneath you on the third day, and you have encountered — or nearly encountered — four more since. Each time, the experience is similar: the engine's vibration shifts register, the stone changes pitch under your boots, a crack opens or a section of floor sinks slightly, and then the sliding sound, the heavy mineralized body pressing against mineralized channel walls, and the warm organic sulfurous breath pushing upward. They are below. They are always below. They do not come to the surface.

You have one piece of direct evidence. In the large basin — the amphitheater, the one with standing water — you knelt at the edge and put your hand in, and something large moved against your fingers and then away. The contact lasted less than a second. What you felt: a surface that was hard but not stone-hard, more like the chitin of the surface fauna but thicker, denser, with a granularity to it, a texture of overlapping plates or segments. It was warm — warmer than the water, warmer than the air, as warm as the stone. It was moving with purpose, not drifting. It was large enough that your hand contacted only a small portion of it, and the portion you contacted was curved, a flank or a side, and then it was gone, and the water closed over the space where it had been with a sound like a slow swallow.

The large things live in the channels. They are part of the engine's deep biology, the part you cannot reach. They are not rare — you have encountered enough of them, in enough different locations, to understand that the channel system is populated, that the warm veins and thermal conduits are not just geological features but habitats, corridors, the arteries of a world that extends downward beyond your ability to imagine. The large things move through these corridors the way the surface fauna move through the basins: following the gradient, seeking the richest output, feeding on something — you do not know what. The mineral deposits? The chemical soup in the fluid-permeable fault lines? Each other?

They are not the same thing, any more than the surface fauna are all the same thing. The one that passed beneath you on the third day produced a sliding sound that was continuous, a single body pressing through a single channel. The one you felt in the amphitheater water was different — that one had come up, or near up, surfacing through a fissure into the standing water, and its movement through the water was not a slide but a pulse, a rhythmic contraction that you felt as a pressure wave against your hand. Different locomotion. Different body. Different species, or different phylum, or different kingdom.

There are others you know only by their sounds. One produces, deep in the stone, a series of low percussive impacts — rhythmic, deliberate, spaced at intervals of several seconds. It might be striking the channel walls. It might be the sound of its body moving in a way that involves repeated contact with stone. It might be something else entirely. You cannot map the sound to a source. You hear it from below and from far away, and it does not come closer, and it does not go away.

Another produces a sustained vibration that is almost indistinguishable from the engine's own output, except that it has a periodicity the engine does not. The engine hums at a constant frequency — established, settled, balanced. This other vibration rises and falls. It rises and falls on a cycle of approximately ninety seconds, and you have sat on the bare stone and counted the cycles and felt them pass through you like a slow tide, and you understand that this is a living thing breathing, or moving, or simply existing at a scale where metabolism becomes a geological event.

## The Middle Strata

Between the surface fauna in their basins and the large things in their deep channels, there must be a middle. You know this the way you know an ecosystem must have intermediate levels — not because you have seen it, but because the logic of the system requires it. The surface fauna feed on the substrate and the thread-growth and each other. The large things in the channels are too large to feed on the surface fauna individually; the calorie math does not work. Something must connect them. Something must live in the middle depths, in the stone between the surface and the channels, in the porous layers where the engine's heat is rising but has not yet reached the air.

You find evidence of it in the walls of the deepest basins, where the stone is exposed in cross-section by the engine's slow erosion. The walls are not solid. They are layered, and some of the layers are not stone — they are organic, or were organic, or carry the fossil texture of organic processes. Tubes. Bored holes. The negative space of bodies that once occupied this rock and moved through it and left their passages behind. The passages are old — filled in with mineral deposits, partially collapsed, integrated into the stone's structure so thoroughly that they have become geological features. But they were made by things. Living things that bored through warm porous stone and lived in the boreholes and fed on something the stone provided.

Are they still there? The passages are old, but the engine is old, and the engine is still running, and the system does not discard what works. You press your ear to the basin wall and listen. You hear the engine's hum. You hear the clicking of the surface fauna behind you. And — possibly — you hear something else. A very faint, very slow sound, like the sound of something shifting its weight in a narrow space. A settling-in sound. A sound that might be an organism in the wall, in the stone, in the middle depth, going about its business at a pace so slow that your presence at its boundary is a brief and irrelevant event.

You think about the deep-sea vents on Earth, the tube worms and the blind shrimp and the entire ecosystems that organize themselves around chemical energy in the dark. You think about the organisms that live in the basalt crust beneath the ocean floor, in the pores and fractures of the rock itself, existing at scales of space and time that make surface life look frantic and ephemeral. The plateau is a version of this, you think. A continental-scale version. The engine is the vent. The stone is the ocean floor. And what lives here is what always lives in such places — the things that have found a source of energy and organized themselves around it and have no need of anything else.

## What Does Not Live Here

Nothing that requires light. Nothing that requires a day. Nothing that moves by sight or orients by stars or reads the position of a sun. Nothing that sleeps on a schedule dictated by an external rhythm, because there is no external rhythm. The engine's hum is constant. The warmth is constant. The dark is constant. If the things that live here sleep, they sleep locally, individually, on cycles that are their own and not the world's. The world does not cycle. The world runs.

Nothing that migrated here from somewhere else. There is no somewhere else. The plateau is bounded — not by visible barriers, but by the limits of the engine's thermal field. Beyond the plateau, the stone goes cold, and the warmth goes out of it, and the channels narrow and the deposits stop and the basins go dry. The life that lives here has always lived here. It originated here, or it originated when the engine originated, or the distinction between the engine's geology and the life's biology is a distinction that was never really drawn. The stone processed mineral. The processing created chemical gradients. The gradients created opportunity. The opportunity was taken. The takers became the fauna. The fauna became part of the processing. The engine and the life are not separate systems. They are one system, viewed from different depths.

Nothing that knows you are here. You have walked across this plateau for — weeks? months? — and nothing has responded to you as a foreign element. The clickers investigate you and move on. The thread-growth contracts when you touch it and expands when you stop. The large things pass beneath you and do not surface. You are a disturbance so small and so brief that the system does not register you. You are a warm body walking across a warm stone in the dark, and the difference between you and the stone is, to the things that live here, negligible. You are not prey. You are not predator. You are not a vector or a seed or a threat. You are a texture. You are a brief roughness on a surface that has been smooth for a billion years.

This is not loneliness. Loneliness requires the possibility of company. This is something else. This is the experience of being in a place that is fully inhabited — teeming, organized, balanced, complete — and finding that your presence is irrelevant to its completeness. The plateau does not need you. The engine does not need you. The clickers do not need you. You are here, and you are watching, and you are the only thing watching, and nothing cares that you are watching, and the dark goes in all directions, and the ground hums, and the water in the basins laps gently at the stone, and the clicking goes on and on and on, and you walk, and you walk, and you walk.
