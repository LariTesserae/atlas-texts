# What Lives There

## The Workers

They are not a people in the way outsiders might expect—no shared ancestry, no ancestral dress, no folk songs carried down from a founding generation. They are whoever came and stayed. Over the long decades the Condenseries has operated, the workforce has accumulated like mineral crust: layer upon layer of arrivals, each one depositing something into the collective and losing something to the wet heat. The result is a community that shares a culture but not a bloodline, a population defined entirely by adaptation to a place that does not welcome human life.

Their bodies tell the story of the plateau's pressure. The permanent squint comes first—narrowed eyes in faces tanned dark by the reflected light, the skin around the orbits creased into deep folds that make even young workers look weathered. Hands thicken. The fingertips develop a roughness that is not quite callus—the mineral water toughens skin through a chemical process closer to tanning. After a few seasons, the workers' hands take on a slightly blue-grey tint from prolonged contact with the thermal flow, as if the plateau is slowly claiming them.

They move slowly. Not from lethargy—there is nothing lethargic about a worker crossing the drying-rack platform with a load of fresh crystal—but from a kind of bodily wisdom about the cost of motion in saturated heat. Every movement is intentional. They do not gesture unnecessarily. They do not fidget. When they stand still, they are profoundly still, conserving moisture and energy in an environment that takes both without return. New workers learn this within weeks; the ones who do not learn leave or collapse.

Their throats change. The damp air and the mineral particulates suspended in it alter the mucosal tissue over time. Older workers speak in voices that carry a faint rasp even in the meal halls—a texture like stone dragged across stone, softened by age but never smooth. They do not mind. They have learned to value communication that does not require voice.

## The Children

There are children. This surprises visitors, who imagine the Condenseries as a place of labor only, a worksite without domestic life. But the workers came, and some stayed, and some brought families or made them. Children grow up on the plateau knowing nothing else—no green world, no rain that falls, no soil that yields. To them, the Condenseries is not strange. The lowlands are strange. When caravans arrive from below, the children crowd around the pack animals and touch the wooden crates that carry food they have never seen growing, fruit with skins that hold moisture against an interior that is not mineral, not thermal, just sweet.

The children learn the gestural language before they learn speech. Their hands become fluent in the plateau's silence before their voices find the meal-hall acoustics. They play in the steam columns, chasing condenser lizards with quick movements that the adults would never waste energy on, their metabolisms young enough to afford the expenditure. They dare each other to touch the thermal pools—never deep, never long, just a fingertip to the surface and then the shocked withdrawal and the laughter that is the sharpest sound the plateau ever holds.

They attend lessons in the work halls between shifts. There is no formal school, but the older workers teach what they know: the procedures for maintaining the ancient engines, the timing of crystal harvest, the reading of the plateau's moods through the behavior of animals and the vibration of stone. The children learn that the vapor-glider is not a bird, that the spring-strider is not an insect, that the condenser lizard is not a lizard in any way that matters. They learn that the things that live here live here because they became what the plateau required, and that this is not a tragedy but a fact.

## The Old Workers

The longest residents are difficult to distinguish from the infrastructure. They have lived on the plateau for so long that their bodies have calcified into routine—the same walking paths, the same hand positions for the same tasks, the same postures during rest that minimize heat exposure and moisture loss. Their skin has taken on the blue-grey of prolonged mineral contact, darker on the hands and forearms where the thermal water touches daily, fading to ordinary brown on the chest and back.

They hold institutional knowledge that exists nowhere else. They know which valves stick in cold weather and which pipes leak when the thermal flow increases. They know the sound a pump makes before it fails—not an auditory sound but a vibration, a change in the frequency that travels through stone and metal and into the feet of anyone standing close enough. They know the procedures for the machines they do not understand, the sequences of operations that keep the extraction running, passed down through demonstration and repetition because the original documentation has long since dissolved in the wet air.

When an old worker dies, the community gathers in the largest meal hall. They speak the person's name—using their voices, the meal-hall acoustics carrying the sound in a way that the open plateau never permits. They share food brought from the lowlands, the rare and precious flavors of a world they do not inhabit. They do not memorialize with monuments or inscriptions. The infrastructure itself is the memorial: every pipe the worker patched, every valve they replaced with carved stone, every adjustment they made to the system that keeps the Condenseries running. Their work persists in the functioning of the settlement, and when a pipe sweats or a tank fills or a crystal forms on a suspended string, the old workers are still there, still participating in the labor that defined their lives.

## The Condenser Lizards

They are everywhere, and they have been here longer than the workers.

The lizards do not think of the Condenseries as a settlement. They think of the plateau as home and the infrastructure as geography—a pipe is a warm surface to press against, a cistern wall is a sheltered place to rest, a drying rack is an inconvenient interruption in an otherwise useful thermal channel. They have adapted to the workers' presence the way they have adapted to everything: by absorbing it into their behavior without acknowledging it as separate from the environment.

Their scales carry mineral growths that are not decorative but functional—crystalline deposits that form on the hide over years of contact with thermal water, building into rough plates that both camouflage the lizard against the stone and insulate it from temperature fluctuations. An old lizard, found beneath a water tank or in a crevices along the cistern walls, may carry enough mineral accumulation to weigh twice what it would without the deposits. They move slowly under this weight, but they move with purpose, and when they reach a thermal surface and flatten themselves to absorb heat and moisture, the stillness that settles over them is complete.

The workers know individual lizards by the pattern of their mineral growths. They name them—not with spoken names but with gestures, particular configurations of the hands that identify a specific animal by the shape of its crystal deposits. The lizards do not know they are named. But they learn to recognize specific workers by scent and movement, and they allow certain people to approach closer than others, a tolerance that has developed over generations of cohabitation.

When a lizard dies, the mineral deposits remain. The workers find the husks—empty scales crusted with crystal, the organic tissue dessicated by the heat and the chemistry—and they leave them where they lie. The crystal growths on a dead lizard sometimes develop into formations that the workers harvest alongside the tank-grown dendrites, a secondary yield that the markets below value for its unusual origin. No one speaks of this. It is simply another way the plateau converts life into wealth.

## The Spring-Striders

They are harder to know.

The striders live at the edges of the thermal pools, their long legs carrying them across water surfaces that would swallow anything heavier or less precise. They are pale—not white but translucent, the mineral-saturated light passing through their bodies and giving them an appearance of insubstantiality, as if they are not quite present. When they stand still, which is most of the time, they are difficult to see against the glowing water, visible only by the dimples their feet create in the surface tension.

They hunt the bacterial mats—the chemosynthetic growths that form the plateau's foundation of life, converting heat and sulfur into the energy that everything else feeds on. The striders' hollow beaks pierce the mat and draw out the fluid beneath, a nutrient-rich suspension that sustains them without the need for any other food source. They are, in this sense, parasites of the plateau itself—drinking the waste product of a chemical process that occurs in the deep stone, beneficiaries of a food chain that begins in heat and ends in their long, still bodies.

The workers watch the striders for signs of thermal change. A strider that leaves the water surface and moves to higher ground signals a pressure shift in the reservoir below. A strider that abandons a pool entirely signals something more serious—a potential rupture, a geyser formation, a structural failure in the mineral crust. The workers trust the striders more than they trust their own instruments, and the instruments are old and the striders are ancient in their adaptation, and this trust has been earned across generations of accurate prediction.

The striders do not nest in the Condenseries. They nest along the eastern thermal channels, where the mineral formations create overhangs that shelter their eggs from the bright light. The eggs are translucent, like the adults, and they absorb heat from the surrounding stone. The workers know where the nests are and avoid them during maintenance of the eastern channels. This avoidance is not written anywhere. It is simply understood.

## The Vapor-Gliders

They never land.

This fact defines their existence so completely that it shapes every aspect of their biology. Their legs are nubs, functionless remnants of an ancestry that included ground-dwelling. Their wings are membranous sheets stretched between elongated finger-bones, designed not for flight but for drifting—catching thermal currents and riding them, adjusting angle and orientation with minute movements that keep them suspended in the steam columns that rise from the cisterns and the hot springs.

Their bodies are slender tubes, streamlined for life in saturated air. They extract water directly through their skin—osmotic membranes that pull moisture from the air the same way the condenser lizards pull it from wet surfaces, but adapted for a medium rather than a substrate. They are, in a sense, living condensers: biological systems that perform the same function as the industrial infrastructure below them, pulling value from the atmosphere through surface chemistry and specialized organs.

They hunt the insects that breed in the shallow pools—small flying things that have adapted to the mineral heat, their life cycles compressed into days, their populations exploding and collapsing in patterns that the vapor-gliders have learned to follow. The gliders' ultrasonic clicks map the steam columns the way bats map caves, and they gather in loose formations above the central cistern at dawn, when the thermal flow increases and the insects rise.

Workers identify the glider columns the way they identify weather. A dense column means strong thermal flow and good harvest conditions. A scattered column means instability—fluctuations in the reservoir pressure that may indicate a coming eruption. The gliders do not know they are watched. They live their entire lives between ground and sky, and when they die, they fall, and the plateau receives them.

## The Bacterial Mats

They are alive, and they are the reason anything else is alive here.

The mats form at the edges of the thermal pools, where the water temperature drops from scalding to merely hot and the mineral concentration reaches the specific saturation that chemosynthetic life requires. They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize. They take their energy from the sulfur compounds dissolved in the thermal water and the heat that flows beneath the plateau's crust, and they convert that energy into biomass through chemical processes that have nothing to do with sunlight.

The mats are colorful in ways that the green world would find alien. Turquoise and deep blue where the sulfur content is high. Rust-orange where iron oxides dominate. White where the chemistry tips toward calcium and the mat thickens into crusted shelves that the spring-striders pierce with their hollow beaks. The colors shift and blend, forming patterns that change with the thermal flow, the temperature, the mineral content of the water feeding the pool.

They are the first tier of a food chain built on chemistry. The striders feed on them. The insects that the vapor-gliders hunt breed in the shallow water at the mat's edge, where the chemosynthetic growth provides substrate and nutrient. The condenser lizards feed on the insects and the strider carcasses and the vapor-glider falls. Everything that lives on the plateau lives because the mats live, and the mats live because the plateau bleeds heat and minerals from the deep earth.

The bioluminescent strains are the night's only light. They glow blue-white in the dark hours, marking the water's edge with a cold radiance that has nothing to do with the sun. The workers navigate by this light when they must move at night, following the glowing margins of the pools, reading the brightness for information about the mat's health and the thermal flow's stability. The glow is not for the workers. The glow is not for anything. It is a byproduct of the chemistry that sustains the mat, a waste product of the process that keeps everything alive, and it is also the most beautiful thing the Condenseries contains.

## The Pack Animals

They are not from here, but they have been changed by here.

The caravans that carry the crystals down to the markets and bring supplies back up the mountain use animals bred for the altitude and the wet heat—stocky, hairless creatures with wide feet that grip damp stone and metabolisms adapted to operate in oxygen-poor air. They are not native to the plateau. They were bred in the lowlands, descendants of pack stock that was selectively chosen over generations for tolerance of the conditions the Condenseries imposes.

They do not like the plateau. They tolerate it. They stand in the loading areas with a patience that is not docility but resignation, their wide feet planted on the mineral ground, their hairless sides steaming in the heat. They eat feed brought from below—grain and dried vegetation, the plant matter that the plateau cannot produce. They drink from the catchment reservoirs, the collected condensation that is the only water on the plateau cool enough for their systems.

The workers care for them with a practicality that contains affection. They know each animal by temperament and by the specific way it moves on the wet stone—some are steady, some are nervous, some are stubborn in ways that require particular handling. The caravans are the Condenseries' lifeline to the world below, and the animals are the caravans' engine, and the workers understand this dependency with a clarity that the extraction industry's remote management does not share.

When an animal dies on the plateau, it is a significant loss. The condenser lizards will drag what they can into crevices, but the bulk of the carcass must be dealt with by the workers. They render it where it falls, separating what can be used—hide for gaskets, bone for tools, fat for the lamps that light the work halls—from what cannot. The mineral ground absorbs the rest over time, the heat and the chemistry doing what soil and microorganisms would do in a greener world, but faster and more completely.

## The Machines

They are not alive, but they are not entirely dead.

The original engines that power the pumps and lifts were built by people who understood principles the current workers do not. They run on a mechanism that involves pressure and heat and the movement of water through constrained channels, and they have run for longer than anyone on the plateau has been alive. The workers maintain them through procedures memorized and passed down—sequences of actions that produce specific results, without understanding of why those actions work or what the mechanism beneath them is actually doing.

When a machine fails beyond the procedures' ability to repair, the workers do not mourn but they do pause. They stand around the silent engine in the bright silence and they look at it the way they might look at a colleague who has collapsed mid-shift. Then they salvage what they can and they fabricate replacements—carved stone valves, treated bacterial-mat gaskets, mineral-patch pipes that integrate into the original infrastructure with an organic logic that the original engineers would not recognize but that functions.

The machines have become something the engineers did not intend: a collaboration between human maintenance and geological process. The mineral water that flows through them deposits scale that reinforces joints and seals leaks. The heat that powers them also degrades them, and the repairs that address the degradation introduce new materials that the water's chemistry acts upon in new ways. The Condenseries' infrastructure is not aging; it is evolving, each repair a mutation, each adaptation a response to the environment's selective pressure. The machines live in the way that rivers live—changing course, depositing sediment, wearing down in some places and building up in others, shaped by the medium they channel.

## What Lives Here, Together

The Condenseries is a community in the fullest sense: a collection of organisms and systems that depend on each other, that have adapted to each other, that have been shaped by the same pressures into forms that fit the same environment. The workers and the lizards and the striders and the gliders and the mats and the machines and the pack animals and the children—all of them occupy the same plateau, breathe the same saturated air, move through the same bright silence.

They do not think of themselves as a community. The workers think of themselves as workers. The lizards do not think. The machines do not think. But the system that includes all of them thinks in the way that ecosystems think—through feedback and response, through adaptation and equilibrium, through the slow accumulation of changes that produce a functioning whole.

What lives in the Condenseries is not any single thing. What lives here is the plateau itself, expressing its conditions through every form that survives on its surface—the heat in the lizard's scales, the light in the strider's translucence, the silence in the workers' hands, the chemistry in the mat's glow. The settlement is the plateau made conscious of itself through the organisms that inhabit it, and the organisms are the settlement made biological, and the distinction between the two has dissolved over generations of shared immersion in the same mineral water, the same saturated air, the same bright, silent, heat.
