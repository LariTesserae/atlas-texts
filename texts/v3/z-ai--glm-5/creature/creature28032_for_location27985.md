# Those Who Dwell on the Plateau

## The People of the Expanse

They call themselves the Held—though "call themselves" implies a choice in the matter. The name emerged generations ago, a recognition of what the plateau does: it holds you. Not captures, not traps. Holds. The way cold preserves, the way flatness contains, the way time accumulates in corners of this place like silt in still water.

They are shorter than lowland peoples would be, stocky in build, with broad chests that house lungs adapted to thin air. Their skin has pallor that shifts grey in certain light—not unhealthy, but shadowed, as if their bodies have learned to disappear against stone. Their hair grows thick and dark, worn long and braided with plant-fibers in patterns that indicate family lineage, settlement origin, trade profession. A person's history can be read in their braid, though the reading requires knowledge that only the Held possess.

They do not count years. Instead, they count distances—the reach of a particular root-system, the spread of a moss-pillar, the extension of a family's trade route. When they speak of age, they say "I have seen the channel near my home shift three times" or "My grandmother's grandmother walked as far as the tall grass fields." Time is measured in accumulated experience of place, not abstract duration. The plateau has taught them that years lie. Stone does not.

Children are raised collectively in each settlement—not from any ideology but from practical necessity. Adults rotate through childcare duties, and every child knows dozens of figures as "parent" in the functional sense. Blood relation matters less than proximity and contribution. A child learns to call anyone who feeds them "mother" or "father," and the terms carry no confusion. The Held have many words for the specific shapes of relationship, but none for exclusive ownership of another person. Such a concept would be meaningless here. You cannot own what the plateau holds.

Their hands are remarkable. Every person possesses skills that elsewhere would be specialized—cordage-making, stone-shaping, plant identification, water-finding, distance-walking, weather-reading. Children begin learning at the age where lowland children might first walk unaided; by puberty, they are already competent in the basics of survival. The knowledge is not taught formally but absorbed, passed through gesture and example, the way blood passes through a family line. A grandmother shapes a stone blade while children watch; later, those children pick up stones and find their hands already know the motion.

## The Changed

Not all who live here remain entirely human.

The plateau changes those who stay long enough. The Held do not speak of this directly—they have no word for it, perhaps no concept that separates "changed" from "adapted." But certain individuals among them carry differences that go beyond acclimation.

There are those who navigate by something other than sight. The unreliability of distance, the mutability of paths—these should make navigation impossible, yet some walkers find their way across impossible stretches with perfect accuracy. They describe no method, claim no special knowledge. They simply walk in the correct direction, and the plateau adjusts around them. These pathfinders are valued above almost anyone else; without them, trade between settlements would be guesswork, and guesses on the plateau lead to disappearance.

Some seem to hear the pillar-mosses. Not in words—the mosses have no language, no mouthparts, no apparatus for speech. But certain individuals pause near the taller columns and tilt their heads, listening, and afterward they know things: the location of a water source, the path a channel will take next season, the stability of a particular route. The mosses do not speak, and the listeners do not claim the mosses speak. But something passes between them. Information, perhaps. Or something like information, translated through a medium that has no name in any tongue.

A few carry memories that are not their own. This is different from the ordinary discrepancies that plague the plateau—those are errors, confusions, the inevitable result of living in a place where time pools and eddies. These inherited memories are specific, detailed, consistent across individuals who have never met. A child in one settlement describes a building she has never seen with architectural precision; a woman in a distant town confirms the description and adds details about the interior. A man wakes knowing the exact route to a spring that dried up three generations ago, and when investigators follow his directions, they find water where none should exist.

The Changed do not form a separate class. They live among the ordinary, work alongside them, marry and raise children and age and die. But they are known, and consulted, and valued. They are the Held's interface with the plateau's deeper strangeness—living bridges between the human community and whatever the plateau is becoming.

## The Solitaries

Not everyone chooses to live in settlement.

Scattered across the Expanse are individuals who have walked away from community entirely. Not hermits in the traditional sense—they do not seek solitude for contemplation or escape. They seem, instead, to have been called. The plateau needs them, though what it needs them for remains unclear.

They live in the open, or in the shelter of particularly large moss-pillars, or in structures they have built themselves from found materials. Their shelters are strange—not the uniform low stone of the settlements, but asymmetrical constructions that follow no architectural logic. Walls that curve for no reason. Entrances that face directions other than east. Rooms that seem too small for use until you enter them and find they are larger inside than they appeared. The Solitaries build what the plateau tells them to build, and they do not explain.

They do not farm. They do not store food. They do not, as best anyone can determine, eat. And yet they persist—some for decades, known by travelers who have passed them at intervals over years, unchanged in appearance despite the passage of time. When asked how they survive, they give answers that are not answers: "The cold feeds me." "The fog is sufficient." "I have finished with hunger."

They are not quite revered. The settlement-dwellers regard them with a complicated mixture of respect and unease. They are not dangerous—no record exists of a Solitary harming anyone. But they are not entirely present, either. Conversations with them leave people unsettled, aware that they have been speaking with something that used to be fully human and is now something else. A transitional form, perhaps. A preview of what the plateau might make of everyone, given enough time.

## The Ungrew

The children who are born wrong.

Not wrong in body—the Held have no concept of physical disability as "wrong," and children born with limbs that function differently are simply accommodated, their strengths identified and cultivated, their needs met without fuss. The plateau has room for all configurations of flesh.

But some children are born with something missing. Not an organ, not a sense—something more fundamental. They do not grow. Not physically; they mature in size and proportion as normal. But they do not accumulate. They do not become more than they were.

A normal child among the Held learns continuously, building skill upon skill, knowledge upon knowledge, memory upon memory. By adulthood, they are dense with accumulated living. The Ungrew remain light. They do not remember yesterday clearly. They do not develop skills beyond the basic. They do not form attachments that deepen over time. They are present, fully present in each moment, but each moment is their first and only. They are born old in the sense that they will never become anything other than what they already are.

They are not unintelligent. Some display remarkable immediate capacities—the ability to solve problems in the instant they arise, to respond to situations with appropriate action, to perceive things about the environment that others miss. But none of it remains. The next day, the next hour, the next moment, and it is gone. They must learn again. They must discover again. They are each a perpetual beginning.

Families care for them. Communities absorb them. They are given simple tasks that do not require continuity, and they perform these tasks with satisfaction that is genuine if transient. They seem content, these children who never become more. They do not know what they are missing. They cannot know. Knowing requires accumulation, and accumulation is exactly what they lack.

Some among the Held believe the Ungrew are not defective but adapted—humans shaped for a plateau where time does not hold constant, where past and future blur, where the accumulation of experience might be a trap rather than a gift. In a place where you might walk for a day and find only an hour has passed, perhaps the ability to exist only in the present is not a limitation but a protection. The Ungrew cannot be disoriented by temporal anomalies because they have no temporal baseline to disrupt. They are always exactly when they are.

They are loved. They are mourned as well, quietly, by parents who will never see their children grow, who will never hear them say "I remember when—" because they do not remember when. But they are loved, and they are held, and they live their light lives on the heavy plateau until they die, as all the Held do, and return to the slow soil from which they came.

## The Settlement-Builders

The structures no one remembers building.

In the oldest towns, there are rooms whose origins are lost—not forgotten through the passage of generations, but absent from memory entirely. No stories exist about their construction. No tools match the particular marks on their stones. They are simply there, and have always been there, and no one questions them because questioning would require a context in which they might not have been there, and that context does not exist.

Some of these structures are still in use—storage chambers, meeting halls, spaces where people gather and work and live without noticing that the proportions are wrong, that the angles do not quite join, that light falls in ways that make no geometric sense. They have been used for so long that their strangeness has become familiar, then invisible, then non-existent. The mind edits what it sees, correcting the architecture to match expectation. A room might have five walls, but you will see four, and the fifth will simply be behind you, and you will not turn around, and you will not notice that you have not turned around.

Other structures are avoided. Not through any explicit agreement; people simply do not go there. Paths curve around certain buildings without anyone deciding to curve them. Children play everywhere except near those particular walls. The avoidance is ancient, instinctive, passed down through posture and hesitation rather than language. The words for these places do not name them but gesture toward them: "the place we do not go" is not a name, but everyone knows which place is meant.

What lives in these places?

Nothing that can be seen. Nothing that moves or makes sound or leaves traces. But there is something—a presence, a pressure, a sense of being observed from within. Those who have accidentally entered (and there are a few, sleepwalkers, wanderers, the confused and the lost) report nothing specific: a feeling of being known, being assessed, being found acceptable or wanting, and then being released back into the ordinary world. They do not remember what happened inside, but they emerge changed—calmer, often, or more purposeful, or suddenly certain of a decision they had been unable to make. The buildings judge and then return, and what they judge remains a mystery.

The Held do not speak of this. The avoidance is maintained silently, the presence accepted without comment. The buildings are part of the plateau, and the plateau holds everything, even things that might not belong to it.

## The Dead

The dead remain among the Held, but not as spirits.

The plateau's cold preserves flesh. A body buried in the shallow soil—a body left on the open stone—does not decay in any ordinary sense. It desiccates slowly, moisture leaving over months and years, and what remains is not corpse but something else. Preserved tissue, intact features, skin like old parchment over bone. The dead look like the living, only still. Frozen in the patience that defines everything here.

The Held do not bury their dead. They place them—on platforms of woven reed, in the shelter of moss-pillars, in special chambers carved into the root-mountains. The dead become features of the landscape, points of reference, landmarks in navigation. "Turn at my grandmother" is a direction a pathfinder might give, and the instruction is literal: the preserved body of a woman who died decades ago sits in a particular spot, and travelers pass her, and she remains, and the route is marked by her presence.

The dead are spoken to. Not in prayer or ritual, but in casual address, as if they have simply become very quiet. A farmer might mention the weather to her preserved husband as she passes his platform. A child might ask a question of a great-grandparent and wait for an answer that does not come, and then proceed satisfied, as if the silence itself contained response. The dead are present. They hold their place. They do not leave, and the Held do not expect them to.

In some settlements, the preserved dead outnumber the living. This is not remarked upon. The plateau's population has always been small, and its dead have always been many, and the ratio shifts over generations without anyone tracking it closely. The dead are held, and the living are held, and the difference between the two states is one of movement—a temporary distinction. All are preserved. All are waiting. All belong to the plateau, which keeps what it is given.

Some travelers report that the dead move. Not while being watched, but between observations. A body that faced east in the morning faces west by evening. A hand that rested on a knee is now raised, palm up, as if offering or receiving. The changes are always small, plausible as the result of settling or weather or the unreliable nature of memory. But they accumulate. And the dead, over decades and centuries, seem to be slowly rearranging themselves into patterns—circles, lines, arrangements that might be symbolic or functional or both.

The pillar-mosses grow toward the dead. A moss-column that begins near a preserved body will lean in that direction over the years, its growth asymmetric, as if drawn. When moss and body meet, the moss envelops slowly, covering the preserved flesh with new layers of living tissue, incorporating the dead into its architecture. What this means, whether it serves a purpose, what the moss might be building with the borrowed shapes of former humans—these questions do not have answers. They have only the slow, patient evidence of process.

## The Travelers

Not all who cross the Expanse are of it.

There are those who come from beyond—if "beyond" is even a meaningful concept here. They appear on the plateau without warning, walking from directions that lead nowhere, carrying equipment and clothing that do not match any Held style. They speak languages that sound like language, with grammar and rhythm and emotional inflection, but none of the words correspond to any known tongue. They are human in appearance, or close enough to make the differences unclear: limbs in the correct number, faces arranged as faces should be, but proportions slightly wrong, eyes that focus on things invisible to the Held, skin that seems to absorb light rather than reflect it.

They do not stay. They cross the plateau, moving with purpose toward destinations they do not share, and they vanish again, sometimes reaching some horizon and disappearing over it, sometimes simply ceasing to be visible when attention is drawn away for a moment. The Held have learned not to interfere with them. Interference is unwelcome—this much is clear from the few recorded attempts. The Travelers will not be stopped. They will not be turned. They will not answer questions or accept aid. They are passing through, and they are not passing through the Held's world, exactly. They are passing through the plateau itself, and the Held happen to live there, and the Travelers regard them as a landscape feature rather than a population to engage.

What are they? Theories accumulate, as theories do when evidence is sparse. Some say they are the original builders of the ancient structures, still moving through a territory they consider theirs. Some say they are messengers between worlds, the plateau being a place where worlds touch and the Travelers being those who move between them. Some say they are the plateau's immune system, cells in a body too large to comprehend, performing functions that serve a logic beyond human understanding.

The Changed have their own theories, which they do not share with outsiders. They watch the Travelers pass with expressions that might be recognition, or grief, or anticipation. Some pathfinders have reported guiding a Traveler who then guided them in return—led them through a fold in distance to a destination that would have taken months to reach, delivered them in a single day, and then departed without explanation. These pathfinders do not speak of what they saw in the folds, but their eyes have been different since, their hearing more acute, their relationship to the plateau's strangeness more intimate.

## The Moss-Minds

The pillar-mosses are not merely plants.

This takes a long time to understand. The moss-columns seem passive, biological accumulations, no more conscious than a coral reef or a lichen colony. But the Held have learned otherwise, over generations of proximity and attention.

The mosses remember. A moss-column that has been damaged—by weather, by accident, by human interference—will grow back differently, the new tissue incorporating some record of the injury. The pattern is not obvious, but close examination reveals it: a twist in the column's spiral, a change in the density of the growth, a variation in color that corresponds to the severity of the damage. The mosses do not merely heal; they record. They are building an archive of every event that has touched them, encoded in their own flesh.

They communicate, slowly, through fungal networks that connect them beneath the surface. The connections are vast—miles of filament linking pillar to pillar across distances that would take humans weeks to walk. Through these networks, information passes: the location of water, the chemistry of soil, the approach of weather systems, the presence of travelers. The mosses are a distributed intelligence, processing data at a rate that makes human thought seem frantic, deliberating over seasons what a person decides in seconds, acting over centuries what a person enacts in a lifetime.

And they are planning something.

The patterns in their distribution are not random. From the ground, they seem scattered—arriving at moss-pillars one by one, there is no evident structure. But the Travelers have been seen examining the plateau from elevated positions, and those who have observed them doing so report expressions of intensity, even concern. The mosses are placed with intention. They form a diagram, a circuit, a system whose shape becomes visible only from a perspective the Held cannot achieve.

What the mosses are building, what their architecture will accomplish when complete, whether they serve their own purposes or those of the plateau or those of something else entirely—these are unknowns. But the Held have begun to treat the moss-pillars with deliberate respect. They do not damage them. They do not build on the paths between them. They leave offerings of water at their bases during the dry seasons, small gifts that the mosses accept without acknowledgment. They have come to suspect, these humans who live on a plateau that is slowly eroding out of reality, that the mosses are the true inhabitants of this place, and that they themselves are permitted to remain only because they do not interfere with what is being constructed.

## The Fragments

The plateau produces echoes.

Not audible echoes—nothing so simple. But people, or things that resemble people, who appear in the settlements without having walked there, who remember lives that no one else remembers, who carry knowledge of events that might have happened or might happen or might be happening in some other version of the Expanse.

A Fragment arrives knowing the names of people it has never met, claiming relationships that do not exist, referring to conversations that never occurred. It is confused, often distressed, by the discrepancies between its memories and the reality it finds itself in. The woman it calls mother does not recognize it. The house it remembers living in has different occupants. The path to the well runs in the wrong direction.

Fragments are not common—perhaps one arrives in each generation, in each settlement—but they are consistent. They appear, they struggle, they either adapt or they fade. Some integrate successfully, accepting that their memories were wrong or belonged to someone else, and they become new members of the community, their origins a mystery that eventually stops mattering. Others cannot reconcile themselves to the gap between memory and world. They wander, searching for the place they remember, and eventually they wander too far, or they walk into one of the ancient buildings, or they simply vanish, and the Held acknowledge their passing with the same acceptance they bring to everything the plateau offers or takes.

The Changed recognize Fragments. They can identify them, sometimes, before the Fragment itself knows what it is. They treat them with particular attention—guiding them gently toward integration or release, helping them navigate a reality that has rejected or misplaced them. Some Changed have speculated that the Fragments are not errors but messengers, carrying information from adjacent possibilities, from versions of the plateau that are almost identical to this one but not quite. The information is embedded in their memories—in the differences between what they remember and what exists—and careful study of those differences might reveal something about the shape of reality, the nature of the erosion, the destination toward which the plateau is slowly moving.

But such study requires resources the Held do not have, and the Fragments are rare, and the Changed are not researchers in any formal sense. So the Fragments come and go, echoes of other possibilities passing through the thin places in the world, and the plateau holds them briefly, and then releases them, and continues its patient accumulation of time.

## The Waiting

All of these—the Held, the Changed, the Solitaries, the Ungrew, the dead in their preservation, the Travelers passing through, the moss-minds building their slow architecture, the Fragments arriving from adjacent possibilities—they are held together by the plateau, held in its flatness and its cold and its patience, held in a place where reality has worn thin enough to permit their coexistence.

The plateau does not produce heroes. It does not produce events of dramatic significance. It produces continuance. Lives lived within constraints so absolute they have become invisible. Adaptations so thorough they have become identity. A human community that has become part of an ecosystem that has become part of a metaphysical process that has become part of whatever the world becomes when it has finished becoming.

They are held, and they hold each other, and they wait—not for anything specific, but because waiting is what this place teaches. The cold preserves. The flatness contains. The erosion continues. And the people of the Glistening Expanse live their slow lives on the worn-down surface of a world that has almost completed itself, residents of an ending so gradual it has become indistinguishable from permanence.
