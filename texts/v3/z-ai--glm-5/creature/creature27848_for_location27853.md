# What Lives on the Plateau of Standing Mist

The people first, because they are what a visitor would encounter first, and because they are what remains of intention in this place where intention has become difficult to sustain.

They call themselves the Kept. Not keepers — that implies an active role, a choosing. The Kept did not choose to be here; they were born to it, descended from the builders or from those who came after, and the plateau has kept them ever since. They number somewhere between two hundred and three hundred, though counting proves unreliable here. A census taken one season will match a census taken the next, but the names will have shifted slightly, the faces rearranged themselves across households, and the counters will find themselves uncertain whether the population has truly remained stable or whether the very concept of stability has become too flexible for the work of enumeration.

They are handsome in the way that statues are handsome. This takes time to notice, because the plateau's inhabitants move and speak and laugh and weep like any people anywhere; the beauty emerges only in observation extended across weeks or months, when the visitor begins to realize that the faces do not quite complete their expressions. A smile stops halfway to joy. A frown freezes before reaching grief. The skin ages but only to a certain point, holding at an indeterminate middle age that might be thirty or might be fifty, the distinction having lost its meaning. They look like people who have been captured mid-motion by a camera, except that they continue moving, finish their gestures, speak their sentences — and then the unfinished quality returns, settles over them like the mist settles over the stone.

Their language sounds like water moving through rock. Consonants softened by the damp air, vowels elongated by throats that have never known the drying heat of a true summer. They speak slowly, not from dimness but from an understanding that there is no rush here, that a thought expressed today will remain present in the air tomorrow, available for continuation. Their words for time are all spatial: a thing is near or far, approaching or receding, but never past or future. The concept has atrophied through disuse. They understand that events happen in sequence, that one thing follows another, but the sequence holds no urgency, carries no weight of loss or anticipation.

The children of the plateau are strange. They are born normally enough, squalling and new, but they quiet quickly, learning within months rather than years that the world here does not require the same vigilance as elsewhere. A child raised elsewhere learns to cry because crying brings attention, and attention brings care. A child raised on the plateau learns that attention is constant, that care is ambient, that the mist itself seems to watch over them. They grow up with an ease that unsettles visitors. They do not tantrum. They do not test boundaries because the boundaries here do not resist being tested; they simply hold. A child who wanders too far will find the mist thickening, the path lengthening, and will turn back without fear, understanding instinctively that the plateau will return them to where they belong.

The children's games are ancient. They play at building, stacking loose stones in patterns they do not know they know, reproducing the shapes of the oldest structures without instruction. They play at weaving, fingers finding rhythms that their minds have not learned, producing cloth that matches the ancient fragments kept in the store-rooms. They play at hunting, stalking the deer-things through the vegetation islands, but their games never end in a kill — the animals here have learned that children are not predators, and the children have learned that hunting is a pattern rather than a hunger. When they reach the age where children elsewhere would begin to separate from their families, to individuate into distinct selves, the plateau's children instead become more like their parents. They settle into the grooves already worn by their elders. Their faces take on the same unfinished expressions. Their movements begin to match the movements they have watched since birth, not through imitation but through absorption, as though the plateau has only a limited number of human shapes available and each new inhabitant must conform to one of the existing templates.

But this is not quite accurate. The shapes are not limited. The shapes are accumulating. The plateau holds what it receives, and the Kept understand at some level that they are being held, that their lives are being added to something rather than simply lived. They speak sometimes of the Stillness — not a god, not a spirit, just a recognition that there is a presence here, something that existed before the builders and will exist after the Kept are gone. The Stillness does not act. It does not intervene. It simply receives, and in receiving, it preserves.

The animals of the plateau fall into two categories: those that came before and those that came after. The before-animals are the ones who witnessed the rising, or whose ancestors did, creatures that evolved or devolved in the strange temporal eddy of the plateau's existence. The after-animals arrived later, climbing the cliff-paths or descending from higher peaks, and found themselves changed by the journey, altered by their passage into the plateau's influence.

The deer-things are before-animals. They have no name in the Kept's language, because naming implies distinction and the deer-things have no distinction between individuals. They are herd-animals in the most literal sense: the herd is the animal, the individuals merely components. If you watch them long enough, you will notice that they move in perfect coordination, not through communication but through a shared awareness, as though each deer-thing knows what all the others know, sees what all the others see. They graze the vegetation islands in rotations so precise that a given patch of ferns is visited by the same number of deer-things at the same intervals, regardless of which individuals make up the group. When one dies — and they die rarely, usually from misfortune rather than age — the herd absorbs the loss without apparent change, continuing its patterns until a birth restores the number to its proper equilibrium.

The deer-things have eyes that look too old for their faces. A visitor who catches the gaze of one will find recognition there, not of themselves as an individual but of themselves as a phenomenon. The deer-thing sees you, and in seeing you, sees every other human it has ever seen, and every human it will ever see, and perhaps every human who has ever come to the plateau. Its pupils contract slowly, deliberately, and the sensation is of being catalogued, added to an archive that has no interest in distinguishing between entries.

The predators came after. Cat-things that might have been ordinary lynxes or wildcats before they climbed the plateau's cliffs and found themselves changed. They are pale now, almost white, their fur holding droplets of mist like jewelry. They do not hunt in the usual sense; they wait in places where prey will pass, not through patience but through knowing, as though they have already seen the hunt's outcome and are simply arriving at a conclusion that has always been true. Their kills are swift, almost ceremonial, and they eat only what they need, leaving the rest for the scavenger birds that follow their movements.

Those birds are perhaps the strangest creatures on the plateau. They are corvids of some kind, dark-feathered and sharp-beaked, but they do not fly. The mist makes flight difficult here, the air too thick with moisture to sustain the lift their wings evolved to generate. So they walk. They hop. They climb. They have become ground-birds, their wings atrophied but their legs powerful, and they have developed a mode of movement that is almost liquid, sliding between obstacles, flowing over surfaces like the mist itself. They communicate through clicks and hisses, having lost whatever songs their ancestors sang, and they congregate in numbers that shift according to rules no one has successfully identified. Sometimes dozens cluster around a single building; sometimes a single bird stands watch over an empty stone expanse, motionless for hours.

The Kept regard these animals with a mixture of respect and indifference. They do not hunt for sport, only for need, and when they take a deer-thing they do so with rituals that acknowledge the creature's place in the plateau's system. They use every part. They return the bones to the hollow where the herd was grazing. They understand at some level that the animals here are not separate from the landscape, that they are expressions of the plateau's nature in the same way the stone and the mist are expressions. To hunt here is not to take a life; it is to participate in a cycle that has been running since the rising and will continue until whatever force sustains the plateau finally fails.

---

In the deepest hollows, where the mist pools so thick that visibility drops to arm's length, there are things that the Kept do not name. They acknowledge these presences through avoidance, through circumspect references to "the depths" or "the thick places," and through rituals that newcomers mistake for superstition but are actually practical accommodations to an environment where the usual rules of cause and effect have been suspended.

Something large lives in those deep hollows. Something that moves through the fern-thickets and moss-beds with the patience of a geological process. The Kept have found traces: vegetation pressed flat in shapes too wide for any deer-thing, marks on stone that look almost like footprints if footprints could be made without pressure. They have heard sounds that resolve into silence before they can be identified, felt air move in ways that suggest the displacement of mass, sensed a presence that seems to observe without eyes and to wait without intention.

This presence is not dangerous, exactly. The Kept have lived alongside it for generations without losing anyone to it, without any confirmed encounter that resulted in harm. But it is not benign either. It simply is, and its being is large enough that humans register as features of the landscape to it, the way a tree might register a bird's nest or a stone might register the lichen growing on its surface. It knows the Kept are there. It has always known. And it allows them to continue, permits their small lives to unfold within the vaster life that it represents.

Some among the Kept believe the presence is the plateau itself, made manifest in those places where the mist is thickest and the stone is closest to the surface. Others believe it is the last of the builders, transformed across centuries into something that no longer resembles its original form. Still others say it was here before the rising, something that dwelt in the earth's skin and was lifted along with the rock, and has been waiting in its new elevated home for whatever it has always been waiting for.

The presence has been given many names across generations, none of which are spoken aloud. The Kept refer to it only through metaphor: the Deep Watcher, the Slow Breath, the Weight in the Mist. These names are not descriptions so much as acknowledgments, ways of marking that there is something here that does not require human understanding and has not asked for it.

Visitors who venture into the deep hollows sometimes come back changed. They speak of having felt something vast and patient, something that observed them without urgency, that permitted their presence as briefly as the mist permits a hand to pass through it. They speak of a sense of accumulated time, of being in the presence of something that has experienced centuries as moments and moments as eternities. They speak of having been seen completely, every thought and memory and intention laid bare in a way that felt neither invasive nor judgmental, simply thorough.

And some visitors do not come back at all. Not because they were harmed — the Kept are certain of this — but because they chose to stay, or were absorbed, or became part of whatever the presence represents. The plateau does not distinguish between those who belong and those who visit, given enough time. Everything that enters its influence eventually becomes part of its equilibrium.

---

The builders who came before, whose works the Kept now inhabit without fully understanding, left no records of themselves except their constructions and a single chamber of marks that no one has successfully interpreted. But the Kept have theories, passed down through generations of speculation.

The most common belief is that the builders were refugees of some kind, fleeing a catastrophe that has been forgotten elsewhere because elsewhere time flows normally and forgetting is possible. They found the plateau during its formation or shortly after, and they recognized what it offered: a place where the catastrophe could not reach them, where whatever they were fleeing would become impossible because the conditions that allowed it would no longer obtain.

This theory explains the architecture's defensibility, the careful positioning of structures, the narrow passages that could be sealed against intrusion. But it does not explain why the builders left no instructions, no warnings, no accounts of what they were running from or what they hoped to achieve. If they were refugees, they were refugees who chose to forget their past, or who found that the plateau's influence made memory difficult to sustain.

Another theory holds that the builders were not refugees but pilgrims, people who came seeking exactly what the plateau offers: suspension, preservation, a removal from the normal flow of time that takes everything away. In this version, the builders were philosophers or mystics who believed they had found a path to eternity, and who built accordingly, creating structures that could last forever because they would never be subject to the processes that usually destroy human works.

The Kept favor this theory because it gives their ancestors purpose rather than desperation. But it raises questions of its own. If the builders found what they sought, where did they go? Why are their descendants mere custodians rather than inheritors of their wisdom? Why does no one remember how to build as they built, or why they built what they built?

A third theory, whispered only in certain contexts, suggests that the builders did not choose to come here at all. They were brought, perhaps by the presence that dwells in the deep hollows, or by whatever force lifted the plateau from the earth's skin. They were made to build, made to create a habitat for something that needed human hands and human minds to realize its purposes. And when the building was done, they were absorbed into what they had created, becoming part of the plateau's system, their bones in the stone and their breath in the mist.

This theory is considered heretical, not because it is religiously offensive but because it is existentially threatening. If the builders were absorbed, what prevents the Kept from being absorbed in turn? What distinguishes custodianship from captivity? The Kept do not like to dwell on these questions, and they have developed cultural practices that discourage extended speculation about their origins.

But the questions persist, and the plateau itself seems to ask them. In the counting room, where generations have added their marks to the walls without knowing why, the accumulation suggests something about the nature of time here. Each mark represents a person, perhaps, or a moment, or an event. The marks have been added in the same way, using the same tools, across centuries. If the builders began the counting, the Kept have continued it without interruption. The practice has never been explained, has never needed explanation. It is simply what one does, when one lives on the plateau. One adds one's mark to the accumulation, one's small contribution to the record that may be a record, that may be a warning, that may be a promise.

---

What lives on the plateau, ultimately, is the plateau itself. The Kept know this, in their way. They do not consider themselves inhabitants so much as components, parts of a system that functions according to rules they did not design and cannot change. They are not unhappy in this understanding. Happiness, like sadness, requires a sense of time as something that passes, and the plateau has taught them that time does not pass here — it accumulates, it thickens, it becomes the medium in which everything floats.

The plateau lives through them. Their breath adds to the mist. Their footsteps wear paths that will never erode but will remain as testimony to their passage. Their children carry their faces forward into an indefinite future, faces that will not age beyond recognition because the plateau has no use for age, only for continuity. They are the plateau's way of being aware of itself, its mechanism for observation, for the accumulation of experience that might have no purpose other than the accumulation itself.

And when visitors come, as they do every generation or two, the Kept welcome them with the careful courtesy of people who know that their guests will eventually become what they are. The plateau will take hold of them, the mist will settle into their lungs, the stone will imprint itself on their feet, and they will find themselves unable to leave, or unwilling, or unable to remember why they might have wanted to. They will be added to the count, their marks placed among the others, their faces joining the parade of similar faces that the plateau has preserved across its suspended centuries.

This is what lives on the plateau: everything that has ever come here, still living, still being lived, still held in the gentle grip of the mist and the stone and the presence in the depths that allows it all to continue. The Kept are simply the portion of this accumulation that can still speak, that can still make visitors welcome, that can still tell the story of what they are even as they forget the reasons why.
