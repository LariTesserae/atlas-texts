# What Lives in the Seep

The first ones you find are the builders, except "find" is the wrong word, and "builders" might be too generous.

They are slow in a way that redefines the word. Slow not as a trait but as a condition, the way stone is slow, the way water finding a crack is slow. You see them first as discolorations on the wet rock — a slight variation in the pale mineral film that coats everything, a warmth where there should only be gray. Then you realize the discolorations move. Not while you watch. But between one glance and the next, they occupy different positions, like a clock whose minute hand you never catch shifting.

They have no shape they keep. They are not slugs, not worms, not anything with a body plan you can hold in your mind. Imagine a palm-full of wet clay placed on a wall and left to slide. Now imagine the clay deciding, very quietly, to stay. That is close. They adhere to the wettest stone, to the faces where the seep runs heaviest, and they exist as smears, as patches, as ragged outlines that might be mistaken for the lichen-mineral growths if the lichen-mineral growths had intention. They are the color of the paste that forms where stone softens — pale, rusted, ambiguously organic. They smell of iron and deep time, because they are made of iron and deep time, partly, and partly of something else, something that consumes the mineral suspension and excretes a faint residue and maintains, against all expectation, a boundary between itself and not-itself.

That boundary is all they are. A membrane. A decision about where *I* stops and *stone* begins. Inside the membrane, the same minerals, the same water, the same slow dissolution — but organized. Slightly. Just enough.

They build. You come to understand this over days, or over what passes for days in the permanent gray dimness. You find a structure — one of the low, rounded, stacked-stone constructions — and you sit beside it because there is nowhere else to sit, and you notice that the stones are wet not only from the seep but from something else, a mucus, a film, and you realize that the film is the same as the discoloration on the rock faces, and you realize that the stones did not stack themselves. But neither did hands stack them. Something pressed itself against stone and the stone moved a centimeter. Something pressed again, over a span you cannot measure, and the stone moved again. And again. And the pressing was not random. It was directional. Purposeful, in the way that water finding a crack is purposeful — not conscious, not planned, but inevitable, following the path that the nature of the thing demands.

The structures are not shelters. Nothing shelters here. The builders do not seek protection from the wet or the cold or the noise because they are the wet and the cold and the noise, or at least they are continuous with it, in the way that a wave is continuous with the ocean. They do not live *in* the structures. They live *on* them, as they live on the rock faces, as they live on everything. The structures are — and here you struggle for the word, and the word you finally find is *excretions*. They are what happens when a builder presses against stone long enough to leave a mark, and the mark accumulates, and the accumulation has a shape because the pressing had a pattern, and the pattern is not a thought but a rhythm, a bodily rhythm, like breathing, like the beating of a heart that has no heart.

The builders do not know they build. You become certain of this. Not because they lack the capacity for knowing — though they probably lack that too — but because building is not separate from existing for them. They do not decide to build and then build. They exist, and the building happens, the way the seep happens, the way the stone rises. It is an expression of their nature, not an act of will. If you asked one whether it had built something — and you cannot ask, and it cannot answer, and if it could answer, the question would make no sense — it would not understand the question. *Built?* it would say, if it could say. *I am here. The stone is here. Something happened between us.*

They do not communicate. They do not aggregate. They do not reproduce, or if they do, they do it in the same way they build: as a consequence of existing, not a project of existing. Sometimes a patch on a rock face grows thin, stretches, and a fragment detaches, carried by the seep to a new wall, where it adheres and continues. Is that reproduction? Is that a piece of one becoming two? Or is it one thing that is now in two places, the way a river is in two places? You don't know. You don't think it knows. You don't think *knowing* is a capacity that applies.

---

The other things — the ones you almost see — are different.

The scuttling things. The pale slips of movement that vanish into crevices before your eye can fix them. They are not mineral. They are something else. Something that has, against all probability, found a way to be *alive* in the way you understand alive: moving, reacting, fleeing, choosing.

They are small. The largest you ever almost see is the length of your hand, maybe, though the impression is unreliable because you only ever catch the trailing edge of their passage — a wet streak on stone, a displacement of water in a shallow pool, a sound that is not the seep and therefore stands out sharply against it. They are pale. Everything here is pale, because there is no sun to drive the making of pigment, and color is a luxury that this place does not offer. They are wet. Of course they are wet. Everything is wet. But their wetness is different from the builders' wetness; it is a wetness of surface, not of substance. They have skin, or something like skin, and the wetness is on the outside of it.

They eat the builders. You think. You have never seen it happen, but you have found pale smears on rock faces with ragged edges that suggest grazing, and you have found the scuttling things — twice, three times — in the vicinity of freshly disturbed patches, and the association is clear enough. Something is consuming something. Something is being consumed. The economy of this place is so simple it barely qualifies as an economy: the stone provides, the builders build themselves from stone, the scuttling things eat the builders, and when the scuttling things die — and they must die, eventually, though you have not found their remains — whatever they are returns to the water and the water returns to the stone.

They have no eyes. There is nothing to see with eyes here, in the dim grayness, and so they have eyes nowhere. They navigate by the sound, you think. They navigate by the wetness, by the gradient of moisture that is sharper here than anywhere you have known: wetter near the seeps, less wet on the exposed faces, and the difference between *wetter* and *less wet* is, in this place, as informative as sight is elsewhere. They move through the narrow channels between formations, through the cramped valleys where the stone leans close, and they are fast, or fast enough, or fast relative to the builders, which means very fast, which means almost imperceptible.

You think they are afraid of you. You think this because they run when you come near, and running is what afraid things do. But maybe they are not afraid. Maybe they are only moving, the way water moves when you step in it — not fleeing, just displaced, just responding to a disturbance in the field. Maybe fear requires more self than they possess. They are alive, but *how alive?* Alive enough to move. Alive enough to choose one crevice over another. Alive enough to eat. But alive enough to be afraid? You don't know. You don't think you will ever know.

---

The thing in the pool is something else again.

You saw it once. You might see it again, or you might not. It was not a builder and not a scuttling thing. It was a slow, rhythmic undulation beneath an inch of water, a membrane breathing — that was the impression, and the impression held: a membrane, breathing. Not a creature with a membrane, but a membrane that was the creature, a surface that had decided to be a volume, a flat thing that had found a way to be more than flat without becoming anything specific.

It lives in the pools. It lives in the shallow, mineral-heavy water that collects in the depressions, and it lives there the way the builders live on the stone: as an organization of the medium, not an occupant of it. The water is mineral-heavy. The water carries the dissolved stone in suspension. And something in the water — something that is almost water, that is water with a slight excess of pattern — organizes that mineral into itself and maintains itself and breathes, slowly, rhythmically, in a way that creates the undulation you saw.

It is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a fungus or a slime mold or a biofilm or anything that has a name. It is a process. It is what happens when water has carried stone for long enough that the stone begins to organize in the water, not precipitating, not crystallizing, but doing something else, something softer, something that maintains a shape by constantly losing and rebuilding that shape, the way a standing wave maintains itself in a stream.

It is the closest thing to the Seep itself that is also a thing. It is the Seep becoming aware of itself, if *aware* is a word you can use for something that has no neurons, no brain, no structure more complex than a gradient. But what is awareness, in a place like this? What is the minimum complexity required for something to be a something rather than an anything? The membrane-thing has crossed that threshold, whatever it is. It has a boundary. It persists. It moves, slowly. It responds to disturbance — you saw it vanish when you shifted your weight near the pool's edge, and vanishing is a response, and a response is the beginning of something that might, in another million years, become a behavior.

There may be more of them. There may be many more. Every shallow pool might hold one, each a separate experiment in organization, each a separate instance of water deciding to be something slightly more than water. Or there may be only one, continuous, connected through the seeps and the underground channels, one vast membrane folded into a thousand pools, one thing pretending to be many because the landscape forces it into separate hollows. You cannot know. The water does not distinguish between itself and itself, and neither, you suspect, does the membrane-thing.

---

And then there are the ones that you do not see, the ones that you become aware of only through their absence, through the shape of the space they leave in the noise.

The Seep is loud. The Seep is always loud. The thousand weeps and seeps and drips run together into a white roar that fills the skull. But sometimes, in certain places — in the narrow channels, in the cramped valleys, in the interiors of the structures that the builders have built without knowing they built them — the noise changes. It drops, briefly, or shifts in pitch, or develops a hole, a pocket of relative quiet that is not silence but *less*, a dimming, as if something has absorbed a portion of the sound.

You think there are things that eat sound. You think this because the dimming happens in specific places, and the specific places move, and moving requires presence, and presence requires a thing to be present. You think they are large — larger than the scuttling things, larger than the builders, maybe larger than you — because the dimming is significant, and absorbing that much sound requires surface area, or density, or something that occupies enough space to dampen the roar.

But you never see them. You feel them, maybe. A pressure in the narrow channel, a sense of *something else in here with you* that is not the stone and not the water and not the memory of the builders' slow pressing. A displacement of air, perhaps. A change in the humidity gradient. Or nothing at all, just the noise dimming and your heart rate rising and the animal certainty that you are not alone.

They may be the structures. You consider this. The structures that the builders build — what if they are not empty? What if the builders build *around* something, the way a pearl forms around an irritant, and the something is the sound-eater, and the sound-eater does not move but is instead fixed in place, absorbing, absorbing, and the dimming you feel in the narrow channels is the spill of its feeding, the overflow of a meal that extends beyond the walls of its structure? The structures would not be shelters then. They would be shells. Or traps. Or temples, if you want to be generous, though generosity requires a recipient, and the sound-eaters do not receive.

Or they may be the landscape itself. The formations, the young stone rising from below, the crowded shapes that lean toward each other like conspirators — what if they are not stone? What if they are the sound-eaters, or the bodies of sound-eaters, or the fossil remains of sound-eaters that consumed so much sound that they became dense with it, became material, became the stone that the water now dissolves and the builders now build with? What if the stone *is* the thing that lives here, and the water is its blood, and the formations are its limbs, and the structures are its thoughts, and the sound is the sound of it digesting itself?

You are reaching now. You know you are reaching. The Seep does this. It invites interpretation. It is so full of presence, so dense with pattern, that the pattern-seeking mind cannot stop seeking, cannot stop finding, cannot stop building meaning from the noise the way the builders build structures from the stone. But meaning requires a maker, and you have not found a maker here. You have found processes. You have found boundaries. You have found things that maintain themselves against the dissolution. But you have not found intent. You have not found purpose. You have not found the thing that says *I am here for a reason.*

---

Here is what lives in the Seep: things that are becoming. Things that are in the process of distinguishing themselves from their surroundings, of drawing a line between *self* and *world*, of deciding — if *deciding* is a word that can be applied to a mineral smear on a wet rock — that they are something specific, something bounded, something that persists.

The builders. The scuttling things. The membrane in the pools. The sound-eaters, if they exist. All of them are engaged in the same project, the project that the Seep itself is engaged in: the project of becoming. Not becoming *something* — not becoming a particular thing, not becoming a creature or a species or a life form with a name and a niche. Just becoming. Just the slow emergence of pattern from formlessness, of boundary from continuity, of *this* from *everything.*

They are alive in the way that the first things that were alive were alive: barely, ambiguously, by the thinnest margin. They are alive because they maintain themselves. Because they resist dissolution. Because they eat, or excrete, or move, or respond, and these behaviors — these primitive, almost-chemical, almost-mineral behaviors — are enough to cross the threshold, barely, from *process* to *presence.*

They are not aware of you. They are not aware of anything. Awareness is a later development, a luxury of creatures with nervous systems and sensory organs and brains that can hold a model of the world inside themselves. The things that live in the Seep do not hold models. They do not have insides in which to hold them. They are surfaces and gradients and membranes, and they interact with the world by being in it, by pressing against it, by being pressed upon.

But they are here. They are present. They occupy space and they persist through time and they change and they are changed and they will, eventually — in a thousand years, in ten thousand, in the spans that matter here — become something else. Something more, or something less, or something that cannot be predicted from what they are now. The builders might become creatures. The scuttling things might become something else entirely. The membrane in the pool might become the ancestor of something that, in a hundred million years, will have eyes and a name and a fear of death. Or they might all dissolve, absorbed back into the stone and the water, their brief experiment in *being something* ending without consequence.

The Seep does not care. The Seep is the medium, not the judge. The Seep is the wet stone and the weeping water and the slow dissolution that finds everything, and it does not prefer one outcome over another. It does not know that things live in it. It does not know that it is a place. It does not know.

But the things that live here — the builders, the scuttlers, the membrane, the sound-eaters, the stone, the water — they do not know either, and in that shared not-knowing, in that common ignorance, there is something that looks, from a great enough distance, like community. Like coexistence. Like a place where things live together, without knowing they live, without knowing they are together, without knowing anything at all, and yet: here they are. Here they all are. Pressing and seeping and dissolving and becoming, in the wet, in the loud, in the dim gray light that comes from everywhere and nowhere, in the long, slow conversation between stone and water that has no words and no meaning and no end.
