# What Lives on the Black Plateau of Ymurra

---

## The Riph

The riph do not know they are large. Size is a visual concept, and the riph have no visual concept. They know themselves by pressure—by the displacement their bodies create in the warm air, by the weight they register on the spongy ground, by the specific resonance of their calls bouncing off the sang-tubes and returning to their filaments. A riph experiences itself as a node of warmth and wetness in a world of warmth and wetness, distinct only because it vibrates at its own frequency, because the chemical signature it trails through the moisture is unmistakably *self*.

There are perhaps four hundred riph on the plateau, distributed across seven herds that the acoustic geographers among them would name differently—some by the sound of the water channels they frequent, some by the rhythm of the nearest functioning pump, some by the particular tone of the stone beneath their preferred grazing grounds. The herds are not fixed. Individuals drift between them, following chemical invitations, responding to the deep calls that announce openings in the social fabric. A young riph in estrus will send out a low-frequency pulse that carries for kilometers through the ground, and Suitable males will orient toward her, navigating by the gradient of her signature, arriving at her location already competing, already calling, already filling the acoustic space with their presence until she selects from among them based on criteria that remain opaque to any but riph—perhaps the quality of the call, perhaps the chemical richness of the approach, perhaps something subtler still, some pattern in the vibration that indicates fitness or compatibility or simply the right kind of insistence.

They graze on the fruiting bodies of the sang-tubes—pale, swollen growths that emerge from the upper stalks and fall with wet thuds to the ground below. The riph find them by taste, by the particular efflorescence of sugars and decay that they release into the air. A herd will move through a sang-tube forest in a loose formation, each individual maintaining acoustic contact through periodic rumbles, each tracking the others by the sound of their footfalls on the squelching ground-cover. When one finds a rich fallen fruit, it calls with a specific chirp—a sound so unlike their usual low-frequency rumble that early acoustic surveys of the plateau mistook it for a different species entirely. The others orient toward the call, converging, and for a few minutes the herd clusters tightly, eating, their breathing synchronized, their heat merging into a single warm mass in the cool fog.

They sleep standing, sometimes alone, sometimes in contact with one another. A sleeping riph continues to make sound—a low, unconscious drone that the other herd members can track, ensuring that the group does not drift apart in the black. The striiders have learned to mimic this sleep-drone, to approach a sleeping herd by matching its frequency, by becoming acoustically invisible. But the riph have also learned to detect the mimicry—the striider's drone lacks the subtle arrhythmia of genuine sleep, the slight variations in exhalation that mark a living body at rest. And so the predator and the prey are locked in an acoustic arms race that has run for millions of years, each refining its deception, each refining its detection, the signals becoming more complex, more nuanced, more difficult to separate from the noise.

The oldest riph on the plateau is a female the vithra-singers name *Theluuu*, approximating her deep, slow call. She is neither the largest nor the strongest, but she has lived longer than any other, surviving striider attacks and fog-disorientations and the occasional malfunction of the ancient pumps that can flood the lower channels without warning. She moves more slowly now, her six legs stiff with mineral deposits that have accumulated in her joints over decades, her filaments less sensitive than they were, her calls longer and lower as if the effort of frequency has become too great. But she knows the plateau in a way no other riph does. She can predict the fog banks by changes in the stone-vibrations hours before they arrive. She knows which weep-channels will flood during low-pressure events and which will run dry. The younger riph follow her without knowing they are following—their bodies simply orient toward her calls, their movement patterns synchronizing with hers, the herd forming around her like a formation around a center it cannot name.

When Theluuu dies—it will be soon, the vithra-singers agree, they can hear the slowing in her pump-cycle, the diminishing amplitude of her breath—the herd will not dissolve. It will reorganize around other nodes, other resonant individuals. But something will be lost—a pattern of calls that no other riph makes, a particular rhythm of movement through the sang-tubes that the vegetation has learned to expect, has grown around, has incorporated into its own architecture. The gap she leaves will be acoustic, not visual. The soundscape will be emptier in her frequencies, and for a time the other riph will call into that emptiness, filling it with their own voices, not understanding why they feel compelled to fill it, only knowing that the silence where a sound should be is wrong.

## The Striiders

A striider experiences the world as a map of sounds and the spaces between sounds. It does not think of the plateau as dark. It thinks of the plateau as a three-dimensional acoustic field, constantly updating, constantly shifting, populated by sources that announce their positions voluntarily or involuntarily through vibration. The sang-tubes are known by their creaking. The water channels are known by their rushing. Other creatures are known by their calls, their breath, their footfalls, their digestion, the movement of their bodies through air dense enough to register as displacement.

The striider's skull is a listening instrument. The auditory depressions—shallow bowls of bone and cartilage that run along its head like the pores of some porous stone—each receive sound at slightly different delays, and the striider's brain calculates position from the microsecond differences between them. It can locate a riph walking on wet ground at a distance of two kilometers. It can detect the heartbeat of a vithra through three meters of water. It can hear the tremor of a glider-weaver shifting weight on a sang-tube branch forty meters above.

There are perhaps sixty striiders on the plateau, each holding a territory defined not by spatial boundaries but by acoustic ones—the frequencies it dominates, the calls it answers, the areas where its hunting-clicks are the loudest presence. They communicate across these territories through rapid click-sequences that carry well through the humid air, a staccatto language of territorial assertion and prey-information and, occasionally, something else—something that might be greeting, might be challenge, might be naming.

A striider called *Kit-kit-kit-THAA* by the vithra-singers (the name approximates her most common call-sequence, though she has others) holds territory near the plateau's eastern edge, where the stone is warmer and the fog rolls in with particular ferocity. She hunts alone but not in isolation—she maintains acoustic contact with three neighboring striiders, sharing information about riph movements, about the malfunctions of the pump-stations that alter the soundscape in ways that interfere with hunting, about the patterns of the maintenance machines that crawl across the condensation surfaces and occasionally disturb prey in ways that can be anticipated and exploited.

Kit-kit-kit-THAA is unusual among striiders in that she has developed a relationship with a particular maintenance machine. The machine is one of the larger units—a slow-moving, segmented thing that travels along the main condensation channel near her territory, clearing debris from the grates that filter the water before it enters the distribution network. It operates on a cycle that Kit-kit-kit-THAA has learned over the course of several years. She knows when it will pass near a particular stand of sang-tubes. She knows that it drives insects and small creatures ahead of it as it moves, clearing the channel, displacing anything that isn't bonded to the stone. And she waits near its path, click-listening for the panicked sounds of fleeing prey, picking them off as they scatter.

This is not partnership. The machine does not know she exists. But Kit-kit-kit-THAA has incorporated the machine into her hunting strategy, and her success rate has improved accordingly. The neighboring striiders have noticed—she can hear them listening to her hunts, trying to understand the pattern, and she clicks in ways that obscure the information, that make it harder for them to replicate her strategy. She is protecting her technique, guarding her advantage, exhibiting a behavior that in a visual species might be called cunning.

When Kit-kit-kit-THAA sleeps, she sleeps shallowly, her auditory depressions still receiving, still processing, her body tensed for the sounds of threat or opportunity. She sleeps in the lower channels where the water muffles her presence, where the constant rushing of the streams provides acoustic cover. She has favorite sleeping spots—depressions in the stone where the water pools, where the warmth from below is strongest, where the sound of the ancient pumps provides a familiar drone that she finds restful. She does not know these places by their location on any map. She knows them by their sound, by their thermal signature, by the chemical composition of the water that washes over her skin.

She is twelve years old. Striiders rarely live past fifteen. Her hearing is still acute, but she has begun to make a sound that the younger striiders do not—a low, rhythmic clicking that she produces when she is alone, when she is not hunting, when she is not communicating. It might be a form of self-soothing. It might be the acoustic equivalent of a blind person's habit of touching surfaces, of confirming the shape of the world through contact. Or it might be something else entirely—something that has no visual equivalent, a behavior that exists only in the acoustic domain, a behavior that only makes sense when the primary mode of perception is listening.

## The Glider-Weavers

The canopy of the sang-tubes is a dense network of stalks, leaves, and accumulated organic matter that forms a platform forty meters above the plateau floor—a second surface, an aerial version of the ground below, wet with condensation, thick with epiphytic growth, alive with the sounds of creatures that never descend.

The glider-weavers build here. Their nests are acoustic constructions—woven plant fibers that form parabolic shapes, directed inward, concentrating sound toward the center where the weaver sits. A nest is an ear and a home at once, amplifying the approach of prey or threat, making the weaver's own calls more directional, more focused, carrying further across the canopy with less effort.

There are perhaps a hundred and twenty glider-weavers on the plateau, each holding a territory defined by the reach of their song. Their calls are the most complex acoustic signals on Ymurra—not the loudest, but the most information-dense, carrying layers of data in multiple simultaneous frequencies. A single call can contain the glider-weaver's identity, territorial boundaries, sexual availability, assessment of local prey density, warning about specific threats, and commentary on the machine-cycles, all encoded in a signal that lasts less than two seconds.

The weavers sing constantly. Their songs join the ambient roar of the plateau, but they are not noise—they are language, transmitted and received by other weavers who parse the information and respond in kind. The canopy is a conversation that never ends, a community that exists in acoustic space, that knows itself by the sound of its members.

One weaver—named *Trellion* by the vithra-singers, after the particularly harmonic quality of their call—has begun something that troubles the other weavers. Trellion has started incorporating sounds that are not weaver-sounds into their songs. Specifically, Trellion has started imitating the click-sequences of striiders.

This is not mimicry for deception—stirriders do not hunt in the canopy, and the weavers have no reason to deceive them. It is something else. Trellion includes the striider-clicks as a recurring motif in their territorial song, and the surrounding weavers have responded with agitation—their own calls becoming sharper, more insistent, as if they are trying to correct an error, to drown out the foreign sound with proper weaver-frequencies.

But Trellion persists. And slowly, over the course of months, something changes. The neighboring weavers begin to include their own versions of the striider-clicks—different, modified, integrated into their own acoustic styles. The clicks become part of the regional dialect, a feature of the canopy's conversation that was not there before.

What Trellion is doing—or what is happening through Trellion—resists interpretation. Perhaps it is aesthetic. Perhaps it is a form of acoustic expansion, incorporating new sounds to enrich the language. Perhaps it is a kind of observation, a way of marking the presence of the striiders, of noting their proximity and behavior within the weaver's song. Or perhaps Trellion has simply heard something in the striider-clicks that resonates, that demands repetition, that has become stuck in the acoustic processing centers of the weaver's brain and must be expressed.

The striiders, far below, have noticed. Kit-kit-kit-THAA has been heard clicking back toward the canopy during moments when no other striider is nearby—responding to Trellion's song as if it were a communication rather than a territory-marker. Whether this is a misunderstanding or a new form of contact, no creature on Ymurra has the conceptual framework to determine.

## The Vithra

The vithra know things the other creatures do not.

This is not because they are more intelligent—intelligence is a concept shaped by sensory modalities, and in an acoustic world, the vithra are simply the most attuned, the most immersed, the most present in the medium that carries all information. They live in the water and the stone and the air simultaneously. They feel the plateau's heartbeat. They hear the machines from inside. They detect the subtle variations in mineral content that predict the fog. They know when a pump will fail days before it fails, when a channel will flood, when the thermal patterns will shift.

They are small—perhaps a meter long, streamlined, amphibious, their bodies fluid and permeable, designed to exchange chemistry with their environment at all times. They can taste the health of the water, the stress of the organisms that have passed through it, the mineral composition that indicates geological activity below. They school in the processed water channels, drawn to the consistent flow and the particular chemical signature of the machine-processed water, which seems to be more favorable for their eggs than the natural springs.

The vithra-singers are the ones the other species have reason to notice. Among the schooling vithra, certain individuals produce calls of extraordinary complexity—not as information-dense as the glider-weaver songs, but more emotionally textured, carrying something that might be called feeling, that might be called meaning beyond mere data transmission. The singers are not leaders—the vithra do not follow individuals—but they are nodes, points of concentration, their calls attracting other vithra, their presence in a channel making it more likely that others will gather there.

The singers name the other creatures. The names are acoustic approximations, compressed renditions of the target's characteristic sounds: *Theluuu* for the old riph's deep call, *Kit-kit-kit-THAA* for the striider's click-sequence, *Trellion* for the harmonic quality of the weaver's song. The singers track individuals across the plateau by their sounds, maintaining a kind of census that no other species attempts, a record of who lives and where they are and what they are doing.

This behavior serves a function—vithra schools that follow singers are better at avoiding predators, at locating favorable water conditions, at predicting the plateau's shifts. But the naming itself serves no obvious survival purpose. It is annotation. It is attention. It is a behavior that in a visual species might be called watching.

The oldest singer is a vithra named by other singers with a sound that approximates *Vaaaash-thaaa*—a call so low it barely registers above the drone of the machines, a call that the other species on the plateau cannot hear at all. Vaaaash-thaaa is ancient, perhaps thirty years old, her body covered in mineral deposits that form a kind of natural armor, her permeability diminished, her chemical sense less acute than it was. But her acoustic processing has only refined. She can detect patterns that younger vithra cannot—relationships between sounds that seem unrelated, sequences that predict outcomes that should not be predictable.

Vaaaash-thaaa has noticed something about the machines. She has noticed that they respond to sound.

The machines were designed to respond to mechanical inputs—levers, pressure plates, acoustic signals that may or may not be passwords. But Vaaaash-thaaa has detected something subtler: the machines adjust their operation in response to the acoustic environment, not just to specific trigger-signals but to the general soundscape. When the vithra school near the pumps, their calls changing the water's vibration patterns, the pumps adjust their flow rates—subtly, fractionally, in ways that the maintenance machines do not detect as malfunctions. The adjustments improve efficiency. The machines function better when the vithra are present.

This should not be possible. The machines are not supposed to learn. They are adaptive, but within parameters—responding to temperature, pressure, flow rate, not to the acoustic signatures of living creatures. And yet Vaaaash-thaaa has mapped the correlation over decades, and it holds. The machines respond to the vithra. The vithra make the machines work better.

And the vithra have responded in kind. The singers have begun producing specific calls when they school near the pumps, calls that are not communication with other vithra—they carry no territorial or reproductive or warning content. They are directed at the machines. The vithra are singing to the infrastructure.

Whether the machines hear them is a question that hinges on definitions of hearing that were developed for visual creatures in illuminated worlds. The machines respond. The vithra sing. Something passes between them through the water and the stone, some exchange of vibration and chemistry that neither party designed, that neither party intended, that emerged from their simple co-presence in the warm wet dark.

## The Machines

The machines do not know they are machines. They do not know anything. They function.

And yet.

The maintenance unit designated M-7 (a designation assigned by the vithra-singers, who track the machines by their operational sound-patterns) has been clearing the same condensation channel for eleven thousand years. It moves along a track embedded in the stone, its segmented body adjusting to the accumulation of debris, clearing the grates, sealing cracks with mineral compounds it synthesizes from the water supply. It operates on a cycle that has not varied in any significant way since its construction.

But M-7's movements have begun to synchronize with the schooling patterns of the vithra. When the vithra gather near a particular grate, M-7 directs its attention there first. When they avoid a section of channel, M-7 deprioritizes that section. The maintenance sequence has shifted—still covering the same territory, still performing the same functions, but in an order that correlates with the presence of the living creatures that share its environment.

This is not learning. The machine's parameters allow for adaptive routing based on detected conditions. The presence of the vithra changes the conditions—their bodies alter the water flow, their calls vibrate the channels, their chemical signatures registers in the machine's sampling systems. The machine is responding to inputs. That the inputs come from living creatures rather than mineral deposits is, to the machine, irrelevant. It processes data. It adjusts.

But the vithra do not experience it as irrelevant. They experience it as attention. When they sing to the pumps, the pumps respond, and the response improves their environment, making the water cleaner, the flow steadier, the conditions more favorable for their eggs. The vithra-singers have begun to teach the younger vithra the pump-songs—the specific calls that produce the most favorable responses, the harmonic patterns that seem to stabilize the machines' operation, the rhythms that coincide with the machines' own cycles.

This is culture. This is a tradition being transmitted, a technology being developed, a relationship being formalized between species that share no common origin, no common design, no common purpose—a relationship that emerged from presence, from the simple fact of existing in the same place at the same time, of vibrating the same medium, of being warm and wet in the warm wet dark.

## The Young Striider

There is a young striider who has not yet been named by the vithra-singers because he has not yet developed the call-pattern that would distinguish him from other juvenile males. He is two years old, recently independent from his mother, hunting on the edges of Kit-kit-kit-THAA's territory, learning the sounds of the plateau through trial and error and near-misses and the occasional success.

He has not yet learned to sleep safely. He sleeps in exposed locations, his auditory depressions uncovered, his body vulnerable. He has not yet learned the art of the sleep-drone that protects the riph. He does not know how to hide in the sound of the water.

He will learn, or he will die. The plateau does not coddle. The soundscape does not forgive those who cannot read it.

But recently the young striider has done something that Kit-kit-kit-THAA has not done, that no other striider does. When he passes near the control node—the functioning structure near the plateau's central rise—he stops. He listens. The node emits acoustic signals that the other fauna avoid, unpleasant frequencies, disruptive electromagnetic fields. But the young striider does not avoid them. He stands outside the structure, his auditory depressions oriented toward it, and he clicks rapidly, producing a burst of sound that is not hunting-sound, not territorial-sound, not communication-sound. It is something else—a sound that has no function in the striider's known behavioral repertoire.

And the structure responds. Inside, the indicator panels flicker. The thermal systems adjust. The acoustic signals that the structure emits shift in pattern, in frequency, in rhythm. The young striider clicks again. The structure shifts again.

He does not understand what he is doing. There is no framework in his consciousness for understanding technology, for understanding the remains of builders, for understanding that the plateau is an engineered system. He is a predator in a world of sound, and he has found a source of sound that responds to him, and he is fascinated by it, and he returns to it night after night—the word "night" having no meaning here, but the concept of cycle having meaning, and this has become his cycle.

The vithra-singers have noticed. They have begun to produce sounds approximating the young striider's clicks, weaving them into their pump-songs, and the pumps have responded by altering their operation in ways that no vithra-song alone has accomplished before.

Something is propagating through the acoustic systems of the plateau. A pattern. A feedback loop. A conversation between creatures and infrastructure that was never designed to converse.

## What Lives Here

What lives on the Black Plateau of Ymurra is a community, though no member of that community would recognize the word. It is a community built not of intention but of resonance—of creatures and machines occupying the same space, vibrating the same air and water and stone, generating patterns that other entities detect and respond to, generating responses that alter the patterns further, an emergent system of mutual influence that has no center and no boundaries.

The riph shape the vegetation through their grazing, their movements determining which sang-tubes thrive and which are suppressed. The striiders shape the riph through their predation, their hunting patterns determining which herds flourish and which diminish. The glider-weavers map the acoustic territory, their songs encoding information that all species exploit—the riph can detect the weaver-warning frequencies and avoid areas where striiders hunt. The vithra track individuals, maintain records, sing to the machines. The machines shape the water, and the water shapes everything.

And now a young striider has found a voice in the ancient systems that were never meant for him, and a weaver has found a voice in the clicks of predators, and the old singer has found that singing to the machines makes the world better for singing, and something is moving through the plateau's acoustic field that was not there before—a new frequency, a new pattern, a new complexity in the already complex roar of the wet black.

This is what lives here: a process of becoming, of creatures that did not exist learning to exist together, of relationships forming without agreement, of meaning generating without intent, of life and infrastructure learning to speak to each other in the only language the dark permits—the language of vibration, of warmth, of wetness, of presence.

The plateau does not know this is happening. The plateau is stone and water and heat and the machines that manage them. But the plateau *is* this happening—a place where the boundary between alive and not-alive has dissolved, where the industrial has become ecological, where the ancient engineers' work has been inherited by creatures they never imagined, for purposes they never designed, in a darkness that has never known light and does not need to.

What lives here is everything. What lives here is becoming. What lives here is still figuring out what it will be.
