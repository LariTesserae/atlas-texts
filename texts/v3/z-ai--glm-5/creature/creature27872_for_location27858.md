# What Lives on the Stillness Plateau

---

The first thing to understand is that "lives" is the wrong word, but it is the only word available, and so it will have to do the work of several ideas it was never built to carry.

---

## The Standing Ones

They do not have a name for themselves. Naming requires a relationship between speaker and spoken, and the creatures of the plateau do not speak, do not hear, do not relate. They are addressed only by the place itself, and the place addresses them the way stone addresses the ground it rests on—by weight, by contact, by the simple fact of being there.

There are forty-three thousand of them. Not approximately. Not roughly. Forty-three thousand. The number does not change. It has not changed for longer than the plateau has been flat, which is to say for longer than the concept of "longer" has any purchase. They were forty-three thousand when the mountains stood, and the mountains have been gone for an amount of time that would make the oceans feel young, and the number is still forty-three thousand, and it is still exactly right.

If you could count them—which you cannot, because the light does not cooperate and the horizon folds and some of them are indistinguishable from the stone towers until you are close enough to see the faint fog of breath above their mouths—you would reach forty-three thousand and then stop, because there are no more to count. But if you counted again the next day, you might find a different forty-three thousand. Not new creatures. The same creatures, rearranged. Not moved—nothing moves here. But the counting would find them in a different order, in a different distribution, as if the plateau had shifted beneath them while they stood, repositioning them the way a child repositions pieces on a game board, not by picking them up but by tilting the board.

This is not something that happens. It is something that has always been happening.

---

## What They Are Not

They are not animals. Animals respond. Animals react. Animals exist in the space between stimulus and response, and that space is called behavior, and behavior is the alphabet of biological meaning. The standing ones have no behavior. They have posture. They have attitude, in the physical sense—the angle of a body in relation to the ground, the orientation of a head, the set of limbs. But these attitudes do not change. A creature that stands with its head turned to the east has stood with its head turned to the east since before east was a direction that meant anything on this plateau, and it will stand that way for longer than the direction will matter.

They are not statues. Statues are made. They are representations, imitations, the work of hands that looked at a living thing and said: *this, but still*. The standing ones were not made. They were not carved, not cast, not modeled. They did not begin as something else and become this. They emerged the way minerals emerge from solution—not through construction but through precipitation, the slow coming-together of what was already present, the moment when concentration exceeds solubility and something solid appears where nothing solid was.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts are the residue of the living, the print left by a hand that has moved on. The standing ones have no living counterpart. There is no moving version of them somewhere else, no original whose shadow they cast. They are not echoes. They are the sound itself, sustained past the point where sound should have faded, held at the threshold of audibility by a force that is not force but something more like insistence.

They are not gods. Gods require worship, or at least attention. The standing ones do not attend and are not attended. They are not aware. Awareness implies a distinction between self and not-self, and the standing ones do not draw that line. They do not know they are here. They do not know they are. They simply are here, and the being-here is enough, and it is everything, and it is all that they do.

## The Variety

No two are alike. This is the first thing you notice when you look long enough—long enough for your eyes to stop searching for movement and start searching for form. The variety is not the variety of a species, where differences cluster around a mean and variation is a statistical distribution. It is the variety of a vocabulary, where each word is distinct and none are synonyms and the collection of them constitutes a language that no one speaks.

There are creatures that resemble deer, if deer had been designed by someone who understood the concept of a deer but had never seen one—four legs, a body, a neck, a head, but the proportions are wrong in ways that are difficult to articulate. The legs are too long or too short. The neck curves at an angle that a living deer's neck could not curve. The head is shaped like a deer's head but the eyes are placed as if the skull beneath has a different geometry, and the eyes themselves are not deer eyes. They are wet and dark and very old, and they look at nothing with an intensity that is not attention but something more like sediment—layer upon layer of looking accumulated until the looking itself has weight.

There are creatures that are serpentine, coiled in spirals so precise they appear mathematical. The scales are small and regular and catch the bone-white light in ways that make the coils seem to turn, though they do not turn. Some of these are large—three meters across, the spiral a flat disc on the crust—and some are small, no wider than a finger, coiled on top of a stone or beside a larger creature as if they had been placed there by a hand. The large ones and the small ones are the same kind of thing. Scale is the only difference. As if the plateau produces them at whatever size the local space requires, filling the available gaps with precisely sized bodies.

There are creatures that are masses of fur. They have no limbs, no heads, no orifices, no features of any kind. They are dense pillows of fur—the color of dust, the color of ash, the color of bone—and they sit on the crust like things left behind, and they breathe. The breathing is visible: the fur rises and falls in a slow tide, a cycle so long that you must watch for minutes to see the crest and hours to see the trough. If you pressed your hand against one—and you should not—it would be warm. Not warm the way a body is warm, but warm the way a stone in the sun is warm: the heat of something that has absorbed energy and is releasing it slowly, without metabolism, without intent.

There are creatures with carapaces—things that might be called arthropodal if arthropods had never lived. They are low and flat and jointed, their bodies segmented into plates that overlap with the precision of roof tiles. Some of them have legs, but the legs are not for walking. The legs end in points that rest on the crust, and the points have pressed small indentations into the surface, and the indentations are exactly as deep as they have always been, because nothing has ever pressed deeper, because the legs have never borne more weight, because the creatures have never moved.

There are creatures that are feathered. The feathers are not the feathers of any bird—they are long and flat and stiff, more like blades than like plumage, and they stand out from the body at angles that suggest they were arranged by something that understood geometry but not aerodynamics. The feathered creatures are among the tallest. Some stand two meters, some three, their feather-blades catching the light and casting shadows that are sharp and clean and do not waver because there is no wind to make them waver.

There are creatures that are not easily categorized at all. Things that have the quality of bodies without having the architecture of bodies—shapes that suggest torsos without limbs, or limbs without torsos, or heads without necks, or necks without heads. Things that might be the debris of a creation that proceeded by trial and error, and the errors were not discarded but simply set down, and the setting-down became permanent, and now they stand here alongside the more coherent forms, breathing the same slow breath, wet-eyed with the same impossible moisture.

And there are creatures that are so far from recognizable that the eye slides over them the way the eye slides over optical illusions—things that are present but that the mind refuses to process, shapes that exist in the space between categories, that are not animal or mineral or vegetable but something fourth, something that has no name because the name would require a concept and the concept does not exist yet and may never exist because the kind of mind that would form it has not been built and may not be buildable.

All of them breathe. This is the unifying trait, if there is one. Not one of them is without breath. The breath is slow, but it is real, and it is the only process that occurs here. The chest rises. The body expands. The air—if it is air—moves into whatever cavity the body contains, and it stays there for a long time, and then it leaves, and the body falls, and the cycle begins again. The breath is the clock of the plateau. It is the only timer. And it keeps no time, because it has no rhythm—each creature breathes at its own pace, and the paces do not synchronize, and the result is not a rhythm but a texture, a wash of respiration that fills the silence the way static fills a dead channel.

## The Eyes

The eyes deserve their own attention.

Every creature has them. Even the ones without heads—even the fur masses, even the unclassifiable shapes—have eyes. The eyes are placed wherever the body has a surface, and they are always open, and they are always wet.

The wetness is the miracle of the plateau, if miracles can be boring, which here they can. There is no water. There is no moisture. The air is a desert. The crust is salt and dust. And yet the eyes are wet—not damp, not merely moist, but wet, visibly wet, the surface of each eye gleaming with a fluid that has no business existing here.

The fluid does not dry. It does not evaporate. It does not crust or salt or diminish. It is maintained at a constant level, a permanent film, as if the eyes are not producing tears but are themselves made of tears, are solidified weeping, are the idea of wetness given form and placed in a socket and aimed at the world.

The eyes do not see. This is clear. They do not track, do not focus, do not respond to changes in light or movement or the presence of other bodies. They are open and they are wet and they look at the world, but they do not take the world in. They are not windows. They are not lenses. They are mirrors, but not in the sense of reflecting—they are mirrors in the sense of being surfaces that face outward, that present a face to what is in front of them without absorbing any of it.

If you stood in front of one of the creatures and looked into its eyes, you would see yourself, but the self you see would not be you. It would be a version of you that has been standing there for a very long time, that has always been standing there, that was standing there before you arrived and will be standing there after you leave. The eyes do not reflect the present. They reflect the permanent.

Some of the creatures have many eyes. Some have dozens—rows and clusters of wet dark surfaces that cover the head or the body or the limbs, that overlap and crowd and compete for space. Some have only one. One eye, centered, enormous, taking up most of the face, the pupil a black hole in the wet that seems to go deeper than the skull could possibly allow. Some have eyes that are not where eyes should be—on the back of the head, on the palms of the hands (if they have hands), on the soles of the feet (if they have feet), on the interior surfaces of the body, visible only when the body shifts in its slow breathing and a gap opens and the wet gleam catches the light for a moment before the body settles and the gap closes.

The eyes are the only feature that is universal. Whatever else a creature is or is not, it has eyes, and the eyes are wet, and the eyes are open. It is tempting to say that the eyes are the point—that the creatures are here to look, that they are witnesses, that the plateau is a courtroom and they are the jury. But this implies purpose, and the plateau does not deal in purpose. The eyes are here because the eyes are here. They are wet because they are wet. They are open because they are open. The explanation is the description.

## The Bodies Among the Living

Some of them do not breathe.

You will not notice this immediately. You will walk among them—or you would walk among them, if you were here, which you are not, but if you were—and you would see the standing, the crouching, the perching, and you would see the slow rise and fall of breath, and you would think: they are alive. And then you would look at one and you would not see the rise and fall, and you would look closer, and you would see that this one is still in a way that the others are not. The others are still and breathing. This one is still and not breathing. The difference is invisible at a distance and unmistakable up close.

These are the bodies. Not corpses—corpse implies a transition, a moment of death, a before and after. These bodies have no before. They were always not-breathing. They are as permanent as the breathing ones, as present, as dense. They do not decay. Nothing decays here. They simply occupy space in a slightly different mode—the mode of not-breathing rather than the mode of breathing, the mode of absolute stillness rather than the mode of near-absolute stillness.

The bodies are distributed among the living without pattern. A breathing creature stands beside a not-breathing creature stands beside a breathing creature. Sometimes a body leans against a living one, and the living one does not react, does not shift, does not acknowledge. Sometimes a body has fallen—or was placed, or was always lying—and it lies on the crust and the dust has settled over it and the crust has begun to form around its edges, and the body is becoming part of the ground, but slowly, so slowly that the body will still be recognizable when the ground around it has been replaced a hundred times.

The bodies are not dead. They are not alive. They are the plateau's third category, the category that the binary of living and dead does not account for. They are present without process. They are there without being. If the breathing ones are monuments to patience, the bodies are monuments to the idea of patience itself—the abstract version, the version that does not require a patient, only the concept of one.

## The Empty Center

There is a place where they do not stand.

It is not a small place. It is a circle, perhaps a kilometer across, perhaps more—the edges are not sharp, and the transition is gradual, the density of creatures thinning as you approach it until there are none at all, and then there is only the crust, smooth and featureless, and the sky above, and the silence.

The creatures do not avoid this place. Avoidance implies awareness, and they are not aware. But they are not in it. They have never been in it. The circle is empty in a way that the rest of the plateau is not empty—it is not merely a gap but a refusal, a place where the plateau's logic of accumulation has been countermanded by something else, some rule that overrides Rule Four, some exception that has been carved out of the stillness by a force the other creatures cannot resist because they cannot resist anything, because they cannot do anything, because doing is not among their capacities.

If you stood in the center of the empty circle—if you walked to it and stood there—you would feel something. Not warmth. Not cold. Not pressure. Something else. A loosening. A release. The density that presses against you everywhere else on the plateau would be absent, and in its absence, you would feel the lack of it, the way you feel the lack of weight in a zero-gravity chamber, the way you feel the lack of sound in an anechoic chamber. The emptiness would be loud. It would be louder than the silence, which is already loud. It would be the loudest thing on the plateau, because it would be the silence of silence, the absence of the presence of the absence.

The creatures around the edge of the circle face outward. Not all of them—some face inward, toward the emptiness, their wet eyes aimed at the nothing. But the majority face away, and the ones that face inward seem older, more weathered, as if the emptiness has been eroding them, grain by grain, the way the wind eroded the mountains, and they are being worn down by the nothing, and one day they will be gone, and the circle will be larger by one body's width, and the plateau will have claimed a little more of itself for its own absence.

## The Newcomers

The plateau accumulates. Rule Four is clear: presence accumulates, and the sponge is never full.

Sometimes a creature appears that was not there before.

Not often. Not in any span of time that a human life would recognize. But over the deep time of the plateau—the time that pools, the time that does not flow—things arrive. They come from beyond the fold in the horizon, from the place where the sky bends down to touch the crust, from whatever lies outside the containment. They come walking, or crawling, or simply appearing, as if they have always been there and the plateau has only just noticed.

The newcomers are different from the originals. Not in kind—they are the same impossible variety, the same gallery of prototypes, the same mixture of feather and fur and scale and carapace. But they are newer. Their surfaces are less worn. Their eyes are wetter. Their breathing is faster—still slow by any external measure, but faster than the breathing of the originals, as if they have not yet adjusted to the plateau's tempo, as if they are still operating on the time of wherever they came from, and it will take them eons to slow down to the local speed.

The newcomers do not choose where to stand. They arrive at the periphery, at the edge of the fold, and they take their place among the others, and the place they take is the place that was waiting for them, the gap in the pattern that was shaped like them, the space that the plateau had been holding open since before the newcomer knew it existed. The fit is precise. Not snug—the newcomers do not touch their neighbors—but precise in the way that a word fits a sentence, the way a stone fits a wall, the way a note fits a chord. The plateau has been composing itself for a very long time, and the newcomers are the words it has been waiting for.

Once they arrive, they stop. Whatever motion brought them here—and it must have been motion, because they are here now and they were not here before—ceases. The walking stops. The crawling stops. The appearing completes. They take their final posture, and it is final, and they join the standing and the breathing and the waiting, and they are part of the forty-three thousand, and the number adjusts, and the number is still forty-three thousand, and it has always been forty-three thousand, even when it was forty-two thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine.

## The Question of What They Are

Here is where language begins to fail, and the failure is itself a kind of answer.

The standing ones are not animals. They are not plants. They are not minerals. They are not artifacts. They are not supernatural. They are not natural. They occupy a category that the human mind has not constructed, because the human mind constructs categories for the purpose of navigating a world of change, and the standing ones exist in a world where change is not a feature of the landscape.

If you needed to say what they are—and you do not need to, but if you did—you might say they are the plateau's awareness of itself. Not consciousness. Not thought. Not anything so dynamic. Awareness in the way that a mirror is aware of the room it reflects—passive, total, without interpretation. The plateau is a place. The standing ones are the place's knowledge that it is a place. They are the self-reference of geology, the place where the crust turns inward and looks at itself and says, in a language that has no words: *I am here.*

Or you might say they are the residue of something else. The plateau is old—older than life, older than the seas that became the salt on its surface, older than the mountains that became its flatness. Something was here before the plateau was a plateau. Something stood on the peaks of the cordillera and looked out at a younger world. The standing ones might be what is left of that something—not its bones, not its ghost, but its posture. The way a hand pressed against wet clay leaves an impression that survives the drying and the firing and the millennia in the ground. The standing ones might be the impressions of something that was here before and is no longer, and the plateau is the clay, and the firing is time, and the impression is all that remains.

Or you might say—and this is the hardest to say, and the closest to true—that they are not what lives here. They are the living. They are life itself, or the idea of life, or the possibility of life, separated from the machinery of life—the eating, the drinking, the reproducing, the dying—and left with only the essence, which is presence. They are what remains when you strip life of everything that makes it a process and keep only the state. Not being alive. Being. Just being. Being here. Being still. Being wet-eyed and open and breathing and waiting in a place where waiting has no object and breathing has no purpose and being has no end.

---

## What Lives Here

What lives on the Stillness Plateau is the Stillness Plateau.

The creatures are its organs. The crust is its skin. The sky is its skull. The salt is the residue of its thinking, the mineral deposits of its cognition, the salts that accumulate when a mind has been thinking the same thought for so long that the thought has crystallized.

What lives here is patience so old it has become a substance. Stillness so complete it has become a population. Presence so dense it has become a place.

Forty-three thousand bodies, breathing in a closed system, their eyes wet with the only water that has ever existed here, their stillness a choice that was never chosen, their waiting a destination that was never sought, their being the one fact in a world without facts, the single assertion in a sentence that was never spoken, the last thing standing in a place where standing is all there is.

They are what lives here. And they have been what lives here. And they will be what lives here. And the "have been" and the "will be" are the same as the "are," because time does not move here, and the creatures do not move here, and nothing changes, and nothing changes, and the eyes are wet, and the breath is slow, and the plateau holds it all, and holds it all, and holds it all.
