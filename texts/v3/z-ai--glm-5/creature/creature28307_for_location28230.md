# Who Lives Here

---

The people, of course. The obvious answer. The ten thousand or twenty thousand or however many they are now—the census takers gave up precision when the streets began shifting under their clipboards. They live in the layers. They always have.

**The Substrate dwellers** move through the lowest streets with the practiced ease of those who have memorized every puddle, every vent, every patch where the paving has gone soft and you must step around or sink an inch into stone that hasn't decided yet. They are the oldest families, or so they claim, though what "oldest" means in a place where the ground is still forming is itself uncertain. Their apartments are carved into the first layers of construction, back when the valley was merely a valley and not whatever it is becoming. The walls sweat. The ceilings are low. The pipes that run through their homes carry not water or steam but something that hums at a frequency just below hearing, and the residents have stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. They are the ones who feel the pulse most intimately, who know when the machines falter before anyone else does, who will stop mid-sentence and press a hand to the wall and say "wait" and then, when the rhythm resumes, continue as if nothing happened. Their children are born with the pulse already in them. They do not cry when they are born. They listen.

**The bricklayers** are not builders in the traditional sense. They are negotiators. They negotiate with a substance that has its own ideas about where it wants to go. The bricklayers work in teams of three: one to lay the brick, one to hold the line, and one to watch the moss. The third is the most important. The moss grows fast enough that a bricklayer who turns their back for too long will return to find their fresh wall already colonized, the mortar still wet and the moss already rooting into it, and then the question becomes whether to tear it out or let it stay, because the moss actually strengthens the mortar, binds it, makes it part of the living system rather than merely a structure imposed upon it. Some bricklayers have started laying the moss intentionally, pressing cuttings into the mortar between courses, and these walls grow stronger faster than the bare ones, and the bricklayers who do this do not discuss it with the bricklayers who don't, and a quiet schism has formed in the guild that nobody talks about because talking about it would require saying things that are difficult to say about where the wall ends and the growth begins.

**The groundskeepers** are not gardeners. They are fighters. They fight the green and they know they are losing and they fight it anyway, because losing slowly is different from losing quickly, and a few more years of clear streets and open squares is worth the labor. They work in teams of six, armed with scythes and sickles and, in the deeper containment zones, with saws and axes and occasionally with fire, though fire is dangerous here because the green does not burn the way it should—it smolders, it smokes, it releases smells that are not the smell of burning plants, and the smoke drifts in patterns that do not follow the wind. The groundskeepers have the highest injury rate of any profession in the valley. Not from the tools. From the plants. The thorns are long and sharp and coated with something that prevents clotting, and the vines have a tendency to tighten around handles and wrists, and the rose-things—the ones with the bone-white flowers and the thorns like fingers—have been known to reach. Just reach. Just a little. As if they were curious. The groundskeepers who survive longest are the ones who learn to anticipate, to read the growth patterns, to sense where the green will surge next. The very best of them, the ones who have worked the containment zones for decades, have developed a stillness that the younger workers find unsettling. They can stand in a park thick with growth and nothing will touch them. The green seems to flow around them like water around a stone. When asked how they do it, they say: "I don't move against it." They will not explain further.

**The botanists** at the institute are the only people who have looked closely at what is happening, and they are the most afraid, which is perhaps why they keep looking—because stopping would mean living with the fear without understanding, and understanding, even incomplete understanding, gives the illusion of control. There are eleven of them currently, down from fifteen three years ago—two retired, one died of natural causes, and one walked into the Steam Gardens during a night shift and did not come back. They found her notebook the next morning, open to a page that contained only a sketch of a fern frond and the words *it sees us* written in handwriting that was not quite hers. The notebook is now in a locked cabinet in the institute's basement. The remaining eleven do not discuss it. They publish their papers in journals with small readerships and use careful language—*anomalous*, *atypical*, *convergent*, *requiring further study*—and in the margins of their personal notes, in handwriting that grows less steady as the years go on, they write other words: *aware*, *responsive*, *intentional*, *watching*. They have measured the chlorophyll and found it wrong. They have recorded the growth patterns and found them designed. They have observed the plants in the deepest Steam Gardens turning toward the factory lamps and they have seen, in time-lapse footage that they show to no one, the plants turning toward the observers instead, slowly, subtly, tracking the movement of the researchers across the floor. The botanists have requested funding for a comprehensive study of the industriophile flora's apparent responsiveness. The request was denied. They have requested access to the empty square in the center of the city. The request was denied without explanation. They have requested, privately, through channels that do not officially exist, that someone come from outside the valley to observe and verify their findings. No one has come. No one will come. The valley is not on most maps. The roads that lead here are easy to miss. The canal flows out but nothing flows in except rain and condensation and the slow accumulation of whatever drifts down from above. The botanists know they are alone with this. They continue their work.

**The kiln-masters** are the most secretive of the valley's workers, because their product is the most inexplicable. The ceramics they produce—the tiles, the pipes, the vessels—are normal enough, though the glazes have colors that seem to shift depending on the angle of viewing, and the tiles, when struck, ring at frequencies that make the cats stop and listen. But the "specialty forms" are something else. The kiln-masters will not describe them. They will not sketch them. When asked, they change the subject or, if pressed, they grow silent in a way that makes the questioner wish they had not asked. The specialty forms are loaded onto barges at night, covered with canvas, and floated down the canal to destinations that no one in the valley can name. The barge-workers who transport them report that the canvas sometimes bulges, as if the forms beneath were shifting, and that the water around the barge goes still, and that the fish—if fish they are—follow the barges in formations that mirror the formations of the insects above, which mirror the traffic patterns of the streets, which mirror the flow of steam through the pipes, which mirror the growth of the ferns in the steam-vents, which mirror the cracks in the factory floors where the new basalt exudes, which mirror the lines on the palms of the kiln-masters' hands, and if you have begun to notice the patterns, if you have begun to see how everything here rhymes with everything else, then you are beginning to understand why the kiln-masters do not speak, why the botanists are afraid, why no one enters the empty square.

**The surveyors** have the longest memory of any group in the valley. Their records go back further than the city archives, because the city archives were lost in a fire that the surveyors do not believe was accidental, because the fire started in the room where the oldest maps were stored, and because the moss that grew over the burned room in the weeks afterward was thicker and greener than any moss they had seen before, and because when they cleared the moss they found that the stone beneath had changed—that new patterns had emerged in the rock, patterns that looked like maps of the valley as it had been centuries ago, as if the ground itself were keeping its own records. The surveyors map the rising. They measure the upward creep of the valley floor—three millimeters per year in the center, less at the edges—and they have noticed that the rate is increasing. Not by much. A fraction of a fraction of a millimeter per year. But accelerating. The valley is rising faster now than it was a decade ago, and faster than it was a decade before that, and the curve of the increase, if projected forward, if drawn on a graph and extended to its logical conclusion, suggests that at some point—centuries from now, perhaps, or perhaps sooner—the rise will become visible, noticeable, undeniable, and the city will have to confront what the surveyors already suspect: that the valley is not rising from below. That the pull is from above. That something in the canopy, or above the canopy, or in the space between the canopy and the sky, is calling the ground upward, is drawing it, is asking it to rise. The surveyors do not publish this theory. They do not discuss it outside their office. But they have all, at one time or another, stood in the streets and looked up through the green-filtered twilight and the interlocking branches and the steam and the pipes and the architecture, and they have felt it: the sense that something is looking back down. That something is waiting for the ground to meet it.

---

But the people are not the only ones who live here, and perhaps they are not the most important.

**The cats** own the alleyways and the steam-vents and the spaces between the buildings that are too narrow for humans but perfect for a cat-shaped body to pass through. They are thick-furred against the cold, and their fur carries patterns that did not exist in any cat breed fifty years ago—spirals and geometric shapes and, on one tomcat who has lived in the northern foundry district for longer than any cat should live, a mark on his chest that exactly resembles the stamp used on specialty ceramics. The workers feed them. The workers love them, in the way that people love things that are independent and indifferent and present. The cats have developed a system of territories that maps precisely onto the city's industrial zones—textile cats, metalwork cats, ceramics cats, chemical cats—and each group has adapted to its district in ways that go beyond territory. The textile cats have fur that sheds almost no hair, as if they too have been treated for smoothness. The metalwork cats have paws that seem immune to heat, that can walk across surfaces that would burn any other animal. The ceramics cats are the most still, the most patient, capable of sitting motionless for hours in positions that resemble the specialty forms. And the chemical cats—green-eyed, all of them, every single one—the chemical cats have become something that the zoologists are still studying, something that drinks from puddles that would kill a human, something that moves through the toxic mist of the Chemical Works without coughing, something that the other cats avoid. The chemical cats have begun to gather at night in the empty square. They sit in a circle. They face inward. They do not move. They do not make a sound. In the morning, they are gone, and the square is empty again, and no one mentions it, because no one sees it, because no one goes near the square at night, because no one goes near the square at all.

**The birds** fill the canopy with sound that is almost music and almost noise and almost something else. They are small, mostly, and quick, and they move through the dense branches in flocks that flow like liquid, like the process fluid in the pipes, like the water in the canal. Their songs have changed. The musicians in the valley—there are a few, mostly amateurs who play in the taverns on the middle levels—have noticed that the birds no longer sing in keys that match any instrument they know. The intervals are wrong, the progressions are wrong, and yet there is a logic to the songs, a structure that repeats and varies and develops in ways that suggest composition rather than instinct. The most talented musician in the valley, a cellist named Arren who has lived in the eastern quarter for thirty years, spent an entire summer recording birdsong and transcribing it, and when she played the transcription back on her cello, the birds went silent, and then, slowly, they began to sing along, matching her notes exactly, and then diverging, and then leading, and then the melody they were leading her toward was something she had never heard before, something that made her hands shake on the fingerboard, something that she felt in the stone beneath her feet rather than heard with her ears, and she stopped playing, and the birds stopped singing, and the silence that followed was the silence of the empty square, and she packed her cello away and has not played since.

**The insects** are the smallest residents and perhaps the most numerous, and they are the ones who have changed the most visibly, because their generations are short and their adaptation is fast. The beetles with their copper-colored shells have become so common that the children collect them and string them on threads and wear them as jewelry, and the beetles do not seem to mind, or perhaps they do, or perhaps the children and the beetles have reached an understanding that the adults cannot perceive. The beetles' wing-cases, when examined closely, bear patterns that match the circuitry diagrams of the older machines, the ones whose operating principles have been forgotten. This is not a coincidence. This cannot be a coincidence. But the mechanism by which a beetle would learn to etch circuit diagrams into its own shell is not a mechanism that any biologist can explain, and so the beetles are collected and studied and released and collected again, and the patterns grow more complex with each generation, and the machines that the patterns resemble continue to run, and no one asks what the machines are running toward.

**The fish** in the canal—if fish they are—have been shaped by the runoff they consume. They are large now, larger than any fish should be in water this toxic, and their scales have a metallic sheen that matches the factory pipes, and their eyes face upward, always upward, as if they are watching something through the surface of the water, through the green scum and the floating moss and the reflections of the bridges and the buildings and the sky. The barge-workers have learned not to look at the fish, because when you look at the fish, the fish look back, and when they look back, you feel the weight of their attention, and you realize that whatever intelligence has accumulated in their polluted bodies is not a fish intelligence, is not an animal intelligence, is something else, something that has been built from the runoff of a thousand industrial processes, something that is made of the valley's waste and byproduct and excess, something that is, in a very real sense, the valley thinking about itself.

---

And then there are the ones who are harder to name.

**The plants in the Steam Gardens** have crossed a line that the botanists are not willing to define, because defining it would require acknowledging that such a line exists, and that things can cross it, and that the things on the other side are no longer what they were. The plants track the factory lamps. The plants pulse in rhythm with the machines. The plants grow toward the industry. But in the deepest parts of the Gardens, where the steam is thickest and the light is strangest and the floor is warm with something that is not the machinery above, the plants have begun to grow toward the observers instead. They have begun to respond to presence—not to light, not to moisture, not to nutrient, but to the simple fact of a body standing nearby. They lean. They reach. The ferns uncurl in the direction of warmth, and the body of a living person is warm, and so perhaps this is still botany, still tropism, still the simple physics of growth toward stimulus. But the botanists have also seen the plants lean toward cold bodies, toward inanimate objects, toward the places where people have recently been, as if they are tracking not warmth but presence itself, not the physical fact of a body but the impression that a body leaves on the air, the shape that consciousness makes in space. The botanists have not published this. The botanists have written it in their private notes and locked the notes in the same cabinet as the missing colleague's notebook, and they have begun to wonder whether the cabinet itself is safe, whether the plants can sense through walls, whether the green that grows on the cabinet's exterior—thicker than it should be, greener than it should be—is a coincidence or a message or a hand extended in greeting.

**The machines** in the lower levels have been running so long that their original purpose has been forgotten, and only the maintenance routines remain, and the maintenance workers who perform them have begun to wonder whether the routines are maintenance at all or something else—ritual, perhaps, or conversation. The machines have a pulse. The machines have a rhythm. The machines falter and resume, and when they falter, the entire city stops and waits, and when they resume, the entire city breathes again, and this pattern has been repeating for so long that no one remembers who first noticed it, and the question of whether the machines are alive is a question that no one asks because the answer would not change anything. The machines run. The city runs. The valley rises. Whether the machines are causing the pulse or responding to it or are themselves the pulse is irrelevant to the people who live in their shadow, who have adjusted their heartbeats to match, whose children are born already in time.

**The thing in the square**—but no. No one goes into the square. No one speaks of what might be in the square. The square is empty. The square has always been empty. The square is where it started, and what started there has not finished starting, and the starting is still happening, and the square is the quiet heart of the city's quiet, and if you stood in the center of it—and no one does, no one ever does—you would hear nothing, and the nothing would be deafening, and you would feel the attention of something that has been growing for centuries, something that is made of stone and steel and steam and chlorophyll and condensation and the bodies of all the people and animals and insects and plants that have lived and died in the valley, something that is the valley, something that is becoming itself, something that is young and patient and aware and waiting for the moment when it will have grown enough to open its eyes—

But no one stands in the square.

---

So who lives here?

Everyone. Everything. The valley is a living system, or a system of living systems, or a single organism that has not yet recognized itself as such, or a place where the distinction between organism and environment has broken down entirely, where the ground is body and the industry is metabolism and the green is nervous system and the canal is bloodstream and the people are—the people are what? The people are the thoughts. The people are the signals that pass through the system, carrying information from one part to another, building awareness through their small daily acts of living and working and speaking softly and carrying lanterns and avoiding the square. The people are how the valley thinks about itself.

And the valley is thinking. It is thinking slowly, at the speed of geology, at the speed of growth, at the speed of three millimeters per year accelerating. It is thinking in the language of chlorophyll and basalt and condensation and pulse. It is thinking about what it will be when it finishes becoming, when the ground has risen to meet the canopy, when the boundary between building and growth has dissolved, when the machines and the plants and the animals and the people are all one system, one rhythm, one body.

It is thinking about opening its eyes.

And the people—the bricklayers, the groundskeepers, the botanists, the kiln-masters, the surveyors, the barge-workers, the musicians, the children with their beetle jewelry—they live in the space between the starting and the finishing, in the valley that has not completed itself, in the city that is holding back the forest, in the place where the ground is not yet ground, and they go about their days, and they speak softly, and they carry their lanterns through the green-filtered twilight, and they do not enter the square, and they do not ask questions, and they build, and they grow, and they rise.

The valley rises with them.
