# The Inhabitants

## The Builders

They are not what you would expect, if you came looking for architects. They are small. So small that a single individual could rest on the tip of a human finger and barely be felt. They are pale—translucent, almost—their bodies so thin that light passes through them, bent only slightly by the organs suspended inside like fruit in jelly. They have no eyes. They have no need for eyes. They have something better: they have the structure itself, which they can read with their entire bodies, sensing its thickness, its density, its needs.

They are called nothing, by anyone, because no one has ever named them. But if you watched them long enough—if you had the patience to observe a creature that moves so slowly it seems not to move at all—you would begin to understand what they are. They are the authors of everything. They are the ones who secrete the calcareous material that forms the great pillars and platforms and lattices. They do this individually, each one adding its tiny contribution—a film, a layer, a fraction of a millimeter—and collectively, across billions of individuals and millions of years, they have made the plateau what it is.

They look like nothing so much as small, soft blobs, their edges indistinct, their forms suggesting rather than defining. They have cilia that beat constantly, moving water over their surfaces, drawing in the dissolved minerals that they process into building material. They have glands that produce the calcium compounds they deposit beneath themselves, always building upward, always adding to what came before. They are born on the surface of the structure, they live their entire lives on the surface of the structure, and when they die, they become part of the structure, their own mineralized remains adding one more layer to the endless accumulation.

They communicate, after a fashion. Not with sound or light, but with chemicals—trails of signaling molecules that they leave behind them as they move, trails that other builders can detect and interpret. These trails say *here is good building*, or *here is erosion*, or *here needs repair*. They say *I was here* and *I am working* and *this is the way*. They form a map that is constantly being rewritten, a record of activity that fades over time but is always being added to. The structure itself is their library, their city, their body extended across time.

There are many kinds of builders. Some specialize in thick walls, producing dense material that forms the structural core of the architecture. Others specialize in surfaces, creating the smooth or pocked exteriors that face the open water. Still others produce the lattices—the delicate, porous sections that allow water to flow through, that create the chambers within chambers, the passages that wind and double back. Each kind has its own secretion, its own chemistry, its own way of contributing to the whole. They do not compete. They coordinate, though they have no coordination mechanism that any surface biologist would recognize. They simply build, and in building, they find each other.

The oldest among them—and "oldest" is relative, for their individual lives span mere years—are the ones who have found their way to the interior passages, the deep chambers where the water is still and the chemical signals of their kind have accumulated into something like history. They are larger than their kin, swollen with the minerals they have processed, their bodies almost entirely given over to the glands that produce building material. They will never return to the surface. They will continue to build inward, adding to the interior architecture, until their bodies can no longer sustain the effort and they settle into the floor they have made, becoming part of it, becoming ground for the generations that will follow.

## The Anchored Ones

There are creatures here that have given up movement entirely—or rather, they have given up movement as other creatures understand it. They attach themselves to the structure in their larval stage, and they remain attached for the rest of their lives. They are the filter-feeders, the waiters, the patient ones who let the water bring everything to them.

The crinoid-like animals are the most visible of these. They are not true crinoids, though they share the same basic body plan: a central body anchored to the substrate, and around it, a crown of feathery appendages that spread into the current. These appendages are covered in tiny cilia that catch particles of food—marine snow, organic debris, larvae too small to escape—and move them toward the mouth at the center. They glow faintly, each one producing its own dim light, a phosphorescence that outlines their forms against the darkness. Why they glow is unclear. They do not use light to communicate. They do not use light to attract prey. Perhaps it is a byproduct of some other process, some chemical reaction that serves a purpose unrelated to visibility. Perhaps it is simply something they do, a characteristic without a function, like the color of skin.

But they are not uniform, these anchored ones. There are many forms, many variations on the theme of stillness. Some have bodies like vases, wide and open at the top, their feeding appendages reduced to a ring of tentacles around the rim. Others are long and thin, stretching meters into the water, their appendages arranged in spirals that maximize surface area. Still others are colonial—a single larva that buds and divides and becomes many, growing into branching structures that look like trees or fans, each branch ending in a feeding polyp.

They are not passive, despite their stillness. They are constantly adjusting, reorienting their appendages to match the currents, withdrawing when a predator passes, expanding when food is abundant. They compete for space on the structure, overgrowing their neighbors, chemical warfare happening slowly, over months and years. They have preferences—some settle only on certain parts of the architecture, some only at certain heights, some only where the water moves at certain speeds. They are diverse beyond counting, and every surface of the plateau's structures is occupied by some form of anchored life.

There are the trap-makers among them, too—the ones that do not simply wait for food to drift into their appendages but actively capture it. They look like anemones but are not anemones; their tentacles are armed with nematocysts, stinging cells that can paralyze prey much larger than themselves. They are selective, each species evolved to capture specific prey, and they arrange themselves in patterns that suggest intention—though intention, here, is always a question. They might be responding to chemical cues left by their prey, positioning themselves where the prey will pass, or they might simply be where they are because the currents carried their larvae there, and they survived because they were lucky. The structure does not distinguish between intention and accident. It simply records the result.

And in the deepest chambers, where the water barely moves, there are the chemotrophs—the ones that have given up on food entirely, that feed on the minerals that seep through the ancient walls. They are the strangest of the anchored ones, because they have also given up on light, on movement, on everything that makes life recognizable to surface-dwelling eyes. They are pale, almost colorless, their bodies reduced to the minimum necessary to process the chemicals they need. They are small, clustered in patches where the mineral concentration is highest, and they grow so slowly that a single individual might add a millimeter to its height in a century. They are the most ancient form of life on the plateau, the ones that were here before the structure was tall, before the water was filled with drifting organic matter, when the plateau was young and the ecosystem was simple. They persist. They do not change. They do not need to.

## The Drifters

Not everything is anchored. Not everything stays still. There are creatures here that move, that swim, that crawl across the surfaces of the structure—graceful, in their way, though grace is a word that comes from a world of sunlight and gravity and air.

The glass-crustaceans are the most visible of the mobile ones. They are not true crustaceans—research has determined that much—but they fill the same niche, and their bodies follow similar plans: segmented, armored, with jointed limbs that move in precise rhythms. Their armor is transparent, or nearly so, made of a crystalline material that catches the dim light and fractures it into faint rainbows. Inside their bodies, visible through the clear shell, their organs pulse and move—their digestive systems processing the food they have gathered, their circulatory systems carrying whatever passes for blood, their nervous systems, if they can be called that, coordinating the movements of their limbs.

They are beautiful, these glass-crustaceans, in a way that has nothing to do with human aesthetics. Their limbs are ornamental, scrollwork-like, elaborately branched and fringed with setae that sense the water, that detect the chemical trails left by other creatures, that help them navigate the complex topography of the structure. They move in dances—patterns that seem choreographed, that involve multiple individuals moving in coordination, their paths intersecting and separating in ways that suggest communication. Perhaps they are communicating. Perhaps the dances are their language, their way of sharing information about food, about danger, about the state of the structure. Perhaps they are simply moving, and the patterns are the result of many individuals responding to the same cues. No one knows. The glass-crustaceans do not explain themselves.

They are not the only crawlers. There are other armored things, other segmented bodies, other jointed limbs. Some are small, barely visible, living in the interstices of the structure, in the tiny spaces between one deposited layer and the next. Some are large, their bodies spanning meters, their limbs thick and strong enough to crush smaller creatures. They are predators and scavengers, these crawling ones, feeding on the anchored filter-feeders, on the debris that drifts through the passages, on each other. They keep the ecosystem in balance, preventing any single species from overwhelming the others, and in death, they too become part of the structure, their armored shells settling onto the surfaces and slowly being incorporated, their minerals adding to the endless accumulation.

And there are swimmers—creatures that have left the surfaces entirely, that move through the open water between the pillars and platforms. They are the soft-bodied ones, the ones that flow. Their bodies have no armor, no rigid structures, nothing to protect them from the pressure of the water or the predators that hunt in the darkness. They do not need protection. They are fast, and they are adaptable, their bodies changing shape as they move, flowing through the water like jellyfish but with more direction, more purpose. Some of them have internal structures that provide support—struts of cartilage, meshes of fibrous tissue—but these are flexible, allowing the body to bend and twist and compress and expand. They are the children of the currents, shaped by the water they move through, and they move through it as if they were part of it.

The swarming things are among them—the creatures that move in clouds, that exist individually but seem to act collectively. They are small, each one no larger than a fingernail, but they gather in their thousands, their millions, forming masses that seem from a distance like single organisms. They synchronize their movements, turning and diving and rising together, their collective motion creating shapes that shift and reform constantly. They might be communicating—there are flashes of bioluminescence among them, brief pulses of light that ripple through the swarm—but if they are, the communication is too fast, too complex, too alien to be understood from outside. They feed on the particulates in the water, on the microorganisms that colonize the structure's surfaces, on whatever they can catch. They are prey for the larger swimmers, for the predators that move silently through the darkness, but they are also the predators of smaller things, their collective mouths filtering the water as efficiently as any anchored filter-feeder. They are both and neither. They are the swarm.

## The Predators

They are rarely seen, the large predators. They do not need to be seen. Their presence is felt in other ways—in the sudden silence that falls when they pass, in the way smaller creatures hide or freeze or flee, in the absence of movement that marks their hunting grounds.

The largest of them are the ribbon-wyrms, though that name is a convenience, not a truth. They are not worms, and they are not wyrm, if they were known, would mean something different in this place. They are long, longer than anything else that lives on the plateau, their bodies extending for tens of meters through the water, their forms so thin that they seem like ribbons, like streamers, like fabric caught in a current. They move with a grace that seems impossible, their entire bodies undulating in waves that propel them through the water, and they are fast—faster than anything that size has any right to be. They hunt by sensing the vibrations of other creatures, the pressure waves that movement creates in the water, and they strike with precision, their bodies wrapping around prey and constricting, their mouths—small, relative to their bodies, but equipped with rows of backward-curving teeth—pulling the prey inward to be digested.

They are not common, the ribbon-wyrms. The plateau cannot support many predators of that size. But they are always there, somewhere in the darkness, patrolling the edges of the structure, moving through the open water between pillars, their presence a constant pressure on the ecosystem. They keep the populations of swimmers in check. They prevent any single species from becoming too numerous. They maintain the balance, though "maintain" is perhaps the wrong word—it implies intention, and the ribbon-wyrms have no more intention than any other creature here. They hunt because they are hungry. They eat because they need to eat. The consequences of their actions are not their concern.

There are smaller predators, too—hunters that move through the chambers of the structure, that pursue prey through the passages and lattices. They are armored, many of them, protected from the creatures they hunt by thick shells and sharp spines. They have senses that the ribbon-wyrms lack—eyes, of a sort, adapted to the dim blue twilight, able to detect the bioluminescence of other creatures; chemical sensors that can follow trails through the water; pressure-sensitive organs that feel the movement of prey against the current. They are intelligent, after a fashion, capable of learning the layout of the structure, of remembering where prey tends to gather, of returning to successful hunting grounds. They are patient. They will wait for hours in a passage, motionless, their bodies pressed against the wall, until prey comes within reach. Then they strike, quickly, precisely, and return to waiting.

The trap-door hunters are among the most specialized. They have evolved to live within the structure itself, in chambers that they modify, enlarging the entrances and creating doors—actual doors, made of secreted material, that they can open and close. They wait inside, sensing the water that moves past their doors, and when something touches the trigger hairs they have extended outside, they open the door and grab whatever is there. They are ambush predators, entirely passive until the moment of attack, and they are successful enough that their modified chambers are common throughout the structure, visible as slight irregularities in the surface, as places where the water moves differently.

And there are the parasites—the creatures that do not kill their hosts but live on them, in them, taking what they need without ending the life that sustains them. They are small, often microscopic, and they are everywhere. They live in the gills of the swimmers, in the digestive tracts of the crawlers, on the surfaces of the anchored ones. They are part of the ecosystem too, though their role is harder to see. They weaken their hosts, make them more vulnerable to predators, reduce their ability to compete for food and space. They are a pressure, a constant drain, and they shape the evolution of everything else. The creatures of the plateau have evolved defenses—immune systems, cleaning behaviors, symbiotic relationships with other organisms that help keep the parasites in check. It is an arms race, fought across millions of years, and neither side has won. Neither side ever wins.

## The Symbionts

Not everything here is either builder or built-upon. There are creatures that have found other ways to live—ways that involve partnership, cooperation, mutual dependence so complete that the partners cannot survive without each other.

The cleaner-crawlers are the most visible of these. They are small crustacean-like animals that make their homes on the surfaces of the larger swimmers, crawling across their hosts' bodies, picking off parasites and debris and dead tissue. They are welcome, or at least tolerated—the swimmers do not eat them, do not shake them off, seem to recognize that they provide a service. They are not passive servants, either. They defend their hosts from other small creatures that might try to colonize them, fighting with their claws and their chemical weapons to maintain their territory. They have, in a sense, domesticated their hosts—they have trained them to be still while they work, to return to the same places where the cleaner-crawlers live, to seek them out when they need cleaning. It is a relationship that benefits both, though it is hard to say who benefits more.

There are other symbionts, deeper and stranger. There are the nitrogen-fixers, microorganisms that live in specialized organs inside the glass-crustaceans, processing nitrogen compounds that the crustaceans cannot process themselves, providing nutrients that the crustaceans cannot obtain from their food. The crustaceans provide a protected environment and a steady supply of raw materials; the nitrogen-fixers provide the products of their metabolism. Neither can live without the other. They are a single organism, in a sense, two species that have become one.

There are the light-makers, too—the bacteria that produce the bioluminescence that many of the plateau's creatures display. They live in special chambers within their hosts' bodies, fed and protected, reproducing when their hosts reproduce, passing from parent to offspring in ways that ensure the partnership continues. They are the reason the anchored ones glow, the reason the swarming things flash, the reason the darkness is never entirely dark. They produce light as a byproduct of their metabolism, and their hosts have evolved to use that light—for what purpose, no one can say for certain. Perhaps for communication. Perhaps for attraction of mates. Perhaps for nothing at all. But the partnership persists, because it works, because it has worked for millions of years, because neither partner has any reason to change.

And there are the deep symbionts—the organisms that have become so integrated with the builders that they are no longer distinguishable. They live inside the builder's cells, processing minerals, producing energy, carrying out functions that the builders could not carry out alone. They have been there so long that they have lost most of their own identity, their own ability to live independently. They are organelles now, part of the machinery of the cell, but they were once free-living creatures, something like bacteria, something like the nitrogen-fixers that still live outside cells. They were captured, long ago—millions of years ago, before the plateau was what it is—and they have been part of the builders ever since. They are the reason the builders can secrete the calcareous material that forms the structure. They are the reason the plateau exists.

## The Migrants

There are creatures that do not belong to the plateau, not entirely—creatures that come from elsewhere, that pass through on their way to somewhere else, that use the structure as a waystation, a landmark, a feeding ground.

They come from the depths below. The trenches that surround the plateau are not empty; they are full of life, life that has evolved in darkness and pressure and cold, life that exists on the chemical energy that seeps from the earth's interior. Some of this life comes up. The creatures that live in the trenches migrate vertically, following the chemical gradients, seeking the boundary where their world meets the world above. Some of them reach the plateau. Some of them stop here, finding food and space and conditions they can tolerate. They are strangers, these visitors, their bodies adapted to pressures and temperatures that the plateau's residents never experience. They do not stay long. They return to the depths, or they die, their bodies settling onto the structure and becoming part of it, their strange forms preserved in the calcareous layers, evidence of their passing for any who know how to read it.

They come from the waters above. The surface is far away—a different world entirely, a world of sunlight and warmth and storms—but the plateau is connected to it by the column of water that extends from the seafloor to the air. Things drift down from above: organic debris, dead bodies, larvae seeking a place to settle. Some of the larvae find that place on the plateau, settling on the structure and becoming part of the ecosystem. They are new, these settlers, their genetic material adding to the diversity of the plateau's life. They bring with them the heritage of the surface world, adaptations that may or may not be useful in the depths, traits that may succeed or fail. Some of them survive. Some of them thrive. Some of them become new species, given enough time, diverging from their surface ancestors, becoming something that belongs to the plateau and nowhere else.

And there are the vertical migrants, the creatures that move between worlds on a regular basis. They are few—the energy required to move through hundreds of meters of water is substantial, and the conditions change dramatically with depth—but they exist. They feed on the plateau, on the abundance of life that has accumulated here, and they return to the shallows to breed, to rest, to whatever it is they do when they are not feeding. They are the links between worlds, carrying nutrients and genetic material and parasites and who knows what else from one environment to another. They are rare, and they are important, and they are almost entirely unstudied. The plateau does not study them. The plateau simply is, and the migrants come and go, and the ecosystem continues.

## The Collective

It is tempting, when describing the inhabitants of the plateau, to list them one by one—to identify species, to classify forms, to organize the diversity of life into categories that can be named and counted and understood. But this approach misses something essential. The inhabitants of the plateau are not separate. They are not individuals living alongside other individuals, competing for resources in a world that is indifferent to their existence. They are a collective, a system, a single vast organism spread across species and generations and millions of years of time.

The builders build the structure. The structure provides habitat for the anchored ones. The anchored ones filter the water, removing particulates, making it cleaner for the swimmers. The swimmers eat the parasites that would otherwise overwhelm the anchored ones. The predators keep the populations in check, preventing any single species from overrunning the others. The symbionts provide services that their hosts cannot provide for themselves. The migrants bring new genetic material, new nutrients, new possibilities. The structure itself—the calcareous architecture that rises from the abyssal plain—is the product of all of this, the record of all of this, the physical manifestation of the relationships that connect every creature on the plateau.

There is no separation here between living and non-living, between organism and environment. The structure is made of the remains of creatures, and it is home to creatures, and it is built by creatures, and it is maintained by creatures, and it is eroded by creatures, and it is repaired by creatures. The creatures are made of the minerals they obtain from the structure and the water and each other. The minerals come from the earth below and the water above and the remains of creatures that have died. Everything is part of everything else. Everything is connected.

This is not metaphor. This is not poetry. This is simply how the plateau works. The creatures that live here have evolved to be part of this system, and the system has evolved to include them. Remove one piece, and the whole shifts, adjusts, finds a new equilibrium—because the system is resilient, because it has survived for millions of years, because it has weathered changes that would have destroyed anything less flexible, less interconnected, less profoundly alive.

The collective has no name, because names are for things that can be separated from other things. The collective has no purpose, because purposes are for things that can be distinguished from the activities they pursue. The collective simply is, and it has been for longer than any individual creature can remember, and it will continue to be for longer than any individual creature can imagine.

The inhabitants of the plateau are the plateau. The plateau is the inhabitants. There is no difference. There has never been any difference. There will never be any difference. This is the deepest truth of this place, the truth that takes the longest to learn, the truth that transforms everything once it is understood.

The water moves through the structure. The creatures move through the water. The minerals move through the creatures. The structure grows. The structure erodes. The structure grows again. The cycle continues. The inhabitants continue. The plateau continues.

It is enough. It has always been enough. It will always be enough.
