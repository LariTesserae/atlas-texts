# The Verdant Press

There are three of them. Or there were. The number has a way of becoming uncertain.

---

The one who is most clearly present is the person who tends the eastern press, the machine nearest the entrance that no longer functions as an entrance. She is old in the way the boardwalk is old—her original materials have been replaced so many times that only the pattern of her movement persists. She walks the path between the feed-chute and the output tray in steps that have worn a shallow groove in the bark-surface, a private tributary to the boardwalk's main channel. Her feet know the route. Her hands know the rollers. She has been doing this long enough that the distinction between her knowing and the machine's knowing has become academic.

She wears gloves. Everyone wears gloves here—the secretions of the inner bark surfaces will blister exposed skin after prolonged contact, and her gloves are the thick rubberized kind, industrial surplus, though the rubber has softened and warped in the humidity until it has taken on the texture of something grown. Her sleeves are long, rolled at the cuffs, the fabric a color that was perhaps once white but has become the same green-amber as the filtered light, as though the room has stained her into conformity. Her hair is short, practical, grey going white, and it holds the damp the way everything here holds the damp—without resistance, without complaint.

Her name is Maren. Or it was. The name has been worn smooth by use, the way the gears are worn smooth, and it fits her loosely now, a label that applies to her function more than her self. When she speaks—and she rarely speaks, the noise of the room making conversation an effort she has largely abandoned—her voice has the same quality as the water-sound: thin, steady, receding into the background. She says things like *that one's not dry yet* and *the sap's running slow today* and, once, to someone who may not have been there, *I remember when this was louder.* She does not elaborate on what she means by louder.

Maren does not own the room. She does not own the machines. She does not own the paper. The relationship is closer to tenancy, and even that word implies more separation than actually exists. She lives in the room the way the machines live in the room—installed, maintained, operating at the pace the system has established. She sleeps in a alcove formed where two root-walls meet, a space that has grown to accommodate her the way a tree grows around a fence post, not because it intends to but because the obstruction is persistent and the growth is patient. The alcove contains a pallet of pressed fiber—her own production, sheets from years ago that have begun to sprout at the edges, the white root-hairs threading into the fabric of her bedding until the bed has become semi-alive, a dull organism that breathes when she breathes, warm when she is warm, cool when she is absent. She finds this comfortable. She has stopped noticing it.

She eats what the room provides, which is not much. There are growths—fungal, not plant, taking advantage of the damp and the cellulose-rich substrate—that appear along the lower root-walls in pale shelves and brackets. Some are edible. Maren knows which ones. She has been eating them long enough that the knowledge has become tactile rather than cognitive; her hands select the right growths the way her feet find the right path. She supplements this with supplies she brings from outside, but *outside* is a word that has lost its force for her. She goes out, sometimes, to the settlement that exists beyond the canopy, but the journeys are becoming less frequent and shorter, and she returns with less and less, and the room takes more and more of the space in her attention, the way the root-walls take more and more of the space in the chamber, growing inward so slowly that the compression is only visible across decades.

Maren is not unhappy. The word does not apply. She is functioning. She is integrated. She is, in the most literal sense, a worker: she works, and the working has become the largest part of what she is. When the press runs, she is part of the press. When the paper sheets off the rollers, still warm, still faintly responsive, she catches them and stacks them and the stacking is a motion her body performs with the same autonomy as breathing. She has been doing this for—I don't know. She doesn't know. The room doesn't measure time in units that correspond to her memory. She has been doing this for long enough that she cannot clearly recall when she started, and the room has no interest in reminding her.

---

The second one is less certain.

There is evidence of him on the catwalks—the upper mechanisms require maintenance, and Maren does not climb. Someone does. The brass fittings on the highest roller assemblies show recent oil-stain, the kind of fresh mark that fades in this environment within a day. A handprint on the rail of the northern catwalk, five fingers visible in the grime, narrower than Maren's. He moves through the upper canopy where the light is thicker and the water falls more directly, and he adjusts valves and clears blockages in the sap-lines that feed the boilers, and he does this work in the hours when Maren is sleeping, or when she is focused on the lower machinery, or when the room's attention is elsewhere.

His name is—and here the room resists, the way it resists all certainty—Jonas. Or Tomas. Or something that was once a name and has been worn down by the acoustics of the space, the water-sound and the machine-rhythm eroding it into a collection of consonants and a vowel that could be several things. Maren has called him by name; he has answered. The name itself is not the point. The point is that he exists, or existed, or exists intermittently, a figure who occupies the upper reaches of the room and tends the systems that Maren cannot reach.

He is younger. Or he was younger. The evidence suggests someone who came to the room after Maren, who learned the upper mechanisms from her instruction or from the machines themselves, who has not yet been fully integrated but is in the process of integration. His gloves are newer. His sleeves are longer, as though he is still cautious about the room's secretions, still protecting skin that has not yet adapted. He moves differently than Maren moves—less certainty, more attention, the way someone walks when they are still learning the terrain. But he moves. He is present. He is becoming part of the system.

Sometimes, in the early hours of the cycle, when the light through the canopy is at its dimmest—though it never fully darkens, the bioluminescent lamps maintaining their pale vigil—you can hear two sets of footsteps on the boardwalk, two rhythms in the machine-noise, two bodies occupying the same humid space. They do not speak to each other during these hours. The noise of the room makes speech a gesture rather than a communication, and they have developed a system of gestures—hand signals, positions, the angle of a head—that conveys what needs conveying. *The feed is slow.* *The pressure is high.* *This sheet is ready.* The messages are simple because the work is simple, repetitive, the same six impressions per minute, the same cycle, the same rhythm.

He has begun to sleep in the room. Not in an alcove—not yet—but on the boardwalk itself, near the base of the eastern press, where the warmth from the boiler radiates through the iron frame and into the planking. He curls there, in the noise and the damp, and the room accepts his presence the way it accepts all persistent warmth: gradually, without comment, growing around him so slightly that neither he nor Maren would notice the adjustment. In a decade, perhaps, he will have an alcove. In two decades, the alcove will have grown to fit him. In three, he will not remember when he began to sleep there, or why.

---

The third is not a person. Or was a person. Or is what remains of a person after the room has finished with them.

In the western wall, where the root-mass is thickest and the water runs in the deepest channels, there is a shape. It is not a shape the room made. The growth has incorporated it—grown around it, grown through it, the root-fiber threading through fabric and bone with the same patient insistence it brings to everything it touches. The shape is human-sized, or was. It sits in a depression in the root-wall that might once have been an alcove, might once have been a station, might once have been a place where someone worked and slept and ate fungal shelves and wore gloves and walked the boardwalk and tended the machines.

Maren does not mention this shape. She walks past the western wall on her circuit and does not look at it. The room does not acknowledge it either, precisely—the growth has absorbed it the way the growth absorbs everything, without ceremony, without memorial. It is part of the wall now, the way a fence post becomes part of a tree, the wood growing around the obstruction until the obstruction is no longer an obstruction but a feature, an inclusion, a knot in the grain.

This was, perhaps, the first. Or a predecessor. Someone who was here before Maren, who tended the machines when the machines were newer and the room was younger and the growth had not yet established the patterns it would maintain for the next century. Someone who slept in an alcove and ate what the room provided and walked the same lines until the lines were worn into the bark and the feet were worn into the path and the person was worn into the room.

There is no horror in this. The room is not a predator. It is not patient because patience implies a goal being waited for; the room simply continues, and the continuation includes whatever is present, whatever is warm, whatever persists. The shape in the wall persisted. The room grew around the persistence. That is all. That is enough.

The paper the presses produce—the living paper, the sheets that sprout at the edges when left in the damp—carries the fibers of everything the room has touched. The root-mass draws from the whole of its substrate, and the substrate includes the shape in the western wall, the way it includes the boardwalk and the old bolt-holes and the remains of the lattice-work that trained the original cuttings. The paper is made of what the room is made of. The room is made of what the room has consumed. The distinction between growth and absorption is, in this place, purely a matter of timing.

---

So: three. Or two and a remainder. Or two and a becoming. The number depends on when you count and how you define *lives.*

Maren lives here. She works the eastern press and she sleeps in her alcove and she eats the fungal shelves and she walks the boardwalk and the room is, for her, the world—the room and the settlement beyond it are not separate places but a continuum, and she moves along the continuum with the same slow inevitability that the sap moves through the root-valves, going where the system directs, at the pace the system maintains.

The younger one lives here, or is learning to live here, or is being learned by the room. He climbs the catwalks and he tends the upper valves and he sleeps on the boardwalk near the eastern press and the room is growing into him the way it grows into everything that stays. He does not resist this. He may not know it is happening. Maren may know. Maren may have watched this process before, may have watched it several times, may understand that the room's equilibrium includes the replacement of its workers at a pace that feels, from inside the system, like the natural order of things.

And the shape in the wall. Who lived here. Who is now part of the room's substrate. Who contributes, through the slow alchemy of root and fiber and pressure, to the paper that sheets off the rollers and is stacked and carried away and printed on and read and discarded and left in the damp and sprouted and grown into new rooms, new chambers, new growth.

The room breathes. The presses turn. The water falls. Maren walks her circuit. The younger one climbs. The shape in the wall does nothing, which is also something, which is also a kind of living, if you use the word the way the room uses it—to mean *persisting,* to mean *remaining,* to mean *being present long enough to become part of the pattern.*

---

There are no others. No animals, no insects, no birds. The plants have made the space inhospitable to everything except what the room needs, and the room needs Maren and the younger one and the machines and the water and the sap and the slow cycle of growth and impression and growth. The room does not need more than this. The room has never needed more than this.

Outside, there is a settlement. There are other people. There is a world that continues in the way worlds continue—loudly, quickly, with events and changes and the accumulation of history. But from inside the Verdant Press, that world is a rumor. A distant light filtered through too many layers of canopy. The room is complete. The room is sufficient. The room has negotiated its terms with its inhabitants, and the inhabitants have accepted the terms, and the acceptance is so thorough that it no longer feels like acceptance but like nature.

Maren catches a sheet off the roller. It is warm. It is faintly damp. At its edges, already, the finest white threads are beginning to seek substrate. She stacks it with the others. She does not look at the shape in the western wall.

She doesn't need to. She knows it's there. She knows she will be there, eventually, in the way that a sheet of paper knows it will return to the pulp from which it came—not through knowledge, not through anticipation, but through the simple fact of what it is, and what the room is, and the agreement they have made without ever making it.

The press turns. The water falls. The noise continues. The room breathes.
