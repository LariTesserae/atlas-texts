# The Inhabitants of the Abyssal Plain of Constant Dissolution

## I. The Mats

Begin with what is always present. The bacterial mats do not live here so much as they *are* here—extensions of the seafloor's chemistry into something that might, if you squint, be called biology. They have no individuals. No generations. The same smear of purple-black metabolizing has been respiring in place for perhaps forty thousand years, drawing hydrogen sulfide from cold seeps below the sediment, turning chemical potential into the barest flicker of what might be called life.

They are slow beyond comprehension. A single metabolic cycle—intake of substrate, transformation, release of product—takes approximately eleven hours. They exist in a state so close to geological that scientists argued for decades about whether they qualified as living at all. They do. Barely. They reproduce through fission every four hundred days, roughly, though the measurement means nothing because the parent and daughter cells do not separate; they simply expand the mat, adding another whisper of thickness to a structure that might extend three meters into the sediment and cover hectares of seafloor.

They remember. Not in any way that involves neurons, but they respond to chemical signatures they encountered millennia ago. A particular isotope ratio, a specific organic compound falling from above, and the mat will pulse—will alter its metabolic rate by minute fractions, will send rippling responses through its extent. When the whale fall arrives (once every three hundred years in this particular patch, statistically), the mats closest to the corpse bloom. They sense the death before any mobile creature does, taste the chemical signature of what is about to become available. They expand upward, reaching, in growth spurts that might cover a centimeter in a week.

They have been here for three billion years in some form. They watched the oxygenation of the oceans. They survived mass extinctions that killed ninety percent of everything else. They will survive what is coming next. They are not impressed by anything. They have no capacity for impression.

## II. The Griefers

The scavengers came first, millions of years ago, descending from shallower waters into this place of dissolution. They were recognizable once—descendants of hagfish, of lamprey, of things that fed on the dead and dying. But the pressure changed them. The darkness changed them. The slow dissolution changed them.

They are called, in the few research papers that mention them, *Necrophages abyssalis*, though the name is more taxonomy's need for labels than any accurate description. The researchers who first described them called them "griefers" in their notes, a nickname that stuck—not from any human emotion, but from the old word for heavy, for serious, for weight.

The griefers are built for economy. They have no swim bladder. No air bladder of any kind. Their bodies are water held in shapes defined by cartilage and reinforced collagen, dense as the medium around them, perfectly matched to the pressure so they feel nothing at all. They have no bones to speak of—calcification is expensive and dissolution is constant. Their skeletons are reminders, suggestions. They are held together by the pressure as much as by any internal structure. If you brought one to the surface, it would come apart. Not explode, as deep-sea fish are said to do, but simply... relax into component parts. The pressure is what gives them coherence.

They are large by the standards of this place—perhaps three meters in length when fully grown—but they rarely appear so. They fold themselves into the particulate, compressing their bodies into shapes that present minimal profile to the current. They drift. They wait. They have been waiting, some of them, for fifteen years for a significant food fall. They can wait another fifteen. Their metabolisms run so slowly that a heartbeat—if their circulatory systems could be said to beat—takes four minutes. They consume perhaps four calories per year. They have no concept of hunger. They have only patience.

When the whale fall comes, they are the first to know. The mats might sense the chemistry, but the griefers feel the displacement in the water, the pressure wave of something massive settling onto the plain. They arrive within hours, materializing from the murk—not individually, but together. They have no social structure, but they share information through chemical trails, through bioluminescent pulses too weak for human eyes to register but perfectly legible to those who have evolved to read them. A whale fall draws every griever within a six-kilometer radius. They arrive in numbers that suggest coordination, but there is no coordination. There is only response.

They do not compete. Competition requires surplus energy, and nothing here has surplus. They take positions around the corpse and begin the slow work of dissolution. They do not eat in any recognizable sense. They secrete enzymes into the water around the carcass, enzymes that break down tissue at the molecular level, that turn muscle and blubber into a chemical soup that the griefers then absorb through skins designed for exactly this purpose. It takes them months to reduce a whale to bone. They share. Not from altruism, but from economy. The chemical trail of feeding griefers draws others, and the presence of others accelerates the breakdown. A solitary griever would take years to process a whale. A community of forty takes one hundred and twelve days.

They have no predators. Nothing here has the energy to make them prey. They have no prey except what falls. They are an ending, not a middle. They exist to reduce what was living into what can be used by the mats. They are essential. They are also, perhaps, the loneliest creatures in the ocean.

## III. The Drifters

There are things that move through the water column above the plain, passing through but not of this place. They are rare enough that you might drift for weeks and see none. But they exist.

The first type researchers called *Planula profunda*, though it is not a planula in any meaningful taxonomic sense. It is a descendant of jellyfish, perhaps, or perhaps something that evolved independently into a similar shape—convergent evolution producing the only body plan that makes sense for this pressure. It is a bell, perfectly transparent, perhaps two meters across, filled with water denser than the surrounding medium. It moves by pulsing, but each pulse takes seven minutes. Between pulses, it simply hangs, suspended, waiting for the momentum to carry it forward another meter.

It is a filter feeder, but not of particulate. It feeds on the bacteria suspended in the water column, on the organic molecules too small for anything else to bother with. It is a net thrown wide across the smallest scales of existence. It has no brain, no central nervous system, only a nerve net that coordinates the slow pulse and detects chemical gradients. It can sense food and move toward it over distances of perhaps fifty meters. It can sense danger—what passes for danger here—and move away. It does not think. It does not need to think.

It reproduces by releasing gametes into the water column during periods of particular chemical saturation, periods that might come once every decade. The gametes drift until they encounter other gametes, and then they develop into free-floating larvae that eventually settle onto the seafloor and metamorphose into a polyp stage that might live for three hundred years before releasing a single medusa. The medusa drifts for perhaps six months before dissolving, quite literally, into the water around it. Its molecules become food for the mats. Its brief existence is a gamble that the polyp stage—a barely-living stalk anchored to the sediment—will survive long enough to try again.

The second type of drifter is larger, stranger, and so rarely seen that some researchers doubt it exists at all. The research submersibles that have encountered it—and there have been only three confirmed sightings—report a shape that defies easy description. It seems to be a colony organism, something like a siphonophore, but built of components that should not work together. There are feeding structures that might once have been polyps. There are locomotive structures that resemble nothing in any taxonomic database. There are light-producing organs that pulse in sequences suggesting communication, though what a colony organism at four thousand meters depth might need to communicate, no one can say.

It moves faster than it should—perhaps three kilometers per hour, which is breakneck speed in this place. It disappears into the murk before any observation can be completed. The few tissue samples that have been recovered contain DNA sequences that match nothing in any database, mitochondrial markers that suggest a lineage split from other animals before the Cambrian explosion. It might be a survivor. It might be an experiment in alternative evolution that has been running in isolation for five hundred million years. It might be nothing. The drifter does not care what it might be.

## IV. The Burrowers

The sediment is not empty. Below the surface, extending perhaps twenty meters into the compressed particulate, there is a world that never sees water. The burrowers live here—organisms that have so fully adapted to life in the sediment that they cannot survive in open water at all.

They are worms, essentially. Long, thin, segmented creatures that move through the sediment by eating it, digesting the organic matter trapped between grains, and excreting what remains. They are blind, eyeless, senseless except for chemical receptors that can detect organic content at concentrations below one part per million. They live in tunnels they create themselves, tunnels that collapse behind them as they move, leaving only the faintest trace in the sediment stratigraphy.

They are hermaphroditic, each individual possessing both male and female reproductive organs. They meet rarely—in fact, they almost never meet at all. Reproduction occurs through a process researchers have never directly observed but have reconstructed from sediment samples: when two tunnels cross, the worms exchange genetic material through the thin wall of sediment that separates them. They never touch. They never see each other. They deposit sperm packets into the shared wall and move on. The packets dissolve into the other worm's tunnel and fertilize whatever eggs are present. The resulting larvae burrow outward, away from the parent tunnel, beginning their own slow journey through the sediment.

A single burrower might travel perhaps one hundred meters in its lifetime. That lifetime might be two hundred years. They exist in densities of perhaps one per cubic meter of sediment, which means that at any given moment, there are billions of them beneath the plain. They are the most numerous animal life here. They are also the least seen.

They are important. They process the sediment, turning over the top twenty meters of seafloor every million years or so. They oxygenate what would otherwise become anoxic, they redistribute organic matter that would otherwise be trapped in surface layers. They make the plain livable for everything else. They are the engineers of the deep, though they will never know this, never know anything.

## V. The Fish That Were

There are fish here. Not many. Perhaps one per cubic kilometer of water column above the plain. But they exist, and they have been here longer than the continents have held their current shapes.

They are not recognizable as fish. The pressure has pushed their skeletons inward, compressing their bodies into shapes that minimize surface area while maximizing internal volume. They are approximately spherical, perhaps thirty centimeters across, with fins reduced to rudders and stabilizers at the margins. They have no swim bladder; they are neutrally buoyant by virtue of body composition alone. Their bodies are composed primarily of water and gelatinous tissue, with actual muscle making up less than five percent of their mass.

They are ambush predators. They cannot chase; chasing requires energy. They cannot flee; fleeing requires energy. They hang in the water column, barely moving, waiting for something to come within the ten-centimeter radius of their mouths. When something does—a shrimp, a copepod, a larval something that has descended from above—they open their mouths. The opening takes approximately three seconds. Water rushes in, carrying the prey item with it. The mouth closes. The fish digests. The whole process costs perhaps a fraction of a calorie. The fish might eat once every six months.

They are hermaphroditic, like the burrowers, but they do reproduce sexually. When two fish encounter each other—an event that might happen once in a decade—they exchange genetic material through a process that takes four hours and leaves both individuals exhausted for months afterward. The resulting eggs are released into the water column, drift upward for perhaps a thousand meters, and then hatch into larvae that are themselves spherical and formless. The larvae drift back down over the course of years, metabolizing their yolk sacs at rates that make the parents look energetic. By the time they reach the adult depth, they are perhaps one centimeter across. They grow. They wait. They have been doing this for forty million years.

They have a name, *Globefish abyssalis*, but it means nothing to them. They have no names. They have only the pressure, the darkness, the rare encounters, the slower digestions. They are templates for what fish could be, stripped of everything unnecessary. They are survival reduced to its barest form.

## VI. The Ones That Wait

There are things here that do not fit any category. They are not alive, exactly. They are not dead. They are waiting.

They are precipitates, mineral formations that grow at the boundary between chemical gradients. Manganese nodules, growing around cores of shark tooth or whale ear bone. Iron-manganese crusts forming on exposed rock. Carbonate structures precipitating from the saturation of ancient shells dissolving.

But some of them are... different. Some of them seem to respond to stimuli. There are nodules that move, slowly, over periods of decades, toward sources of particular chemical gradients. There are crusts that pulse with very faint bioluminescence when disturbed, though they contain no biological material that any analysis has ever detected. There are structures that seem too regular, too deliberate, too designed.

The researchers who study them call them "abiotic anomalies" and try not to think too hard about what that might mean. They are probably just chemical processes that happen to mimic some characteristics of life. Probably. The ocean is vast and old, and strange things happen at four kilometers depth.

But there are stories. Submersible pilots report nodules that seemed to follow them, drifting a few meters over hours of observation. Samples that behaved differently in the lab than they should have. A particular formation, observed once, that seemed to have a... shape. A suggestion of symmetry. Something that looked almost like a body, curled in sleep, waiting.

These stories are not in any official report. They are told in bars, at conferences, in the quiet hours of the night watch. They are dismissed. They are forgotten. They are remembered, uneasily, by those who were there.

The plain holds secrets. The plain does not share them.

## VII. The Whale Falls

The whale fall is the event that defines existence here, but it is also an ecosystem—a temporary community that flares into being and then collapses over the course of decades.

A whale that dies at the surface does not sink immediately. It floats, bloated with decomposition gases, for perhaps a week. Then it deflates and begins the long fall. It takes approximately three hours to reach the plain. By the time it arrives, it has been picked clean by scavengers from every depth zone it passed through. What remains is skeleton, some connective tissue, and the deep organs protected by the rib cage.

The griefers arrive first, as noted. They work for months, reducing the soft tissue to chemical traces. Then they leave. What remains is bone, dense with lipids that nothing else can process.

Enter the bone worms. *Osedax* species have been known to science since 2002, but the species that live at this depth are something else entirely. They are larger, slower, more patient. They attach to the bone surface and extend root-like structures into the marrow, breaking down the lipids with symbiotic bacteria. They have no mouth, no stomach, no digestive system of their own. They are farms, hosting bacterial colonies that process what they cannot.

A single bone worm can live for sixty years on a single whale skeleton. It grows slowly, extending its root network deeper into the bone, producing eggs that are released into the water column and drift until they encounter another whale fall. The odds of finding one are infinitesimal. The odds of finding one at the right stage of decomposition are even smaller. And yet the bone worms persist. They have persisted for millions of years. They will persist for millions more.

The whale fall ecosystem supports other temporary residents—polychaete worms, crustaceans, mollusks that exist nowhere else. They appear when a fall arrives, breed furiously for a few years, and then die when the resources run out. Their larvae drift in the water column, dormant, waiting for the chemical signature of a new fall. Some have been waiting for decades. Some will wait for centuries. Some will never find what they're waiting for.

The whale fall is a lesson. It is what abundance looks like in the deep—a corpse, a gift, a brief flowering of consumption. And then dissolution. Always, dissolution.

## VIII. The Absent

It is worth noting what is not here.

There are no whales. Whales can dive deep, but not this deep. The pressure would collapse their lungs, their rib cages, their bodies that evolved for a world of air and light. The whales fall, but they do not visit.

There are no sharks. The sharks that hunt in the deep—the Greenland shark, the megamouth, the goblin shark—all stop far above this depth. There is nothing here for them. No prey worth the energy, no temperatures their bodies can tolerate, no light to navigate by. The sharks stay where life is thick. Here, life is thin.

There are no humans. Humans have visited, in submersibles, but they are visitors—tourists passing through a place that does not notice them. The pressure would kill them in an instant without their technology, and the technology is so fragile, so temporary, so utterly inadequate to the scale. Humans are absence here. They are the shape of what is missing.

There are no gods. If there were gods of the deep, they have left or died or dissolved. The plain is too old for worship, too patient for meaning. The darkness does not judge. The pressure does not forgive. There is simply process, ongoing, indifferent.

## IX. The Constant Dissolution

The dissolution is the point. The constant, patient erosion of everything toward flatness, toward entropy, toward the final state where nothing rises and nothing falls and the plain extends forever in perfect equilibrium.

The water dissolves the rock. Not quickly—a meter of basalt might take a million years to disappear—but it dissolves. The calcium carbonate of ancient shells dissolves as the water becomes undersaturated. The organic matter falling from above dissolves, its carbon released into the cycle. Even the manganese nodules dissolve, eventually, their accumulated layers peeling away into the medium that built them.

The creatures here are part of this process. They accelerate it. The griefers dissolve tissue that would otherwise take years to break down. The burrowers dissolve the sediment, digesting it, passing it through their bodies. The bacterial mats dissolve chemical gradients, metabolizing the differences into sameness. Everything here works toward the same end—flatness, stillness, the heat death of this small corner of the universe.

The dissolution is patient. It has been working for six hundred million years. It will work for another six hundred million. The plain will flatten. The mountains will become swales. The swales will become flatness. The flatness will become perfect.

And then, perhaps, something will change. A spreading center will shift. A continental shelf will collapse. A new mountain will be born, and the dissolution will begin again.

But not soon. Not soon at all.

## X. The Witness

You are here. You are reading this. In some sense, you are the only witness this place has ever had.

The plain does not care. The dissolution does not pause. The griefers continue their slow orbit of nothing in particular. The mats breathe their chemical breath. The burrowers process the sediment one gram at a time. The fish hang in their places, waiting.

But you are here. You have imagined this place, held it in your mind for the time it took to read these words. You have given it a kind of reality it did not have before—not the reality of existence, which it has in abundance, but the reality of being witnessed, of being thought.

It will not last. You will close this document, or scroll away, or simply forget. The plain will dissolve from your memory as it dissolves everything else. In a week, you will remember only a sense of darkness, pressure, deep time. In a year, you will not remember it at all.

But for now, you are here. And the plain, in its infinite patience, its constant dissolution, its endless erasure toward flatness—

—for now, the plain holds you.
