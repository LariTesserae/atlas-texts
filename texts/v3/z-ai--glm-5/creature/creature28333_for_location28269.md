The Resident

There is someone who lives on the Weeping Terrace.

Not always—there are gaps, absences that last weeks or months, periods when the terrace holds only its usual population of birds and lizards and insects. But someone returns, repeatedly, stays for extended periods, knows the place in a way no casual visitor could.

---

They are not young. This is clear from the way they move—carefully, with a distributed attention that keeps track of the damp rocks, the loose stones, the edges where footing becomes uncertain. They know which surfaces are reliable and which are not. They know where the moss is thick enough to hold weight and where it is merely decorative.

Their age is difficult to estimate. The elevation thins the face, creates a certain skeletal clarity that reads as aging in the young and reads as endurance in the old. Their skin is weathered—sun-darkened, wind-dried, but also somehow hydrated from within, as if the constant moisture of the terrace has worked its way into their system over time. They do not look dried out. They look like something that has learned to hold water.

Their clothing is indeterminate. Layers that might once have had distinct colors have faded to a uniform grey-brown, matching the stone, matching the dried fern fronds, matching the birds. The garments are loose, worn thin in places, clearly maintained—there are mends, patches, areas where the fabric has been carefully reinforced—but they are not new, and they are not replaced often. The overall impression is of someone who has become part of the terrace's visual palette.

---

They arrive already wet.

This is the first sign that they understand the place. They do not try to stay dry. They wear the dampness as the terrace wears it—accepting, unresisting, moving through the weeping rock's constant exhalation without attempting to protect themselves from it.

They drink from the shallow basins. Not always, and not exclusively—they bring supplies, water skins, sometimes a small pot for boiling—but they also drink directly from the pools that the limestone has carved. They know which pools are fresh and which have sat too long. They know the taste of each basin, how it changes through the day as the temperature shifts and the rock breathes differently.

They eat simply. Dried provisions brought from below. Sometimes the small herbs that grow at the ferns' base—medicinal, perhaps, or merely familiar. They do not hunt the birds. They do not trap the lizards. The insects they tolerate with a patience that suggests long practice, waving them away from their face without urgency, without irritation.

---

They speak, but not often, and not loudly.

When they speak, it is to themselves—a low murmur that blends with the water-sound, words that might be prayers, might be counting, might be nothing more than the verbal equivalent of thinking aloud. The birds do not startle at their voice. The lizards do not flee. The rhythm of their speech has become part of the terrace's acoustic ecology, another layer in the soundscape, accepted by the permanent residents.

Sometimes they sing. Not songs with melody or structure—sustained tones that rise and fall with the wind, breath-powered sounds that explore the resonant frequencies of the space. They seem to be testing something, measuring how far the voice carries, how it changes in the thin air. Or they might simply be making sound for the sake of making sound, filling the emptiness with something human before falling silent again.

They have a name for the place. Not "The Weeping Terrace"—that is someone else's name, perhaps the name it is known by in the villages below. They call it something different, a word or phrase in a language that might be local dialect, might be private invention. When they say it, they say it to the rock itself, as if addressing the mountain rather than discussing the location.

---

They are not always alone.

Sometimes another person climbs to the terrace—a local guide, perhaps, or a pilgrim making the ascent for reasons of their own. The resident receives them with a formality that suggests ritual rather than hospitality. They offer water from the basins. They indicate where to sit—always the same stone, a flat shelf near the center, protected from the wind, with a clear view of the emptiness without being too close to the edge.

These conversations are brief. The visitors do not stay long. The emptiness works on them as it works on everyone—the agitation, the need for walls and boundaries and the density of lowland spaces. They ask questions the resident does not always answer. They leave offerings sometimes—food, bundles of herbs, once a small mirror that the resident hung from a fern stem and then, weeks later, removed.

The resident never leaves with them. The visitors descend alone, and the terrace settles back into its smaller population, and the weeping rock continues its slow exhalation.

---

They sleep in a niche that is not visible from the terrace's center.

A fold in the rock face, barely large enough to lie down in, protected from the wind by the stone's own geometry. They do not build a shelter. They do not improve the niche in any permanent way—they simply occupy it, night after night, bedding down on a mattress of dried fern fronds and accumulated moss that they maintain but do not expand.

The niche is dry, or dry-ish. The weeping rock's moisture does not reach this particular fold, or reaches it less, or reaches it differently. They have found the one corner of the terrace where a person can sleep without being constantly wet, and they use it, and they make no attempt to extend its protection.

They sleep deeply but briefly. Mountain sleep—short hours of genuine rest rather than long hours of shallow dozing. They wake before dawn, when the terrace is at its coldest, when the condensation is heaviest, when the birds are beginning their first calls. They move to the edge to watch the light rise through the valley mist below.

---

They have been coming here for a long time.

This is not guesswork. The evidence accumulates. The moss near their sleeping niche is worn differently—the same subtle pressure that a hand or hip or shoulder applies night after night, year after year. The path they walk from niche to water-basin to edge has worn into the stone imperceptibly, a gleaming thread of repeated contact. The birds treat them as part of the landscape—no alarm calls when they approach, no sudden departures, only the small acknowledgments that birds give to features of their environment that are known, predictable, unchanging.

The lizards are bolder with them than with any other visitor. The resident can sit motionless for extended periods—hours, potentially—and the lizards will bask on the rocks near them, will climb onto their unmoving feet, will treat them as an extension of the stone. The resident does not interact with them. They simply share space, lizard and human, holding warmth in the thin sunlight.

The weasel-like creature—the shy mammal that others rarely see—shows itself to the resident. This is significant. The terrace's largest predator, such as it is, has learned that this particular human is not a threat. It emerges at dusk sometimes when the resident is sitting by the weeping wall, and they occupy the same space without conflict, the mammal hunting among the ferns while the human watches or thinks or simply exists.

---

They have a practice.

This is perhaps the wrong word, but it is the closest approximation. They do something, when they are on the terrace, that goes beyond simple occupation.

They observe the water. Not in a scientific way—not measuring, not recording—but in a sustained, contemplative way. They watch it emerge from the rock. They track its path down the stone face, its pooling in the basins, its evaporation and return. They taste it at different times of day. They hold their hands in the shallow streams and sit without moving.

They observe the emptiness. They stand at the edge—not dangerously close, but close enough—and look outward at the vast nothing that surrounds the terrace on three sides. They do not look down. They look out, into the blue, into the distance that contains no features, no landmarks, no points of reference. They stand there for extended periods, breathing the thin air, letting the emptiness work on them.

They observe the garden. They know each fern individually—not by name, necessarily, but by position, by health, by the particular curve of its fronds. They notice when a new plant emerges. They notice when one dies back. They do not intervene, do not cultivate, do not try to improve the garden or expand it. They simply know it, track it, hold it in their awareness with a completeness that looks like devotion.

---

What they are doing here is not clear.

They are not a hermit in the traditional sense—they interact with visitors, they maintain connections below, they leave and return according to patterns that suggest a life beyond the terrace. They are not a scientist, not a pilgrim in any recognized tradition, not a refugee from anything that can be easily named.

They might be waiting. For what, it is impossible to say—the question has been asked, and the resident has offered no answer that satisfies. They might be maintaining something—a vigil, perhaps, or a presence that matters for reasons that are not visible. They might be learning something—the water, the stone, the emptiness, the slow patience of a place that has persisted for longer than human counting.

They might simply be someone who found, in this improbable pocket of life at the edge of a vast emptiness, something they could not find elsewhere. A way of being that the terrace makes possible. A clarity that the altitude produces. A relationship with moisture and stone and silence that answers a need the resident cannot articulate and perhaps does not fully understand.

---

When they are gone—during their absences, when they descend to the villages below for supplies or obligations or reasons known only to them—the terrace continues its existence. The rock weeps. The birds call. The lizards bask. The wind shifts and stills and shifts again.

But something is different during their absence. Something subtle, something that might be imagined, might be projected by visitors who know the resident exists and notice their lack. The terrace feels less observed. The garden continues its cycles without witness. The emptiness presses in without anyone standing at its edge, meeting it, holding the boundary of human awareness against the vast blue nothing.

And then they return. The climb is difficult at their age, or at any age—they arrive winded, weary, needing time to recover. But they make the ascent, repeatedly, and they settle back into their niche, and they resume their practices of water and emptiness and attention.

They are old enough now that each departure might be the last. Each return might be the final one. The terrace does not require them—the rock wept before they came, and will weep after they are gone. But they have become part of its pattern, part of its rhythm, another cycle in a place defined by cycles.

They are the terrace's human component. Not essential, not permanent, but present. A voice that speaks to the stone. A body that drinks from the basins. A consciousness that meets the emptiness and does not flinch.

They live there, insofar as anyone can be said to live in a place that will outlast them by centuries. They live there, and they return, and they practice the strange particular attention of someone who has found exactly the place they needed, and has learned to need nothing more.
