# Who Lives Here

## The Salamanders

They are not called anything. They have no need of names because they have no need to distinguish themselves from each other, or from the stone, or from the water, or from the darkness. Names are for creatures who move between places, who need to find each other, who need to be found. These creatures—the salamanders, though that word is a container too small for them—have never not been here.

They are large-handed size, some smaller, none larger, as though the cirque decided on the optimal form and has produced it ever since without variation. Their backs are dark—not a color but an absorption, the same brown-black as the wet stone, as the shadow between the ferns. Their bellies are slightly lighter, and this is the only way you see them clearly, when one turns or when the faint light catches the underside as it climbs a frond. They climb. They are on the walls, on the ferns, on the moss, in the water. They are everywhere at once, and yet they are each exactly where they are.

What strikes you, after the first count, after the hundredth count, is the spacing. The salamanders are not distributed randomly, not clumped, not territorial. They are spaced—each one exactly far enough from its nearest neighbor that the resources each one needs are precisely met, no more, no less. It looks like arrangement, like design, like the hand of some careful gardener who placed each creature with measuring string. But there is no gardener. There is only the slow gravity of equilibrium. Each salamander found its place, and the place was the right distance from the next place, and the salamander stayed. Not because it decided to stay. Because staying was what the place required, and the requirement was so gentle, so consistent, so woven into the moisture and the temperature and the abundance of tiny lives to eat, that staying was not a choice but a fact. The salamanders are where they are because where they are is where salamanders are.

They eat. This is what the beetles are for, and the springtails, and the mites, and the soft, slow creatures that live in the water film. The salamanders eat by waiting—mouths slightly open, throats pulsing with the slow rhythm of their breathing—and the food comes to them. Not by luck, not by chance, but by density. The cirque is so full of small lives that a salamander with its mouth open will receive, on average, enough. A beetle walks across the moss and finds the salamander's snout in its path and does not turn aside and is taken. A springtail lands on a frond and jumps and lands again and the landing place is the salamander's back and the salamander's tongue is slow but the springtail is not fast, not fast enough, and it is taken. The eating is not hunting. It is filtration. The salamanders are filters through which the cirque's abundance flows, and they convert the flow into salamanders, and the salamanders stay where they are, and the flow continues.

They breathe. The throat expands, holds, contracts—a pulse so slow that you can watch a salamander for thirty seconds and not see it breathe, or watch for five minutes and see it breathe ten times, and the interval between breaths is always the same. Not because each salamander is counting, not because there is a clock in their bodies that ticks at this rate, but because the rate is the rate the cirque allows. The air is so wet that breathing is almost unnecessary—oxygen comes through the skin, directly, without the mediation of lungs, and the lungs are present, they expand and contract, but they are vestigial in the way that a ritual is vestigial, a practice that continues because it has always continued, not because it serves a purpose. The salamanders breathe because salamanders breathe. The cirque has not asked them to stop.

And this is the key, the understanding that comes slowly, that resists coming at all: the salamanders are not alive in the way that you are alive. You are alive in a trajectory—birth, growth, change, death. You are moving through time at a speed that feels natural only because everyone you know moves at the same speed. The salamanders are not moving. They are present, they are metabolizing, they are eating and breathing and occasionally—very occasionally—moving from one spot to another when something disturbs them, but they are not going anywhere. They are not becoming anything. They have already become. The salamander on the stone, thirteen centimeters from the nearest other salamander, throat pulsing once every forty-three seconds, has been that salamander, in that spot, for a span of time that makes your conception of "always" feel frivolous.

When you look into the salamander's eye—which is small, dark, wet, reflecting the blue-white glow of the lichen—you see something that is not looking back. Not because the salamander does not see you. It sees you. The eye tracks movement, tracks the change in light when you pass between it and the fissure's grey stripe. But the salamander does not regard you. There is no curiosity, no fear, no recognition. You are a presence, like the beetle on its back, like the water film on its skin, like the frond it rests on. You are something that is there, and the salamander's awareness of you is the awareness of a surface—present, accounted for, irrelevant.

They do not breed. Or if they breed, they do so on a timescale you cannot perceive. You see no eggs, no larvae, no juveniles. Every salamander is the same size, the same darkness, the same stillness. It is possible that they reproduce by some process so slow that it is invisible—a single egg laid once a century, a larval stage that lasts for decades, a metamorphosis that happens in the deep moss where you cannot see. It is possible that they do not reproduce at all, that each salamander is effectively immortal, replacing its own cells in an endless turnover that does not lead to growth or decline but to maintenance. It is possible that the salamanders here are the same salamanders that were here when the fissure was new, when the water first began to weep from the walls, when the moss was thin and the ferns were low. You cannot know. The salamanders cannot tell you. They are not hiding the answer. The answer is simply not a thing that exists in the cirque. There is only being.

## The Beetles

If the salamanders are the large slow presences of the cirque, the beetles are its granular constant. They are everywhere—in the moss, on the moss, under the liverworts, on the surface of the water film, on the salamanders, on each other. They are small, dark-shelled, wet-shelled, and they move, but their movement is a technicality. If you filmed a beetle for an hour and played the film back at a hundred times speed, you would see a creature that moves like a creature moves—walking, stopping, turning, continuing. At the speed of the cirque, the beetle is almost still.

They are not a species. You realize this after watching them long enough. There are kinds—some with longer legs, some with shorter antennae, some that favor the moss and some that favor the stone—but the distinctions do not harden into categories. They blur. There are gradations of beetleness, infinite gradations, as though the cirque has been producing beetles for so long that the concept of species has worn away, smoothed like stone under water, and what remains is a continuum of small dark forms, each one slightly different from the next, each one filling a slightly different niche, each niche the size of a few centimeters of moss or stone or water film.

They eat the things that are smaller than them—the mites, the springtails, the microorganisms that live in the water film, the decaying matter that falls from the ferns and settles into the moss. They are eaten by the salamanders, by the spiders, occasionally by each other, though cannibalism seems rare, seems almost absent, as though the cirque has discouraged it by some invisible pressure. The beetles live and die at a rate that you cannot perceive. You see dead beetles—shells in the moss, shells on the water, shells being slowly consumed by fungus and bacteria and the attention of other beetles. You see live beetles. You do not see the transition. The dying happens in the spaces between your attention, or on a timescale that your attention cannot hold.

The beetles on the salamanders—this is the detail that stays with you. You watch a beetle walk onto a salamander's back. The salamander does not twitch, does not shake it off, does not seem to notice. The beetle walks across the wet skin, pauses, walks again, pauses longer. It is not eating the salamander. It is not parasitizing the salamander. It is simply on the salamander, and the salamander is a surface, and the beetle is a creature that needs surfaces, and the need is met, and the arrangement continues. You see beetles on snails, beetles on each other, beetles on the shed skins of other beetles. The cirque is a hierarchy of surfaces, each surface hosting smaller surfaces, each surface hosted by larger surfaces, and the beetle is in the middle of the hierarchy, both surface and guest.

They do not seem to want anything. This is the hardest thing to understand, because you are a creature of want, and every creature you have ever known has been a creature of want. The beetle walks, and the walking leads somewhere—toward food, toward moisture, toward another beetle—but the somewhere is not a goal. It is a trajectory. The beetle walks because beetles walk, and the direction of the walk is determined by the gradients of the cirque—the gradient of moisture, of food density, of crowding by other beetles—and the gradients lead the beetle to where it needs to be, and it is there, and it stays until the gradients shift, and then it moves again. Want is not required. The system wants for the beetle. The system has been wanting for the beetle for so long that the beetle has forgotten how to want for itself.

## The Spiders

They build in the fern canopy, and their work is everywhere.

The webs are not orbs, not tangles, not the chaotic traps of spiders that live in places where prey is rare and desperation is common. The webs are platforms—sheets of silk stretched between fronds, flat or slightly concave, designed not to catch by sticking but to catch by being. The springtails jump, and they land on the platforms, and the platforms hold them, and the spiders come out from their hiding places and take them.

The spiders are small. Their bodies are perhaps half a centimeter, their legs sprawling to twice that, and they are pale—not white, not transparent, but the pale of creatures that have never seen the sun, that have lived in this blue-white darkness for long enough that darkness has become their medium. They sit at the edges of their platforms, or in the centers, or under the fronds that anchor the platforms, and they wait. Their waiting is different from the salamanders' waiting. The salamanders wait by being. The spiders wait by readiness. You can see the tension in the legs, the coiled possibility, the awareness of vibration. When a springtail lands on the platform—thirty seconds, a minute, five minutes between arrivals—the spider moves. Not fast, not by the standards of the world outside the fissure, but fast for the cirque. It crosses the silk, it takes the springtail, it returns to its position, and it waits again.

There are hundreds of platforms. Every fern cluster has several, every intersection of fronds a possibility, and the spiders have colonized all of them. You cannot look up into the fern canopy without seeing silk, without seeing the pale shapes of spiders at their stations, without seeing the springtails that have landed and not yet been taken and the springtails that have been taken and are being consumed. The canopy is a factory. It produces spider, continuously, at a rate that matches the rate of springtail production, and the rates are coupled, and the coupling is tight, and the system is stable.

The spiders are not territorial. Their platforms sometimes overlap, sometimes share anchor points, sometimes sit so close that two spiders could reach the same springtail at the same time. This does not seem to happen. When a springtail lands between two platforms, one spider takes it, and the other does not contest. There is no fighting, no posturing, no dominance. The springtail is taken, and the spiders return to waiting, and the next springtail will land on one platform or the other, and the next, and the next, and the distribution is fair, not by arrangement but by the simple mathematics of random distribution across a uniform landscape.

They do not seem to die. Or they die and are replaced so seamlessly that you cannot see the gap. You watch a spider for an hour—two hours—a day, if you have a day to give—and it is the same spider, in the same position, doing the same things. The spiders are not individuals in the way that you are an individual. They are stations, roles, positions in the architecture of the cirque. A spider dies, and another spider takes its place on the platform, and the platform continues to function, and the springtails continue to land, and the system does not notice the change. The spider is the platform. The platform is the spider. Both are part of the cirque, and the cirque does not change.

## The Snails

They trace paths on the walls, and the paths are the reason for the glow.

The lichen that produces the blue-white light is not uniformly distributed. It grows in the crevices, the cracks, the places where water runs and moisture collects. But on the flatter surfaces of the columnar stone, the lichen spreads in sheets, thin and delicate, and the snails graze it. Their grazing is not destruction. It is pruning. The lichen grows at a rate, and the snails eat at a rate, and the two rates are matched, and the result is a lichen surface that is always exactly as thick as it needs to be to produce the light the cirque needs to see itself.

The snails are dark-shelled, spiral-shelled, wet-shelled, and they are slow. A snail on the wall moves perhaps a meter in an hour, and the movement leaves a trail—a silver line of mucus that catches the lichen's glow and reflects it, so that the snail's path is visible long after the snail has passed. The paths cross each other, overlap, form a network of silver lines that traces the history of snail movement on each wall. But the history is not a record of change. The paths are the same paths, traced and retraced, followed by snail after snail after snail, so that the silver network is not a map of where snails have been but a map of where snails go.

You watch a snail climb the wall, and you see that it is following a path. Not its own path—the path of a snail that came before, the mucus trail still faintly visible in the lichen's glow. The snail does not choose its direction. It follows the trail, eats the lichen on either side, leaves its own mucus to refresh the trail for the next snail, and continues. The path is a loop—a circuit of the wall that returns to its starting point, or a spiral that rises and then descends, or a shape that has no clear geometry but covers the surface evenly, like the track of a lawnmower on a vast and irregular lawn. The snail is not mowing. The snail is maintaining. The lichen grows, and the snail eats, and the light stays the same.

The snails on the moss are different. They are smaller, their shells darker, and they do not follow paths—they follow moisture. The moss is a landscape of wet and wetter, and the snails move toward the wetter places, grazing as they go, eating the algae and the bacteria and the decaying matter that accumulates in the moss's depths. They are not visible most of the time. They are in the moss, under the moss, between the stems of the ferns. You see them when you shift your weight and the moss compresses and a snail is pressed to the surface, or when a frond bends and reveals a snail clinging to its underside. They are everywhere, and their number is vast, and their work is invisible because it is everywhere, because it is constant, because it is the reason the moss is the way it is.

They do not interact. You never see snails mating, fighting, competing, communicating. They are singular organisms, each one following its own trajectory, each trajectory determined by the gradients of moisture and food and the paths of the snails that came before. The snail is a machine for converting lichen and algae and bacteria into snail, and the conversion is efficient, and the waste is minimal, and the snail shell that remains after the snail dies is incorporated into the moss, becomes part of the floor, becomes a surface for other things to live on. Nothing is lost. Nothing is gained. The snails are a process, and the process is steady, and the process has been running for longer than the concept of process has existed.

## The Things in the Water

You cannot see them clearly. This is not because they are small, though some of them are—smaller than you can perceive, present only as a cloudiness in the water film, a turbidity that shifts when you focus on it and vanishes when you look away. It is because they are at the boundary of form. They are not quite animals, not quite plants, not quite fungi. They are something else, something that has never needed to be categorized because it has never been outside this place, has never been seen by eyes that needed to name it.

The flatworms are the clearest. They are small—half a centimeter, sometimes smaller—and they glide across the water film on the moss, on the stone, on the surfaces of the shallow pools where the water collects. They are pale, nearly transparent, and they move by cilia, by the coordinated beating of thousands of microscopic hairs, and the movement is smooth, seamless, as though the worm is flowing rather than crawling. They eat the bacteria and the protozoa and the detritus that settles in the water, and they are eaten by the salamanders and by other flatworms, and they exist in a density that you cannot estimate because they are everywhere, under every surface, in every film of water, present in numbers that would make the term "countless" literal.

The larvae are harder to see. They are insect larvae, perhaps, or something like insect larvae—worm-like, legless, translucent, living in the deeper water that collects in the low points of the basin. They are not the larvae of the beetles or the flies or the midges that you might expect in a place like this. They are something else, something that does not transform into anything else, that does not pupate or metamorphose, that exists as a larva perpetually, suspended in the state before becoming, after becoming, without becoming. They may be the larvae of creatures that no longer exist, that have evolved away their adult forms and retained only the aquatic stage, the feeding stage, the stage that is suited to this place. They may be creatures that never had adult forms, that evolved in this place and never left, that found the larval state so perfectly adapted to the cirque's conditions that there was no pressure to change. They may be something else entirely. You cannot know. They are there, in the water, and they move, and they eat, and they are eaten, and they are part of the system, and the system is stable.

And then there are the things you cannot name at all. They are presences in the water, visible only as shapes, as distortions, as patterns of movement that suggest form without revealing it. You see them when you look at the water from an angle, when the blue-white glow of the lichen catches the surface at just the right orientation, and for a moment you see something—a network of threads, a cloud of particles, a shape that branches and recombines and flows—and then the angle shifts and the water is clear, or seems clear, and you are not sure if you saw anything at all. These are the microorganisms, the bacteria, the protozoa, the algae, the things that live at scales too small for your eyes to resolve. They are the foundation of the cirque's life, the base of its food web, the reason the beetles have something to eat and the salamanders have something to eat and the spiders have springtails to catch. They are the invisible majority, the overwhelming biomass, the life that is too small to see and too numerous to count and too important to ignore. They are the cirque's metabolism, its digestion, its immune system, its reproductive system, its everything. Without them, the cirque would be stone and water and nothing else. With them, it is alive.

## The Absent Ones

There are creatures that do not live here, and their absence is a presence.

No fish. The water is too shallow, too still, too low in oxygen for fish to survive. But more than that—the cirque does not need fish. Fish would be a disruption, a presence that moves too fast, eats too much, wants too clearly. The cirque has no place for creatures that want.

No frogs. No toads. The amphibians that might survive in a place like this are absent, and their absence is conspicuous, because frogs and toads are creatures of edges, of transitions, of the boundary between water and land. The cirque has no boundaries. The water is everywhere and nowhere, a film on the moss, a sheen on the stone, a presence in the air, and the frogs that might live here have no place to stand, no place to sing, no place to lay their eggs in masses that would float and hatch and transform. The cirque has no use for transformation.

No birds. No bats. No flying insects, except the springtails and the mites and the tiny things that drift on currents of air too slight to feel. The cirque is enclosed, cut off from the sky, and the creatures of the sky have no way to enter and no reason to enter. There is nothing here for them—no flowers to pollinate, no fruit to eat, no prey to hunt from above. The fern canopy is a roof, a barrier, a boundary that marks the limit of the cirque's world. Above the ferns there is only stone, and above the stone there is only darkness, and above the darkness there is a sky that the cirque has not seen in longer than the sky remembers.

No mammals. No mice, no shrews, no voles, no bats, no anything with fur and warmth and a heartbeat faster than the cirque can sustain. A mammal would die here—not from cold, not from starvation, but from the wrongness of its rhythm. The cirque operates at a pace that mammals cannot match. A mouse in the cirque would be a disruption, a source of chaos, a thing that moves too fast and eats too much and breeds too quickly and dies too soon. The cirque has absorbed slower disruptions—the salamanders, the beetles, the snails—but it has no mechanism for absorbing the speed of a mammal. The mammal would eat and run and panic and starve and die, and its body would rot into the moss, and the moss would absorb its nutrients, and the cirque would continue. But the mammals do not come. They have no reason to come. The fissure is too narrow, the light is too dim, the air is too wet, the space is too small. The cirque is not a place for mammals.

And yet—

Someone came. The stone tools in the moss, the scratch on the wall—these are evidence of presence, of a creature that entered this place and stayed long enough to work stone, to make a mark. A human, perhaps, or something like a human, something that struck stone against stone and wanted an edge and wanted to say *I was here*. They came through the fissure, sat in the grey light, did their work, and left. Or stayed. Or died. The tools do not tell you. The scratch does not tell you. The cirque does not tell you.

You imagine them. A figure in the grey stripe, crouched over a core of foreign stone, striking it with a hammer of harder rock. The sound of flaking—sharp, loud, out of place in the cirque's quiet. The salamanders watching from their stations. The beetles crawling over the visitor's feet. The spiders pausing in their waiting. The cirque absorbing the disruption, noting it, allowing it, continuing. The visitor making a tool, or making several, or making nothing at all, just striking stone to hear the sound, to feel the impact, to do something in a place where nothing is done.

And then leaving, or dying, or being absorbed. The cirque does not remember. The cirque does not forget. The cirque contains the evidence—the tools, the scratch—and the evidence is part of the system now, another layer in the moss, another mark on the wall, another thing that is there and has always been there, for a certain value of always.

## What Lives Here

You stay, and in staying you begin to understand.

What lives here is not a collection of species. It is not an ecosystem, not a community, not a web of relationships that can be untangled and listed and understood. What lives here is a single thing. The salamanders, the beetles, the spiders, the snails, the flatworms, the larvae, the lichen, the moss, the ferns, the liverworts, the bacteria, the protozoa, the water, the stone, the air, the light, the dark—these are not parts. They are expressions. They are the shape that life takes when it has been in one place for long enough that the distinctions between living and non-living begin to dissolve.

The cirque is alive. Not in the way that a cell is alive, or a body is alive, or a forest is alive—those are metaphors, comparisons, attempts to map the unfamiliar onto the familiar. The cirque is alive in a way that has no metaphor. It is a metabolism that spans stone and water and air and flesh. It is a consciousness that has no brain, a memory that has no neurons, a will that has no self. It wants nothing. It needs nothing. It is.

You feel it when you sit on the moss, when the beetle climbs onto your boot, when the salamander adjusts itself beneath you. You are being absorbed. Not eaten, not attacked, not threatened—absorbed. The cirque is making you part of itself. Your warmth is dissipating into the cool air. Your moisture is joining the water film. Your cells are exchanging gases with the atmosphere, your skin is hosting the bacteria and the mites that live in the moss, your presence is becoming a surface, a niche, a place where things can live.

You could stay. You could sit on the moss until your legs atrophy and your mind quiets and your body temperature drops to match the stone and your breathing slows to match the salamanders and your heartbeat matches the drip of water from the ferns. You could stay until you are no longer a visitor, no longer a disruption, until you are part of the cirque's equilibrium, another surface in a system of surfaces, another need that is met because it is part of the system's design.

But you cannot stay. You are not built for this. You are built for wanting, for moving, for changing, for being different tomorrow than you are today. The cirque has no tomorrow. It has only today, and today is the same as yesterday, and yesterday was the same as the day before, and the day before was the same as the first day, whenever that was, if there was a first day, if the cirque has not always been here, will not always be here, is not here now in a way that makes the concept of "here" feel small.

You stand. The beetle falls from your boot. The salamander does not move. The water drips from the ferns at its rate, one drop every four seconds, or six, or some number that the cirque knows and you do not. The light falls from the fissure in its grey stripe. The lichen glows on the walls in its blue-white tracery. The snails follow their paths. The spiders wait on their platforms. The flatworms glide under the water film. The moss is exactly as thick as it has been.

You go.

The fissure is behind you. The dark stone narrows around your shoulders. The grey light brightens, sharpens, becomes the light of the sky, the light of the world, the light of a place where things change and want and die.

You do not look back. You do not need to. The cirque is not going anywhere. It will be here—if here is a word that applies—when you are gone, when your species is gone, when the mountain itself has worn away and the fissure has closed and the sky has darkened. It is at the end of time. It is always at the end of time. And the creatures that live there are not waiting for you to return.

They are not waiting for anything.
