# What Lives in the Hollow

## The Tool-Users

They have no name for themselves. If they have names for anything, it exists in a language of touch and pressure—vibrations felt through the stone, the specific dampness of a crack that promises food, the texture of a rock edge against the sensitive pads of their forelimbs. They are called nothing by no one, and they persist.

There are perhaps forty of them in the Hollow, though counting is difficult because they spend most of their time motionless, pressed flat against the limestone, indistinguishable from the wet streaks of condensation. Their bodies are the size of a human palm, stretched long, with limbs that seem slightly too large for their frames. The hindlegs are frog-like, folded compactly, capable of launching the creature in a single desperate leap away from threat. But the forelimbs—those are the anomaly. They extend from the shoulder in a way that suggests arms rather than legs, terminating in a splay of four digits that can spread wide or clamp tight. The middle digit is longer than the others, almost a finger, and it can rotate at the joint.

They are amphibians, probably. Their skin is naked and damp, pale as milk, thin enough that you can see the dark pulse of organs beneath. They have no eyes, only shallow depressions where eyes might once have been, thousands of generations ago. The depressions are sensitive to light—they know when they are in the beam and when they are not—but they do not see. Instead, they navigate by smell, by the feel of air currents on their skin, by vibrations conducted through stone into the bones of their feet.

They live alone, even in the presence of others. You might find three of them working the same stretch of wall, each focused on its own crack, its own project, separated by feet of stone that might as well be miles. They do not communicate. They do not seem to recognize one another as kin. When they encounter each other in the open, they pause, touch noses, move on. The interaction lasts seconds. It contains nothing you could call social bonding.

And yet they tolerate each other's presence. They do not fight over the fungal threads or the pale insects that sometimes emerge from the rock. If one has found a productive crack, others will work nearby but not interfere. This is not generosity. This is not arrangement. It is simply that they have no framework for competition. In the Hollow, there is enough. There has always been enough. The concept of scarcity has never been necessary.

They live for perhaps eight years. They lay eggs in the dark pools, transparent spheres that sink to the bottom and wait. The tadpoles—if they can be called that—are smaller, simpler, living entirely in the water until their limbs develop and they drag themselves onto stone. The adults do not stay to watch. They do not return to the pools after laying. The next generation is released into the world without ceremony or acknowledgment.

Their tool-use is their only strangeness. Each one carries a shard of limestone, selected and shaped over weeks or months, wedged into a crease of skin behind their forelimb. The shard is personal—abandoned only if broken, guarded if dropped, retrieved with frantic energy if lost. They use the edges to scrape, to pry, to dig. They do not teach this behavior. The young emerge from the pools already reaching for stone, already testing edges, already compelled toward the work. It is written into them somehow, a memory that lives in the body rather than the mind.

What are they making? Nothing, mostly. A sharper edge. A deeper crack. A pattern of scratches on a rock face that will be worn away by condensation within days. The work has no goal beyond itself. It is something they do, like breathing, like waiting in the light.

## The Aerialists

They are not insects. They are something older, something that branched from the tree of life before insects learned their particular tricks. They have six legs, but the legs are vestigial, tucked against their bodies in flight, useless for walking. They have wings—four of them—layered like cellophane, so thin that light passes through and renders them invisible except at certain angles. They have no eyes either. They navigate by currents, by the feel of warm air rising from the stone, by smell so acute it functions as a kind of vision.

They spend their lives in the light column. They rise on the thermals that the geothermal heat creates, drifting upward in slow spirals, and when they reach the top of the beam, they fold their wings and fall, catching themselves just before the stone, riding the updraft again. They are eating, probably. Something in the light—dust, microorganisms, spores drifting down from the wound above—sustains them. Or perhaps they are simply living, expending energy in the only way available to them, burning calories they have stored in their pale translucent bodies.

They are fragile. If one falls out of the light column, it will die within hours. Its wings will dry and crack. Its body will cool and cease to function. The others will not notice. They will continue their spiraling ascent, their slow fall, their endless riding of the warm air.

At night, they cling to the ceiling in clusters of hundreds, their wings folded together, their bodies still. They do not sleep. They wait. The pause in their activity is not rest but suspension—a state in which they consume almost nothing, exist almost not at all. When the light returns, they will drop from the ceiling and catch the first thermals of the day.

There are thousands of them, but they weigh almost nothing. The Hollow holds them in its upper reaches like a thought it has not yet finished.

## The Crawlers

The flat, many-legged things live in the wet. They are not beautiful. They are not ugly. They simply are—white, segmented, elongated, with legs that fringe their bodies like the legs of a centipede but softer, translucent, made for gripping stone rather than striking prey. They move by compression and extension, their bodies concertina-ing across the limestone, and they can fit through cracks that seem too small for anything to pass. When they find a pool, they submerge themselves completely and drink for hours, their bodies swelling visibly with the moisture.

They eat detritus. The shed skin of tool-users. The corpses of aerialists that fell wrong. The waste that anything produces. They are the Hollow's cleaning system, processing what would otherwise accumulate, returning it to the stone in a form that the fungal threads can absorb. They are essential. They are ignored. They move through the spaces between other lives, under the notice of the tool-users, below the flight of the aerialists, in the damp margins where nothing else wants to be.

They have no eyes. They have no apparent senses at all except for touch and something chemical—an ability to detect moisture, to find food, to locate others of their kind for the brief couplings that produce the next generation. They are not social. They are not solitary. They simply exist in numbers, overlapping, processing, continuing.

When they die, they dissolve. Their bodies break down within hours, absorbed by the stone, consumed by the fungal threads, erased as thoroughly as if they had never been. This is their gift to the Hollow: they leave nothing behind.

## The Burrowers

They live beneath the surface. You see them only when they choose to emerge—blind faces pushing up through the damp soil near the pools, bristles waving, mouths opening and closing in a way that suggests tasting the air. They are larger than the tool-users, heavier, slower. Their bodies are cylindrical, designed for moving through earth, and their forelimbs are spades that can push aside stone and soil with surprising force.

They come up for the fungal threads. Deep below the Hollow, in cracks and crevices that no light reaches, networks of pale fungus spread through the limestone, feeding on minerals and moisture and the waste that filters down from above. The burrowers have found them. They move through the fungal territory, eating as they go, and when they reach an area they have depleted, they dig toward the surface, following the scent of the threads that grow in the light.

They are the only creatures in the Hollow that move between worlds. Below, in the deep dark, they have constructed tunnels—complex networks that extend far beyond the chamber, connecting to other spaces, other voids in the stone. The Hollow is not their home. It is a stop on a route they have traveled for generations, a place where food can be found, where moisture collects, where they can surface and breathe air that has not been filtered through rock.

When they finish eating, they return below. The soil closes behind them. The Hollow has no evidence of their passage except the disturbed earth, the new holes that have appeared near the pools, the fresh surfaces exposed to the air.

## The Garden

The plants are an anomaly. They should not be here. They have no business existing in a limestone cave with only a few hours of light per day. And yet they cling to their ledge, their roots threading into microscopic cracks in the stone, their leaves spreading wide and thin to catch the golden beam when it sweeps across their small territory.

They are not a single species. They are a community—an accident of seeds that fell through the wound above, somehow found purchase on this one damp ledge, and survived. There are ferns, stunted and pale, their fronds translucent enough to read through. There are small flowering things that have never flowered, that produce only leaves, that reproduce through runners that spread beneath the condensation. There are mosses that form a base layer, holding moisture, creating soil from their own decay.

The garden is six feet across. It has existed for perhaps three hundred years—long enough for the plants to adapt, to lose their color, to develop the ability to track the light. They move slowly, incrementally, their stems bending toward the beam as it approaches, their leaves tilting to catch the maximum illumination. At night, they fold inward, protecting their tender surfaces, waiting.

They support life. Small insects—translucent, wingless, adapted to the dark—live in their roots. The tool-users sometimes visit, climbing the ledge with difficulty, eating what they can find. The aerialists occasionally land on the broad leaves, resting for moments before returning to the light column. The garden is a world within the world, a small dense patch of life in the vast emptiness.

## The Fungal Threads

They are everywhere. You cannot see most of them. They thread through the limestone in layers too thin to detect, breaking down the stone itself, extracting minerals, creating the voids and cracks that the other creatures use. They are the Hollow's foundation, its oldest residents, present before the collapse, present before the light, continuing in their patient work regardless of what happens above.

What you can see are the fruiting bodies—small pale protrusions that emerge from the damp cracks, convoluted surfaces that release spores into the still air. They are not mushrooms in any sense you would recognize. They are more like corals, branching and dense, their surfaces covered in tiny pores that breathe and release and absorb. They have no color. They have no smell. They exist in the margins, in the spaces between the visible lives, and they sustain everything.

The tool-users eat them. The burrowers seek them out. The crawlers process their waste. Even the garden depends on them—the soil on the ledge is built from fungal decay, from the slow breakdown of stone into something that roots can grasp. Without the threads, the Hollow would be sterile. Just rock and water and emptiness.

They reproduce without sex. They exchange genetic material through contact, through the merging of threads beneath the surface, and they have done so for longer than the Hollow has existed. They are patient beyond comprehension. A single colony might be thousands of years old, spreading slowly through the limestone, creating and consuming and waiting.

## What Is Not There

No vertebrates with eyes. No predators that hunt. No prey that flees. No competition, no territory, no dominance hierarchies or mating displays or parental care. The Hollow has no use for these things. They require energy, and energy here is scarce.

No sound. The creatures do not vocalize. They do not need to. Sound in the Hollow dies quickly, absorbed by the still air, and there is nothing to communicate that cannot be communicated through touch or presence.

No death in large numbers. The ecosystem is too sparse for die-offs. Individuals expire, dissolve, are absorbed. The populations remain stable because they cannot grow beyond what the space supports.

No future that differs from the present. The Hollow has found its equilibrium. It will remain this way until something changes—the wound closes, the geothermal heat fades, the light is blocked. And then everything will end, quickly, because there is no reserve, no surplus, no capacity to adapt to a new reality.

---

## What It Means

The Hollow does not mean anything. It is not a lesson, not a symbol, not a demonstration of nature's resilience or life's persistence. It is simply what happened: rock collapsed, light entered, life followed. The creatures here are not brave or noble or inspiring. They are present. They continue. They will continue until they cannot.

The tool-users will shape their stones. The aerialists will ride the thermals. The crawlers will process the waste. The burrowers will move between worlds. The garden will track the light. The fungal threads will break down the rock.

And the beam will fall through the wound in the ceiling, and the Hollow will hold them all, as it has, as it will, until something changes.

That is all. That is enough.
