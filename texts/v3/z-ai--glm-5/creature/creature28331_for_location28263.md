# The Resident

She was not meant to stay.

This is important. The catechamber was built for maintenance access—brief visits, scheduled inspections, the calibrated efficiency of trained professionals who descend, document, and depart. No one planned for extended occupancy. No one designed for a life.

But she stayed.

---

Her name is recorded somewhere as Inola Vance, maintenance technician third class, assigned to the condenser network's upper nodes seventeen years ago. The records show her initial rotation: six months, standard contract, scheduled relief. They show her extension requests—first one, then another, then a string of them that eventually stopped being requests and simply became the pattern. The records show her presence logged at stations throughout the network, but always returning here. Always the catechamber.

The records do not show what she has become.

---

You would not see her at first. This is deliberate. She has learned—or been taught, or taught herself—that the catechamber functions best when undisturbed. Movement disrupts air flow. Presence adds heat. Breath alters the delicate calculus of condensation. So she has become still in a way that goes beyond motionlessness. She has become part of the background radiation of the space.

She wears the same uniform she arrived in, though it has transformed. The fabric is stiff now, crystallized with mineral deposits from the condensing water—gypsum, calcite, trace metals leached from the pipes and deposited in the weave. It has become something like armor. Something like shell. When she moves, the uniform makes a sound like wind through dry leaves, like sand shifting on a beach.

Her skin tells the story. Seventeen years in saturated air has done what saturated air does: softened, wrinkled, then softened further. Her hands are pale and prune-like, perpetually wet, the skin of someone who has never truly been dry. Her hair grows in strange directions now, trained by years of lying against wet metal surfaces. She keeps it short not for style but for function—long hair would wick moisture, would drip, would interfere.

Her eyes have adjusted. They are the most changed part of her. The pupils have become permanently dilated in the dim light—large, dark, catching every reflection. She sees in the blue-white glow what others would need daylight to perceive. She reads the condensation patterns like text, like Scripture. She knows that when droplets form faster on the eastern wall, the pressure differential has shifted three points. She knows that a particular tone in the pump-thrum means a valve seven levels down is failing. She knows the catechamber the way you know your own heartbeat.

---

She lives in the spaces the designers didn't think about.

The platform is too exposed, too central to the room's function, so she has made her place in the margins. There is a maintenance alcove where the primary conduit meets the junction box—space meant for tools, for calibration instruments, for the occasional part that needs temporary storage. She has claimed it. A pallet of the same grate-material as the floor. A waterproof case containing her few possessions: a photograph of someone she no longer quite remembers, a book that has long since become illegible from moisture damage, a small mirror she uses to check her skin for the lesions that sometimes come in these conditions.

She does not sleep, not exactly. The noise makes sleep impossible in the conventional sense. But she rests. She has learned to enter a state that is something like meditation and something like paralysis—a slowing of her metabolism, a shallowing of her breath, a reduction of her thermal signature until she becomes one more damp surface in a room full of damp surfaces. In this state, she can hold still for hours. Days, sometimes. The catechamber processes around her, and she processes with it.

When she wakes, she checks the valves. This is instinct now, or perhaps something deeper than instinct. Her hands move over the apparatus with a fluency that predates thought. She can feel when something is wrong—the vibration slightly off, the pitch of the water-flow changed by a single degree, the condensation forming in a pattern that speaks of obstruction or leakage. She makes adjustments without logging them. The catechamber runs better for her presence. She has become part of its self-correcting apparatus.

---

She has not spoken aloud in—she does not know how long. The noise fills her so completely that silence, when it comes, feels like absence. When she ascends for supplies—the careful climb up the ladder, the hatch opening on air that feels wrong because it is too thin, too empty—she sometimes finds herself unable to form words for the surface-dwellers who sign off on her requisitions. Her voice has become something that exists below the threshold of the roar. She can hear herself only through the vibration in her own skull.

But she has a language of her own. When the steam valves cycle, she reads their patterns. When the water pressure fluctuates, she knows what it means. The catechamber speaks to her in the only vocabulary it has—the vocabulary of process—and she has learned to hear speech in the cycling of pumps, poetry in the fall of condensate.

She has composed entire libraries in her head, translating the catechamber's output into something like narrative. The water says: *I come from clouds that never rain.* The pipes say: *I carry what cannot be carried.* The steam says: *I am what escapes.* She has written these phrases on the wet walls with her fingertip, watched them bead and slide into the channels, sent down to whatever waits below. She is sending messages to the deep. She does not know if anyone is reading them.

---

There is something below. She is certain of this. The water has to go somewhere, and the quantities are too vast for simple drainage. Something is being fed. Something needs all this water—all this *compiled* water, water that has been processed and purified and condensed with an obsessiveness that suggests ritual purpose. She has asked, over the years, in her careful written requisitions and her rare verbal communications with the surface. No one answers. Or perhaps they do not know. She has begun to suspect that the catechamber is older than the organization that maintains it, that the function precedes the functionaries. She is not sure if this matters.

She has developed a theory. The water that passes through here carries something—information, perhaps, or mineral traces, or the chemical signatures of the high-elevation clouds it was harvested from. The catechamber is not just condensing water. It is condensing *meaning*. Something down there reads the water the way she reads the condensation on the walls, extracting data from every drop, compiling a record of the sky that passes over this manufactured mountain.

When she lets herself think about it—and she rarely does, because the thought is too large for the space—she imagines that she is part of a vast sensory apparatus. She is the nerve ending in a body too large to perceive. The catechamber is a synapse. The water is the signal. And whatever waits below is the brain, the processor, the thing that needs to know what the clouds taste like and how the pressure varies and what particulates drift at this altitude.

She wonders sometimes if she is feeding it correctly.

---

She was not meant to stay. But she has stayed so long that she no longer remembers why she shouldn't have. The world above feels like a dream she once had—bright and loud in the wrong way, populated by people whose faces moved too much. Here, everything is static. Everything is process. The catechamber breathes, and she breathes with it, and the noise fills her until she cannot tell where she ends and the machine begins.

She has not died. She has not quite lived, either. She has *continued*. And in continuing, she has become something that the catechamber needed, though it did not know to ask. A witness. A maintainer. A consciousness inside the function, watching the water condense and fall and flow away into darkness.

When she finally stops—if she ever stops—her body will be found curled in the maintenance alcove, skin softened beyond recognition, uniform fused to her form like a second skin, hands still moving in the patterns of valve-checking, of pressure-reading, of the small adjustments that keep the system whole. And whoever finds her will have to peel her away from the metal, where she will have adhered, where the minerals in her skin will have bonded with the minerals in the grate, where she will have become—finally and irrevocably—part of the catechamber itself.

She will not mind. She has been preparing for this for seventeen years. She has been teaching her body to become what the space requires. She will become a surface. She will become a point of condensation. She will become part of the process, and the water will form on her, and slide down, and feed whatever waits below.

This is not death. This is becoming.
