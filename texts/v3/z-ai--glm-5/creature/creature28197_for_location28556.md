# The Inheritors

The first thing to understand about what lives here is that *living* is not quite the right word for what they do.

They persist. They continue. They are.

---

## The Schools

The fish who move through the streets like weather are called, by the few scholars who have visited this place, by the names of their surface-dwelling cousins: *cyclopterids*, *notacanthids*, *ophidiids*. But these names are approximations, best-guesses based on skeletal structure and fin-placement, and the fish themselves do not use them. They do not use any names. They do not need them.

What they *do* need, they have in abundance: each other.

A school of five hundred individuals moves through the northern district at approximately the same time each cycle—not a daily cycle, for there are no days here, but a tidal cycle, the subtle rhythm of pressure-changes that propagate all the way down from the surface and register in the sensitive organs of the deep. These fish are pale, eyeless, their bodies reduced to the essential: a spinal column, a digestive tract, a lateral line system so exquisitely developed that it amounts to a second brain, a distributed consciousness that lives in the space between bodies rather than within any single skull.

They are individuals, technically. Each fish has preferences, tendencies, a slightly different swimming style that would be recognizable if anything down here recognized such things. But they are also components of something larger. When the school turns, it turns as a single organism, the decision rippling through the group at speeds that exceed individual reaction-time. When a predator approaches, the school contracts and expands, fragments and re-forms, the fish at the edges taking risks that the fish at the center never experience—and yet these roles rotate, shift, distribute the danger across the whole over time.

They spawn in the courtyards. The females rise slightly in the water column, releasing pheromone-dense clouds of eggs; the males follow, releasing their contribution; and then both sexes descend, exhausted, to drift through the streets in a post-spawning torpor that leaves them vulnerable. Many are taken during these periods. The predators who live in the deeper channels have learned to recognize the chemical signature of exhaustion, and they are patient. They wait.

But the schools are ancient. They have been spawning in these courtyards since before the buildings were buildings, when this seafloor was simply seafloor, unmarked and unstructured. The fish remember this, in their way—not as memory, not as conscious recollection, but as instinct, as the deep genetic knowledge that this place is safe, that the currents here are predictable, that the walls provide shelter even if they do not understand what walls are.

Their blindness is not an absence. It is a fullness. The lateral line system constructs a world of pressure and displacement, a three-dimensional map that updates in real-time, that perceives not just the presence of obstacles but their movement, their density, their acoustic signature. A fish can sense a predator's approach from the displacement of water thirty body-lengths away. A fish can perceive the difference between a wall of stone and a wall of anemones by the way the water flows around it—stone pushes, anemones absorb.

They do not know they are blind. They do not know what sight would be.

They are complete.

## The Sessile Cities

If the swimming fish are the weather of this world, the sponges and anemones and tube-worms are its architecture—the patient, unmoving, eternal presence that defines the boundaries of the space.

The sponges came first. Their spores drifted down from somewhere above, millennia ago, and found purchase on the stone surfaces of the empty city. They are ancient now, some of them—the oldest colonies span entire walls, their bodies thick and layered, the individuals at the bottom long dead and calcified into structural support for the individuals at the top. A sponge-colony is a graveyard and a living city in one: generations stacked upon generations, the past physically supporting the present, the living harvesting water that the dead once filtered.

They have preferences. The orange-pale sponges (they are not truly orange; the color is inferred from chemical signatures that would read as orange to eyes that could see) favor the north-facing walls, where the current brings nutrients from the thermal vents. The bone-white sponges favor the shaded channels, the slower water, the spaces where they can grow dense and thick without being torn apart by the flow. Competition happens at the borders, where two colonies meet and must negotiate the boundary—usually through chemical warfare, the release of compounds that inhibit the other's growth, a slow-motion battle that takes decades to resolve.

The anemones are younger, more mobile, more aggressive. They do not form permanent colonies; they aggregate, drawn together by chemical signals, by the presence of successful feeding, by the simple logic that where one anemone thrives, others will too. But they move. Given enough time—a year, a decade—a single anemone can detach and drift to a new location, re-attaching elsewhere, abandoning its previous neighbors for new ones.

They are hunters, in their way. Their tendrils drift in the current, tasting the water for the chemical signatures of prey—small crustaceans, larval fish, organic particles. When something edible brushes against a tendril, nematocysts fire, injecting toxins, paralyzing the prey, drawing it slowly toward the central mouth. The process is slow, invisible, silent. An anemone feeding looks identical to an anemone resting; the distinction is meaningless to anything but the prey.

The tube-worms build. Their calcium-carbonate casings rise in thickets from the lower walls, from the plaza-floors, from anywhere the water moves slowly enough to permit such delicate construction. A worm can extend its feeding-appendages from its tube, filtering particles from the water; when danger approaches—a predator, a strong current, the touch of something unfamiliar—it retracts instantly, sealing its tube with a opercular plate, becoming stone-becoming, invisible, safe.

These three groups—sponges, anemones, worms—constitute the first layer of colonization. They are the surface upon which all other surfaces depend. Larvae settle on them; fish hide among them; predators hunt through their thickets. They are the city's new skin, grown over the old skeleton of stone, transforming the structures into something organic, something alive.

They do not think. They do not remember. They simply are, and in their being, they make the city what it has become.

## The Predators

In the deeper channels, where the water flows slower and the dark thickens, the predators wait.

They are not cruel. Cruelty requires intention, requires the capacity to imagine another's suffering and choose to cause it. The predators have no such capacity. They have hunger, and they have patience, and they have bodies evolved over millions of years to convert hunger into survival.

The largest of them is called, by those who name things, a *gulper*—though this name is generous, is almost affectionate, describes an animal that inspires fear but also a kind of respect. Its body is elongated, almost eel-like, with a head that seems too large for its frame, a jaw that can unhinge to swallow prey larger than itself. It does not hunt actively; it drifts, nearly invisible in the dark, its body producing almost no electrical field, its movement minimal, a patient suspension in the water column until something edible comes within range.

Then it strikes. The expansion is sudden, violent—a generation of water displaced as the jaw opens, as the throat expands, as the prey is sucked in by the vacuum created. The strike takes less than a second. The digestion takes days.

The gulpers are solitary. They have no social structure, no communication, no recognition of each other beyond the basic chemical signal that says *this is one of mine*. When two gulpers meet, they ignore each other. When a gulper finds another's kill, it ignores it—fresh kills are not their prey, not their concern. They want living things, things that move, things that register on the lateral lines as presence rather than absence.

Smaller predators hunt differently. The *snatch-fish*—another approximate name—are active pursuers, their bodies streamlined for bursts of speed, their fins adapted for sudden turns. They hunt in pairs, though not cooperatively—each snatch-fish attempts to catch prey before its partner can, competition rather than collaboration. They favor the schools, the dense aggregations of small fish whose numbers make targeting easy. A snatch-fish can eat its own body-weight in a single feeding, and then it rests, motionless in a channel, digesting, for days.

The strangest predator is also the smallest: a colonial organism, a mat of specialized cells that spreads across surfaces and releases neurotoxins into the water. It does not move. It does not hunt. It simply waits for prey to brush against it, to settle on it, to come close enough for the toxins to take effect. Then the mat absorbs the dying body, dissolving it over hours, incorporating its nutrients into the collective.

The other inhabitants have learned to avoid these mats. The fish-schools flow around them, registering something in the chemical signature, a warning encoded in the water. The predators do not hunt near them, knowing that the toxins affect them too. The mats are avoided, left alone, permitted to grow in their patient, silent, dangerous way.

They have been growing for centuries. The oldest mats span square meters, thick layers of predatory patience, slowly accumulating the organic matter that drifts into their reach.

## The Deep Grazers

Not everything down here hunts. Some things simply eat.

The deep grazers are large, slow, almost philosophical in their approach to survival. They drift through the city at speeds that barely register as movement, their mouths filtering water continuously, their bodies processing the marine snow that falls from above, the organic particles that drift from the vents, the waste products of all the other organisms that share their world.

The largest of them resembles nothing so much as a floating boulder, a massive body protected by a thick carapace, its legs reduced to vestigial stumps, its existence reduced to the basics: filter, process, excrete, reproduce. It has no predators—nothing down here can crack its shell, nothing has the jaw-strength or the patience. It moves through the city like a mobile terrain-feature, a piece of geography that happens to breathe.

Smaller grazers are more active. The *scroll-swimmers* are named for the spiral shape of their shells, for the way their bodies coil and extend as they move. They browse the sponge-colonies, scraping away layers with specialized mouth-parts, processing the material in digestive systems designed for low-nutrient diets. They are the gardeners of the deep, keeping the sponge-colonies from overgrowing, maintaining the balance that allows all the other species to coexist.

But the grazers are also carriers. They move between districts, between plazas, between the thermal vents and the slower channels, and they carry with them the larvae of other species, the spores of sponges, the eggs of fish that have adhered to their shells. They are the transportation network of the deep, the vectors that connect the disparate parts of the ecosystem, that keep the gene-pool mixed and the populations viable.

They do not know this. They do not know anything.

## The Vent-Dwellers

Around the thermal vents, where the water is warmest and the mineral content highest, life is densest—and strangest.

The bacterial mats are the foundation. They are not visible, not exactly, but they register as a texture, a thickness in the water, a faint chemical signature that says *life* in a way that the surrounding medium does not. They oxidize sulfur compounds, they metabolize dissolved metals, they convert geology into biology, the heat of the earth's core into the foundation of a food chain.

On these mats, other things grow. The *tube-gardens* are named for their appearance: thickets of long, white tubes, each housing a worm that extends chemosynthetic bacteria-rich appendages into the mineral-rich water. They are farmers, in a way—cultivating the bacteria that feed them, harvesting the energy that rises from below. They have no mouths, no digestive tracts. They have only the internal gardens that make their existence possible.

The vent-fish are different from their cousins in the city proper. They are smaller, denser, their bodies adapted to the higher temperatures and the chemical richness. They live fast lives—spawning early, growing quickly, dying young, their bodies recycled by the bacterial mats within days of death. They do not form schools; they do not need to. The density of food around the vents makes aggregation unnecessary, and the constant competition for position makes cooperation impossible.

But the strangest vent-dwellers are not animals at all.

They are structures. Growths. Calcium-carbonate formations that rise from the vent-openings in patterns too regular to be entirely natural—spires and arches and platforms that channel the flow of warm water, that direct the mineral-rich currents toward specific locations, that seem almost designed.

They were not designed. They grew. The bacteria that live in their surfaces deposit minerals in response to chemical gradients, and over centuries, those gradients have produced patterns, the accumulated result of millions of small decisions made by organisms that cannot decide.

And yet the patterns persist. They have persisted long enough to become architecture, to become a city within a city, to become a place where the vent-fish gather and the tube-worms cluster and something about the arrangement suggests intention, suggests meaning, suggests that even here, in the deepest warmest dark, life cannot help but build.

## The Spiral Ones

And then there are those who do not fit.

You notice them first in the patterns: a school of fish swimming in perfect circles, their bodies twitching in unison, their movements coordinated in a way that schools do not coordinate. An anemone-colony grown in a spiral, its tendrils arranging themselves in a geometry that seems too precise to be accidental. A tube-worm thicket rising in a helix, each worm positioned exactly so, the whole formation approximating a shape that the worms could not conceive.

These are the Spiral Ones.

They are not a separate species. They are individuals—fish, anemones, worms, occasionally a grazer or a predator—who have been... touched. Changed. Their behavior altered, their growth-patterns shifted, their existence bent toward some purpose they cannot understand and no observer has explained.

They tend to cluster near certain buildings. The orientation of these structures is specific: doorways facing inward, windows facing downward, walls aligned with currents in ways that seem deliberate. The Spiral Ones who live near them show the strongest effects—circles that are tighter, spirals that are more precise, coordination that extends across species boundaries in ways that should not be possible.

A Spiral Ones fish will follow a Spiral Ones anemone's chemical trail, not as predator or prey but as... companion. Fellow traveler. Participant in something that neither can articulate. A Spiral Ones tube-worm will grow toward a Spiral Ones sponge, will form connections, will share nutrients across species lines in ways that no biologist would predict.

The effect spreads slowly. A fish that spends enough time near a Spiral Ones structure will begin to show symptoms—a twitch, a tendency to circle, a gradual alignment with the pattern. The change takes weeks, months, sometimes years. It is not harmful, exactly—Spiral Ones individuals live as long as their unchanged cousins, reproduce as successfully, function as well in the ecosystem.

But they are different. And they know it, in whatever way such things can be known.

The other inhabitants avoid them. Not fearfully, not aggressively, but with the quiet withdrawal of animals who sense something wrong, something off, something that does not fit the patterns they understand. The Spiral Ones live in their own district, their own quarter of the city, their own section of the warm dark—a place where the currents move differently, where the chemical signatures are strange, where the buildings themselves seem to participate in the pattern.

If you asked them what they are doing—and you could not ask, there is no asking down here, no language in which to frame the question—they would not be able to tell you. They are not conscious of the pattern. They do not choose to participate.

They simply do. They are moved by something older than instinct, something encoded in the stones themselves, something that the original builders left behind not as a message but as a frequency, a resonance, a slow song that certain individuals can hear and most cannot.

The Spiral Ones are not unhappy. They are not suffering. They are simply... aligned. Organized. Part of something that exceeds their individual lives, that connects them to a purpose they cannot name.

Perhaps it is beautiful, in its way.

Perhaps it is what the builders intended, all those centuries ago, when they shaped stone and ceramic into forms that would outlast their civilization by millennia. Perhaps they did not build a city. Perhaps they built a song, a pattern, a way of being that could be inherited by whatever came after.

The Spiral Ones are what came after.

They do not know what they are.

## The Old Ones

And then there are those who were here before.

Before the city. Before the stone was shaped. Before anything built or designed or intended—there was life in the warm dark, life that had been here for millions of years, life that would be here for millions more.

The Old Ones are not a species. They are an age.

They are the bacterial mats that underlie everything, the chemical-processing foundation on which all other life depends. They are the archaea that thrive in the hottest vents, metabolizing sulfur compounds at temperatures that would cook other organisms. They are the deep-dwelling colonies that grow so slowly they are nearly geological, their boundaries expanding by centimeters per century, their existence measured in epochs rather than lifespans.

You do not see them. You do not interact with them. They exist below the threshold of perception, below the scale at which the city's inhabitants operate. And yet they are the basis of everything—the ones who made the warm dark livable, who established the chemical cycles that all other organisms exploit, who were here first and will be here last.

They have no consciousness, no awareness, no sense of self. They are process rather than being, metabolism rather than existence. And yet, in their patient, invisible way, they are the ones who truly inhabit this place—not the city, but the rock below it, the vents below that, the deep hot living earth from which all warmth and all life ultimately flows.

They are the ones who will inherit the city in the end, when the stone has crumbled and the water has receded and all that remains is the slow patient metabolism of things that were never young and will never die.

## The Visitors

Occasionally, things come down from above.

They are rare—the pressure, the distance, the darkness are too much for most surface-dwelling organisms. But sometimes a deep-diving whale descends past the point where it should be able to survive, its body adapted for depths that would crush lesser creatures. It passes through the city without stopping, without seeing, its clicks and whistles registering on the inhabitants' senses as something vast and alien and temporary, a weather-event rather than a presence.

Sometimes a piece of wreckage drifts down—a fishing-net, a shipping-container, a fragment of something built on the surface and lost to the waves. It settles on the seafloor, becomes a new surface for colonization, is incorporated into the ecosystem within decades. The inhabitants do not distinguish it from natural stone. It is all the same to them: surface, structure, something to grow on.

Very rarely, a vessel comes. Mechanical, powered, filled with creatures who breathe air and see with eyes and move through the warm dark in ways that disturb the water and register as wrong. The inhabitants sense these visitors from far off—the electrical fields of their equipment, the displacement of their movement, the chemical signature of their existence. They do not flee. They do not approach. They simply adjust, flowing around the intrusion like water around a stone, returning to their patterns once it has passed.

The visitors never stay long. The pressure, the dark, the cold-if-not-for-the-vents are not meant for them. They come, they observe, they leave. The city remains.

And in the wake of their passing, the water settles, the particles drift, the schools reform—and the warm dark continues its patient, its ancient, its unbroken breathing.

## What Lives Here

So.

What lives in the Submerged City of Warm Dark?

Everything that can.

Everything that finds in this place the conditions for survival: the warmth, the nutrients, the surfaces, the shelter, the absence of the surface's cruel illumination. Everything that has evolved or adapted or drifted into this ecology and found a niche, a role, a way to continue.

Fish that do not know they are blind. Sponges that do not know they are ancient. Predators that do not know they are feared. Spiral Ones that do not know what they are part of. Old Ones that do not know anything at all.

And beneath it all, beneath the visible and the nameable, something else.

A presence.

Not a god—the sea does not worship, and the gods of the deep are philosophical constructs, not entities. Not a spirit—spirits require consciousness to observe them, and consciousness here is thin, distributed, doubtful. Not a ghost—there is no haunting here, only habitation, only the simple fact of continuation after the builders have gone.

A pattern, perhaps. A resonance. A frequency that the stones were tuned to emit and the water was shaped to carry and the inhabitants have learned, in their various ways, to hear.

The Spiral Ones hear it most clearly. The schools hear it as instinct, as the urge to gather and move in synchrony. The sessile colonies hear it as a guide to growth, to expansion, to the patient work of becoming a city's new skin. The predators hear it as a boundary, a limit, a sense of where not to hunt. The Old Ones hear it as background, as the medium in which they have always existed, as the hum that has underscored their patient metabolism since before anything else was alive to hear.

And you, if you are here—if you are suspended in the blood-warm water, floating between the towers, surrounded by the dense, living, breathing silence—you might hear it too.

Not with your ears. Not with any sense you can name.

But as a feeling. A presence. A sense that the city is not empty, has never been empty, cannot be empty because the stones themselves remember what it was to be shaped by intention, and that memory has become a kind of life.

The city is inhabited.

It has been inhabited since before it was a city.

And it will be inhabited until the sea itself is gone, until the warmth fades and the dark becomes absolute, until the last bacterium in the deepest vent finally stops its ancient work and becomes, once more, simply mineral.

That is what lives here.

That is what the warm dark holds.
