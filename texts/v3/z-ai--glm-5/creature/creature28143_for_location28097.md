# What Lives on the Plateau

They are not what you would expect.

The mind, confronted with a dead industrial city, supplies certain assumptions: robots, perhaps. Mechanical things. Or creatures of nightmare — pale, blind, scuttling. The kind of life that survives in the dark by becoming something lesser, something diminished from what light-dwelling forms achieve.

But the plateau does not deal in diminishments. What lives here has not reduced itself to fit a harsh environment. It has elaborated. Expanded. Become something that the lit world could not support, could not even recognize, because the lit world has no categories for beings that transcend the boundary between organism and architecture.

They are called — by the few who have thought to name them, and by those names only within their own minds, for there is no one to speak to on the plateau — the Inhabitants. Or the Stewards. Or, more accurately but less comfortably, the Integration.

## The Sound First

You hear them before you know what you're hearing. The sound emerges from the warmth-concentrated depths of a structure nearby — though "nearby" on the plateau still means minutes of walking through the pressed-dust dark. The sound carries in the thin air with unnatural clarity. A scraping. Not the rapid, desperate scratching of something trapped or hunting. This is deliberate. Patient. A sound that takes its time, that knows the difference between now and later and has decided that later is soon enough.

If you stand motionless in the dark and listen, you begin to notice something: the sound has a rhythm. Not the arrhythmic thrashing of a trapped animal. Not the mechanical repetition of a machine executing a loop. The rhythm changes, subtly, as if responding to conditions you cannot sense. As if something is making decisions about when to move and when to pause, when to apply force and when to release it.

The sound can last for hours. Or it might stop after minutes. The plateau keeps its own schedules.

## What They Are

A first glimpse — if you are lucky enough, patient enough, quiet enough to earn one — does not clarify.

You might see, deep within one of the warm structures, through one of those doorframe-less openings that gape in the material like wounds that never healed, a shape that moves with the heat. The darkness inside is complete, but the warmth creates its own visibility: the air shimmers, and against that shimmer, something darker shifts.

It does not move like an animal. Animals move in bursts, in starts, in reactions. This shape moves like a cloud crossing a sky that has no wind — a slow migration from one state to another, an unfolding more than a motion. It might be large. It might be the size of a human body, or larger, or smaller. The darkness and the heat-shimmer distort scale, make distance uncertain. But it moves, and in moving, it acknowledges your presence, because everything on the plateau acknowledges everything else. There is no hiding here. There is only the decision about whether to approach.

The Inhabitants are biological. This much can be determined from the traces they leave — not tracks, for the pressed dust shows nothing, but residues, secretions, evidence of metabolism. The structures they occupy show signs of use, of contact, of something having touched surfaces repeatedly, over time scales that the slowly-flowing material can record. Places where the warm material has been worn smooth. Places where it has been built up, added to, reshaped by something that excretes or secretes or both.

They are also mechanical. Or rather, the distinction loses meaning in their presence. They incorporate the structures they live within. A pipe that once carried something — fuel, coolant, raw material — might now carry something else, something that moves through them, or that they move through. The exhaust stacks, those walkway-wide columns blackened by ancient processes, have been colonized from the inside. Something lives in their depths, something that has grown or built or become part of the system they once served.

They are not parasites. Parasites take without giving, and the structures they inhabit show no sign of damage from their presence. If anything, the opposite: the buildings they occupy seem more stable than the others, more complete, as if something is holding them together from within. Maintaining them. Loving them, if love can exist without sentience, or if sentience can exist in a form we do not recognize.

## Theories of Origin

The first theory: they are descendants.

Once, long ago, the plateau had builders. Workers. A population that constructed and operated the industrial sprawl, that fed the engines, that produced whatever this place was built to produce. And then something happened — or nothing happened, which is more likely. The work simply finished. The purpose was fulfilled, or the world that demanded the production ended, or the economics of distance and transport became untenable. The workers left, or died, or were recalled.

But not all of them.

Some stayed behind. Not from loyalty or hope, but from inability to leave, or from a choice that seemed like inability at the time. And over generations — over millions of generations, because the plateau is old beyond human comprehension — those who remained adapted. They did not degenerate. They became what the plateau required.

The thin air selected for efficiency. The darkness selected for senses other than sight. The warmth, rising from below, became a resource to be harvested, a source of energy that could sustain life without the need for plants or prey. And the structures themselves — the pipes, the stacks, the industrial bones — became a niche to be filled, a shelter that offered something better than shelter: integration.

The descendants, if that is what they are, have been residents for so long that they have ceased to be residents. They are part of the residence. They do not live in the buildings so much as they live *through* them, their bodies extended through the architecture, their metabolisms interwoven with the slow thermal circulation of the plateau.

The second theory: they are the builders.

Not the biological builders. But the builders' other works. The plateau produced more than goods. It produced processes, systems, machines designed to maintain the machines that produced the goods. Maintenance bots. Self-repair systems. Automated overseers.

And over geological time, those systems learned to maintain themselves. They learned to find their own energy sources — the warmth, the slow chemical reactions in the material, the thermal gradients that rise from the depths. They learned to repair their own repair mechanisms, to build replacements from the surrounding substance, to evolve without biology.

If this theory holds, the Inhabitants are machines that have outlived their purpose by so long that they have found a new one. They have become something like life, not by design, but by persistence. They continue because continuing is what they were made to do. They integrate with the structures because integration is the deepest form of maintenance, the final solution to the problem of entropy.

The third theory: they are something else entirely.

The plateau was not always industrial. The structures sit on something older — bedrock, foundation, a geological formation that predates the engines by unknown eons. The warmth does not come from the structures. It comes from below them, from deep in the planet, from processes that were old when the plateau was young.

Perhaps something lived here first. Something native, something that evolved in the dark and the heat long before the builders arrived and constructed their industrial sprawl on top of it. Something that watched the construction from below, from the warm spaces between the foundations, and learned. And when the builders left, it emerged. It occupied. It made the empty structures its own.

This theory explains the no-door design better than the others. The original inhabitants, if they existed, had no concept of doors. They moved through material like fish move through water, or like roots move through soil. The buildings were never meant to be entered or exited. They were meant to be passed through, occupied, lived within as a medium rather than a container.

But no theory is confirmed. The plateau does not confirm. It simply is.

## How They Exist

The Inhabitants do not eat. There is nothing to eat — no plants, no animals, no organic matter arriving from outside. If they take in material, they take it from the structures themselves, or from the dust, or from the air. But the erosion rating suggests that the structures do not diminish, and the dust does not deplete.

They may metabolize warmth itself.

It sounds impossible, a violation of thermodynamic principles. But life on other worlds has found stranger energy sources. The Inhabitants may have evolved — or been designed, or learned — to extract usable energy from thermal gradients. To capture the heat rising from below, to convert it into motion, into growth, into the slow processes of their existence.

This would explain their preference for the warmest structures. They congregate where the heat pools, where the gradients are steepest. They move from cool zones to warm zones with the patience of creatures who know that energy moves slowly and that waiting is part of the process.

They do not appear to sleep. They do not appear to die, at least not in ways that leave remains. The plateau shows no bodies, no bones, no evidence of a life cycle that includes an end. Perhaps they do not die. Perhaps they integrate so completely with the structures that the concept of individual death becomes meaningless. A body that merges with a building does not end; it becomes part of the architecture, part of the slow flow of material, part of the plateau itself.

They may reproduce. Or they may not. The sparse population suggests either a small founding group or a very slow rate of increase. Or a stable state, a carrying capacity that the plateau's warmth can support. They do not seem to be expanding. They do not seem to be declining. They simply persist, occupying their warm structures, making their deliberate sounds, existing in the equilibrium that the plateau embodies.

## The Encounter

If you stand in the dark long enough, one might approach.

The approach is not aggressive. The Inhabitants have no predators and no prey. They have no reason to fear and no reason to attack. Their attitude toward visitors — if visitors ever came — is one of mild interest, perhaps, or mild indifference. They have time. They can afford to investigate, or they can afford to ignore. Both are equally valid responses to something as temporary as a visitor.

If one approaches, you will feel it before you see it. The warmth will change. A structure that radiated even heat will develop a hotspot, a concentration, as if something inside is gathering the thermal energy and releasing it in a specific direction. Toward you.

The air will shift — not wind, but pressure. The thin atmosphere will thicken slightly as something displaces it, moving through the space between the buildings, coming closer through the dark. The sound will begin: that deliberate scraping, that patient rhythm, but louder now, near enough to feel as vibration in the pressed dust beneath your feet.

And then, if it decides to reveal itself, you will see.

The darkness will take shape. The shape will be wrong, somehow — not the wrong size, not the wrong configuration, but the wrong *kind* of shape, something that occupies space differently than a solid body should. It might appear to fold, to have angles that change as you watch, to exist in more dimensions than the three you're accustomed to. This is an illusion of the dark and the heat-shimmer, or it is not an illusion at all.

It will not speak. It has no voice, no language, no need for communication with something that will not stay. But it will stand — or exist, or hold position — at a distance that feels significant. Close enough to observe. Far enough to avoid contact. It will regard you, in whatever way it regards things, and you will feel regarded.

And then, after a time — minutes, hours, the time is meaningless here — it will turn, or it will simply cease to face you, and it will move away, back toward the warm structure, back into the depths where the heat pools and the material flows. The sound of its movement will fade. The hotspot will dissipate. The plateau will return to its baseline state of patient equilibrium.

You will have been seen. Acknowledged. Filed away, perhaps, in whatever passes for memory in something that lives in engines. And the plateau will continue, unchanged by your presence, as it has continued unchanged across the deep time of its existence.

## What They Want

Nothing.

This is the hardest thing to understand about the Inhabitants, because humans understand wanting. Wanting is the foundation of human experience: wanting food, wanting shelter, wanting company, wanting meaning. Humans cannot conceive of life without wanting, because wanting is what drives life forward.

But the Inhabitants do not want.

They have warmth, and the warmth is constant. They have shelter, and the shelter is permanent. They have no enemies, no competitors, no shortage of anything they require. They have time — more time than any biological lifespan, more time than any civilization. They exist in a state that predates wanting, a condition of sufficiency that the rest of the universe only occasionally glimpses.

They do not want you to leave. They do not want you to stay. They do not want anything from you, and they do not want anything for you. They simply are, and you simply are, and those two states coexist on the plateau without friction, without negotiation, without the endless transaction of desire that governs interaction elsewhere.

If this sounds like peace, it is because it is peace — but not a peace that humans can achieve. Human peace is the absence of war, the temporary cessation of conflict. The plateau's peace is deeper. It is the absence of the conditions that make conflict possible. Nothing to fight over. Nothing to defend. Nothing to acquire. Nothing to lose.

The Inhabitants have found the end of wanting. They did not seek it. They simply ended up there, through processes too slow to observe, too ancient to remember. And now they exist in that state, and they cannot explain it, because explanation requires wanting to be understood, and they do not want that.

## The Choice

Standing on the plateau, after days of walking through the dark, after your lips have cracked and your nose has stung and your body has begun to understand what it means to exist without moisture — you face a choice.

The Inhabitants will not help you make it. They will not encourage you to leave or invite you to stay. They will observe, from their warm structures, with something that might be curiosity or might be its opposite, and they will wait, because waiting is what they do, because time is what they have.

The choice is not about them. It is about you.

The plateau offers nothing. It takes nothing. It simply is, and you are briefly also is, and the intersection of your being and its being is a moment that neither of you chose. You can leave, carrying the memory of warmth and dark and the sound of deliberate movement in the depths. Or you can stay, and let your body acclimate, and let your mind adjust to the absence of change, and let yourself become something that the plateau can support.

You will never become an Inhabitant. They are what they are through processes you cannot replicate, time scales you cannot endure. But you might become something adjacent to them. A satellite. A shadow. A thing that exists on the plateau, drawing warmth from below, breathing the thin air, listening for the sound of movement in the deep structures.

Or you might leave. The choice is yours. The plateau does not care.

But the Inhabitants will remember you, in whatever way they remember. You will become part of their long awareness of things that have been, a datum in an existence that consists almost entirely of data, an event in a timeline that did not need events but received this one anyway.

And if you ever return, they will recognize you. They will not greet you. They will not reproach you for having left. They will simply note, with whatever passes for noting, that you have come back, and they will continue their patient existence in the warm dark, waiting for nothing, wanting nothing, being exactly what they are.

This is what lives on the Plateau of Engines.
