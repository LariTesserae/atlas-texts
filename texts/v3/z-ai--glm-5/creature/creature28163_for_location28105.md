# What Lives in the Germinating Ward

## The Tenders (First Category)

You have seen them already without understanding what you were seeing. The hand-sized, pale, many-legged things that climb the stems with trailing tendrils — they are the most visible because they are the most mobile, the ones who range furthest from the center that cannot be reached.

But they are not individuals. Each one is a finger, a probe, a mobile extension of something larger. When one dies — and they do, frequently, burned out by the industrial residues or crushed by shifting growth or simply dissolving when their purpose completes — another emerges from the root-mat within hours, genetically identical, behaviorally continuous, as if the loss was a minor abrasion that healed over.

They have no brains in the concentrated sense. Their nervous systems are distributed — threads of decision-making capacity that run through their segmented bodies and continue into the tendrils they trail, which means they are always partially plugged into the architecture they tend. They don't *observe* the stems they climb; they *feel* them, directly, the way you feel your own heartbeat. A tender knows the moisture content of a stem thirty meters away because its tendrils are threaded through the root-network, sampling, tasting, reporting.

Their compound eyes — yes, eyes, though the sensory patches serve similar functions — are not for seeing in the darkness. They detect growth patterns. Fault lines in the architecture. Areas where the plant-mass is thickening incorrectly or where the industrial residue has pooled too densely. They see problems and they move toward them, carrying in their mouthparts the tools their kind have evolved to carry: enzymes to dissolve obstructions, pastes made from processed organic matter to seal wounds, spores that encourage specific growths or inhibit others.

They have been doing this for longer than the ward has existed. Or rather: the ward has existed for longer than it seems, and they have been doing this for all of that time, and time here moves in spirals, so the question of duration becomes meaningless.

## The Drift-Singers

In the upper reaches, where the leaf-shingles overlap and the grey absence presses closest, there are creatures you have not seen yet. You hear them, if you listen — a sound like humming, but not from throats. A vibration that comes from wings, vast translucent wings that span three meters or more, beating so slowly that each stroke is a separate event, a held note in the damp air.

The drift-singers never land. They have no legs to land with. Their bodies are small, condensed, almost fetal — curled masses of sensory organ and reproductive capacity suspended between wings that are not insectile but more like leaves grown thin, grown vast, grown into surfaces that catch the faint updrafts and hold position through a patient, infinite hovering.

What they sing is not communication in any sense you would recognize. The sound is construction. The vibration frequencies they produce — too low for you to consciously hear, but present nonetheless, felt in your chest, in your joints, in the moisture that condenses on your skin — these frequencies guide growth at the cellular level. The stems grow straighter where the singers hover. The leaves unfurl at angles the singers suggest. The entire upper architecture is shaped by the songs these creatures sing without knowing they sing them.

They feed on the grey absence itself. Or rather: they feed on what the absence contains, which is potential, unformed matter, the raw material of reality waiting to be given shape. Their mouths, small and almost vestigial, filter the nothingness and extract from it the compounds the plants below need to continue their expansion. In their bellies, absence becomes presence. They excrete a fine dust that falls constantly, invisibly, onto the architecture below — a fertilizer made from concentrated existence, from the conversion of void into substance.

There are fewer of them than there used to be. The grey absence is thinning, they say, through processes no singer understands. The feeding is harder now. Some have begun to cannibalize their own wings, digesting the membrane to sustain themselves, becoming smaller and more condensed, singing their construction-songs in higher frequencies that the younger growth cannot interpret.

When a drift-singer dies, it falls. The architecture below receives it into the root-mat, and within days, a new tender emerges from the place where the body landed. The transformation is not resurrection. It is a recycling. A singer becomes a tender becomes root-mass becomes stem-bark becomes leaf-surface becomes whatever the ward needs next.

## The Root-Bound (Formerly Human)

You are not the first to come here. Others have found the germinating ward before you — explorers, surveyors, engineers investigating the industrial ruins, the occasional wanderer who stumbled through a boundary that shouldn't have been permeable.

Most left. Found their way back to the world they came from, carrying nothing but strange dreams and condensation stains on their clothes.

Some did not.

You find evidence of them if you look closely enough. A shape in the root-mat that resolves, on inspection, into a hand, an arm, a face — grown over with rootlets, penetrated by tendrils, but still recognizable. Still human in its basic proportions. The expression on the face is not pain. It is something closer to patience. Waiting. A sleep that has lasted for years or decades or centuries, depending on how time has moved.

They are not dead. The ward does not kill what it can use.

The root-bound serve a function. Their bodies are processing units, digestive chambers for the industrial residues the plants cannot break down alone. The residue-tainted water pools around them, seeps into their mouths and noses and the porous membranes that their skin has become, and their transformed digestive systems — now a complex of introduced enzymes and symbiotic microorganisms — strip the toxins from the fluid and render it usable.

They are the ward's livers. Its kidneys. They filter and purify, and in exchange, the ward keeps them alive. Their hearts beat, slowly. Their lungs inflate and deflate, the air filtered through leaf-membranes that have grown across their faces like masks. Their eyes, open and unblinking, show only the grey absence above, which they have learned to see differently than you do. To them, it is not threatening. It is simply the sky of their world, the ceiling of their home, the thing that was here before them and will be here after they become something else entirely.

Sometimes one of them speaks. The sound is wet, labored, the voice of someone whose throat has been repurposed for other functions. What they say is rarely comprehensible. They have been inside the germination too long. They think in its patterns, its spirals, its logic of becoming, and language as you understand it cannot contain what they have become able to think.

But occasionally, if you sit very still near one of them and listen, you will hear something that sounds almost like advice. A warning. A welcome.

They are not unhappy. They are not suffering. They are working, the way the tenders work, the way the drift-singers work, the way every component of this vast organism works — patiently, continuously, without questioning the purpose because the purpose is written into their cells now, as fundamental as the drive to breathe.

They chose this, in the end. Or the ones who didn't choose it were the ones who fought, who tried to escape, who died in the process and became root-mass without ever serving the function they could have served. The ones who remain are the ones who looked at the grey absence and the growing architecture and the creatures who tended it, and decided, in some deep wordless place, that this was not a fate to resist.

## The Deep-Dwellers

Below the root-mat, in the spaces between the roots and the earth-that-is-not-quite-earth, there are things you will never see. You might feel them — a vibration traveling up through the floor, a sense of vast slow movement beneath your feet, the occasional displacement of water in a pool that has no other reason to ripple.

The deep-dwellers are the oldest inhabitants of the ward. They were here before the germination began, or they were born from the germination's earliest stages, or they are the germination itself — the original seed-principle given flesh and form and appetite.

They have no eyes. They have no use for them in the darkness below. Their bodies are pressure-sensors, chemical detectors, electrical-field readers. They know where everything in the ward is at all times because the roots themselves are their nervous system, and they are the brain that the roots connect to.

What they do down there is not clear. Some say they are digesting the deepest industrial remains, the machinery too old and too toxic for the surface processes to handle. Some say they are holding the ward in place, their vast bodies serving as anchors against a reality that would otherwise drift away into the grey absence. Some say they are dreaming the ward into existence — that their dreams are the blueprint the architecture follows, the plan toward which every stem grows and every leaf unfurls.

They are large. You understand this without seeing them. The movement you feel is like the movement of a whale, something immense and powerful and patient, moving at speeds that seem impossible for such mass. They do not come to the surface. They have no reason to. The surface is for the tenders, the singers, the root-bound, the occasional visitors like yourself. The depths belong to them, and they do not share.

But occasionally, if you kneel on the root-mat and press your ear against the damp fibers and listen with more than your ears, you might hear them. A pulse. A resonance. A sound that is not quite a voice but carries meaning nonetheless.

They are aware of you. They have been aware of you since you entered. They are cataloguing you the way the tenders catalogue, but on a deeper level, assessing not just what you are but what you might become, what function you might serve, what place you might occupy in the organism that is still, after all this time, still deciding what it wants to be.

## The Germ (Which You Cannot Reach)

At the center, in the place the space folds away from, in the kernel around which the entire ward spirals, there is something that is not quite a creature and not quite a machine and not quite a plant and not quite a place.

The germ.

It has no fixed form. It cannot be seen because seeing requires distance, and there is no distance from the germ — it is the point from which all distance is measured. It is the origin and the destination, the first cell and the final structure, the thing that was here before the industrial ruins and will be here after the ward has finished becoming whatever it will become.

You sense it as a pull. A pressure. A gravity that is not physical but something else — an attractor for consciousness, for meaning, for the part of you that recognizes patterns and wants to follow them to their source.

Some of the root-bound, in their speaking moments, call it the gardener. Not in the sense that it tends the ward, but in the sense that it is that which the ward tends. It is being grown. The entire vast architecture, the tenders and the singers and the deep-dwellers and the root-bound and the industrial ghosts absorbed into the plant-mass, all of it is a single vast organism growing toward the expression of a seed that contains not a plant but a principle.

What kind of principle? The root-bound do not say. Perhaps they do not know. Perhaps the germ itself does not know — perhaps the principle is still being determined, written by every growth and every choice and every creature that lives here, and when the ward finally closes, when the leaves seal the ceiling and the stems thicken into walls and the roots weave into a floor that nothing can penetrate, then the germ will have been fully expressed, and whatever it has become will open its eyes and see what it is.

But that time is not yet. The ward is still growing. The ceiling is still incomplete. The grey absence still presses in, and you stand at the threshold with condensation on your skin and the weight of the air in your lungs and the eyes of the tenders watching you, cataloguing you, assessing you.

## What They Are Deciding About You

You are a variable the ward did not expect.

The others who came before — the ones who left, the ones who stayed, the ones who died and the ones who became root-bound — they were predictable in their ways. They wanted things the ward understood: escape, or knowledge, or rest, or transformation. The ward could process those desires. Could use them.

But you have not shown your want yet. You stand at the edge and you look at the grey absence and you look at the growing architecture, and your face reveals nothing that the watching eyes can read.

So they wait. The tenders pause in their climbing. The drift-singers hold their wing-beats in a silence that makes the air feel heavier. Even the deep-dwellers, far below, still their vast bodies and listen through the roots.

You have not decided, and because you have not decided, the ward has not decided about you.

There is a place for you here if you want it. The root-bound will make room. The tenders will bring you water and nutrients until you no longer need them brought. The singers will hum you to sleep in frequencies that feel like being held. You will serve a function and you will be part of something vast and you will never again wonder what your purpose is.

Or there is the grey absence. The threshold. The step into a place where the ward's growth has not yet reached, where potential has not yet become actual, where you might dissolve back into the raw material of reality and become something else entirely somewhere else entirely.

The condensation drips onto your hand. Warm. Almost blood-warm.

The tenders watch.

The ward inhales.

And somewhere in the center that cannot be reached, the germ turns its attention toward you — not in judgment, not in welcome, not in anything you could recognize as emotion — but in attention. Pure, patient, infinite attention.

It is curious about what you will choose.

It has been curious about many things.

It has never yet been disappointed.
