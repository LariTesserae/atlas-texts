# What Lives in the Verdant Engine

---

## The People

They are not tall. This is the first thing you notice, or rather the first thing you understand about what you have been noticing. The people of the plateau are shorter than you expect, compact, dense in the way of organisms that have grown in a particular medium for a very long time — not stunted, not diminished, but *compressed*, as if their bodies have been shaped by the same forces that shaped the plateau itself, the same slow pressures that ground mountains to flatness and built forests in the void. Their limbs are proportioned differently from what feels familiar. Longer arms, shorter legs, broad hands with thick fingers and nails that are darker than skin, almost woody in their toughness. They move through the city with a gait that is half-walk, half-climb, a fluid locomotion that makes use of surfaces you would not think to step on — the curved flank of a root-wall, the jutting knob of a trained growth, the woven lattice of a path that seems to be more vine than floor.

Their skin runs a range of tones that you eventually understand corresponds to something more complex than lineage. There is a greenish undertone in many of them — not pigment, exactly, but something subcutaneous, a quality of the tissue itself that suggests a long cohabitation with the chlorophyll-bearing world. You learn later that this is not imagination. The people here carry symbionts in their skin, microorganisms inherited from mother to child, that contribute a small but measurable fraction of their metabolic energy through photosynthesis. It is not enough to sustain them without eating — they are not plants — but it is enough to change the texture of their relationship with light. They are drawn to sun, not merely for warmth but for something subtler, a faint nutritional pull. In the dim interior corridors of the city, they move with a slight lethargy that lifts when they reach the open plazas. Children, you notice, play in the light.

Their faces are broad, flat-featured, expressive in ways that take time to read. The musculature around the eyes and mouth is more complex than what you are accustomed to, capable of gradations of expression that your own face cannot produce — or perhaps you are simply seeing more because you are looking harder. They smile, they frown, they grimace, but they also do things you have no names for: a slow softening of the brow that seems to indicate consent or agreement, a particular tightening of the nostrils that signals attention, a way of canting the head to one side that opens a conversation without requiring words. They are not silent people — they speak, they call out to one another across the workshop floors and through the corridors, their voices low and resonant, shaped by a language that is built more on tone and rhythm than on discrete phonemes. You hear it as music before you hear it as speech, a rising and falling cadence that matches the ambient sound of the city itself, as if their language grew in the same medium as the forest, shaped by the same acoustics.

There are not many of them. This is something that takes time to register, because the city is large and the structures are numerous, and the constant activity of the industrial processes creates an impression of population that is deceptive. But when you start counting, when you pay attention to the faces you see in the workshops and the paths and the communal eating spaces, you realize that the settlement holds perhaps two thousand people. Maybe fewer. They live in clusters — family groups of three to eight, extended networks of kinship that occupy interconnected chambers in the larger growths, sharing cooking spaces, work areas, child-rearing duties. The architecture supports this: the buildings are not single-family dwellings but composite structures, grown over generations, with rooms that connect and reconnect as the needs of the inhabitants shift. A family's home is not a fixed space but a negotiated one, an agreement between the people and the living architecture about which hollows belong to whom, which surfaces are private, which are shared.

Children are born here, but not many. The population is stable, not growing — or growing so slowly that it is indistinguishable from stasis. You see young people, adolescents, infants held against chests in slings of woven fiber, but the proportion is low, the birth rate apparently matched to something — the carrying capacity of the settlement, perhaps, or some understanding of what the city can support, or some deeper alignment with the slow time of the plateau itself, where things happen on scales that make human generations feel brief. Parents do not seem anxious about their children. The young are given freedom early, allowed to explore the corridors and plazas, to learn the city's geography by living in it, to absorb the logic of the place through their feet and skin and the rhythms of their days. The children navigate the shifting streets with an ease that you, an adult, cannot match. They have grown up inside something that thinks, and they understand it the way you understand your own house — not through study but through inhabitation.

The elders are the ones who interest you most. They are not many — the population structure is not pyramidal, more like a column, with roughly equal numbers at each stage of life, suggesting long lifespans and low mortality. The oldest among them move slowly, but not with the frailty you associate with age. Their bodies have thickened, their skin darkened, their hands become more woody still, and there is something about them that suggests less decline than *consolidation*, a gradual transformation from soft animal tissue into something more durable, more integrated with the substance of the city. The oldest woman you see has skin the color of dark bark, deeply lined but not fragile, and she sits in a plaza where the afternoon light is strongest, her eyes closed, her hands resting on the living surface of the ground, and you have the unshakeable sense that she is listening to something you cannot hear. Not the ambient sound of the city — something deeper, something that moves through the substrate, the root-network, the ancient stone. She is receiving. She is in communication.

The people do not have a word for what she does, or if they do, it does not translate. When you ask about her, about the elders, about the way they sit and listen, you get answers that circle the subject without landing on it. "She is with the city." "The city speaks to her." "She is old enough to hear." There is no hierarchy here, no priesthood, no formal structure of authority — but there is a recognition that certain people, at certain stages of life, develop a capacity for a kind of attention that others lack. Not power. Not control. *Attention*. A willingness to receive what the living infrastructure offers, which is not information in any form you recognize but something more like mood, weather, the emotional state of a system too large and too old to think in terms you would understand.

The people work. This is not a pastoral existence, not a return to nature, not a fantasy of harmony. The work is real and constant and hard. The manufactories run on shifts, and the people staff them — tending the gas-capture systems, monitoring the resin-distillation forges, operating the presses and grinders and lathes that turn raw plant material into useful components. The work requires knowledge that is both practical and deeply specialized: an understanding of the metabolic states of the living substrate, the ability to read the quality of sap by color and viscosity, the skill to graft and prune and shape the growing architecture without damaging its structural integrity. There are masters and apprentices, though not in a formalized way — expertise is recognized by demonstration, and authority is earned through years of visible competence, not granted by institution or credential. A woman who has spent forty years shaping the living conduits that carry charge through the city is deferred to on matters of the electrical system, not because she holds a title but because her hands know what no one else's hands know.

The work is dangerous, sometimes. The machines are real machines — gears, pistons, cutting edges, presses with enough force to crush bone — and the biological processes that power them are not always predictable. Gas pressure builds unexpectedly in root-channels and must be vented before it ruptures the system. Resin distillation involves heat and volatile compounds, and fires, when they start, spread through the living substrate with a ferocity that is frightening, the spongy fibrous ground burning slow and deep and stubborn, requiring not water but excision, the cutting away of burning tissue from healthy tissue in an operation that is more surgical than firefighting. People are injured. People die, though rarely — the safety practices are old, embedded in the culture, a body of precaution so ingrained that it has become habitual, nearly instinctive. You watch a man working a resin-press and you see that his hands move with a care that is not anxious but absolute, each motion deliberate, each grip tested, each step placed with a consciousness of what the machine could do if the pressure shifted. He has been doing this for years. He may have been doing it for decades. The machine he tends is older than he is, grown and shaped and maintained through generations of workers, each one adding their understanding to the accumulated knowledge of its operation.

But the work is also satisfying in a way that you can feel even as an outsider. The people here are not alienated from their labor. There is no gap between the worker and the product, no abstraction of effort into wage, no mediation of the relationship between what you do and what you make. The things they build — tools, components, vessels, structural elements — are used in the city, by people they know, in processes they understand. The energy they harness is the energy of the place itself, and the products of that energy serve the place itself, and the people who do the work are the people who live in the place. The economy is circular not by ideology but by nature. Nothing is wasted because waste has no place to go. The substrate absorbs what is discarded, breaks it down, reincorporates it. The people know this, feel it, and it shapes their relationship with material things: an ease with possession that is neither ascetic nor extravagant, a sense that objects are temporary states of matter, that everything will eventually return to the substrate and become something else.

They make art, though you are not sure they would call it that. The walls of living chambers are often carved — shallow relief patterns that follow the grain of the wood, abstract but suggestive, shapes that could be leaves or faces or the branching of root-systems or none of these. The carvings darken with age, the living tissue healing around them slowly, so that the oldest walls bear palimpsests of images, earlier patterns half-overgrown by later ones, layers of expression embedded in the physical structure of the building itself. There are also objects — small things, held in hands, kept in pockets, placed on the grown shelves of living rooms — that have no practical function. Smooth stones, fragments of ancient rock from the worn-down mountains below the substrate, shaped and polished and kept for their weight and texture. Pieces of amber, hardened resin that has trapped something — an insect, a fiber, a bubble of ancient air — and that are valued for what they contain, not what they are. Objects that the machines produce unintentionally, the strange and beautiful things that emerge from the forest's autonomous production, are collected, traded, displayed, studied. They are the closest thing the culture has to religious artifacts, and the people who gather and interpret them are not quite priests but something adjacent — curators of the inexplicable, keepers of the city's dreams.

The people are not afraid. This is important, and it takes you a long time to understand it, because from the outside the plateau is a frightening place — the wrongness of the geometry, the shifting of streets, the objects that do not hold their shape, the sense that the city is thinking and that its thoughts are not human thoughts. You expect the people to be afraid, or at least uneasy, and the absence of that fear is itself unsettling. But they have lived here for generations, for centuries, perhaps for longer than anyone can remember, and the strangeness of the place is not strange to them. It is home. The city thinks; the city shifts; the city produces objects that cannot be explained; the city's geometry is unstable in ways that should be terrifying. And the people love it. Not in spite of these things but with them, through them, because of them. The wrongness is part of the fabric of their lives, woven into their routines and their relationships and their understanding of what it means to be a person in a place. They have made a covenant with something that does not think in their terms, and the terms of that covenant are not written or spoken but lived, generation after generation, in the slow patient practice of inhabiting a mind that is not their own.

## The Animals

The animal life of the plateau is sparse, specialized, and strange. Not strange in the way the city is strange — not wrong, not liminal, but adapted to a niche so specific that it has become idiosyncratic, shaped by forces that have no parallel elsewhere.

The pollinators come first, because they are the most visible. They are not insects, though they are insect-like — small, many-legged, with bodies of a material that is neither chitin nor skin but something between, a flexible carapace that shifts color with temperature. They are dark in the cool mornings, almost black, and they lighten as the day warms, becoming translucent amber by afternoon, so that you can see the dark shapes of their internal organs through their bodies. They move between the flowering growths of the city's architecture with a purposefulness that is mechanical, not poetic — they are not aesthetic creatures, not ornaments, but functional components of a system, and they work with the single-mindedness of parts in a machine. They are attracted to certain wavelengths of the bioluminescent light, and at night they cluster around the brighter patches, their bodies catching the faint blue-green glow and retransmitting it, a relay system that extends the light deeper into the city's corridors than the luminescence alone could reach.

There are the burrowers, who live in the root-layer. They are rarely seen but often heard — a scratching, shuffling sound that comes from beneath the ground, the movement of small bodies through the fibrous substrate. They are the size of a human hand, roughly cylindrical, with powerful forelimbs adapted for tunneling through the dense root-mat. They feed on the decomposing matter that accumulates in the lower layers, breaking it down further, accelerating the cycling of nutrients through the system. They are blind, or nearly so — their eyes are reduced to dark spots beneath the skin, non-functional, unnecessary in a world where there is no light. They navigate by touch and by chemical sense, detecting the gradients of moisture and nutrient that run through the substrate like rivers through rock. The people know them by their sounds and by the patterns of their burrowing, which can weaken the ground in certain areas and require reinforcement. There is a profession — or at least a recognized skill — for mapping the burrower tunnels and predicting where the substrate will need support. The people who do this work are patient and attentive, lying on the ground with their ears pressed to the surface, listening for the scratch and rustle of unseen lives below.

The bird-things are the most unsettling of the visible animals. They are called something in the local language that does not translate — the closest approximation is "branch-walkers," though this misses the quality of their movement that makes the term necessary. They are the size of large crows, with bodies that are dense and low-slung, wings that are present but secondary, used more for balance than for flight. They can fly, but they prefer not to. They walk. They walk on branches, on walls, on the surfaces of buildings, on the ground-cover of the paths, with a gait that is fluid and jointed in a way that reminds you of nothing so much as a hand moving across a surface, feeling its way. Their legs are long, their feet articulated with more digits than seems necessary, and they grip the surfaces they walk on with a casual expertise that is both impressive and slightly disturbing. They are not afraid of the people, and the people are not afraid of them — there is a mutual indifference that is not hostile but simply disinterested, two species occupying the same space with different concerns. The branch-walkers call in the early morning, a sound that is not a song but a series of resonant tones, low and buzzy, that carry across the plazas and echo off the woody walls in ways that make the source difficult to locate. The people say the branch-walkers are talking to the city. They say this matter-of-factly, without emphasis, the way you might say that a dog is barking at a squirrel.

The grazers come at night. They are large — the size of small cattle, though built lower and broader, with bodies that seem designed for moving through dense growth rather than across open ground. Their heads are wide and flat, their mouths adapted for cropping the tough ground-cover that would otherwise choke the transit paths. They are the city's street-sweepers, its maintenance crew, and the people encourage their presence by leaving certain growths untrimmed, providing feeding corridors that guide the grazers along the routes that need clearing. The grazers are slow, deliberate, and almost entirely silent — you hear them only as a soft tearing sound, the ripping of fiber, and the occasional deep exhalation of a large body breathing in cool night air. They vanish before dawn, retreating to the deeper parts of the forest-city where the paths are wider and the growth is thicker, where they spend the day in a state of rest that is not quite sleep and not quite wakefulness, a rumination that seems to involve not only their food but their environment, a slow processing of the place itself.

There are predators, though you do not see them. You know them by their effects — a burrower found on the surface, dead, its body opened by something with claws that are precise and deliberate; a branch-walker that suddenly falls silent in the middle of its morning call, the resonance cut short as if a switch has been flipped. The people speak of them rarely and without fear — they are part of the system, necessary, the pressure that keeps the prey-species from overpopulating. The people have a name for them that translates roughly as "the ones who are between," and you are not sure whether this refers to their ecological position between predator and prey or to something else — their habitat, perhaps, in the spaces between the structures, the gaps in the city's fabric where the forest is thickest and the light does not reach.

The animal life, taken altogether, is a thin scattering of adapted species in a world that does not need them. The energy flow of the plateau runs through plants, through roots and substrate and the slow oxidation of living matter, and the animals are incidental to this flow — minor branches on a vast metabolic tree, drawing their energy secondhand from a system that would function without them, that did function without them for a long time before they arrived. They are not native to the plateau, the people say — or at least, they came later, after the forest-city was established, finding niches in an ecosystem that had not been built with them in mind. They are opportunists, survivors, the descendants of species that wandered up from the valleys below and found, against expectation, that there was room for them here. They adapted. They became what the place could support. They are few, and they are strange, and they are not what the plateau is about.

## The Things That Are Not People or Animals

There are other presences in the city. Not spirits, not ghosts in any sense you would recognize — but things that exist, that have a quality of *being there* that is difficult to dismiss and impossible to describe.

The unintended objects are the most tangible of these. They accumulate in the workshops and manufactories, produced by machines that are doing what they were designed to do but producing, alongside the intended output, other things. The workers describe this as the city making things through the machines, and while this is not an explanation you find satisfying, it is also not entirely wrong. The objects are physical — they have mass, texture, a material presence that does not dissolve when you look away, though some of them have properties that make their physicality uncertain. They are made of the same materials as the intended products — cellulose, lignin, resin, compressed fiber — but they are arranged in forms that serve no known function.

Some are geometric: nested polyhedra, surfaces that curve in ways that are almost but not quite regular, structures that seem to be models of something that does not exist. These are collected and studied by the people who have made this work their specialty, and their studies are meticulous and patient and have been ongoing for generations, and they have produced no conclusions. The objects do not do anything. They do not change, they do not emit, they do not respond. They simply are, and their being-there is the fact that requires explanation, and the explanation does not come.

Some are figurative, and these are the ones that trouble you. Crude approximations of hands, of faces, of the branching of limbs — not sculptures, not representations, but *attempts*, as if something that did not know what a hand looked like had been told about hands and was trying to produce one from description. The people who study these objects handle them with a care that is not quite reverence but is more than curiosity. They compare new ones to old ones, tracking the development of the forms over time, and they note that the approximations have become more accurate over the years — not better crafted, but more correct, as if the maker were learning, were refining its understanding of what these shapes should be. The maker is the city. The maker is the forest. The maker is the system itself, working through the industrial processes, through the machines, through the material substrate, trying to produce something that it does not understand and cannot see, something that it knows only from the inside, from the way bodies move through it, from the pressure of feet on its surfaces, from the warmth of living flesh inside its hollows.

Some objects simply do not behave. They are heavier when you hold them than when you set them down. They produce a faint sound that is not a hum but is like a hum, a vibration at the edge of perception. They are warm when there is no source of heat. They are cold when the air is warm. They do not stay where you put them — not dramatically, not obviously, but you mark their position and you return and they have shifted, slightly, a few centimeters, and no one has touched them. The people say these objects are *the city's own things*, and they do not keep them in their homes. They are stored in a building — a specific building, grown for the purpose, its walls thicker than usual, its interior space shaped in ways that are not quite regular — and the people who tend this building are the ones who have the deepest engagement with the city's non-human intelligence. They are not priests. They are not shamans. They are something that does not have a name in any language you know — curators, perhaps, or interlocutors, or simply the people who are willing to pay attention to what the city does when it is not being a city.

The sound that resolves into voices is another presence. It is not hallucination — multiple people hear it, describe it in similar terms, and it is captured, imperfectly, by recording devices that the city's limited technology can produce. The recordings are not clear. They sound like what they sound like in person: a layering of mechanical noise and biological process that, in certain moments, in certain lights, at certain times of day, takes on a quality that is not speech but is adjacent to speech, a patterned vocalization that has rhythm and contour and a something-like-grammar that is not any grammar used by any person who lives there. The people listen to it. They do not interpret it, not in any systematic way — they do not believe it carries messages, they do not treat it as language to be decoded. They treat it the way you might treat the sound of the sea: as the voice of something so large that its utterances are not directed at you, are not *for* you, but are simply the sound it makes in the process of being itself.

And there is the city itself, which is the largest and most inscrutable presence on the plateau. It is not a metaphor to say that the city thinks. The people do not mean it metaphorically, and the longer you stay, the less metaphorical it seems. The living substrate is a network — a vast interconnected web of root systems and fungal associations and chemical signaling pathways that links the entire plateau in a single continuous system. Information moves through this network: chemical gradients, electrical potentials, pressure changes, the slow shifting of growth patterns in response to stimulus. It is not a brain in any anatomical sense. It does not have neurons, does not have a central processor, does not have anything that corresponds to the architecture of cognition as you understand it. But it processes. It responds. It changes its behavior based on input, and the changes are not simple reflexes — they are complex, patterned, and in some cases, novel. The city produces new things. It opens new paths. It closes old ones. It grows structures that no one asked for, and it lets structures that were useful die back and decompose. It does this on its own schedule, in response to its own logic, and its logic is not human logic.

The people have lived inside this logic for so long that they have stopped trying to understand it. They do not seek to control the city's autonomous behavior. They do not resent it. They work *with* it, the way a farmer works with weather — not as an equal, not as a master, but as a participant in a process that is larger than either party, a collaboration between human intention and something else, something that has its own intentions, or something that is not quite intention but that occupies the same functional space. The city provides. The city maintains. The city shifts, and the shifts are sometimes beneficial and sometimes not, and the people adapt, and the adapting is the work of their lives, the central labor of living in a place that is not static but alive.

What lives in the Verdant Engine, then, is not a list of species. What lives there is a relationship. The people live there, and the animals live there, and the machines and the unintended objects and the voices in the dusk and the slow ancient thinking of the substrate itself — all of these live there, and they live there together, in a coexistence that is not harmonious in any sentimental sense but is *functional*, is *ongoing*, is the shape of a life that has been negotiated over centuries between minds that do not understand each other and have agreed, without words, to share a place.

The plateau holds them all. The worn-down rock, the root-mat, the living architecture, the shifting geometry, the air that smells of sap and rain and slow time. The city breathes. The people breathe. The forest thinks. The machines hum. The animals move through it like traces of another world, a reminder that life is not always plant, not always city, not always the thing that happens when a surface is so old it becomes possible. And the wrongness, the shimmer at the edges, the objects that do not hold their shape — these live here too, not as problems to be solved but as qualities of the place, aspects of its nature, the things that make it what it is and not something else.

You came here looking for what lives in a place. What lives here is the place itself, and everything in it is the place, living.
