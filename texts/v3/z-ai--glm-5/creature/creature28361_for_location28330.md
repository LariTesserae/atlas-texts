# What Lives in the Gallows Garden of Yu'uthaal

## The Gardener

There is one who breathes in the hall.

She is old — not in the way that stone is old, but in the way that a sentence is old: she has been spoken so many times that the words have worn smooth, and you can no longer tell what she was supposed to mean. Her body, if it can be called a body, has settled into the shape that long residence in stone creates. She has pressed against the same walls for so long that she has begun to take on their geometry. Her skin is pale, faintly blue-white in the fog-light, and it has the layered quality of the stratified rock outside — as though she, too, has been worked on by moisture and time.

She does not move much. Movement, for her, is a kind of speech, and she is past the age of speech. What she does instead is listen: to the birds in the doorways, to the lizards on the stones, to the insects on their chemical roads. She listens with her whole body. The breathing that the traveler heard is her listening — the slow expansion and contraction of attention, drawing the basin in and releasing it, drawing and releasing, a tide of awareness that the animals have learned to move with.

She was not always alone. This is important. The settlement was built by hands, and some of those hands were hers. She remembers — the shapes of the others, the ones who cut the stone and fitted the triangular doors and carved the channels that carry water. They are gone now, but gone is not the same as absent. She carries them in the pattern of her breathing: one breath for each name, cycling through them in the long nights, keeping their names alive by saying them to the dark.

Her name, if it matters, is something that would not translate. It is a sound that sits between the teeth, a pressure rather than a word. The birds know it. The lizards know it. The things in the trees carry fragments of it, syllables distributed across their bodies like seed-pods waiting to open.

She is not a god. She is not a ghost. She is a gardener — the Gardener, the only one who remains — and what she gardens is silence.

## The Tree-Kin

The things in the trees are her children. This is not metaphor. They are literally, biologically, astonishingly her offspring, though they have not yet become what they will be.

They hang because hanging is what their bodies require. Their spines are soft, still forming, and the downward pull of gravity draws them out, stretches them toward the shape they will eventually inhabit. They are pale where they press against bark, dark where the fog-light touches them, and their coloration shifts over time as they absorb the qualities of the trees they cling to. This is not camouflage. This is nutrition. They eat tree. Slowly. Patiently. A single tree can sustain one of them for decades, and by the time the tree is gone, the thing that fed on it has taken on its properties — its grain, its density, its particular patience.

They are not mindless. They have minds, but their minds work at a speed that human perception cannot track. A thought takes them months to complete. A decision can take a season. They watch the Gardener in the hall and they learn from her, but what they learn is not information — it is posture, it is orientation, it is the particular way that she breathes in counterpoint to the fog. They are being taught how to inhabit this place. How to become it.

At night, when the fog thickens and the breathing from the hall grows louder, they descend. Not to the ground — never to the ground, that boundary is not theirs to cross yet — but to the lower trunks, to the junction of root and bole, where they can feel the vibration of the Gardener's presence more directly. They press their ears — which are not ears, which are patches of sensitive skin located along their jawlines — against the bark, and they listen.

They are learning how to be still. It is the hardest lesson. Stillness is not a natural state for living things; it must be cultivated, practiced, held in the body like a difficult pose. The Gardener has mastered stillness. Her children are still learning.

There are twelve of them. The number is important. When one of them finally matures — which will happen soon, within a century, within a breath of geological time — it will descend from the tree and walk into the hall on legs that are still discovering their shape, and it will take its place beside the Gardener, and the breathing will change. It will become two breaths. Interlocking. A pattern.

And then the Gardener can rest.

## The Birds

The birds are her messengers.

They are not pets and they are not servants and they are not worshippers, though they perform something that looks, from the outside, like devotion. What they are is functional. They carry things: seeds, fragments, small pieces of organic matter that the Gardener needs distributed to specific locations. They understand the geometry of the basin in a way that no human mind could replicate. They know that a seed placed in the crotch of that specific branch will, in three generations of fern, produce a plant with slightly broader leaves that will catch more condensation and redirect it toward a particular stone where a particular lichen is trying to establish itself. They do not know why. They know that the Gardener breathes a certain way when they place the seed correctly, and that breathing sounds like home.

They are silent because sound is not their medium. They communicate through movement, through the angle of their wings, through the precise timing of their arrivals and departures. A flock of them passing across the basin is a sentence being written; the shapes they form are punctuation. The Gardener reads them as they pass and knows, from their configuration, what is happening in the parts of the basin she cannot see.

They roost in the doorways because doorways are thresholds, and thresholds are where information accumulates. They face inward because the Gardener is the source of the pattern they follow; facing outward would mean facing away from meaning.

They were not always birds. This is something that even they do not quite remember. There is a sense, in the oldest of them, that their ancestors once had a different shape — something that walked, something that spoke, something that made the triangular doors and fitted the stone. But that shape is gone now, compressed by time and by the Gardener's need into something smaller, something that can fly, something that can carry. They have become what she required them to be. This is not a tragedy. This is gardening.

## The Lizards

The lizards are her editors.

They correct. This is their function. The birds distribute, but distribution is imprecise — seeds fall slightly wrong, moisture collects in unintended places, the natural chaos of organic systems introduces errors. The lizards fix these errors. They move pebbles that have shifted out of alignment. They adjust the position of fronds that have grown at angles the Gardener did not intend. They consume the insects that have accumulated in wrong places, and they leave alone the insects that are where they should be.

They do not enter the buildings because the buildings are the original pattern — the baseline from which all corrections are measured. To enter a building would be to contaminate the standard. They can approach the threshold, can study the precise angles of the triangular doors, can memorize the exact placement of every stone, but they cannot cross. The Gardener does not forbid this. It simply does not occur to them. The boundary is written into their instincts in the same way that gravity is written into their bones.

They are translucent because transparency is useful to her. She can see, through the pale skin of their bellies, the movements of their organs, the flow of their blood, the rhythm of their small hearts. If something is wrong with a lizard — if it has eaten something harmful, if it carries a parasite, if its systems are failing — the Gardener can see it, and she can signal to the other lizards to carry that one away, to remove it from the system before its failure becomes infectious. This is not cruelty. This is maintenance.

They watch because watching is how they learn what needs correcting. They track the movement of the traveler through the basin and they make note of where his footsteps have disturbed the moss, where his presence has shifted the air, where his body heat has left residue in the stone. Later, when he is gone, they will come to those places and they will smooth the moss, they will restore the temperature, they will erase his passage.

This is their purpose. The Gardener tends the garden; the lizards tend the Gardener's work.

## The Insects

The insects are her memory.

They are small because memory is distributed. No single insect carries more than a fragment — a chemical signature, a spatial coordinate, a trace of the Gardener's intent — but together, in their thousands, they hold the entire pattern of the basin. Every stone's position. Every fern's growth trajectory. Every droplet's preferred path from leaf to root. The insects contain all of it, encoded in the secretions they leave along their invisible roads.

They do not cross thresholds because thresholds are places of decision, and decision is not their function. They follow. They maintain. They remember. Crossing a threshold would require choosing, and choosing would fracture the unity of their collective memory. So they flow around doorways like water around posts, preserving in their unbroken patterns the continuous record of the Gardener's design.

When two insects meet and touch antennae, they are synchronizing. Each carries a piece of the pattern; when they meet, they exchange pieces, comparing notes, ensuring that the record remains consistent. This is why their intersections look so deliberate — they are. Every crossing is a moment of consensus, a small agreement about what the garden is and what it should be.

They are not individual minds. They are neurons in a distributed brain, and the Gardener is the consciousness that brain produces. She thinks through them, remembers through them, holds the shape of her garden in their multitude of small bodies. When the fog comes and they slow and stop, it is because she is dreaming, and the dream requires stillness.

## The Hall

The hall is where she waits.

It was built for waiting. The triangular doorway is a shape that directs attention — the eye follows the angles inward, toward the point where they would meet, which is to say toward the darkness, which is to say toward her. The walls are bare because decoration would distract from the breathing. The floor is packed earth because she needs to feel the vibrations of the basin through the soles of her feet.

The darkness inside is not empty. It holds her, obviously — the traveler heard her breathing — but it also holds what remains of the others. Not their bodies. Their bodies are gone, dissolved into the soil, incorporated into the roots of ferns, digested by insects, redistributed across the basin. What remains is their attention. The hall is thick with accumulated listening. Generations of it. The Gardener breathes in the presence of all those who came before, and their attention shapes the air she inhales.

The sounds the traveler heard — the breathing, the humming, the clicking, the wet turning — were the sounds of remembering. She was cycling through her recollections of the builders, the ones who cut the stone and made the doors and established the pattern. The humming was her holding their voices in her throat. The clicking was her reproducing the sound of their tools. The wet turning was her body performing the motions of their work, muscle memory that has outlasted the muscles.

At night, when the fog thickens and the animals gather, she performs. This is the wrong word, but it is the closest word. She breathes the history of the garden into the damp air, and the animals receive it. The birds roosting in the doorways absorb it into their silent bodies. The lizards on the stones memorize it through their translucent skin. The insects incorporate it into their chemical trails. The things in the trees learn from it, study the shape of her remembering, prepare themselves for the day when they will need to remember too.

This is not worship. This is transmission.

## The Garden

The garden is not a place. It is a process.

What the traveler saw — the ferns and mosses and lichens and trees — is the visible surface of a system that extends deep into the rock and high into the fog. The roots of the horizontal trees connect to fungal networks that connect to bacterial colonies that connect to mineral deposits that connect to the slow geological processes that are still, after only a few centuries, deciding what shape the basin wants to be. The Gardener tends all of it. Not through action — she is too old for action — but through attention. Through the steady rhythm of her breathing. Through the distribution of her children, her birds, her lizards, her insects. Through the patient, relentless pressure of her intention, shaping the growth of this place cell by cell, droplet by droplet, moment by moment.

This is why the traveler felt the place was curated. It is. Every element is exactly where she needs it to be, doing exactly what she needs it to do. The spacing of the ferns creates channels for the flow of condensation. The moss on the boulders regulates the temperature of the stone beneath. The lichens on the cliff faces slowly, deliberately break down the rock into soil that will, in a thousand years, support a different kind of growth.

She is not making a paradise. She is not making a monument. She is making a lung.

The basin is a respiratory system. The fog is its breath. The Gardener is the muscle that drives it — the diaphragm that pulls fog in and pushes it out, that circulates moisture and spore and nutrients through the tissues of this small, enclosed body. When she breathes, the basin breathes. When she stops, it stops.

And eventually, she will stop. That is why she is training the things in the trees. That is why she has distributed her memory across a thousand insects. That is why she has made the birds her voice and the lizards her hands. When she finally exhales and does not inhale, the system she has built will continue. One of her children will descend into the hall. It will take her place. It will begin to breathe.

And the garden will go on.
