What Lives in the Bocca Highlands

## I.

There is a frog that lives only in the mineral pools of the thermal zone. It is small enough to sit on your thumb, and it has no name in any human language because no human language has ever needed to name it. Science would call it something Latinate, diagnostic, a description of its characteristics rendered in dead syllables. But here, in the warm wet breath of the plateau, it is simply what it is: a soft brown body with skin so thin you can watch its heart beat.

Three beats per second when it is still. Faster when it moves. You can see the heart through the translucent membrane of its chest—a dark knot pulsing in a bed of pale tissue. Behind it, the digestive tract coils like wet rope, and above it, the eyes bulge, golden, watching, always watching. The frog does not blink. It has no eyelids. It has never needed them. The perpetual moisture of the plateau keeps its eyes wet, and the diffuse light requires no shielding.

It is called, in the grammar of the plateau's internal logic, something that translates roughly to *the one who waits in warm water*. This is not a name. It is a function. The frog does not think of itself as an individual. It thinks of itself as a component—a sensor, a processor, a node in a network that covers the thermal zone in a living web of amphibious attention.

---

## II.

The frogs communicate in ways that are not entirely sound.

They do chirp—the wet-fingers-on-glass sound described before—but this is only the audible edge of a much larger signaling system. Their skin, permeable and sensitive, picks up vibrations in the water: the displacement of a footfall, the subtle pressure change of a fumarole exhaling, the particular ripple of a lizard sliding into a pool. They read chemicals dissolved in the mineral-rich water: the faint traces of sulfur that indicate a vent is about to release steam, the organic compounds left behind by insects, the alarm pheromones released by other frogs when something approaches that eats them.

A frog in the amber pool knows what is happening in the milky-blue pool fifteen meters away. It knows because the water tells it. The network of shallow channels connecting the pools carries information like a nervous system carries pulses, and the frogs are the neurons, receiving and processing and sometimes, when necessary, signaling back.

When a predator enters the thermal zone—a lizard, usually—the frogs nearest to it stop calling. Then the frogs near those frogs stop. Then the next ring, and the next. Within thirty seconds, a radius of silence expands outward from the intrusion, mapped by the absence of sound. The predator cannot locate its prey by listening. It must hunt by sight, and sight is poor in the mist.

The frogs know this. They have evolved for this. Their brown bodies match the wet stone, their translucent skin catches the diffuse light and breaks their outline. They are patient. They can wait for hours without moving, their hearts slowing to two beats per second, one, half, their metabolism dropping into a doze while their skin stays alert, tasting the water for the signal that danger has passed.

---

## III.

The lizards are named, in the plateau's grammar, *the swimmers who eat the ones who wait*.

They are longer than your hand, sleek and dark, with bodies that have forgotten how to walk on land. Their ancestors, millions of years ago, were terrestrial—ordinary lizards that climbed and ran and basked on rocks. But the thermal pools offered something that dry land could not: constant warmth, constant water, and a food source that was already trapped, already concentrated, already waiting to be harvested. Over evolutionary time scales, the lizards moved in. They stopped leaving. Their legs, no longer necessary, began to shrink.

Now they are almost legless—two small flippered appendages near the tail, useful for steering but not for walking. They cannot climb out of the pools. They do not need to. The pools contain everything they require.

They swim with sinuous undulations, their bodies like flexible cables, their tails flattened into rudders. Their eyes are set high on their heads, and when they surface, only the nostrils and the eye ridges break the water. They can hold a position in the warm mineral water for hours, hovering, watching, their metabolism elevated by the heat so that they need to eat more frequently than their ancestors would have, but also able to move faster, strike faster, process information faster. The thermal pools have made them into creatures of accelerated function. They live shorter lives than a lizard of comparable size would live in a temperate climate—five years, maybe six—but they live those years at a pace that would make a lowland reptile seem comatose.

They are not social. They tolerate each other in the pools because they must—there are only so many warm mineral basins, and leaving is impossible—but they do not cooperate. Each swimmer hunts alone, patrolling its chosen pool, rising to breathe and then sinking again, scanning the depths for the brown shadows of frogs or the glint of insects on the surface. When they are not hunting, they rest in the warmest parts of the pools, where the mineral vents emerge from the stone, letting the heated water flow over their bodies, their metabolisms running in a simmer of low activity.

They have no predators. Nothing else can enter the pools and survive. The water is too warm, too mineral-saturated, too hostile. The swimmers are the apex predators of a contained ecosystem, and they will never leave it.

## IV.

There are birds here, though they are not like the birds of other places.

The most common is a small creature, about the size of a sparrow, with a body that is almost entirely darkness—soot-brown feathers that would look black if they ever dried, but never do. It does not sing. It has no song. Its syrinx, the organ that in other birds produces melody, has atrophied into something simpler—a valve that can produce clicks and rattles and a low grinding sound that might be distress or might be communication or might just be the noise of existence.

What it does have is a bill. The bill is extraordinary. It curves downward, long and thin and absurdly delicate, like a forceps extended beyond the bird's face, and it is designed for one purpose: probing. The bird uses it to probe the moss, the epiphytes, the dense mats of vegetation that cover every surface. It inserts the bill into crevices too narrow for any other creature to access, and it extracts the insects that live there—larvae, beetles, springtails, the teeming micro-fauna of the plateau's green skin. Tap tap tap. The sound is mechanical, regular, patient. The bird works like a machine, and it has been working for as long as there have been birds here.

They do not fly much. Their wings are short and rounded, adapted for short bursts between branches, not for distance. The mist makes long flight dangerous—you could fly into a cliff, into a tree, into the ground—and there is no reason to leave. The plateau is bounded by impassable edges. Everything the bird needs is here. It was born here, it will mate here, it will die here, and its body will fall into the moss or the water and be processed by the same ecosystem it spent its life harvesting.

They nest in the crotches of trees, deep in the moss, where the moisture is thick enough to keep their eggs damp but not so thick that they will rot. The female lays two eggs, pale and speckled, and she tends them alone. The male does not help. The male's role ends at copulation. This is not neglect; it is simply how the species has organized itself. The female builds the nest, incubates the eggs, feeds the chicks when they hatch, and teaches them, over the course of several weeks, how to tap, how to probe, how to find the insects that live in the green layers. Then she leaves. The chicks fledge. They begin to tap. The cycle continues.

There are no predators that hunt the birds. The lizards cannot leave the water. There are no snakes on the plateau, no mammals large enough to climb. The birds die of accident, of age, of disease, and their bodies disappear into the system that produced them, becoming moss, becoming mineral, becoming the next generation of insects for the next generation of bills to harvest.

---

## V.

The insects are a system, not a collection.

There are beetles. They are large, dark, and slow—their wing-cases heavy and grooved, designed to shed the constant water that tries to collect on their backs. They do not fly unless they must. They walk across the wet stone, across the moss, across the bark of trees, grazing on algae, on fungal hyphae, on the decaying matter that falls from the canopy above. They are the recyclers. Everything that dies on the plateau passes through them or through their larvae at some point, broken down and redistributed into forms that other organisms can use.

There are damselflies. They are thinner, more delicate than their lowland cousins, their bodies almost weightless, their wings iridescent in the diffuse light. They hunt at the edges of the streams and pools, hovering above the water, dipping to lay eggs in the film of moisture that covers everything. They live short lives—weeks, days—and they spend those lives in a constant hunt for smaller insects, for mites, for anything small enough to be caught in the basket of their legs and consumed in flight.

There are springtails. They are microscopic, countless, a living carpet that covers every surface where moisture accumulates. They eat the bacteria, the fungal spores, the organic detritus that settles from the air. They are eaten by the beetles, by the birds, by the frogs. They are the foundation of the food web, the layer between the living and the non-living, and they are everywhere, invisible until you look closely enough to see that the moss is not still, that the stone is not bare, that the whole plateau is shifting and crawling and processing at a scale below the threshold of ordinary attention.

And there are midges. Tiny, gnat-like, they emerge from the water in clouds when the mist thins, breeding in the mineral-rich pools, feeding on the microbial mats, providing food for everything that can catch them. They are the reason the damselflies exist. They are the reason the frogs can find meals small enough to swallow. They are the smallest visible link in the chain, and they are present in numbers that cannot be estimated—a billion, perhaps, or ten billion, or more, each one a speck of protein that will be consumed and transformed and consumed again in a cycle that has no beginning and no end.

---

## VI.

The microbial mats are the oldest living things on the plateau.

They coat the bottoms of the thermal pools in layers that are sometimes millimeters thick, sometimes centimeters, depending on the depth of the water and the mineral content and how recently a swimmer has disturbed them. They are communities, not individuals—bacteria and archaea and primitive algae living together in symbiotic sheets, each species processing what the others cannot, sharing resources, producing waste that becomes food for another layer, another type of cell.

The top layer is photosynthetic. It is green, or blue-green, depending on the species that dominates in a given pool, and it uses the diffuse light that reaches the water's surface to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar. Below it, the light dims, and different organisms take over—sulfur-eating bacteria that metabolize the hydrogen sulfide seeping from the vents, iron-processing archaea that harvest energy from the oxidation of dissolved minerals, methane-producing organisms that work in the anaerobic depths where the pools meet the stone.

The mats are alive in a way that larger organisms are not. They do not move. They do not respond to stimuli in any visible way. They simply grow, layer by layer, century by century, building up thickness and complexity and depth. A mat that is three centimeters thick may be thousands of years old. The bacteria at the bottom have not seen light since the mat was millimeters thin. They have been processing the same minerals, in the same dark water, since before human civilization existed. They will continue long after it ends.

They are the foundation. Everything that lives in the thermal pools—the frogs, the swimmers, the insects that lay eggs in the water—depends on them, directly or indirectly. They are the producers. They are the engine. The plateau breathes, and the mats are what it breathes for.

## VII.

There are larger things that live here, though they are rarely seen.

A mammal, perhaps. Something descended from a rodent or a shrew, isolated when the plateau rose and adapted over millions of years to the conditions that surrounded it. It would be small—nothing large could survive on the limited resources of a forty-square-kilometer island in the sky—and it would be elusive, hiding in the densest moss, emerging only at the times when the mist was thickest, when visibility was lowest, when it could move undetected between groves.

It would eat insects, probably. The birds have no competition for the probing niche, but the ground-level insectivore niche is open, and something would have filled it. It would have fur, but the fur would be dense and water-repellent, almost otter-like, to handle the constant moisture. It would have eyes adapted to low light—large, perhaps, or sensitive in ways that lowland eyes are not. It would have no natural predators, because nothing large enough to eat it exists on the plateau, and it would have no reason to fear anything except perhaps the swimmers, which cannot leave the pools and would not be interested in it anyway.

It might exist. It might have been seen, once, in the corner of an eye, a shadow moving through the moss that disappeared when the observer turned. It might be real, and it might be rare—so rare that even the plateau's internal logic barely registers it, a ghost in the system, a legend among the frogs, a rumor in the clicking of the birds.

Or it might not exist at all. The ecosystem might be complete without it, a closed loop of insects and frogs and lizards and birds and microbes, balanced and self-sustaining, needing nothing else.

The plateau does not reveal its secrets. The mist does not clear. The shape in the moss stays a shape, undefined, and then it is gone.

---

## VIII.

And then there is the thing that might be watching.

This is not paranoia. This is not projection. The plateau is a system of observation—a network of frogs tasting the water, of birds tapping the bark, of insects grazing every surface, of microbial mats processing every chemical that passes through them. Something is always sensing something. The whole landscape is a sensory organ, stretched across forty square kilometers, breathing and feeling and responding.

When you stand on the wet stone, when you feel the exhale of warm air rise around you, you are being perceived. Your feet displace water in the channels. Your body heat adds to the thermal signature of the plateau. Your breath mingles with the breath of the ground. You are leaving traces—chemical, thermal, physical—that will be read by the organisms around you, processed, integrated into the system's continuous assessment of what is here and what it means.

The frogs know you are there. Their chirping has adjusted, shifted in frequency and timing, to account for your presence. The birds have registered you—a large shape moving through the mist, something to be observed and tracked and, if necessary, avoided. The swimmers have felt your vibrations through the water, and they have surfaced to look, to assess, to determine whether you are food, threat, or nothing at all.

And beneath all of this, in the microbial mats and the stone channels and the roots of the moss, something older and slower is aware. Not conscious in any human sense. Not thinking, not planning, not reasoning. But processing. Responding. Adjusting. The plateau has been incorporating disturbances into its system for longer than anything human has existed. It will incorporate you. It will incorporate your stay, your departure, your absence, your memory. You are part of its metabolism now.

When you leave—if you leave—you will have changed the system. Slightly. Imperceptibly. But changed. The water you displaced will flow differently. The heat you added will dissipate into the air. The chemicals you left behind will be processed by the mats, integrated into their layers, become part of the slow geological record that is building itself in the mineral pools, one molecule at a time.

---

## IX.

But the plateau is not full.

This is what you understand, standing there in the mist. The landscape is alive, abundantly so, layered and complex and busy with processes—but it is not crowded. There is room here. The frogs occupy their pools, but the pools are not full. The birds work their territories, but the territories are not contested. The insects swarm, but they do not overwhelm. The ecosystem has found its balance, and the balance includes space—space for moisture to collect, for minerals to deposit, for the slow breathing of the ground to continue without obstruction.

This is why the industrial metaphor holds. A factory at capacity is chaotic, strained, every surface in use. A factory at equilibrium runs at a fraction of its potential, efficient and steady, with margins built into every process. The plateau is the latter. It could support more life—ten times more, perhaps, or a hundred—but it does not. The frogs limit themselves. The birds space their nests. The swimmers tolerate each other but do not crowd. Something in the system, some ancient regulatory mechanism, keeps abundance below the threshold of strain.

It might be the minerals. The water that seeps through the stone is rich in sulfur, in iron, in compounds that become toxic at high concentrations. Organisms that multiply too quickly might poison themselves. It might be the heat—too many bodies in a pool might raise the temperature past the tolerance of the frogs, past the tolerance of the mats. It might be something simpler: space, territory, the behavioral adaptations that prevent overcrowding in any ecosystem.

Or it might be the breathing. The plateau exhales, and in the exhale there is information. Chemical signals drift upward, disperse into the mist, settle onto surfaces. The organisms read them. They know what the plateau can support. They adjust.

This is speculation. It cannot be confirmed. But something is keeping the balance, and it has kept it for a very long time, and it shows no sign of failing.

---

## X.

The plateau's inhabitants do not know they are inhabitants.

This is important. The frog does not think of itself as living on the Bocca Highlands. The bird does not know that the plateau has edges, that beyond the escarpments lies a different world. The swimmer has no concept of a world outside the pools. They are here, and they have always been here, and they will always be here, as far as any of them can conceive.

They are not limited by this ignorance. They are defined by it. Their lives are contained, complete, meaningful within the boundaries of the system that produced them. The plateau is not a place they inhabit; it is the condition of their existence, the medium through which they move, the grammar in which their lives are written.

You, standing there, are the exception. You know there is a world beyond. You know there is a lowland, a sky, a sun hidden behind the mist, a planet spinning through space. You know you can leave. You know you will leave. And this knowledge separates you from everything around you, makes you a visitor in a landscape of residents, a temporary presence in a system of permanents.

The frogs do not envy you. The birds do not wonder what lies beyond the haze. The swimmers have no interest in what might exist outside their warm mineral prisons. They are complete. They are sufficient. They are what they are, and they will continue to be what they are, long after you have gone.

---

## XI.

At night, the plateau does not sleep.

There is no darkness here, not really. The mist glows faintly, reflecting the starlight that filters through, and the mineral pools give off a dim bioluminescence—the bacteria in the mats producing light as a byproduct of their metabolism, pale blues and greens that shimmer below the water's surface. The frogs keep calling. The birds keep tapping. The swimmers keep swimming. The system runs on the same rhythm it has run on for millennia, paused for nothing, waiting for nothing.

The temperature drops slightly—not much, a degree or two, enough to notice but not enough to change anything. The moisture thickens. The breathing of the ground continues, exhale, inhale, exhale, inhale, a rhythm that measures time in a way that has nothing to do with clocks or calendars or the rotation of planets.

Something moves in the darkness. A shape, perhaps, that was not there before. Or perhaps just a shadow, an illusion of mist and dim light, a trick of an eye that expects to see pattern and invents it where none exists. The plateau does not explain itself. It does not reveal what it holds in the hours when the light is lowest. It simply continues, patient, steady, breathing.

By morning—if morning can be said to exist in a place where the sun is never directly visible—the mist will be exactly as thick as it was. The pools will glow exactly as they did. The frogs will call from the same positions, the birds will tap from the same branches, the swimmers will surface in the same spots to breathe and watch and slip back beneath the mineral water.

Nothing will have changed. Everything will be exactly as it was. The machine will have run through another cycle, processing and producing and waiting for the next cycle to begin.

---

## XII.

What lives in the Bocca Highlands?

Everything that can. Everything that has found a way to exist in the wet, the warm, the mineral-rich, the isolated. Frogs with transparent skin. Lizards that cannot leave their pools. Birds that probe with needles of bone. Insects in uncountable number. Microbes older than memory. And perhaps—perhaps—something else, something that moves through the moss without being seen, that leaves traces that disappear before they can be identified, that haunts the edges of the plateau like a rumor of itself.

But more than any of these, what lives here is the system itself. The network. The web of water and stone and warmth and life that covers the plateau and gives it its character. The frogs are part of it. The birds are part of it. You, standing there, are part of it. And when you leave—if you leave—you will still be part of it, carried in the slow processing of the mats, the chemical memory of the water, the record of a presence that will take centuries to fade.

The plateau exhales. The mist swirls. The light stays soft. And in the warm wet breath of the ground, life continues, patient and permanent, waiting for nothing, needing nothing, simply being what it is.
