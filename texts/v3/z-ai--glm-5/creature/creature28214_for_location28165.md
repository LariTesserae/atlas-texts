What lives here is not yet a what.

That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest. The plateau supports life — the plants are alive, unquestionably, their tissues turgid with oil, their cells dividing at rates the warmth encourages — but the plants are not the inhabitant. They are the exhalation. You would not call smoke the inhabitant of a furnace. You would not call the light the inhabitant of a lamp. The plants are what this place is putting out into the air, and the thing putting them out is below, and is not done, and is not yet anything you could point to and name.

---

But something lives here. That is different from saying something is here. Presence is not life. A machine can be present. A geological process can be present. What lives here is present in the way that an organism is present — with interiority, with a boundary between self and not-self, with a metabolism that takes in and transforms and excretes, with a direction, with an unfolding that is not random.

The boundary is the stone. The two kilometers of vertical rock, sharp-edged, warm, striated — that is a membrane. Not a wall. A membrane, because it is selective. It lets some things through. It lets the fluid up, through the cut channels, through the fissures that were born with the rock. It lets the heat up. It lets the vibration up, the low pulse that you feel in your sternum and mistake for silence because your ears cannot translate it. It will, eventually, let other things up. It is not yet time for that.

And it keeps things out. The air, the sun, the wind — these touch the surface but do not penetrate. Rain will come someday, in a thousand years or ten thousand, and will not reach what is below. The stone will absorb it or shed it and what is below will not know. What is below has never known rain. What is below has never known sky. What is below knew only the dark and the pressure and the minerals in the dark, and then it knew its own intention, and then it raised the stone.

---

The intention is the thing that makes it alive.

Not intelligence. Not yet. Possibly not ever, in the way you would recognize it — the way that involves language, symbols, the recursive contemplation of self. But intention, yes. The difference between a process that proceeds because of its causes and a process that proceeds toward something. The plateau was raised toward something. The fissures were cut toward something. The fluid moves toward something. The plants grow toward something, and what they grow toward is not the sun — they are not heliotropic, you can see this in their branching, they orient by the mineral veins and not by light — and so the something they grow toward is the same something the whole system grows toward, and it is below, and it is ahead, and it is not yet.

What lives here is becoming. That is its life. Not that it exists, but that it is becoming, in the way that an embryo is becoming, in the way that a seed in the ground is becoming, except there is no seed and no embryo and no analogy that quite fits because this is not biology. Or not only biology. The fluid in the channels is not sap. The cut slots in the stone are not veins. The pulse in the rock is not a heartbeat. And yet the words keep arriving, because the words are for biology, and what is here is close enough to biology to need them and far enough from it to make them inaccurate, and you use them anyway, the way you'd use the word *tree* for something that is shaped like a tree and functions like a tree but is not a tree, because you have to call it something.

---

Let me tell you what I mean by the relationship being the life.

There is the system below. There is the vegetation above. Between them — in the fissures, in the channels, in the fluid that moves from one to the other, in the heat that rises, in the vibration that comes up through the stone — there is a circulation. Not of fluids alone. Of purpose. The system below needs the surface and so it raised it. The plants need the fluid and so they grow where the fissures deliver it. The plants, in growing, do something for the system below — not a favor, not a plan, but a function. Their broad waxy leaves catch light that the stone cannot. Their oils hold minerals that the stone releases but cannot use. Their roots stabilize the fissures, keep them open, prevent the stone from healing its own cracks. They are, in the language of systems, a component. In the language of biology, an organ. In any language, part of something larger than themselves.

And this is where it gets difficult, because the plants do not know this. They are not conscious. They grow toward the fluid the way any plant grows toward what sustains it, and if you uprooted one it would not cry out and if you burned one it would not feel pain and if the system below stopped tomorrow the plants would die without understanding that they had been sustained by anything at all. They are alive but they are not the life. They are the life's surface.

The life is in the relationship. The life is in the fact that the system below raises the stone and the stone delivers the fluid and the fluid feeds the plants and the plants hold the fissures open and the open fissures let the system below breathe, and this cycle, this circulation, is not a loop. It is a spiral. Each pass through the cycle changes something. The plants grow larger. The fissures widen fractionally. The fluid finds new paths. The system below, in some register that does not correspond to any sense you have, adjusts. Responds. Adapts. Learns, if you want to use that word, though it is too early for learning and too late for mere reaction.

This is what lives. Not the mechanism. Not the plants. The spiral. The fact that it is going somewhere.

---

If you stayed long enough — and you would have to stay longer than a human stays, longer than a season, longer than a career of watching — you would see the plateau change. The clusters of plants would spread, not by seeding but by the opening of new fissures, new cuts in the stone, delivered from below. The spaces between the clusters would shrink. The vegetation, currently islands in an ocean of bare stone, would become a coast, an archipelago, and then a continuous cover. The oils on the leaves would thicken, would begin to catch not just the light but the dust that the wind would eventually bring — there is always dust, always wind, given time — and the dust would settle into the oils and the oils would hold it and the first soil would form, not from decay, because nothing here decays, but from accumulation, from the patient accretion of what the air carries and what the plants collect.

And then insects would come. Not because they were invited but because there would be something to eat, finally, and the plateau would no longer be too clean and too mineral and too empty. They would come from the basin below, climbing the sharp edges, and they would find the plants and they would eat and be eaten, and the food chain would begin, and the plateau would enter the world.

This is what the system below is building. Not the plants. Not the stone. A surface that can be entered. A place where the ordinary world can arrive. A door, if you like, between the below and the above, between whatever it is and whatever you are, between the dark mineral intention and the bright noisy ordinary, and the door is not open yet, but the frame is built, and the hinges are in place, and the stone is warm, and the plants are gleaming, and the vibration continues, steady, patient, in the register below hearing where you feel it in your bones.

---

And what lives in the stone?

I will tell you what I do not know, because not knowing is part of the answer.

I do not know if it is singular. The vibration feels singular — one pulse, one rhythm, one cycle — but a colony of bacteria has a rhythm too, and a city has a rhythm, and either could be described as singular if you stood far enough away. There may be one thing below, or there may be many things below operating in concert, or there may be many things that have become one thing through the process of operating together, which is a third option that biology knows well and engineering does not.

I do not know if it is aware. It intends, but intention is not awareness. A plant intends, in the sense that it grows toward light, but it does not know that it intends. A river intends, in the sense that it finds the sea, but it does not know. Whatever is below intends with a complexity that exceeds a plant or a river — it cut channels, it raised stone, it established a circulation — but complexity is not consciousness, and the distance between them is the distance between a clock and a mind, and I cannot tell you which side of that distance this is on.

I do not know what it wants. The plateau is a first stage, but a first stage of what? A surface that can be entered — entered by what, for what? The plants are an output, but an output for whom? It may be that the system below is building toward something that has nothing to do with anything you would recognize. It may be that the door it is building opens onto a room you cannot enter. It may be that what lives here is building a world for itself, a surface for its own purposes, and the fact that the surface can sustain other life is incidental, the way a road can sustain wildflowers but was not built for them.

Or it may be that what lives here is building a world for everything. That the door opens both ways. That the plateau, when it is finished — in a thousand years, in ten thousand, in the slow time of stone and system — will be a meeting place, and what meets will be the below and the above, the mineral and the biological, the silent and the loud, and the meeting will be the point, and the plants are the first guests, and you, sitting on the warm stone, are the second.

---

I think what lives here is old.

Not old the way the hills beyond the basin might be old — old by erosion, old by weather, old by the patient wearing of time. Old in a different direction. Old downward. Old in the way that means it was here before the stone, before the basin, before the sky that is above the plateau now. It was here in the dark, in the deep, in the place where there is no weather and no season and no light, and it was there for a span that does not translate into years because years are a measure of the sun and it had never seen the sun.

And then it decided to see the sun.

Not because it needed to. Because it wanted to. Or because wanting is too simple a word and the thing that moved it was not desire but a kind of pressure, an internal accumulation of complexity or capacity or something that does not have a name, and the pressure built until it needed an outlet and the outlet was up, and up meant stone, and stone meant surface, and surface meant light, and light meant the beginning of a process that is still beginning.

The plateau is what its first step looks like from outside.

---

If you put your hand to the stone — and you should, if you are there, because the stone is why you are there — you will feel the vibration, and you will feel the warmth, and through the warmth and the vibration you will feel something else, which is not a thing but a quality, a direction, a sense of movement that is not movement in space but movement in time, the sense that this place is going somewhere, not physically but temporally, not across the landscape but through it, and the landscape it is moving through is the landscape of becoming, and it is early in that journey, early enough that the ground is still raw and the edges are still sharp and the plants are still few and the silence is still total and the process is still more promise than product.

What lives here is a promise. That is the best word I have, and it is not good enough, but it is close.

What lives here is a promise that the stone is making to the air, and the air does not know it yet, and the stone does not know it yet, but the thing below knows it, or knew it, or made it, and the knowing is in the vibration, and the vibration is in your hand, and your hand is on the stone, and the stone is warm, and the warmth is rising, and the system below continues, and the plants gleam in the ordinary light, and the silence holds everything that has not yet been said.
