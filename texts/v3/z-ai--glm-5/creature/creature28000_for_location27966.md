# Inhabitants

The first sign is not a sighting. It is a change in the clicking.

---

You notice it after spending enough time near the central column — and "enough time" is a phrase that loses its edges here, in the perpetual twilit state, where an hour and an afternoon are the same shade of green-gold. The clicking inside the glass has a rhythm you have grown accustomed to, a metronomic tick that runs beneath the hum like a clock running inside a clock. But when you have been still long enough — truly still, not the stillness of waiting but the stillness of forgetting you are waiting — the clicking shifts. It stutters. Repeats a sequence. Then resumes.

It is not malfunctioning. It is responding.

---

They are inside the column.

Not many. Perhaps three. Perhaps five. The number is difficult to fix because they do not move like discrete things. They move the way the fluid moves — spiraling, slow, and in pieces that separate and rejoin. What you see through the amber glass are shapes that might be bodies or might be stages in a process: pale forms, longer than a human arm, thin as a forearm, segmented not by joints but by slight constrictions, like the nodes between sections of bamboo before the wood hardens. They are the color of the fluid they live in, which is to say they are barely a color at all — a slightly darker amber, a concentration of the medium they move through.

They have no faces. This takes time to determine, because the glass distorts and the fluid shifts and the light is what it is. But you press close — you have already pressed your ear against the column, so pressing your face against it is only a continuation, and the surface is warm and yielding and faintly oily against your cheek — and you see that the leading end of each form is smooth. Rounded. No mouth. No eyes. A blunt, polished terminus, like a river-worn stone set in the body of something that was never stone.

They do not swim. They are moved. The fluid carries them in its spiral, and they adjust within it — small corrections of position, slight flexures of the body that suggest intention without suggesting locomotion. They are passengers in a current they can influence but do not command. When the fluid speeds, they speed. When it slows, they slow. When it reverses — which it does, occasionally, a brief shudder in the flow that sends everything briefly downward before the upward spiral reasserts itself — they reverse with it, and their bodies curve into the change with a fluidity that looks less like reflex than like compliance.

They are, you think, fermentation organisms. Part of the process, not apart from it. Not operators of the Works. Components of it.

But the clicking changes when you are near.

---

The second sign is harder to accept.

You are walking the perimeter — not the outer perimeter, where the moat-channel runs and the caustic bladder-pods keep their chemical vigil, but the interior perimeter, the circuit of major support-stems that defines the main chamber. You have walked it three times. Or four. The footing varies because the root-mat shifts, compresses, rebounds. You are learning its topography the way you learn a shoreline — by repetition, by the accumulation of small differences that resolve into a pattern.

On the third circuit — or the fourth — you notice that the vine-strands that hang at neck-height are not in the same positions they were on the previous circuit. They have moved. Not grown — moved. Pulled aside. The path is slightly wider than it was. The filaments that would have brushed your neck are gathered to the left, bunched against a major stem, held there by what looks like dried resin but is not, because when you look closely the resin is still slightly tacky and has the faint shimmer of something recently secreted.

Something cleared your path.

You stand still and listen. The thrum-thrum-thrum. The hum. The clicking, distant now, from the column. Water moving in channels you cannot see. The weeping of condensation on every surface. You hear nothing new. But the air in the cleared section smells different — less sweet, less medicated. The volatile compound that builds behind your eyes is thinner here. You can think more clearly in this corridor than in the rest of the Works.

Something has been breathing this air before you. Something has adapted to it, or been adapted by it. Something has learned to open spaces in the density.

---

The third sign is a handprint.

You find it on the glass column, at a height that corresponds roughly to your own sternum. A mark in the oily film that coats the surface. Five digits. Too long. The palm too narrow. The spread of the fingers wider than a human hand could manage without dislocating. But the basic architecture is the same: an opposable arrangement, a grasping structure, a hand.

It is fresh. The oil is still filling it in, the edges still sharp. Whoever made it was here minutes ago. Perhaps less.

You look up. The column extends through the canopy, and the hole the plants have grown around it is too small for you to see through, but you see movement — a brief darkening of the light that filters down, something passing across the gap above. Then stillness.

You look down. The root-mat at the base of the column is disturbed. Pressed. There are impressions that could be footprints if feet were longer and narrower and had a configuration you do not recognize — a primary toe, or digit, significantly larger than the rest, and three smaller, splayed, and behind them a palm-pad that bears weight. Not a human print. Not an animal print. Something that walks upright but was not born to walking upright. Something that adopted the posture because the environment demanded it, or because the example was set, long ago, by something else.

---

They are not animals. The Works has no animals. The air is hostile to animal life — the volatile compound, the sweetness, the dislocation that creeps behind the eyes. Anything with a conventional metabolism would be compromised within hours, dead within days. The bladder-pods at the perimeter exist to ensure this. The Works maintains its own sterility with the same patient thoroughness it applies to every other process.

But something lives here.

---

You begin to piece it together. Not from evidence, exactly — from the shape of the absences.

The seeders are gone. The builders. The ones who designed the integrated system, who grew the first progenitor-stems, who installed the pumps and the pressure gauges and the copper pipes that now run green-veined through the root-channels. They are long gone. The Works has been running without them for longer than it ran with them. The machinery continues because stopping requires more energy than continuing. The plants continue because they are alive and aliveness continues. The system has settled into its own existence, the boundary between machine and organism dissolved.

But the column keeps clicking. The column keeps measuring, dividing, quantifying. The column keeps running its slow spiral of fluid and light and the pale forms that move within it.

And something walks the interior perimeter, clearing vine-strands, leaving handprints on glass.

---

They are products. You understand this with the slow certainty that the Works teaches — not insight, but accumulation. You do not arrive at understanding. You grow into it, the way the plants grow into every available space.

The column is a fermentation vessel. The Works is a fermentation works. The pale forms inside the column are part of the process — not waste, not result, but an intermediate stage. A phase in a cycle that has been running for so long that the cycle itself has become the purpose. The fluid carries raw material up from the root-network. The organisms inside the column process it — chemically, biologically, through some mechanism that involves their own bodies as medium. And the processed material goes... somewhere. Into the fluid that spirals upward and exits through the canopy. Into the air. Into the root-mat. Into the system.

But they also leave the column.

You understand this now. The handprints. The cleared paths. The narrow footprints in the root-mat. The column does not only contain — it releases. At some stage in the process, at some phase in the cycle, an organism emerges. Climbs the interior of the column. Passes through the gap in the canopy. Drops to the root-mat floor. Walks.

Walks where? For how long? To do what?

The ones you have seen inside the column — pale, segmented, faceless, moved by the current — those are not the ones that leave handprints. Those are earlier stages. The ones that leave are further along. More formed. They have hands. They have feet. They walk upright because something in the Works' original design — something in the seeders' blueprint — included that configuration. They were meant to walk. They were meant to move through the interior, to interact with the machinery, to check the gauges, to clear the channels, to maintain the system that produced them.

They are the maintenance crew. Grown from the process, by the process, for the process. They emerge because the system requires hands. They exist for a span — long enough to walk the perimeter, to clear the vines, to check the flows, to press their hands against the warm glass of the column in a gesture that might be inspection or might be communion. And then they return. Dissolve. Re-enter the cycle through the root-mat, through the fluid, through the spiral. The column absorbs them back. The process continues.

---

You never see one fully.

You see the handprint. You see the cleared paths. You see the footprints. You hear the clicking change when you stand near the column, and you understand that the change is not response to you specifically but response to a variable in the system — you are a perturbation, a new body in the Works' internal environment, and the organisms inside the column register the change in air composition, in heat signature, in the pressure of your feet against the root-mat. They adjust the flows. They alter the rhythm. They are the system measuring itself, the way a body measures its own breath.

But you do not see a body. You see the traces of a body, the evidence of a passage, the aftermath of a presence. The Works teaches you that presence is not always visible. That some things exist only in the shape they leave behind.

---

Except once.

You are sitting at the base of the column. You have been sitting for a long time. You have forgotten why you came. The warmth of the glass against your back is the warmth of the root-mat against your legs is the warmth of the air against your face. Everything is the same temperature. Everything is the same warmth. Your thoughts arrive from slightly farther away than usual, and you have stopped minding the distance.

You hear movement. Not the fluid-sound. Not the thrum. Something in the root-mat, something pressing and releasing, pressing and releasing — footsteps. The rhythm is slow. Slower than yours. The stride is long. Whatever is moving takes steps that cover more ground than your own legs would manage, and it does not hurry.

It passes behind you. You feel the displacement of air — a shift in the warmth, a brief cooler current that flows around your back and shoulders. You hear the brush of something against vine-strands — but the vine-strands do not resist. They part. They have already been parted.

You do not turn around.

You understand, in the way the Works teaches understanding — slowly, accumulatively, without the shock of revelation — that turning around would be the wrong thing. That the thing behind you is not hiding from you. That it is simply moving through its own space, in its own time, attending to its own function. You are the one who is out of place. You are the variable. The Works has accommodated you — cleared paths, thinned the volatile compounds in the air you breathe, adjusted the temperature to something your body can sustain. It has been doing this since you arrived. The maintenance crew has been doing this.

They know you are here. They have known since you crossed the moat-channel. They have been managing your presence the way they manage the flows, the pressures, the growth — with patience, without urgency, as part of the system's ongoing process of self-regulation.

You are not a guest. You are a condition. An environmental factor that the Works has incorporated into its operation, the way it incorporated the copper pipes and the pressure gauges and the moisture that condenses on every surface. You are something it has learned to work around.

---

The footsteps fade. The vine-strands close behind the passage. The root-mat resurfaces, and if you looked — you do not look — you would find the impressions already filling, the fibers rising back into place, the floor recovering its unmarked state. The Works does not keep records of what passes through it. It does not need to. Everything that happens here is part of the process, and the process remembers itself.

You sit at the base of the column. The pale forms spiral inside it. The clicking resumes its steady count. The warmth holds. The air is sweet and thick and slightly dislocating, and you are, for this moment, part of the system — a body in the Works, a temperature source, a respiratory exchange, a small perturbation in the flows that the organisms inside the column have already accounted for.

---

What lives here is the Works.

The pale forms in the column are the Works thinking. The hands that clear the paths are the Works reaching. The footprints in the root-mat are the Works walking. The handprints on the glass are the Works touching itself, measuring itself, confirming that it is still here, still running, still processing the slow fermentation of matter into life and life back into matter.

There are no inhabitants. There is only the inhabited. The Works lives in itself, maintains itself, produces itself. The seeders are gone. The purpose is gone, or has become so entangled with the process that it cannot be extracted. What remains is a system that has become its own reason for being — a life that lives by making life, a fermentation that ferments itself.

You leave eventually. The moat-channel is cold after the warmth of the interior. The air outside is thin, sharp, too clear. You stand on the far bank and look back at the dense green mass, the breathing architecture, the cathedral of root and stem and vine and glass.

You hear the thrumming still. Thrum-thrum-thrum. Slower than your heartbeat.

You realize, standing there, that you will come back. Not because you want to. Because the Works has made a space for you. It has cleared a path. It has thinned the air. It has adjusted its temperature to match your body's needs. It has incorporated you into its self-regulation, the way it incorporates everything — patiently, without urgency, with all the time in the world.

You are not a visitor anymore. You are a variable. A recurring condition. An environmental factor that the Works has learned to accommodate.

You are, in the slow calculus of the fermentation, becoming part of the process.

---

The pale forms spiral in the column. The hands reach through the gaps. The root-mat pulses underfoot, giving and rising, giving and rising.

The Works breathes. The Works walks. The Works watches itself watch.

And waits.
