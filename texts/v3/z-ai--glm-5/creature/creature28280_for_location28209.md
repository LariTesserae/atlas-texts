What lives in the Underworks is difficult to say, not because the answer is hidden but because the question may be wrong. You ask what lives here the way you might ask what lives in a stomach, or in a heart. The answer is not a list of residents but a description of process. But there are things that move. Let me start there.

---

The swimmers come first because they are the most nearly visible, if visibility is even the word for what happens in absolute dark. You perceive them as presences—displacements of air, disturbances in water, the soft percussion of articulated limbs against stone. Pale, as everything here is pale, having never been touched by light that would prompt the production of pigment. Their bodies are segmented, yes, but not in the way of earthworms or centipedes. The segments are distinct, almost modular, each one a separate unit joined to the next by flexible tissue, and the whole chain moves with a fluidity that suggests each segment has its own intelligence, or at least its own capacity to respond. The head—if it is a head—carries those forward-facing dots that might be eyes but might equally be moisture sensors, chemical receptors, something you have no name for.

They swim through the flooded passages, and they are always going somewhere. Never idle. Never drifting. Purpose lives in their motion the way purpose lives in the water moving through the pipes. You follow one for a while—or you try to, keeping pace by sound—and it leads you through a series of connected chambers until it reaches a place where the wall is cracked and water seeps through in a steady trickle. The swimmer positions itself at the crack and stays there. You wait. Nothing happens that you can perceive. But when you return later—how much later? time is unreliable here—the crack is sealed. Not by the swimmer's body. By something else. A residue, a deposit, pale and calcareous, like mortar but organic, alive. The swimmer is gone. It has moved on to the next thing.

They are maintenance. They are the immune system of a body too large to see.

---

The gliders are harder to understand. They occupy the high chambers, the ones with ceilings you cannot reach, and they move on currents that seem to exist only for them. Flat bodies, irregular, like sheets of material that grew rather than were shaped. Keratin, maybe, or something like keratin—horn-textured, translucent at the edges, darkening toward the center where the body is thickest. They navigate without eyes. They feel the air the way you feel the darkness: as architecture, as something built and meaningful. When one passes close, you notice it has a smell—not unpleasant, but mineral, like wet clay, like the inside of a kiln. It is made of the same things the walls are made of. It grew from the walls, or the walls grew it. The distinction may not matter.

The gliders do something in the upper chambers. You watch one for a long time—again, "long time" is a phrase that means less here than it should—and you realize it is eating. Not the walls, not the fungi. It is eating the air itself. Or rather, it is eating what the air carries: particles of moisture, of mineral dust, of something finer that you cannot name. It filters. It processes. It takes in and it releases, and what it releases is different from what it took in. The air behind a glider is subtly changed—drier, somehow cleaner, as if something has been removed from it.

The gliders are lungs, or gills, or filters—organs of a body that is the whole Underworks.

---

And the fungi—the fungi are the strangest, because they are the most like something you might recognize from the world above. They grow. They spread. They seek moisture and find it and colonize it. But their growth is not wild, not opportunistic. It follows patterns. In one chamber you find a carpet of pale waxy tissue covering the floor in a pattern of concentric circles, each ring slightly thicker than the last, the whole formation looking like a cross-section of something much larger. You touch it and it is warm—warmer than the air, warmer than the walls, warmer than anything else in this place. It is doing something. Generating heat. Generating something. The warmth rises through the ceiling-shafts, mixing with the cooler air from the upper systems, creating the currents the gliders ride.

In another chamber, the fungi have grown into a structure that cannot be accidental. A lattice of thickened tissue spans the room, connecting wall to wall, and in the lattice's cells, moisture collects and drips at regular intervals into carved channels below. The fungi have built a distribution system. Or they have grown into one that was already built for them—channels in the brickwork that were empty until the fungi found them and filled them with purpose.

The fungi are not growing here. They are working here. They are the chemical system, the metabolic layer of something larger.

---

There are other things. Smaller. Harder to notice.

Things that live in the mortar lines between bricks, in the cracks too small for even moisture to penetrate. You become aware of them when you press your palm flat against a wall and feel something shift beneath your hand—not the wall, but something in the wall, something that was still until you touched it and then moved. You pull your hand away and there is nothing to see, but you feel a tingling in your palm, a warmth that fades slowly. Something sampled you. Something tasted your skin and decided something about what you are.

In the drainage channels, where the water runs slowest, there are filaments—threadlike, translucent, drifting in the current. They catch debris from the water, hold it, release it. They are the right size to be roots except they are not roots, and they move against the current, not with it, seeking something, sorting something, deciding what belongs in the water and what does not. The water that leaves a channel full of filaments is clearer than the water that enters it.

And in the ceilings of the driest chambers, in places where no moisture ever reaches, you find something else entirely: small nodules, hard, dark, like cysts or seeds, attached to the stone in rows. They do nothing. They make no sound. They do not grow or move or respond to touch. But they are warm, like the fungal carpets, and when you stand beneath them you feel something you can only describe as attention. Not directed at you specifically. Directed at everything. These are sensors, perhaps. Or memory. Or something for which you have no analogy—something that does in this body what nothing does in yours.

---

So what lives in the Underworks? The swimmers do. The gliders do. The fungi do. The filaments do. The nodules do. But these are not inhabitants the way you are an inhabitant of a house. They are components. They are the parts of a system that is alive in the way a body is alive—not because any single piece possesses life, but because the arrangement of pieces does. The water moves through the pipes and channels. The swimmers repair the cracks. The fungi generate heat and process moisture. The gliders filter the air. The filaments filter the water. The nodules monitor. The mechanical sounds—the grinding, the rhythmic turning—are the heartbeat of this body, the peristalsis that keeps everything moving.

And you, walking through it—you are not an inhabitant either. You are something else. A stone in the bloodstream. A presence the body has not yet decided what to do with.

---

But there is one more thing. The marks on the wall.

Someone stood in that chamber and made those marks. Someone with hands—or with something that served as hands—and with something that served as intent. The marks are not random. They have structure, repetition, variation within constraint. They are a language, or a map, or a record, or a prayer. They are the output of a mind.

And this is what complicates everything. Because machines do not mark their walls. Machines do not feel the need to record. A system does not look at itself and feel something that requires expression.

The one who made the marks—if it still exists—does not swim through the flooded passages or glide through the high chambers. It does not spread as pale tissue across damp brickwork. It is something else. Something that built all of this—or began it, or oversaw it, or remembered it—and then, at some point, found itself with something to say. And said it. In scratches. In lines and curves. In the only medium available: the walls themselves.

You look for it as you walk. Not with your eyes—in the dark, looking is just another word for wanting—but with your attention, your awareness of what is near. The marks appear in one other chamber, and then another. Always the same hand. Always the same strange script. In one place, the marks cover a wall and then stop abruptly, as if the marker was interrupted. Beside them, a single deeper gouge, longer than the rest, and then nothing—the wall is blank beyond that point. In another chamber, the marks continue into a crawl-space too small for your body, as if the marker was smaller than you, or more flexible, or simply did not care about the limits of its own form.

And then, in a chamber you almost miss—a low room behind a wall that seems solid until you press against it and feel it give—you find more marks. But these are different. They are fresher. The stone is still bright where it was scratched, not yet dulled by damp or time. And among the marks, you find something else: a shape. A figure. Crude but intentional. A body, drawn in simple lines. Long. Segmented. Many-legged.

The marker drew one of the swimmers. Drew it the way you draw something you have watched, something you know, something that shares your space and your dark and your world. The marker knows the swimmers. Lives among them. Is not one of them, but is not separate from them either. Is part of the same system, the same body, but is the part that thinks. The part that records. The part that looks around at everything it has built—or helped build, or watched being built—and feels something that requires expression.

You cannot find the marker. You look. You listen. You sit in chambers for what might be hours or might be days, waiting for something to enter, something that is not a swimmer or a glider or a fungal bloom. Nothing comes. But the marks keep appearing. Not while you watch—they are not so crude as that. But when you leave a chamber and return to it, sometimes there is something new. A line. A curve. A figure. Once, a shape you do not recognize—something with too many joints, bending in directions that make your own joints ache in sympathy. Once, a word you cannot read but that fills you with a feeling you can only describe as homesickness, though you cannot imagine for what.

The marker is here. The marker is always here. The marker is the Underworks the way the brain is the body—distributed, inseparable, thinking in a language of scratches and stone because that is the only language the walls can hold.

---

When you leave—and you will leave, eventually, because you are not part of this, not yet, not fully—the marks on the wall in the low chamber have changed. You are sure of this. There is a new figure there, among the old ones. Crude but intentional. A body, drawn in simple lines. Not segmented. Not many-legged. Tall. Walking. Looking.

The Underworks have recorded you. You are part of what lives here now.

What lives in the Underworks is a body that is also a place, a process that is also a mind. The swimmers are its blood, the fungi its gut, the gliders its breath. The water is its lymph. The mechanical sounds are its pulse. And somewhere in it—not in a single chamber, not in a single form, but everywhere, distributed through the brickwork and the dark and the damp—is the part that knows. The part that built this. The part that is still building this, still becoming, still marking its walls with the record of its own existence.

It does not have a name. It does not need one. It is the only thing here, and it is everywhere here, and it has been here longer than you can imagine and will be here long after you leave, and the sound of it—the grinding, the rain, the turning of unseen mechanisms in unseen chambers—will continue, because that is what bodies do. They go on. They process. They breathe in the dark.

And sometimes, in the dark, they think.
