# Preparing for the Root-Cavity of Iyesh

## What You Are Actually Walking Into

Before anything else—before gear, before vaccinations, before mental preparation—you need to understand what this place *is*. Not the poetic version. The practical version.

You are entering a living organism. Not a cave that happens to have biological components. A living organism that happens to have a cavity inside it. The distinction matters because it changes every single assumption you carry about enclosed spaces. Caves are geology. They are stable, predictable, and fundamentally inert. The Root-Cavity of Iyesh is none of these things. It breathes. It sweats. It heals over wounds. It regulates its own temperature through metabolic processes. It shifts its internal geometry over time as root-mass grows and decays. You are not exploring a cave. You are being swallowed by something large and accepting that its stomach happens to be hospitable enough to survive in, provided you prepare correctly.

The sixty people who live there have spent generations adapting to this. You have not. Every advantage you can manufacture through preparation, you will need.

## Physical Conditioning

### Core Fitness and Mobility

The entrance requires you to turn sideways and squeeze between two buttress roots thick as a torso. The descent is slick with condensation. Internal passages are narrow—some barely wide enough for a single body. You will need to move through spaces that compress your chest, that require you to exhale and hold your breath to pass through. You will need to do this while cold, while wet, while carrying gear.

If you are not currently capable of sustained physical exertion in awkward positions—crawling, squeezing, balancing on uneven substrate—begin training months in advance. Focus on core strength, hip mobility, shoulder flexibility. Practice climbing in tight spaces. Practice holding your balance on surfaces that shift beneath you, because the root-mesh floor is not solid. It has give. It has texture. Ridges and depressions and natural steps formed by growth patterns that do not follow the logic of stairs or trails.

You should be able to walk in a low crouch for extended periods. Many passages are not full-height. You should be able to rise from a prone position quickly, because you will be sleeping on the floor and the floor is not a bed. You should be able to navigate by touch in darkness, moving your hands along walls to find grooves, edges, handholds, while your eyes contribute nothing useful.

### Cold Tolerance

The cavity hovers just above the temperature where breath becomes visible. Constantly. It does not waver. This is not the kind of cold that comes in waves—you cannot wait it out, you cannot brace through a particularly bad stretch and then recover. It is a persistent, soaking cold that enters through your skin and settles into your joints and stays.

If you have never experienced sustained cold in the 4–8°C range while simultaneously being wet, you need to understand what this does to a human body. It is not dramatic. You do not shiver dramatically and then adapt. You shiver, and then you stop shivering because your body calibrates, and then hours later you realize your hands have become clumsy and your thinking has become slow and you are losing the ability to make good decisions. This is the real danger of the cavity—not the deep-crawlers, not the darkness, but the slow erosion of function that constant cold produces in a body that is not adapted to it.

Train for cold exposure before you go. Cold showers. Cold immersion. Extended periods outdoors in wet clothing in temperatures near 5°C. Learn what cold does to you specifically—how it affects your mood, your coordination, your judgment—so you can recognize the signs early and respond before you are compromised.

### Breath and Cardiovascular Fitness

The air is humid. Humid air feels different to breathe. It carries more water vapor, which means every breath delivers slightly less oxygen per volume. You will not notice this at rest. You will notice it when you are climbing, when you are squeezing through a passage with gear on your back, when you are working to keep up with an inhabitant who moves through this terrain the way you move through your own home.

The walls breathe on a forty-second cycle, expanding and contracting, drawing humid air through capillary channels and releasing it cooler and cleaner. This means air quality varies by location and by timing. In certain pockets, the air is thicker, wetter, harder to draw. Improve your cardiovascular fitness to give yourself a buffer. If you can sustain moderate exertion at 85% maximum heart rate for twenty minutes in normal conditions, you will have enough reserve to handle the additional strain of breathing dense humid air during physical effort underground.

## Immune System Preparation

This is the most critical medical concern, and it is not optional.

The text is explicit: visitors sicken within days. The cavity is a closed system, evolved over centuries, and it does not welcome outsiders. Your immune system has not encountered the microbial ecology of this place. The inhabitants have immune systems of ferocious capability, developed over generations of exposure. You do not have this. You will arrive with a naïve immune system facing an environment that is dense with unfamiliar organisms—inhalable spores, skin-contact mites, water-borne microbes, surface fungi, and organisms that may not fit neatly into any category your immunology training prepared you for.

### Pre-Visit Medical Protocol

Consult with someone who understands exotic environments—ideally someone with experience in deep cave ecosystems, tropical rainforest microbiomes, or prolonged isolation environments. Tell them specifically what you are facing: a closed biological system with high humidity, constant temperature, dense fungal and insect populations, and water that is biologically processed rather than mechanically filtered.

You will likely need a broad-spectrum antibiotic as a backup, not a preventative. Starting antibiotics before exposure may disrupt your own microbiome in ways that make you more vulnerable, not less. Carry them. Use them only if you develop symptoms that suggest a serious infection rather than minor adaptation illness.

Speak with an immunologist about whether there is any benefit to immune-boosting protocols in the weeks before your visit. The evidence for most supplements is weak, but the cost of trying is low. Vitamin D, zinc, and adequate sleep in the weeks before departure will not hurt you and may help your immune system respond more effectively to novel challenges.

### Skin Preparation

The inhabitants have developed skin tolerance for constant dampness that would macerate surface-dwellers. Maceration is what happens when skin is wet for too long—it softens, breaks down, becomes vulnerable to infection. This will happen to you if you do not prepare.

Begin by toughening your skin in the weeks before your visit. Extended exposure to moisture—long baths, time in humid environments—will help your skin begin to adapt, but it will not fully adapt. You need to bring barrier preparations. Not oils, which will wash away in the constant damp. Not standard lotions, which will become food for the organisms that live on every surface. Talk to someone who works in wound care about skin-protectant formulations that create a breathable barrier and persist in wet environments. Apply these to high-risk areas—feet, groin, underarms, the backs of your knees, anywhere skin folds against skin—before entering the cavity, and reapply regularly.

Your feet are the most critical concern. You will be standing and walking on wet, textured, living substrate for extended periods. Your feet will be wet for the entire duration of your visit. If you have ever had trench foot, you know what prolonged wet exposure does. If you have not, understand that it is not an inconvenience—it is a debilitating condition that can end your ability to walk. Bring multiple changes of foot coverings. Rotate them. Dry them against your body when you sleep, using your own heat. Bring barrier preparations designed for feet specifically.

### Respiratory Preparation

You will be inhaling biological material with every breath. Spores. Mite fragments. Microbial particles. The dust-crickets leave behind fine particulate when they shed exoskeletons. The air is alive in a way that surface air is not, even in the most biodiverse surface environments.

If you have any history of respiratory sensitivity—asthma, allergies, any condition that makes your airways reactive to particulate—this is a serious concern. The cavity will stress your respiratory system. Consult with a physician about carrying an emergency inhaler or bronchodilator, even if you have never needed one before.

Consider whether you should wear a breathable particulate filter during your first days in the cavity. This is a tradeoff: the filter will reduce your exposure to airborne biological material, but it will also make it harder to breathe in an already humid, oxygen-reduced environment, and it will mark you as an outsider in a community that is already assessing you. If you choose to wear one, bring several, as they will become clogged with moisture quickly. Wean yourself off the filter as your body begins to adapt, if you adapt. If you develop respiratory symptoms, do not push through. Leave the cavity.

### The Water Question

The water in the cavity is biologically processed, not mechanically filtered. It is potable for the inhabitants. It may not be potable for you. It tastes slightly sweet, slightly mineral, slightly alive. Some visitors cannot stomach it.

The question is whether to bring your own water or drink the cavity's water. Bringing your own is safer in the short term but presents problems: you cannot carry enough for an extended stay, the weight is significant, and the act of bringing external water into a closed system may disrupt the local ecology or be seen as a rejection of the community's resources. Drinking the cavity's water is riskier in the short term but more sustainable for a longer stay.

A compromise: bring a small supply of your own water for the first few days, while your gut biota begins to adjust. Bring a portable water purification system—not a filter, which will clog immediately in this environment, but a chemical purifier or UV system—as a backup. Transition to the cavity's water gradually, mixing it with your supply, increasing the proportion over several days. If you develop gastrointestinal symptoms, revert to your own supply and reconsider whether your body can adapt to this environment at all.

Accept that the water will taste strange. Accept that it will feel different from any water you have drunk before. This is not a problem to solve. It is a reality to accommodate.

## What to Bring

### Clothing

Everything you wear will be wet within minutes of entering the cavity. Everything. The moisture is pervasive—it rises from below, condenses from above, and moves laterally through the root-mesh. Your clothing will not dry during your visit. It will remain damp until you leave.

This changes your clothing strategy entirely. You are not dressing for warmth in the conventional sense. You are dressing to manage wet insulation—to create layers that trap body heat even when saturated, and to manage the rate at which cold reaches your skin.

Base layer: a moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool layer that fits close to the skin. Not cotton. Cotton loses all insulating value when wet and will actively work against you. Merino wool retains approximately 60% of its insulating value when wet and continues to wick moisture away from the skin. A synthetic base layer—polypropylene or similar—performs similarly and dries faster, though it will not dry in this environment.

Mid layer: a thicker insulating layer, also wool or synthetic. Fleece is acceptable. This layer provides the bulk of your insulation. It will become wet and stay wet, but it will continue to trap body heat in the air pockets between its fibers.

Outer layer: a breathable waterproof shell. Not a heavy raincoat—you will sweat inside it and the humidity will overwhelm any membrane—but a lightweight barrier that slows the rate at which external moisture reaches your insulating layers. Vent it constantly. The goal is not to keep water out entirely, which is impossible, but to manage the rate at which it enters.

Bring multiple base layers. At minimum, three complete changes. You will not be able to dry clothing, but you can wring it out and rotate. A fresh base layer, even one that will be wet within minutes, provides a brief window of improved comfort and reduces the risk of skin maceration.

Do not bring cotton. Do not bring down insulation, which becomes useless when wet. Do not bring anything that cannot be compressed into a small pack, because you will be squeezing through narrow passages and everything you carry must fit through spaces barely wide enough for your body.

Footwear is a particular challenge. You need something that provides traction on wet, textured, living substrate. You need something that protects your feet from the organisms that share the floor with you. You need something that can be wet for days without degrading. Consider approach shoes with aggressive tread, designed for wet rock climbing, in a synthetic material that will not rot. Bring multiple pairs of socks, all wool or synthetic. Rotate them constantly. Dry used socks against your body while sleeping.

Gloves are essential. You will be touching walls constantly—to navigate, to brace yourself, to find handholds. The walls are alive. They are covered in organisms. They are coated in moisture. Your hands will be in constant contact with biological material. Bring thin gloves that allow dexterity while providing a barrier. Bring multiple pairs. They will become wet and stay wet, and wet gloves are worse than no gloves if they cause skin breakdown.

### Light Sources

This is a cultural and practical question that requires careful thought.

The inhabitants navigate by touch, by smell, by air currents. They have adapted to the bioluminescent fungi and the near-total darkness. They have not asked for artificial light, and bringing a powerful light source into their space is an act of disruption. It changes the character of the space. It makes you a disturbance rather than a guest.

That said, you are not adapted to their darkness. You will be functionally blind without assistance. This is a safety concern.

A compromise: bring the dimmest usable light source you can find. A single low-lumen LED headlamp with a red light mode, which preserves whatever night adaptation your eyes can achieve. Use it sparingly. Use it only for navigation in areas you have not yet learned. Turn it off when in the company of inhabitants unless they indicate otherwise. Never use a bright light. Never use a light that flashes. Never use a light that could be mistaken for a natural signal in a space where the only light is the faint glow of fungi.

Bring extra batteries. The cold and humidity will reduce battery life significantly. Seal your batteries in waterproof containers, because everything in the cavity becomes wet, including the contents of your pack.

### Sleeping System

You cannot bring a conventional sleeping bag into the cavity. It will become wet immediately and remain wet, losing all insulating value. It will also be impossible to compress through narrow passages.

The inhabitants sleep communally, in piles, beneath woven-grass blankets, sharing body heat. This is not a cultural preference. It is a survival strategy. Solitary sleep in the cavity's cold risks never waking. You will need to accept that sleeping in the cavity means sleeping near other people, or you will need to find another solution.

If communal sleep is offered to you, accept it. This is both a practical necessity and a gesture of inclusion from a community that has no obligation to include you.

If you cannot accept communal sleep—for reasons of trauma, personal space, or any other cause—bring the warmest synthetic sleeping bag you can compress into a small pack, accept that it will be wet and partially useless, and plan to sleep in the warmest chamber you can find, wrapped in every layer of clothing you brought. Understand that this is a compromise that carries real risk. The cold in the cavity is not aggressive, but it is patient, and it does not stop.

A sleeping pad is essential. The floor is wet and it draws heat from your body faster than the air does. A closed-cell foam pad will not absorb water and will provide a barrier between you and the substrate. An inflatable pad will be more comfortable but may be punctured by the texture of the root-mesh floor. A closed-cell pad is the safer choice.

### Food

The inhabitants eat New Skin—the pale, living layer that carpets the cavity's surfaces. It is harvested with curved bone blades and regrows within days. It tastes of mushroom and mineral and is described as pale and slightly rubbery.

You should try it. It is their food, their primary sustenance, and accepting it is part of being present in their space. But you should not rely on it as your sole food source during your visit, for two reasons: first, your gut is not adapted to it and may not process it efficiently, leaving you undernourished; second, the cavity's ecosystem is carefully balanced, and taking more New Skin than you contribute to the system's maintenance is extraction without reciprocity.

Bring concentrated, dense, nutritionally complete food that requires no preparation and can be eaten cold. Energy bars, dried meat, nut butters, whatever you can compress into the smallest possible volume. Bring enough for your entire planned stay plus a buffer. Seal everything in waterproof containers, because the dust-crickets will reduce any organic matter left unattended to skeleton within hours. Do not leave food out. Do not discard food waste in the cavity—carry it out with you or dispose of it as the inhabitants direct.

Accept that eating in the cavity will be strange. You will be eating in near-darkness, in cold, surrounded by the sounds of organisms you cannot see, with the taste of the cavity's own food still in your mouth. This is part of the experience. Do not fight it.

### Tools and Equipment

A walking stick or staff is useful for probing the floor ahead of you, testing the texture and stability of the root-mesh, and providing a third point of contact on uneven terrain. Make it lightweight, non-metallic, and short enough to maneuver in tight passages. The inhabitants use bone tools, and a metal staff would be out of place in their environment—visually, tactilely, and in the sounds it makes against their living walls.

A small knife is essential. Not a large blade—a small, sharp tool for cutting cord, opening packages, trimming material, and performing the small tasks that constantly arise in a space where everything is handmade and hand-maintained. Keep it dry, which is to say, keep it oiled and wiped, because the constant moisture will corrode even stainless steel over time.

Cord is useful. Lightweight, strong, non-absorbent. You will need it for securing gear, creating improvised shelters within sleeping chambers, hanging items away from the floor where dust-crickets roam, and a hundred other purposes. Bring twenty meters of paracord or similar, in a dark color that will not be visually disruptive in the low light.

Waterproof bags for your gear are not optional. Everything you bring—food, clothing, medical supplies, electronics—must be protected from the constant moisture. Use roll-top dry bags in multiple sizes. Test them before you leave by submerging them in water for an hour. If any moisture penetrates, the cavity's humidity will find the same gap.

A small first-aid kit focused on three concerns: skin breakdown, respiratory distress, and gastrointestinal illness. Include antifungal cream, because the cavity is a fungal environment and your skin will be under constant challenge. Include bandages that adhere when wet, because dry bandages will not stay dry. Include rehydration salts, because gastrointestinal illness in a cold, wet environment will dehydrate you faster than you can recover without assistance.

### What Not to Bring

Do not bring electronic devices beyond a minimal light source and perhaps a simple timekeeping device. The humidity will compromise anything with a screen or circuit. The light from screens will disrupt the inhabitants' adaptation to darkness. The sounds of devices will carry in ways that make you a disruption.

Do not bring weapons. The inhabitants have no use for them and the space does not permit the kind of movement a weapon would be designed for. The threats in the cavity are not threats that weapons address. You cannot fight the cold. You cannot fight the darkness. You cannot fight the ecosystem.

Do not bring scented products. No scented soap, no scented lotion, no perfumed anything. The inhabitants navigate by smell. Scent is information for them, and introducing artificial scents into their environment is the equivalent of shouting in a library or spray-painting over signage. It is not just rude. It is disorienting to them, and they will not be able to tell you this.

Do not bring anything you cannot carry through a passage barely wide enough for your body. This is a hard constraint. Test your packed gear by carrying it through the narrowest doorway you can find, sideways. If it does not fit, leave it behind.

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

### The Darkness

You have never experienced darkness like this. Even in the darkest surface environment, there is light—starlight, reflected light, ambient light from distant sources that your eyes can gather and amplify given enough time. The cavity's darkness is different. The only light sources are bioluminescent fungi that provide illumination comparable to a weak crescent moon—the kind that outlines shapes without revealing color. Your eyes will not adapt to this. There is not enough light to adapt to.

Understand what functional blindness means in a space like this. You will move by touch. You will identify people by the sound of their breathing, the rhythm of their footsteps, the particular texture of their skin when they touch your arm to guide you. You will eat food whose appearance you can only guess at. You will sleep in a space where you cannot see the walls, the ceiling, or the people next to you. You will wake in the same darkness and there will be no dawn, no gradual brightening, no signal that a new period of wakefulness has begun except the community's collective decision to begin moving.

This is psychologically taxing in ways that are difficult to anticipate. The brain expects visual confirmation of its environment. It expects to see the boundaries of the space it occupies. It expects to see the faces of the people it is with. In the absence of this input, the brain begins to fill in the gaps—sometimes accurately, sometimes not. You may experience visual phantoms, shadows that seem to move, shapes that are not there. This is normal. Your brain is not broken. It is working with insufficient data and making its best guesses.

Before you go, spend time blindfolded. Hours at a time, in your own home, navigating by touch. Learn the panic that comes when you cannot see and practice pushing through it. Learn to trust your hands, your ears, your sense of smell. Learn to move slowly, deliberately, testing each step before committing weight. This is the mode you will be in for the duration of your visit.

### The Living Environment

The walls breathe. The floor shifts. The ceiling has things hanging from it that respond to vibration. The air carries organisms that will settle on your skin and may migrate across your body while you sleep. The water tastes alive. The food grows on the walls.

For someone accustomed to inert environments—where walls are walls, floors are floors, and the space you occupy is fundamentally passive—this is a profound shift. The cavity is not passive. It is active in ways that are subtle and constant. You will feel the walls expand and contract against your hand when you touch them. You will feel the floor settle under your weight like a dog adjusting to your presence. You will feel the air move in patterns that do not correspond to any ventilation system you understand.

Some people find this deeply disturbing. The boundary between self and environment becomes blurred when the environment is responsive. You touch the wall and it is warm for a half-second, then cools rapidly, pulling the heat from your palm. It is interacting with you. It is processing your presence. This is not a metaphor. This is what is happening.

Prepare for this by spending time in environments that are similarly alive but less extreme. A greenhouse. A dense forest. A tidal pool. Practice being in spaces where the environment is not arranged for your convenience and does not respond to your preferences. Practice observing without controlling. Practice the particular humility of being in a space that does not care whether you are there.

### The Insects

You will have things crawling on you. This is not a possibility. It is a certainty. The glisten-mites are pinhead-sized organisms that cluster in bioluminescent zones and migrate across skin during sleep, seeking warmth or moisture or simply moving because movement is what they do. The inhabitants have developed a callousness to small crawling things that is not natural but learned. You will need to learn it too, or you will not sleep.

The dust-crickets are everywhere. Thumb-length, nearly transparent, leaving fine particulate behind when they shed. They do not bite. They swarm to any organic matter left unattended. You will hear them constantly—a dry scratching, like paper on paper.

The deep-crawlers are the ones that test visitors most severely. Centipede-like, longer than a forearm, they live in the spaces between the root-mesh and emerge when they detect significant movement above. They are not aggressive. They have been known to crawl across sleeping humans—across legs, torsos, once, according to story, across a face—and then continue on, indifferent.

If you cannot tolerate the idea of a large arthropod walking across your body while you are unable to see it, you need to address this before you enter the cavity. Exposure therapy with a qualified practitioner, using images, videos, and eventually live specimens, can reduce the panic response that large crawling things trigger in most humans. You will not eliminate the discomfort. You will learn to manage it, to override the panic with reasoned assessment: it is not attacking you, it is passing through, it will be gone in seconds.

Accept that you will be sharing your sleeping space with organisms you cannot see and cannot control. The inhabitants accept this. It is part of the cost of living in the cavity. You can accept it too, or you can leave. There is no third option.

### The Silence and the Sound

The cavity is quiet in the way a body is quiet when it is not speaking. There is a base layer of sound—the susurrus of water, the rustle of insects, the creak of root-mass—but it is constant, background, and you will stop hearing it within hours. What you will hear instead is the absence of the sounds you are accustomed to: wind, birds, traffic, the hum of electrical systems, the ambient noise of a world full of machines and weather and other people's activities.

The silence is not total, but it is deep enough that your own sounds become significant. Your footsteps. Your breathing. The rustle of your clothing. Every sound you make will be audible to everyone around you, and in a culture that values quiet by necessity, this will mark you as an outsider.

Practice being quiet. Not just not talking—being quiet in your movements, in your breathing, in the way you handle objects. Walk softly. Breathe through your nose. Close containers carefully. If you must speak, speak in murmurs, close to the ear of the person you are addressing, with a hand on their arm to provide context and locate the communication in physical space rather than acoustic space.

### The Social Reality

The inhabitants will not welcome you warmly. They will be quiet, watchful, assessing. This is not hostility. It is the appropriate response to a stranger entering a closed community of sixty people in a space where resources are carefully balanced and every new body is a strain on the system.

Do not expect to be hosted. Do not expect to be entertained. Do not expect to be taught. You may be offered guidance—a hand on your arm, directing you around obstacles. You may be offered food—a strip of New Skin, pale and slightly rubbery. You may be assigned a place to sleep and told, through gesture rather than words, where you should be and where you should not.

Accept these offerings as they are given, without projecting expectations onto them. The inhabitants are not your guides, your hosts, or your friends. They are people living in a difficult environment who have allowed you to visit, and their tolerance has limits that you will not be able to perceive until you cross them.

Understand that their economy is not transactional. Goods appear in the Deep Markets, sit in recesses, and are taken by those who need them, with contributions left by those who have surplus to share. Do not try to buy things. Do not offer payment. If you have something useful to contribute—a tool, a material, a skill—leave it in a recess where it will be found. Do not seek credit for it. Do not ask for anything in return. The system works because it is a flow, not a trade.

### The Absence of Day and Night

There is no day. There is no night. There is only the rhythm the community agrees upon, organized around the cavity's cycles and their own needs. Work periods begin when the cold is least bitter—when the root-mass has completed a full expansion cycle and the walls are radiating stored heat. Rest periods follow. Sleep is communal. Then the cycle repeats, stretching and compressing according to the community's needs and the cavity's signals.

Your circadian rhythm will be destroyed within the first day. Your body expects light and dark in cycles, and it will not receive them. You will feel sleepy when you should be alert and alert when you should be sleeping. You will lose track of how long you have been in the cavity, because there is no external reference for time.

Bring a simple timekeeping device—a mechanical watch, not a digital one, because digital devices will be compromised by the humidity. Check it if you need to, but understand that the inhabitants do not organize their lives by hours and minutes. They organize by the cavity's signals: the rhythm of the breathing walls, the pattern of moisture rising, the temperature shifts that indicate the root-mass's metabolic cycles. If you want to understand their experience, try to sync to their rhythm rather than imposing your own.

## Cultural Protocols and Behavior

### Observation Before Action

When you enter the cavity, do nothing until you have watched the inhabitants do it. Eat when they eat. Sleep when they sleep. Work when they work, if they allow you to participate. Move when they move, stop when they stop. The cavity has rhythms, and the community has rhythms nested within those rhythms, and you will not learn either by asking questions. You will learn by watching and mirroring.

Do not ask questions that can be answered by observation. Do not ask the inhabitants to explain their lives to you. They have no obligation to translate their existence into terms you can understand. If you are confused, stay confused. Watch longer. The answer will come, or it will not, and either way you will be fine.

### Reciprocity Without Transaction

The cavity's economy is flow-based, not transaction-based. This is a concept that most surface-dwellers find difficult to internalize. You take what you need from the Deep Markets and leave what you can spare. No one tracks who took what. No one keeps a ledger. The system works because everyone in the community has internalized the principle that taking more than you need threatens the system that keeps everyone alive, including yourself.

As a visitor, you are a net drain on this system. You consume food, occupy space, produce waste, and contribute nothing to the cavity's maintenance during your stay. You can offset this by contributing labor, if the inhabitants allow it—helping to clear water channels, tend fungus gardens, harvest New Skin. But do not assume your help is wanted or needed. Ask by gesture, by offering, by waiting to be directed. Do not initiate work without invitation.

When you leave, leave something behind. Not something large or valuable. Something useful, something that will enter the flow and find its way to whoever needs it. A tool. A material that does not exist in the cavity. Something that says: I was here, I received, I gave, the system continues.

### Respecting the Cavity

The inhabitants listen to the cavity. They read changes in air pressure, shifts in the rhythm of the breathing walls, patterns of moisture rising from below. These signals indicate stability or instability, abundance or scarcity, danger or safety. You cannot read these signals. You do not have the sensitivity, and you will not develop it during your visit.

This means you are dependent on the inhabitants for safety information. If they move, move with them. If they become quiet, become quiet. If they leave a chamber, leave the chamber. Do not ask why. They may not be able to explain in terms you understand—the signal was a shift in air pressure that indicated a passage was about to close, or a change in the wall-rhythm that suggested a water-table rise was imminent, and these are not things that translate into surface-world concepts of risk and response.

Trust them. They have been here for four hundred years. You have been here for a day.

### Interacting With the Fauna

Do not harm the insects. They are part of the system. The dust-crickets process waste. The weave-spiders filter the air. The glisten-mites do things that the inhabitants understand and you do not. Killing them is not just pointless—it disrupts the ecology that keeps the cavity livable.

If a deep-crawler crosses your path, freeze. Let it pass. It is not attacking you. It is moving through its territory, and you happen to be in it. If one crawls across your body while you sleep—and this may happen—lie still. Let it cross. It will continue on, indifferent, seeking the cracks from which it came. Panic will not help you. Movement may provoke a response that stillness would not.

The Hangers that descend from the ceiling respond to certain frequencies of vibration. Running, loud coughing, any resonant sound will cause them to curl upward and retract toward the ceiling. They will descend again once the disturbance passes. They have never attacked. But you move differently when something might retract above you, and this is not a flaw in your psychology. It is an appropriate response to an environment you do not control.

## When to Leave

Visitors always leave. The cavity is not for you. It is for them—for the sixty, the few, the adapted.

Know your limits before you enter and monitor them constantly while you are there. If your skin begins to break down, if your respiratory symptoms worsen rather than improve after the first few
