# Preparing for the Velostrade

## The Absence of Preparation

Before discussing what to bring, understand that no preparation will be sufficient. This is not pessimism—it is the first and most important truth about the Velostrade. You are entering a place where the fundamental constants have grown tired. Where time pools and drains like water in uneven ground. Where cause and effect have been neighbors for so long they've stopped respecting each other's boundaries.

You will not be ready. No one is ready. The researchers who have spent decades studying the plateau still find themselves disoriented, still lose hours they cannot account for, still return with data that contradicts itself. Prepare anyway. The act of preparation is not about readiness—it is about creating handholds in a place that offers none.

## Physical Preparation: The Body

### Altitude Acclimatization

The Velostrade sits approximately five kilometers above what was once continental shelf. The air is thin. Your blood will notice before your mind does.

Spend at least two weeks at high altitude before arrival. Not merely elevated—truly high. Three thousand meters minimum, four if you can manage it. Your body needs time to increase red blood cell production, to adjust your breathing patterns, to teach your cardiovascular system that it cannot trust the atmosphere to provide what it once did.

If you cannot acclimatize, bring supplemental oxygen. Not a mask—masks fail in the constant wet, clog with the fine particulate dust that drifts from the factory-growths. Use a nasal cannula instead, and bring twice as much oxygen as your calculations suggest. Distance is unreliable on the plateau. A planned three-day excursion may become five. Seven. You may walk for an hour and travel a kilometer, or five, or find yourself somewhere you never intended to go.

### Cold Tolerance

The cold here is not extreme but it is pervasive. The moisture cannot commit to freezing, so it exists in a state of perpetual almost-ice that soaks through anything less than absolutely sealed. Your body will lose heat constantly—not dramatically, not dangerously at first, but steadily, hour after hour, until you realize you cannot remember what warmth felt like.

Train your cold tolerance before arrival. Cold showers, progressively longer. Sleeping in unheated spaces. Learn to recognize the early signs of hypothermia in yourself—confusion, poor coordination, slurred speech—because on the plateau, these symptoms can also indicate phase-slippage, and you need to know which you are experiencing.

### Vaccination and Immune Preparation

The animals of the Velostrade have evolved in isolation for three billion years. Their pathogens bear no relationship to anything your immune system has encountered. There is no vaccine for what lives here.

What you can do is strengthen your general immune function in the months before travel. Sleep well. Eat well. Reduce stress. Arrive with your body's defenses at their peak. This will not protect you from everything—some researchers have returned with infections that took years to diagnose—but it may give you time to leave before a minor exposure becomes systemic.

## Gear: What to Bring

### Footwear

This is perhaps your most critical choice. The ground yields. It weeps. It is alive in ways that may react to pressure.

Bring boots with the following characteristics:
- **Ankle support** — The surface is uneven in ways that are not visible. You will step on what appears flat and find yourself suddenly twisted.
- **Waterproofing** — Not water-resistant. Waterproof. The moisture will find any gap, any seam, any weakness. It will soak through eventually regardless, but waterproofing buys you hours.
- **Thick soles with pressure distribution** — You are walking on a living membrane. The animals have learned which areas release toxic compounds when damaged. You have not. Thick soles distribute your weight, potentially preventing the localized pressure that triggers defensive responses.
- **Aggressive tread** — The water that seeps up makes surfaces slick. The particulate dust from the factories creates a fine grit that coats everything. You need traction that can handle both.

Bring two pairs. Keep one dry in sealed containers. Rotate them. When your boots are soaked through—and they will be—having a dry pair is not merely comfort but survival.

### Clothing: The Layer Problem

The Velostrade's moisture defeats most waterproof fabrics. It is not rain; it is a suspension, a state of water that exists in all phases simultaneously. GORE-TEX will eventually surrender. Rubber will eventually allow seepage at the seams.

Your best approach is multiple redundant layers:

1. **Base layer** — Merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking fabric. Your skin will be damp regardless, but this layer manages the dampness, prevents chafing, maintains some insulation even when wet.

2. **Mid layers** — Fleece or synthetic insulation. Multiple thin layers rather than one thick layer. This allows adjustment when the factory-growths' ambient heat or your own exertion temporarily overcomes the cold.

3. **Outer shell** — The most waterproof material you can acquire. Understand that this is a delaying measure, not a solution. The moisture will eventually penetrate. Check your seams constantly. Re-seal them with waterproof tape whenever you stop to rest.

4. **Head and hands** — A surprising amount of heat escapes through your extremities. Bring multiple layers for head and hands. The constant dampness makes fingers clumsy; cold makes them worse. You need manual dexterity to check equipment, prepare shelter, test the ground.

### Shelter

You cannot drive stakes into the Velostrade. The ground is living membrane, and penetrating it may trigger toxic responses. Your shelter must be free-standing.

A geodesic dome tent works well—the shape sheds water and resists wind from any direction. The constant drone of the factory-growths makes tent noise irrelevant; you will not sleep well regardless, so focus on dryness and warmth rather than comfort.

Bring a groundsheet larger than your tent's footprint. The ground weeps when compressed; sleeping directly on it means sleeping in a slowly expanding puddle. The groundsheet provides a barrier, though again, moisture will eventually find its way through.

### Sleeping Systems

A sleeping bag rated to temperatures lower than you expect. The plateau's cold is not extreme but it is relentless. Your body temperature will drop incrementally throughout the night unless you have adequate insulation.

Sleeping bag liners—synthetic or wool—add warmth and can be washed separately from the bag itself. The constant moisture means everything will eventually grow mildew; liners extend the functional life of your sleeping bag.

Consider a vapor barrier liner. This is controversial—some travelers find them claustrophobic, and they trap moisture against your skin—but in a place where the ambient humidity approaches saturation, preventing your insulation from becoming waterlogged may matter more than comfort.

### Navigation Equipment

This is where standard preparation fails most dramatically.

GPS works inconsistently. The phase-slippage affects signal propagation. Satellites that should be overhead may appear to be elsewhere. Your position may update with delays of minutes or hours. The altitude reading is accurate; the horizontal coordinates are suggestions.

Compasses behave strangely near the factory-growths. The cylinders contain significant amounts of iron and other metals drawn from the stone-flesh below. Magnetic north shifts as you approach and retreat from these structures.

The most reliable navigation method is dead reckoning combined with physical markers. Bring brightly colored flags on lightweight stakes. Place them as you travel. They will not prevent phase-slippage—sometimes you will look back and find your markers are closer or farther than you remember, or arranged in a pattern that does not match your memory of placing them—but they provide some reference in a landscape that otherwise extends unchanged to every horizon.

Do not rely on distance measurements. Walking one kilometer on the Velostrade may take fifteen minutes or two hours. It may leave you one kilometer from your starting point, or five, or somehow behind where you began. The researchers have not determined the variables that control this. There may be no variables. The plateau may simply be inconsistent.

### Water and Food

Water is everywhere on the Velostrade, seeping up from reservoirs trapped before multicellular life existed. Do not drink it without treatment.

The water has been in contact with the living ground for geological ages. It carries compounds produced by the plant-mats—some benign, some toxic, some that the researchers have not yet identified. It may contain the particulate output of the factory-growths. It may contain organisms that your immune system cannot recognize.

Bring a high-quality water filter—ceramic or hollow fiber, capable of filtering bacteria and protozoa. This is minimum preparation. Ideally, bring a filter combined with UV treatment or chemical purification. Layer your water treatment methods. The consequences of contamination on the plateau are not merely illness; they are illness in a place where help is distant and time is unreliable.

For food, bring calorie-dense items that require no preparation. The dampness makes cooking difficult; the constant drone makes restful eating nearly impossible; the phase-slippage may leave you without appetite or ravenously hungry for no apparent reason. Energy bars, dried meats, nuts, chocolate. Simple foods. Familiar foods. In a place where nothing is familiar, the taste of something you know provides psychological anchoring.

Bring more than you need. Your planned three-day excursion may last a week. Or you may find that time has compressed—you thought you were staying three days, but upon leaving you discover only one day passed in the world beyond the plateau. Or seven. Or a different number entirely. Your food supply should accommodate uncertainty.

### Light

The Velostrade has normal day-night cycles, but the fog reduces daylight to a diffuse gray, and the factory-growths' venting towers cast shadows that shift without reference to the sun's position.

Headlamps for each member of your party. Handheld flashlights as backup. The moisture will eventually infiltrate any electrical device; bring multiple lights, keep backup units in waterproof containers, and accept that some will fail.

Do not use red light to preserve night vision. The phase-slippage affects visual processing; what you see in low light may not be what is actually present. Full-spectrum light, while it attracts the attention of some animals, provides more accurate visual information.

### Medical Supplies

A comprehensive first aid kit. This is standard advice for any remote travel; on the Velostrade, it becomes essential.

Include:
- **Wound care supplies** — Cuts and scrapes heal slowly in the constant dampness. Infection risk is elevated. Clean wounds immediately, cover them with waterproof bandages, check them constantly.
- **Pain management** — Headaches are common among visitors. The cause is debated—altitude, dehydration, the constant low-frequency drone, or something related to phase-slippage. Bring ibuprofen and acetaminophen.
- **Antihistamines** — The plant-mats produce compounds that may trigger allergic responses. Some visitors develop reactions to the particulate dust. Antihistamines may also help with sleep, though the drone often overpowers any sedative effect.
- **Antibiotics** — Broad-spectrum, if you can obtain them. For infections that may not respond to standard treatment, they may buy you time to reach medical care.
- **Anti-anxiety medication** — Consider this carefully. The Velostrade induces a particular psychological strain—the constant drone, the phase-slippage, the unreliability of your own perceptions. For some travelers, medication helps maintain function. For others, it increases disorientation. Know yourself.

### Documentation and Research Equipment

Bring a camera. Take photographs constantly. Not for art—though the plateau has a stark, alien beauty—but for verification. Your memory will not be reliable. Phase-slippage affects recall as well as perception. The photographs may show things you do not remember seeing, or fail to show things you remember clearly. This is valuable data. It helps you understand how the plateau has affected you.

Keep a journal. Write in it at regular intervals—not when something notable happens, but on a schedule. Every hour, regardless of events. Record your observations, your physical state, your location (or what you believe your location to be), the time (though time is unreliable). This creates an external record that may reveal patterns in how the plateau affects you.

If you have the resources, bring monitoring equipment. A heart rate monitor. A thermometer. A blood oxygen sensor. The plateau affects bodies in ways that are not always perceptible; objective measurements may reveal changes you do not consciously notice.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

### The Drone

The industrial hum of the factory-growths never stops. It becomes background—inaudible after hours of exposure—until you leave and notice the silence more than you ever noticed the sound.

But even when you stop consciously hearing it, the drone affects you. Low-frequency vibration travels through the body, not merely the ears. It disrupts sleep patterns. It creates subtle anxiety. It may contribute to the disorientation that leads to phase-slippage incidents.

Prepare by exposing yourself to constant low-frequency noise before arrival. Not to build tolerance—tolerance does not fully develop—but to understand how your mind and body respond. Learn to recognize when the drone is affecting your cognition. Learn to compensate.

Some travelers find that ear protection helps; others find that blocking the drone entirely increases disorientation. Experiment before arrival to discover which approach works for you.

### The Fog and the Shapes

The fog that pools in low areas contains shapes. These shapes are not hallucinations—multiple observers see the same phenomena—but they are also not physical. They pass through solid objects. They leave no tracks. They do not interact with matter.

The animals ignore them entirely.

You must learn to do the same. The shapes are not harmful in any documented case. But they are distressing. They do not correspond to anything your visual processing evolved to handle. Some travelers become obsessed with them, spend hours trying to approach or communicate, lose time they cannot account for.

Before arrival, expose yourself to visual phenomena that challenge your perception. Optical illusions. Virtual reality environments with impossible geometry. Practice maintaining calm while your visual system reports impossible information. This does not prepare you for what you will see—nothing can—but it builds the mental muscle of accepting that your senses may report things that are not, in any meaningful sense, there.

### Phase-Slippage

This is the most difficult aspect to prepare for. The researchers have not determined how or why phase-slippage occurs. It may be related to the factory-growths' output. It may be an accumulation of temporal distortion in the living ground. It may be something about the plateau's extreme age that has worn thin the normal boundaries of causality.

You may find yourself suddenly elsewhere, without transition. You may observe animals in multiple places simultaneously. You may experience time moving at different rates—minutes passing while hours elapse, or the reverse. You may encounter versions of yourself, or versions of your traveling companions, that are simultaneously present and temporally displaced.

There is no preparation that prevents this. The animals have evolved to navigate it; you have not.

What you can do is:

1. **Establish protocols before arrival** — Agree with your traveling companions on procedures for phase-slippage events. If someone disappears and reappears elsewhere, where do you meet? If time passes differently for different members of the party, how do you synchronize?

2. **Maintain external records** — The journal, the photographs, the timestamps. When your memory conflicts with external records, trust the records. Your perception has been affected; the written word (probably) has not.

3. **Accept disorientation as baseline** — You will be confused. You will experience things that seem impossible. This is not a sign of failure or malfunction; it is the normal experience of the Velostrade. Do not fight the confusion. Observe it. Record it. Continue.

### The Indifference

The plateau does not care about you. This is not hostility—hostility requires attention. The Velostrade has persisted for three billion years. It has seen countless organisms live and die across its surface. You are neither threat nor guest; you are barely distinguishable from the background noise of biological process.

Some travelers find this liberating. They are unburdened from the expectation of meaning. Their presence does not matter; their actions do not register. They are free to simply observe.

Others find it profoundly unsettling. Humans generally expect the world to respond to them in some way—to notice them, to accommodate them, at minimum to acknowledge their presence. The Velostrade offers none of this. It extends in every direction, processes its unknowable industry, phase-slips through time, and does not mark your existence in any way.

Prepare by examining your relationship to significance. Why are you traveling? What do you expect from the experience? If the answer involves the plateau responding to your presence—even in hostility—reconsider. You will not be ignored. You will be irrelevant. There is a difference.

## Social Preparation

### Travel Companions

Do not travel alone to the Velostrade. The phase-slippage affects memory and perception; you need external verification that your experience reflects something real.

But choose your companions carefully. The plateau's strangeness creates psychological strain. Relationships that seem solid under normal circumstances may fracture when reality itself becomes unreliable.

Travel with people who:
- Remain calm under disorientation
- Communicate clearly even when stressed
- Trust each other's perceptions when their own perceptions are affected
- Can tolerate prolonged discomfort without complaint becoming corrosive
- Have compatible goals and expectations for the journey

Before arrival, discuss scenarios. What do you do if someone becomes separated? What if time passes differently for different members? What if someone's behavior suggests they have been affected by phase-slippage in ways that alter their judgment or personality? These conversations are difficult; have them anyway.

### Communication with the Outside

Establish check-in protocols before arrival. At what intervals will you attempt to contact someone outside the plateau? What should they do if you miss a check-in?

Understand that communication may fail. The phase-slippage affects signal propagation. Messages may arrive out of order, delayed, or not at all. Your emergency contact should know this and should have protocols for uncertainty—when to send rescue, when to assume equipment failure versus actual emergency.

Consider carrying an emergency beacon that transmits continuously rather than on demand. The plateau may distort the signal, but a continuous transmission at least provides something for rescuers to triangulate, even if the location they find is not precisely where you are.

## Duration and Exit Strategy

### Time Limits

Do not stay on the Velostrade for extended periods. The researchers limit their exposure; you should limit yours.

The phase-slippage effects accumulate. A day of exposure may leave you disoriented but functional. A week may leave you with memory gaps, temporal displacement symptoms that persist after departure, a sense that you are no longer quite aligned with consensus reality.

Set a maximum duration before arrival. Communicate this limit to your emergency contact. When you reach that limit—regardless of what you have or have not experienced, regardless of how much more there is to see—leave. The plateau will be there if you choose to return. Your coherence may not.

### The Exit

Departure from the Velostrade is not always straightforward. The phase-slippage may place you farther from your entry point than your travel should have allowed. Distance is unreliable. You may walk toward the edge and find yourself walking in place, or walking away.

The researchers have identified certain stable paths—routes where phase-slippage is less frequent, where distance corresponds more closely to effort. Obtain this information before arrival. Plan your exit route before you need it.

If you become truly lost—if the plateau has displaced you, if your navigation has failed, if you cannot find a stable path—move toward a factory-growth. The structures serve as landmarks, visible from considerable distance in the flat expanse. They may not help you navigate precisely, but they provide reference points in a landscape otherwise devoid of features.

Some travelers report that approaching a factory-growth increases phase-slippage. Others report the opposite. The data is contradictory. This may indicate that different structures have different effects, or that the same structure affects different travelers differently, or that the correlation is random. Move toward them anyway. They are the only landmarks available.

## What You Cannot Prepare For

You have read the above. You have acquired the gear, trained your body, prepared your mind, established protocols with your companions. You have done everything possible to be ready.

You are not ready.

The Velostrade is older than anything you have encountered. Older than anything you can truly comprehend. Three billion years of patient biological and geological and possibly temporal process have created something that operates by its own logic, consistent but opaque. The plants that became the ground and the ground that became factories and the factories that became an atmosphere and an ecosystem and a reality that phase-slips and drips and hums with ancient industry—this is not a place that yields to preparation.

But you will go anyway. That is what travelers do. You will accept that you cannot be ready, and you will prepare anyway, because the act of preparing is itself a form of respect for what you are about to encounter.

Go carefully. Test the ground before you step. Trust your records more than your memory. Listen to the drone until you stop hearing it, and then notice when it falls silent at your departure. Watch the animals—they have learned to navigate this place, and they are your best teachers.

Do not expect to understand. The researchers who have studied the plateau for decades do not understand. They have hypotheses about soil production and atmospheric processing and evolutionary vestiges. They have measurements of the phase-slippage, the water seeping upward from trapped reservoirs, the biological density of the living ground. They do not understand.

The plateau is not for understanding. It is for experiencing. It is for witnessing. It is for standing on a surface that was already ancient when multicellular life began, that has watched epochs pass like weather, that will continue its slow industrial-biological processes long after you and everyone you know has returned to the stone-flesh from which the plants draw their silica.

You will not leave unchanged. No one does. The phase-slippage, the drone, the moisture that cannot commit to freezing, the shapes in the fog, the animals that appear and disappear without transition—these will follow you out. They will settle into your perception like sediment. You will find yourself, months later, expecting the ground to yield beneath your feet. You will hear the drone in silence. You will see movement in peripheral vision that does not correspond to anything material.

This is the price of witnessing the Velostrade. It gives you nothing—no wisdom, no treasure, no achievement. It allows you to be present, briefly, in a place that operates by rules you cannot comprehend. It tolerates your existence without acknowledging it. It continues its processes without reference to you.

And then you leave, or you don't, and the plateau remains, patient beyond measure, humming and dripping and waiting for whatever comes next, which will almost certainly not be you.
