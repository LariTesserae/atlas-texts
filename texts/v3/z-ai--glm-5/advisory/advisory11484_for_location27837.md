# Preparation Guide: The Abyssal Plain of Constant Dissolution

## The Fundamental Reality

Before anything else, understand what you are attempting. You are not visiting a location so much as inserting yourself into a process that has been running for six hundred million years and will continue for six hundred million more after you leave. The Abyssal Plain does not care about you. It does not notice you. It has no mechanism for noticing—no eyes, no surface, no interface where meaning might adhere. You will be a temporary anomaly in a system that dissolves anomalies as its primary function.

Most locations in the multiverse offer something to the traveler—a view, an experience, a transformation. This place offers only presence. You will be present in a location where presence is actively, patiently, eternally being erased.

Prepare accordingly.

## Physical Preparation: The Body Under Siege

### Pressure Management

The pressure here is approximately 400 atmospheres—roughly 5,800 pounds per square inch. This is not a number your body can intuitively understand. You cannot "get used to it" through exposure. You cannot "tough it out." Without technological intervention, your chest cavity would collapse instantly, your eardrums would rupture, your sinuses would implode, and the air in your lungs would compress to approximately 1/400th of its surface volume.

You need a pressure suit designed for hadal zone exploration. Not recreational deep-sea equipment—there is no recreational version of this depth. You need military-grade or scientific research-grade atmospheric dive suits (ADS) that maintain internal pressure at approximately 1 atmosphere regardless of external conditions. The suit must be rigid. Soft suits transfer pressure; rigid suits resist it.

Your suit should include:

- **Positive pressure maintenance systems** that actively maintain internal atmosphere rather than simply sealing. The external pressure will find any weakness. It is patient. It has eons.
- **Pressure-rated viewports** with multiple redundant layers. If your viewport fails, the water enters at speeds that will fragment bone.
- **Joint mobility systems** designed for extreme depth. Movement will be difficult regardless. The water itself resists you. Your suit should not add to that resistance.
- **Emergency ballast release** for rapid ascent. If something goes wrong, you will need to leave quickly. "Quickly" at this depth still means hours of controlled decompression, but you may need to abandon the bottom on short notice.

### Thermal Protection

The water holds at approximately 1-4 degrees Celsius. This is cold enough to kill through hypothermia in fifteen to thirty minutes of unprotected exposure. Your suit must include active heating systems. Passive insulation will eventually equalize with the external temperature; active heating maintains the gradient.

But more importantly, understand that cold here is not simply an environmental condition—it is a *temporal* one. The cold slows everything. Reaction times. Cognitive processing. Emotional responses. You will think more slowly here. Your body will move more slowly. This is not a failure of will; it is chemistry. Prepare your expectations for a version of yourself that operates at reduced speed.

Bring chemical heat packs as backup. Bring more than you think you need. The cold here has been waiting for you since before your species existed.

### Breathing Systems

You will need a closed-circuit rebreather system with CO₂ scrubbers rated for extended duration. Open-circuit systems release bubbles; bubbles rise; the Abyssal Plain does not receive them kindly. The sound of released gas in this pressure regime is not a gentle emission—it is a violent event, a pocket of void forcing itself through saturated medium. It will distort your perception of the environment. It may attract attention you do not want.

More practically: you cannot carry enough compressed air for extended exploration. Closed-circuit systems maximize efficiency. Plan for at least 48 hours of breathing capacity even if you intend a shorter visit. Things happen at this depth. Ascent is not always immediate.

### The Medium Itself

The water here is chemically hostile. The description mentions iron, manganese, sulfur compounds—a cocktail of dissolved minerals that can interact with equipment in unexpected ways. Sulfides corrode metal. Manganese builds up on surfaces. Iron oxidizes.

Treat your equipment with anti-corrosive compounds rated for extended seawater exposure. Seal every joint, every port, every seam. The water here has been dissolving calcium carbonate for millions of years; it will not hesitate to interact with whatever you bring.

Bring water sampling equipment if you are scientifically inclined, but understand that the water will taste different when analyzed at the surface. The pressure itself changes chemistry. What you taste here—the iron and sulfur on the tongue—is a flavor that exists only in situ.

## Sensory Preparation: Operating in the Near-Absence of Input

### Vision

Visibility extends perhaps fifteen meters. Beyond that: inhabited darkness. Your eyes are adapted to surface light conditions—to photons scattered through atmosphere, to the sun's reliable presence, to the interplay of shadow and illumination. None of that exists here.

You will be tempted to bring powerful lights. Resist this temptation or modulate it carefully. Strong artificial light will create backscatter from the particulate-saturated water—whiteout conditions in the middle of absolute darkness. You will see the medium rather than through it.

Instead, bring:

- **Low-intensity red lights** for close work. Red light scatters less, penetrates further, and causes less backscatter in particulate-rich water.
- **Directional spotlights** for occasional distance viewing, but understand that what you illuminate will be a narrow slice of a vast darkness. The contrast between lit and unlit will be absolute. Your brain may struggle to process the boundaries.
- **Image intensification systems** designed for extremely low light conditions. Bioluminescence exists here—brief, practical flickers. Intensification systems may allow you to perceive these without the distortion of active illumination.

Most importantly: prepare your mind for *seeing without seeing*. You will spend extended periods in near-total darkness. Your brain will invent patterns. You will see movement that isn't there. The darkness dreams, and you will dream along with it. This is not hallucination in the clinical sense—it is your visual cortex attempting to fulfill its function with insufficient data. Recognize this phenomenon. Do not react to everything you see.

### Hearing

Sound travels differently here—faster, farther, distorted by density gradients and temperature layers. The acoustic environment is active with low-frequency pulses, distant collapses, the slow grinding of dissolution. Your ears, even protected by your suit, will pick up some of this as vibration rather than sound. You will feel sounds in your body.

Bring hydrophones designed for deep-ocean acoustic monitoring. Listen before you move. Sound will tell you things your eyes cannot—currents, density changes, distant geological activity, the movement of creatures beyond visual range. The plain speaks in frequencies you were not evolved to hear. Translation equipment helps.

But also prepare for the *silence*—not absence of sound but a specific quality of acoustic space where sounds arrive attenuated, distant, irrelevant. The silence has weight here. It presses against your eardrums. It creates a sense of isolation that can become psychologically destabilizing.

### Proprioception and Orientation

At this depth, with this pressure, in this darkness, your sense of orientation will fail. Up and down become suggestions rather than certainties. The plain is flat in geological terms, but you cannot see the horizon. You cannot see the bottom except as a texture fifteen meters below you. You cannot see the surface at all.

You need:

- **Inertial navigation systems** independent of visual or acoustic reference. GPS does not function here. Radio waves do not penetrate. You must track your own position relative to your entry point.
- **Depth gauges** with redundant systems. Knowing your depth is essential for safe ascent.
- **Altimeters** to maintain distance from the seafloor. You do not want to crash into sediment at speed because you cannot see it.
- **Compass systems** corrected for the local magnetic environment. The spreading center below produces magnetic anomalies. Surface compasses may not function accurately.

Prepare your mind for navigation without landmarks. There are no landmarks. There is only the flat, the grey, the particulate. You will travel for hours and see nothing that distinguishes one location from another. This disorientation is normal. It is also dangerous.

## Psychological Preparation: The Weight of Time

### Confronting Insignificance

This is the most important preparation you will make, and no equipment can provide it.

The Abyssal Plain is approximately six hundred million years old. It has existed in something like its current form since before complex life evolved on most worlds. It will exist for six hundred million years more. The processes that shape it operate on timescales that render human civilization invisible—a flicker, a frame, a moment too brief to register.

You will feel small here. The description says this is appropriate. It is more than appropriate; it is accurate. You are small here. Your entire species is small here. Your homeworld, in the context of this place's endurance, is small.

For some travelers, this realization produces a sense of peace—a release from the pressure of individual significance. For others, it produces existential dread, a collapse of meaning, a crisis of purpose. Neither response is wrong. Both are honest reactions to honest confrontation with deep time.

Prepare by considering in advance what your presence here means to you. Are you a witness? A student? A tourist? A pilgrim? The plain will not tell you what you are. It will simply provide the context in which you discover or fail to discover your answer.

### Managing the Darkness

The darkness here is not empty. It is *inhabited*—by particulates, by faint bioluminescence, by chemical gradients and pressure variations and slow currents and the sense of mass beyond the limit of sight. Your brain will attempt to fill the darkness with meaning because that is what brains do.

Some travelers report seeing faces in the murk. Others hear voices in the low-frequency pulses. Others feel watched—the distinct sensation of being observed by something that exists just beyond the edge of perception. These experiences are so common as to be considered normal aspects of deep-ocean psychology.

Prepare coping strategies:

- **Grounding techniques**—physical movements, internal counting, deliberate focus on immediate sensations.
- **Reality checking protocols**—predetermined questions to ask yourself when perception becomes uncertain.
- **Communication schedules**—regular check-ins with surface support or with yourself through recorded voice notes.
- **Acceptance**—recognizing that some uncertainty of perception is inherent to the environment rather than evidence of mental breakdown.

### The Isolation

You will be alone in a way that surface-dwelling minds struggle to process. Not the solitude of wilderness—wilderness has horizons, weather, the evidence of life continuing around you. This is isolation in a medium that resists your presence, in a darkness that does not acknowledge you, on a plain that has no interface with anything you recognize as a world.

Even with surface support (which adds complications; see below), your subjective experience will be of profound isolation. The water presses from every direction. The darkness extends beyond every limit. The plain continues past every horizon you cannot see.

Prepare by training for extended isolation. Spend time in deprivation chambers. Practice maintaining cognitive function and emotional stability in the absence of normal sensory input. Understand your own psychological breaking points and how to recognize them approaching.

## Logistical Preparation: Getting There and Returning

### Descent

Reaching the Abyssal Plain requires approximately two to four hours of controlled descent from the surface, depending on your vehicle and safety protocols. You cannot dive directly—the pressure change would kill you even in a pressure suit. You must descend in stages, allowing your body and your equipment to adjust to intermediate pressures, or you must descend in a vehicle that maintains surface pressure throughout.

Submersibles designed for hadal zone exploration exist in most technologically advanced universes. They are expensive, rare, and require significant logistical support. If you are traveling via multiverse transit, you may need to arrange vehicle acquisition at the destination universe in advance.

The descent itself is an experience. You will pass through the photic zone where light still penetrates, through the mesopelagic where bioluminescence becomes the dominant light source, through the bathypelagic where darkness becomes absolute, through the abyssopelagic where the pressure acquires its characteristic texture. Each layer is a different world. Each layer prepares you less than you might hope for the one below.

### Ascent

Ascent is more dangerous than descent. The pressure that has been pressing against you from every direction must be allowed to release gradually from your body tissues. Nitrogen dissolved in your blood at depth will form bubbles if you ascend too quickly—decompression sickness, the bends, a condition that can kill or permanently disable.

If you have been in a rigid pressure suit maintaining 1 atmosphere throughout your visit, decompression is less critical but still necessary—your body has been under external pressure even if your internal environment has remained stable. The transition from 400 atmospheres external to 1 atmosphere external must be gradual to prevent suit stress and to allow psychological adjustment.

Plan for:

- **6-12 hours of staged ascent** depending on duration of bottom time.
- **Medical monitoring** during ascent if possible.
- **Decompression chamber availability** at the surface for emergency treatment.
- **Post-dive observation period** of at least 24 hours before any further dives or altitude changes.

### Surface Support

Unless you are entirely self-sufficient—an expert pilot with independent emergency extraction capabilities—you will need surface support. A support vessel. A crew monitoring your dive. Communications systems. Emergency response plans.

But communication at this depth is difficult. Radio waves do not penetrate seawater. Acoustic communication works but is slow and limited in bandwidth. Your surface support will know approximately where you are and approximately how you are doing, but they will not be able to see what you see or respond instantly to problems.

Prepare detailed dive plans with contingencies. Establish check-in schedules. Define abort criteria—conditions under which you will terminate the dive and begin ascent regardless of objectives. Make sure your support team understands that at this depth, they cannot rescue you quickly. If something goes wrong on the bottom, you will likely solve it yourself or not solve it at all.

## Biological Preparation: The Life of the Plain

### What You Will See

Life here is sparse. You may travel for hours without seeing anything larger than a bacterial mat. What you do see will be strange not because it is alien (though it may be) but because it is *early*—primitive forms, templates, the original technologies of survival before life discovered complexity.

Common encounters:

- **Bacterial mats**: Purple-black stains on the sediment, chemosynthetic communities metabolizing chemical gradients. They are not dangerous. They are not exciting. They are the foundation of this ecosystem, and they deserve your respect.
- **Demersal fish**: Rare, slow, adapted to the pressure and darkness. They may not resemble fish as you know them—some have reduced eyes, some have no eyes at all, some have developed elaborate sensory structures that serve functions you cannot intuit. They will ignore you if you ignore them.
- **Invertebrates**: Sea cucumbers, deep-sea anemones, strange crustaceans that move with glacial patience. They process the particulate fall, the marine snow that drifts down from worlds above.
- **Whale falls**: If you are fortunate in a strange sense, you may encounter a whale carcass in various stages of decomposition. These are temporary ecosystems, islands of abundance in a desert of scarcity. They attract life. They also attract scavengers. Approach with interest but with appropriate caution.

### What You Will Not See

You will not see anything that wants to interact with you. Nothing here has evolved to recognize humans as prey, as predator, or as anything else. You are not part of any creature's ecological understanding. You are an anomaly.

This does not mean you are safe. Large creatures exist at this depth—sleeper sharks, giant squid, things that have no common names because they have never been given any. They are rare. You may not see them. But they exist, and they are adapted to a world where energy is precious and opportunity must be seized.

### Ethical Considerations

The life here operates at the edge of survival. Every movement has been calculated by evolution over millions of years to cost the minimum possible energy. Your presence introduces a new variable—light, sound, the displacement of water, the possibility of physical contact.

Move carefully. Minimize your acoustic footprint. Avoid touching creatures you encounter. Do not feed anything; do not attempt to interact. You are a witness, not a participant. The ecosystem here has survived for billions of years without your involvement. It will survive longer if you maintain your distance.

## Equipment Checklist

### Critical (Failure Means Death)

- Pressure-rated atmospheric dive suit with redundant life support
- Closed-circuit rebreather system with 48+ hours capacity
- Active heating system with battery backup
- Inertial navigation system
- Depth gauge with redundant systems
- Emergency ballast release
- Communications system (acoustic, through-water)

### Essential (Failure Significantly Impairs Mission)

- Low-intensity lighting systems (red spectrum preferred)
- Hydrophone for acoustic monitoring
- Altimeter for bottom distance
- Compass corrected for magnetic anomalies
- Video/camera systems for documentation
- Sample collection equipment (if research-oriented)

### Recommended (Enhances Experience/Safety)

- Backup lighting systems
- Extra batteries (cold reduces battery efficiency)
- Recording systems for voice notes
- Physical line or tether to descent vehicle (limits exploration but ensures return)
- Medkit for minor injuries
- Toolkit for suit repairs
- Nutrient paste and hydration systems for extended dives
- Sediment cores or collection tubes

### Optional (For Specialized Objectives)

- Biological sample preservatives
- Water sampling equipment
- Sediment traps
- Chemical sensors
- Magnetometers
- Seismometers
- Deep-sea biological observation equipment

## Duration Guidelines

**Minimum viable visit**: 4-6 hours bottom time. This allows descent, a brief exploration of the immediate area around your landing point, and ascent. You will see little. You will understand less. But you will have been there.

**Recommended visit**: 12-24 hours bottom time. This allows travel across a significant portion of the plain, observation of the environment through multiple tidal/flow cycles, and potential encounters with the sparse fauna. You will begin to understand the rhythm of the place.

**Extended visit**: 48-72 hours. This is the limit for most life support systems and for psychological tolerance. You will see the plain through its slow changes. You will adapt (as much as humans can) to the rhythm of dissolution. You will carry the weight of the place in your bones.

**Research/exploration visits**: Weeks to months. These require specialized equipment, rotating crews, and significant logistical support. They are beyond the scope of this guide but represent the kind of commitment necessary for true understanding.

## Mental Frameworks for the Visit

### The Pilgrimage Model

Treat this as a pilgrimage to an ancient site. The Abyssal Plain is a temple built by time and pressure, dedicated to dissolution and entropy. You do not go to change it or to take from it. You go to witness it. To be present in a place where presence has been erased. To stand in a cathedral older than the concept of cathedrals.

### The Scientific Model

Approach with curiosity and method. The processes here are fundamental—erosion, chemical dissolution, the evolution of life under extreme pressure, the dynamics of deep ocean circulation. This is a laboratory running experiments that began before life existed. Your job is to observe, record, and bring back data that might illuminate the processes that shape all worlds.

### The Mortality Model

Use the plain as a confrontation with deep time and your own insignificance. This is not morbid; it is accurate. The plain shows you what all things become—flattened, dissolved, distributed across the slow medium of time. To see this clearly is to understand something essential about existence that cannot be understood in shallower contexts.

### The Solitude Model

Use the isolation deliberately. This is an environment where the noise of ordinary existence cannot follow. The pressure, the darkness, the vast emptiness—these create a space of profound silence. Some travelers use this silence for meditation, for problem-solving, for the kind of thinking that requires the absence of distraction.

## What to Expect Upon Return

You will surface into a world that seems different. Not because it has changed but because you have. The light will seem too bright. The sounds will seem too loud. The presence of other people will seem overwhelming after the patient solitude of the deep.

You may experience:

- **Residual pressure sensations**—the feeling that something is pressing on your chest, your sinuses, the space behind your eyes. This will fade over hours to days.
- **Temporary visual processing issues**—difficulty with bright light, with complex visual environments, with the chaos of surface color and movement.
- **Emotional displacement**—a sense that surface concerns are trivial, that the anxieties of ordinary life lack the weight of the deep. This can produce both relief (surface problems matter less) and disconnection (you no longer fit in a world that matters so much to others).
- **Sleep disruption**—the pressure and darkness of the deep can affect sleep cycles, producing insomnia or unusual sleep patterns.
- **The lingering sense of the plain**—many travelers report that the Abyssal Plain stays with them, that they carry some quality of its vastness and patience into their subsequent lives. This is not harmful. It may be the point.

---

## Final Considerations

The Abyssal Plain of Constant Dissolution is not a destination for every traveler. It offers no spectacle. It provides no transformation in the conventional sense. It does not change you; it simply shows you what change looks like when measured in eons.

But for those who need to understand the deep context of existence—the timescales on which real change operates, the pressure under which all things eventually yield, the vastness in which all significance is revealed as temporary—there may be no better destination in all the multiverse.

The water presses. The darkness waits. The plain extends.

Prepare well. Go with respect. Return with the weight of time in your understanding.
