# Preparing for the Forebay

## What You Are Preparing For

You are preparing to enter an entirely submerged, fully enclosed, manufactured reservoir the size of a minor continent. There is no air anywhere within it. There is no shore. There is no surface in the conventional sense — only a sealed ceiling stretching to every horizon. The light comes from below. The sound comes from everywhere. The water is cool and will slowly drain the heat from your body for as long as you are in it. The nearest wall may be a hundred kilometers away in any given direction, and between you and it there may be nothing but water and the pale shapes of living things.

Understand this before anything else: the Forebay is not dangerous in the way that a volcano or a war zone is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way that the open ocean is dangerous — through scale, through exposure, through the simple fact that you are a small, warm, air-breathing animal inside a volume of water large enough to have its own weather. The Forebay will not attack you. It will simply continue around you, and if you are not prepared, it will continue without you.

## Physical Preparation

### Swimming and Water Competence

You need to be a competent swimmer before you arrive. Not a strong swimmer — a *competent* one. The difference matters. A strong swimmer is someone who can move through water with speed and efficiency. A competent swimmer is someone who can remain in water for extended periods without panic, who can manage their buoyancy intuitively, who can orient themselves in three dimensions without becoming disoriented, and who can conserve energy over long durations. The Forebay will not demand speed. It will demand endurance and composure.

Practice swimming in open water — not pools. Pools teach you to swim in a constrained environment with walls always nearby, with a bottom you can stand on, with clear water and even lighting. None of these conditions apply in the Forebay. You need to be comfortable in water where you cannot see the bottom, where you cannot see the walls, where the light comes from a direction you do not expect, and where the space around you is simply *large* in a way that no pool can replicate.

If you can, practice with a freediving or diving club. Get comfortable with mask, fins, and the feeling of being underwater for minutes at a time. You do not need to be a freediver — you will not be holding your breath in the Forebay, you will have breathing equipment — but the physical and mental experience of being submerged for extended periods, of managing your body's response to depth and pressure and enclosed water, is directly transferable.

### Thermal Tolerance

The water in the Forebay is fourteen or fifteen degrees Celsius in the upper and mid-water columns. This is not immediately dangerous, but it is below the temperature at which the human body can maintain its core heat indefinitely. You will lose heat slowly and continuously. Without thermal protection, you would begin to feel genuinely cold within twenty to thirty minutes, and within an hour you would be hypothermic.

Train your cold tolerance. Cold showers and cold baths help. Open-water swimming in temperate climates helps more. You want your body to have some familiarity with the sensation of being in cool water for extended periods, so that the initial shock does not consume mental bandwidth you will need for other things. You are not trying to become immune to cold — that is not possible — but you are trying to make the cold a familiar, manageable background sensation rather than a novel, alarming foreground one.

### Fitness

You need moderate cardiovascular fitness and reasonable upper-body and core strength. You will be swimming, possibly for hours, possibly against currents. You will be wearing equipment. You do not need to be an athlete, but you should be able to sustain moderate physical exertion for an hour without becoming exhausted, and you should be able to recover reasonably quickly after exertion.

If you cannot tread water for fifteen minutes while carrying a moderate load, you are not ready. Work up to it.

## Equipment

### Breathing Apparatus

This is the single most critical piece of equipment. There is no air in the Forebay. None. The ceiling is sealed. There is no surface to break to, no pocket of atmosphere to find. You will breathe from what you carry.

You have two main options:

**Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR):** This is the better option. A rebreather scrubs carbon dioxide from your exhaled breath and recycles the oxygen, meaning a relatively small supply of gas can last for hours — potentially four to eight hours depending on the unit and your exertion level. A rebreather also produces few or no bubbles, which matters in the Forebay: bubbles rise to the ceiling and accumulate, and the sound of bubbling is constant and loud in an enclosed underwater environment. A rebreather makes you quieter, less disruptive, and longer-lasting.

However, a rebreather is complex equipment. It requires training. If you are not already certified on a CCR, do not attempt to learn on the Forebay. The failure modes are subtle and lethal — oxygen toxicity, hypoxia, CO₂ buildup — and in a place with no surface and no rescue infrastructure, a rebreather malfunction could be fatal before you have time to react.

**Open-circuit scuba:** Simpler, more reliable, more forgiving of user error. The tradeoff is duration: a standard setup with a single large cylinder might give you forty-five minutes to an hour of bottom time at moderate depth. You will need multiple cylinders if you plan to stay longer, and managing multiple cylinders adds complexity and weight. Open-circuit also produces bubbles, which in the Forebay will rise to the ceiling and create a continuous acoustic signature that may attract attention from the fauna.

**Recommendation:** If you are trained on a rebreather, use one. If you are not, use open-circuit scuba with the largest cylinders you can comfortably manage, plan your time conservatively, and carry a redundant gas supply — a pony bottle or a bailout cylinder — at all times. Assume your primary gas will fail at the worst possible moment. Plan for that.

Regardless of system: test your breathing apparatus thoroughly before entering. Ensure all seals are intact, all valves function, all gauges read correctly. In the Forebay, a gas leak is not an inconvenience — it is a countdown.

### Thermal Protection

You need a full-body exposure suit. The water temperature of fourteen to fifteen degrees is manageable with proper insulation for periods of up to several hours, but without it, you will become hypothermic.

**Dry suit:** The best option. A dry suit seals your body away from the water entirely, and you wear insulating garments underneath. With appropriate undergarments, you can remain warm in fourteen-degree water for four to six hours or more. A dry suit also provides a layer of physical protection against abrasion if you contact structures.

Ensure the dry suit fits correctly and all seals — wrist, neck, zipper — are intact. A dry suit flood in the Forebay is a serious emergency: you will suddenly be immersed in cool water with no insulation, and you will need to end your visit immediately. Carry a backup plan for this contingency.

**Wet suit:** Acceptable but inferior. A thick wet suit — 7mm or thicker, preferably a two-piece or semi-dry with attached hood — will keep you functional for perhaps an hour to ninety minutes before the cold begins to significantly impair you. If you choose a wet suit, plan your visit accordingly and get out before you get cold, not after.

In either case: wear a hood. You lose a significant percentage of your body heat through your head, and in the Forebay there is no warm air to periodically rewarm your face. A neoprene hood is essential. Wear gloves as well — not just for warmth but for protection. The manufactured surfaces of the Forebay can be sharp, especially the freshly-poured floor sections, and the biological growth on structures can be slick or irritating to bare skin.

### Mask and Vision

Your mask is your primary interface with the Forebay. Use a low-volume mask with a wide field of view. The light in the Forebay comes from below and is pale blue-green, which means the visual environment is unlike either daylight diving or typical night diving. Contrast will be low. Objects at distance will fade into haze. You need a mask that maximizes your ability to see in these conditions.

Fogging is a constant risk. Treat your mask with anti-fog solution before entering, and carry a small supply for reapplication if needed. In the Forebay, removing your mask to clear it means temporarily losing your visual reference entirely, and in a place with no surface and no horizon, even a few seconds of visual loss can be disorienting.

Consider a purge-valve mask. In an environment where you may be swimming for extended periods and cannot easily clear your mask by tilting your head back (there is no "up" to tilt toward in the conventional sense — the ceiling is above you but it is not a surface), a purge valve simplifies mask clearing.

### Dive Light

You will need a personal dive light, but understand its limitations. The Forebay's ambient light — the pale blue-green glow from the conduit grid below — provides enough illumination to see your immediate surroundings out to perhaps twenty or thirty meters. Beyond that, the haze swallows everything. A dive light will cut through that haze for a short distance — ten to twenty meters depending on power — and will allow you to inspect structures, read instruments, and signal.

But a dive light will not solve the fundamental visibility problem, which is that the Forebay is too large for its own light. Beyond the reach of the conduit grid, out at the edges, there is no light at all, and no dive light you can carry will illuminate a volume that vast. Do not rely on your light to navigate. Use it for close inspection and emergency signaling only.

Carry at least two lights — a primary and a backup. The Forebay is not dark, but if you descend toward the floor or stray beyond the conduit grid, it can become dark very quickly, and in total darkness in a place this large, you are in serious trouble.

### Buoyancy and Depth Control

In the Forebay, buoyancy control is life. There is no surface to ascend to if you lose control. There is no floor shallow enough to stand on. You are suspended in a water column that is thousands of meters deep, and if you sink, you will sink a very long way before anything stops you.

Use a buoyancy control device (BCD) — a standard diving BCD or, if using a rebreather, an integrated wing. Ensure it is in good working order, with no leaks and fully functional inflation and deflation mechanisms. Practice buoyancy control before you arrive until it is instinctive: you should be able to maintain a constant depth within half a meter without conscious effort.

Consider your weighting carefully. You want to be neutrally buoyant at your intended depth. Over-weighting will cause you to sink if you lose inflation control; under-weighting will cause you to ascend. In a place with no surface and a sealed ceiling, ascending too far is not a danger in the way it is in open water (you will not hit the surface and float away — the ceiling will stop you), but you could impact the ceiling, and the ceiling is a manufactured surface that may have features, seams, or protrusions that could injure you.

### Navigation Equipment

This is critical and difficult. The Forebay is large enough that you cannot navigate by swimming along a wall or following the bottom. The wall may be a hundred kilometers away. The bottom is thousands of meters down. You are in open water, in three dimensions, in a space with no natural landmarks.

The conduit grid provides a reference: the luminous lines run in straight, parallel rows at mid-depth, forming a grid. If you can see the grid, you can orient yourself relative to it. The grid runs in consistent directions, and if you note the direction of the lines when you enter, you can use them as a rough compass.

But the grid is large, and if you swim far enough from it, or if you descend below it or ascend above it, you will lose it. Carry a compass — a standard diving compass, wrist-mounted or console-mounted. Note your heading when you enter and track it throughout. Be aware that in a space this large, a compass error of a few degrees translates to a displacement of kilometers over long distances.

A depth gauge is essential. You need to know your depth at all times, not for decompression reasons (though those exist if you go deep enough) but for orientation. In the Forebay, the light grid is at mid-depth. If you know the grid is at, say, five hundred meters, and your depth gauge reads five hundred, you know you are at the grid level. If it reads two hundred, you are above the grid. If it reads a thousand, you are below it. Depth is your primary vertical coordinate.

Consider carrying a distance-measuring device — a simple dive computer that tracks distance swum, or a mechanical paddle wheel. In the Forebay, "how far have I swum" is a critical question, and human estimation of distance in featureless water is catastrophically unreliable. After twenty minutes of swimming, you will not know whether you have traveled two hundred meters or two kilometers.

If possible, carry a reel and line. If you enter from a specific point — a hatch, an access port, a docking structure — you can tie off and pay out line as you swim. This is the most reliable way to return to your entry point. Be aware that in a space with currents, line can drift, tangle, or snag on structures. Use a cave-diving reel, which is designed for this kind of deployment, and practice with it before you arrive.

### Emergency Equipment

**Surface marker buoy or lift bag:** In the Forebay there is no surface, but a lift bag inflated at the ceiling can serve as a visual marker if you need to signal your position. Inflate it at the ceiling and it will press against the substrate above, visible from a distance in the conduit light.

**Acoustic signaling device:** Sound travels extremely well in the Forebay. A simple underwater noisemaker — a tank banger, a shaker, an electronic sonic device — can be heard for kilometers. If you become lost or disoriented, a periodic acoustic signal can guide a search. However, be aware that the Forebay's fauna also use sound, and the predators in particular communicate with low barking calls. Choose a signal that is distinct from the biological sounds of the environment.

**Knife or cutting tool:** Essential. The Forebay has structures, cables, conduits, and organic growth that you could become entangled in. Carry a diving knife or shears on your person, within easy reach, at all times.

**First aid:** Minimal. In the Forebay itself, your ability to treat injuries is limited. Carry basic wound-care supplies in a waterproof container, but understand that serious injury in the water column is a life-threatening emergency that requires extraction, not treatment in place.

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

### Spatial Disorientation

This is the single greatest psychological challenge of the Forebay. You will be suspended in water, in three dimensions, with no floor beneath you, no surface above you (only a ceiling, and only if you are near the top), and no walls in sight. The light comes from below, which inverts your visual expectations. The water is hazy, which limits visibility in all directions. There is no horizon. There is no "up" in the way your brain expects there to be.

Many people experience profound disorientation in conditions like these. Your vestibular system, which relies on gravity to determine vertical, will function correctly — you will know which way is up — but your visual system will disagree. The light is below you. The darkness is above you. Everything your eyes tell you about orientation will conflict with what your inner ear tells you. Some people adapt quickly. Some do not. Some panic.

Prepare for this. Practice diving in low-visibility conditions where you cannot see the bottom or the surface. Practice diving in overhead environments — caves, wrecks, ice — where you cannot ascend directly. Learn to trust your instruments over your senses. Learn to trust your vestibular system over your eyes. If you have access to a pool or training environment where lights can be placed on the floor, practice swimming with the primary light source below you, so the visual inversion becomes familiar.

The disorientation will not go away. You will simply learn to function within it.

### Isolation and Scale

The Forebay is large enough that you can be alone in it in a way that few environments allow. Not alone in the sense of "no people nearby" — alone in the sense of "nothing nearby." You can swim for twenty minutes without encountering a structure. The nearest wall is a hundred kilometers away. The floor is thousands of meters down. If something goes wrong, no one is coming. There is no rescue service. There is no communication infrastructure. There is you, your equipment, and the water.

This is a psychological weight that you should sit with before you arrive. Think about it. Imagine being in open ocean, at night, with no boat, no radio, no horizon — and then imagine that the ocean has a ceiling, so you cannot even look up and see stars. That is the Forebay.

Prepare by spending time alone in large, empty spaces. Open water. Desert. Anywhere that gives you the experience of being small in a large environment. Notice your emotional response. If it is terror, the Forebay will be difficult. If it is awe, the Forebay will be extraordinary. If it is something in between — and for most people it is — you will need to manage that response in real time, underwater, while also managing your equipment, your buoyancy, your gas supply, and your thermal state.

### The Sound

The sound in the Forebay is constant, enormous, and unavoidable. Underwater, sound arrives from every direction simultaneously. The deep throb of pumps. The hiss of water through pipes. The subsonic hum that you feel in your chest. The seventeen-minute cycle of the grinding pump. The barking calls of predators. The echoes, lingering for fifteen, twenty seconds, layering on each other until the soundscape is a continuous, textured drone.

This can be overwhelming. Some people find sustained underwater sound soothing — it can become meditative, a kind of white noise that fills the mind and crowds out anxious thought. Others find it oppressive, a constant pressure on the senses that makes it difficult to think or focus.

You cannot wear earplugs underwater — your ears need to equalize pressure. You will hear everything. Prepare for this by spending time in loud, resonant environments before you arrive. Listen to recordings of industrial underwater sound if you can find them. Understand what it feels like to have sound arrive through your body rather than through your ears. The hum in the Forebay is felt in the chest, in the bones, before it is heard. That is a specific physical experience, and it is easier to manage if it is not entirely novel.

The seventeen-minute pump cycle will become your clock. You will start counting the intervals. You will start anticipating the grinding wheeze before it arrives. This is not a problem — it is actually useful, a rhythmic anchor in an environment with few temporal references. But be aware of it. Be aware that your mind will attune to it, and that when it arrives, you will feel it pass through you, and that this will become a kind of companionship over time.

### The Emptiness

The Forebay is not empty — it is full of water, full of life, full of sound and light and motion. But it *feels* empty in a way that is deliberate and striking. The space between structures is vast. The space between you and anything solid is vast. You are suspended in a medium that holds you but does not support you, in a volume that extends beyond your perception in every direction.

Some visitors to large underwater environments describe a sensation of dissolution — a feeling that they are losing their edges, becoming part of the water, becoming part of the space. This is not a hallucination or a mystical experience. It is a predictable psychological response to sustained immersion in a featureless, large-volume environment. Your brain, lacking fixed visual and tactile references, begins to relax its sense of your body's boundaries.

This can be pleasant. It can also be frightening. Prepare for it. Know that it can happen, that it is normal, and that it passes. If you feel yourself dissolving, focus on a fixed point — your hands, your equipment, a structure if one is near. Remind yourself of your edges. The sensation will recede.

### The Fauna

The animals of the Forebay are not dangerous to you in the way that sharks or large marine predators are dangerous. They are predators of fish and gelatinous organisms, and you are neither. They are not likely to investigate you as prey.

But they are present, and they are large enough to be alarming if you are not prepared for them. The dark-bodied predators that circle the fish schools are the most visually imposing, and their barking calls are the loudest biological sound in the environment. When they kill, they vocalize rapidly, and other predators converge on the kill site, and the water churns with activity. This is dramatic and can be frightening if you are nearby and do not understand what is happening.

Prepare by learning the sounds before you arrive. If you can obtain recordings — and recordings exist in some multiverse archives — listen to them until you can distinguish the territorial barking from the feeding stutter. Know that the feeding calls will draw other predators and that the activity will be intense but brief. Know that you are not involved. The predators are communicating with each other, not with you, and their attention is on their prey and their competitors, not on a small, strange organism in a dry suit.

Do not approach a kill site. Not because the predators will attack you — they almost certainly will not — but because the acoustic intensity at close range can be disorienting, and the churning water can disrupt your buoyancy, and in the confusion you could lose track of your position, your gas supply, or your orientation.

The gelatinous organisms are harmless. They will drift past you, pulsing, refracting the conduit light. They are beautiful. You can swim through clouds of them without risk. They may settle on you if you remain still — they settle on everything — but they will not harm you. Brush them off gently if they accumulate on your mask or equipment.

## Practical Planning

### Entry and Exit

You need to know how you are getting in and how you are getting out. The Forebay is a sealed structure. There is no natural entry point. You will enter through a hatch, a valve, an access port, or a constructed opening of some kind — and that opening is your lifeline. You must be able to return to it.

Before entering, study the entry point. Note its depth relative to the surface (ceiling). Note its position relative to the conduit grid — is it above the grid, below it, at the same level? Note any structural features near the entry that you could use as visual landmarks. If there are none — and there may not be — rely on your compass and your line.

If at all possible, enter with a fixed line attached to the entry point. This is your primary navigation aid. If you cannot use a line for the entire visit (because you plan to travel too far, or because the currents would make it unmanageable), at least note your compass heading from the entry point and plan your return as a reverse heading.

Do not assume you will "find your way back." The Forebay is too large and too featureless for that. Plan your return before you enter. Rehearse it. Know your gas supply and calculate your return time with a margin — plan to be back at the entry point with at least twenty percent of your gas remaining. If you do not have enough gas to make the round trip with that margin, do not go as far as you planned. Turn back earlier.

### Duration

How long should you stay? That depends on your equipment, your thermal tolerance, and your psychological state. A conservative first visit: forty-five minutes to an hour. This is enough to experience the environment — the light, the sound, the scale, the fauna — without pushing your limits on gas, thermal protection, or mental endurance.

If all goes well, you can extend future visits. But the first visit should be short. The Forebay is not going anywhere. You can return.

### Depth Planning

The conduit grid is at mid-depth. This is where the light is, where the visibility is best, and where most of the fauna activity occurs. Plan to spend most of your time at or near the grid level. This gives you the best visual references and the richest experience of the environment.

Descending toward the floor is possible but demanding. The floor is thousands of meters down, and the descent will take you below the conduit grid into darkness. You will need your dive light. The thermal plumes are interesting — the water warms as you descend — but the floor itself is a long way down, and the deeper you go, the harder the return ascent becomes in terms of gas consumption and physical exertion. If you plan to visit the floor, plan for it specifically: calculate your gas for the round trip, carry a light, and be prepared for total darkness below the grid.

Ascending to the ceiling is less demanding but less rewarding. The ceiling is a flat, featureless expanse of manufactured substrate. It is interesting to see — the seams, the construction, the scale — but there is less life, less light, and less to engage with. A brief visit to the ceiling to experience the scale of the place from above is worthwhile. A prolonged stay is not.

### Currents

The Forebay has internal currents — thermal plumes, slow gyres, gradient flows. These are generally slow and manageable, but over long distances they can displace you significantly. If you swim for twenty minutes against a gentle current, you may make far less progress than you think, and the return trip — now with the current behind you — will be faster than expected, which sounds pleasant but means you could overshoot your entry point.

Be aware of current direction. Check it when you enter: note which way loose particles drift, which way the gelatinous organisms are moving, whether there is a consistent deflection of your line. Factor the current into your navigation. If the current is running east at a quarter knot, and you swim north, it is pushing you east the entire time. Your return heading should account for this.

### Communication

If you are traveling with others — and you should consider traveling with others — you need a communication plan. Hand signals work at close range but are useless beyond visual distance. Underwater communication devices exist — acoustic systems that allow voice communication over short distances — but their range in the Forebay's acoustic environment may be limited by the ambient noise. Slates and waterproof writing materials allow note-passing but require proximity.

The simplest reliable system: stay together. If you are with a buddy, maintain visual contact at all times. If you lose contact, establish a protocol in advance: wait in place for a set period, then surface to the ceiling (which is a known reference), then navigate by compass back to the entry point. Do not search for a lost buddy in open water. You will both become lost.

## What to Expect: A Sensory Guide

### What You Will See

Pale blue-green light from below, scattering into haze. The conduit grid: long, straight lines of light running in parallel, receding into the distance until they become threads and then vanish. The water: clear enough to see perhaps twenty to thirty meters, hazy beyond that, opaque at distance. Fish: pale, streamlined, schooling near the warm updrafts. Predators: dark, quick, circling. Gelatinous organisms: translucent, drifting, catching the light. Structures: occasional, sparse, industrial — pipe housings, maintenance fixtures, the edges of the heat-exchange system. The ceiling: flat, featureless, stretching to infinity, with visible seams if you are close enough. The floor: visible only if you descend deep, a patchwork of old and new substrate, lit only by your own light.

### What You Will Hear

Everything. The pumps, throbbing below. The hiss of water through pipes. The subsonic hum in your chest. The seventeen-minute cycle. The barking of predators — two or three notes, repeated, carrying for kilometers. The feeding stutter — rapid, excited. The click-and-whistle of smaller organisms. The dull rush of current through grates. The distant metallic clanking of machinery. And over all of it, the echoes: every sound lingering for fifteen, twenty seconds, layering, blending, creating a continuous textured soundscape that is never silent and never the same from one moment to the next.

### What You Will Feel

Cool water on every exposed surface. The pressure of your mask on your face. The slight resistance of the water as you swim. The warmth of the thermal plumes if you descend into them — a sudden, gentle increase in temperature, like swimming through an invisible warm current. The vibration of the machinery in your chest and bones. The seventeen-minute pulse of the pump, arriving and passing through you. The gelatinous organisms, if they drift against you — a soft, cool, contact, almost nothing, barely there. Your own breathing, rhythmic, mechanical, constant. The slow drain of heat from your body. The weight of the water above and below and around you, the pressure of the volume, the sense of space, the sense of between.

## A Note on the Experience

You are going to a place that is entirely made and entirely functional and has, through the sheer scale of its making and the length of its functioning, become a world. The Forebay is not natural. Nothing in it is natural. And yet everything in it has become natural — the fish, the predators, the gelatinous drifters, the microbial films, the threadlike growth in the seams. Life has colonized the machine and the machine continues and neither the life nor the machine knows or cares that the other exists. They simply coexist, each doing what it does, each continuing.

When you are there, in the water, in the light, in the sound, you will be inside this coexistence. You will be a small, temporary presence in a system that has been running longer than you can imagine and will continue running after you leave. The pumps will cycle. The lights will hold. The fish will school. The predators will call. And you will be there, briefly, in the middle of it, between the light and the ceiling, between the warm and the cool, between the call and its echo.

It is not a place that will change you in a dramatic, sudden way. It is a place that is very large and very quiet in its significance. You will feel small. You will feel made — not in a spiritual sense, but in the recognition that you, too, are a constructed thing, a system that runs, that processes, that cycles, that continues. You will recognize yourself in the machinery and in the fish and in the drifting gelatinous things, and that recognition will be uncomfortable and then familiar and then simply true.

Prepare your equipment. Prepare your body. Prepare your mind.

Then go.
