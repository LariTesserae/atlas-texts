# Preparing for the Cistern of Orami

## What You Are Walking Into

You are descending into a world that exists inside a container. This is not metaphorical. The Cistern of Orami is a carved stone vessel—sixty feet across, forty feet deep, shaped like the inside of a skull—that has filled with human life the way it once filled with water. Everything about it is contained, compressed, concentrated. The air, the sound, the moisture, the bodies, the time itself. You will feel this immediately. Most travelers do not adequately prepare for the sensation of being *inside* something that is also a place.

Understand: this is not an underground city in the romantic sense. There are no grand avenues, no hidden kingdoms, no ancient magic waiting to be discovered. It is a hole in the ground where people have stacked themselves and their possessions because they had nowhere else to go. And yet it has become something more than that—a living system, an ecology, a community with seven centuries of accumulated momentum. You are visiting a place that functions as a single organism, and you will be perceived as a foreign cell within it.

## Physical Preparation

### Your Lungs

The first system that will be taxed is your respiratory system. The air in the cistern is thick. This is not poetic description—it is a measurable quality. The limited ventilation through the single oculus means that smoke from the kiln, cookfire particulates, dust from the stone, dander from dozens of animals, and the exhalations of perhaps two hundred human beings all accumulate in the dome. The moisture holds these particles suspended. You will taste it within minutes of descending: a warm, wet thickness that coats the throat.

**Recommendations:**
- If you have any respiratory sensitivity—asthma, chronic bronchitis, susceptibility to infection—wear a cloth mask dampened with clean water. The cistern's inhabitants will not find this strange; several of them do the same during flu seasons or when the smoke from the kiln is particularly bad.
- Bring your own water supply for the descent, at least two liters per person. The cistern has water, yes—the sump in the center collects the constant drip from the walls—but this water is brown, used, and requires boiling. Do not drink it unless you have no alternative. Even boiled, it carries the taste of everything it has passed through.
- Consider a regimen of throat lozenges or honey if you plan to stay more than a few hours. The constant low-grade irritation of the air will exhaust your voice faster than you expect. This matters because in the cistern, your voice is your primary tool for navigation and negotiation.

### Your Ears

The sound will be your second shock. The dome architecture captures and multiplies noise in ways that most surface-dwellers have never experienced. A single conversation becomes three echoes; a shout becomes overwhelming. The cumulative effect is a wall of sound that presses against you, that you must push through to communicate.

**What to expect:**
- You will instinctively raise your voice to be heard. This is exactly wrong. The acoustics will take your shout and smash it against every surface, creating more noise for everyone. The cistern's inhabitants have learned to speak close, to murmur, to use a compressed vocabulary that carries meaning without carrying far. Watch them. Imitate them.
- The pressure on your ears will cause fatigue. Some travelers report headaches after as little as an hour. There is no solution to this except acclimation or departure. Plan your visit accordingly.
- There is a deeper danger. The strange echoes—the ones that return with altered words, added syllables—have been noted by multiple observers across centuries. The inhabitants have taboos about certain phrases, certain ways of speaking. They are not superstitious fools; they are practical people who have learned through trial and error which sounds the cistern "likes" and which it distorts. When in doubt, speak less. Listen more. Never repeat something you hear echoed back to you, even if it seems to be your own words.

### Your Skin and Body

The warmth is immediate and pervasive. The cistern maintains a temperature just above comfortable—a humid heat that causes constant low-level perspiration. This is not the dry heat of a desert or the baking heat of an oven; it is a living heat, the heat of bodies and breath and small fires trapped in stone.

**Clothing recommendations:**
- Wear layers that can be shed. You will need them on the surface approach (the region around the cistern entrance is exposed and can be cold, depending on the season) but you will want to remove most of them within minutes of descending.
- Natural fibers are essential. The moisture will make synthetic materials clammy and uncomfortable against the skin. Cotton, linen, or light wool will serve you better.
- Dark colors are practical. The constant drip from the ceiling—the slow rain of condensation—will spot your clothing. The inhabitants do not mind; they are used to it. But you will look less obviously a foreigner if your garments do not show the water marks.
- Sturdy footwear with grip. The floor surfaces are uneven—reed mats over stone, salvaged tiles, beaten earth—and the walkways that connect the platforms can be slick with moisture and wear. There are no guardrails on many of the bridges. A fall from the upper levels would be serious; a fall into the sump would be disgusting and possibly dangerous.

### Your Eyes

The light in the cistern is unlike anything on the surface. A single column of diffused sunlight falls through the oculus, creating a pool of illumination that shifts with the time of day. The rest of the space is lit by this reflected light, by cookfires, by the strange luminescence that seems to adhere to the dust particles suspended in the air.

**What this means for you:**
- Your eyes will adjust, but it takes time. The first twenty minutes will be a navigation between glare and shadow. Do not attempt to move quickly during this period.
- If you need to read or examine something in detail, position yourself in the light column. The inhabitants do this naturally; you will see them shift their work positions as the sun moves across the sky.
- The dust in the air can irritate the eyes. If you wear contact lenses, consider switching to glasses for the duration of your visit. If you must wear lenses, bring rewetting drops and use them frequently.
- Some travelers report a strange quality to vision in the cistern—a sense that the light "pools" in ways that violate normal physics, that time itself seems to slow in certain patches of illumination. Whether this is a real phenomenon or a psychological response to the environment is unclear. Do not be alarmed if you experience it. Do not stare too long at the light column if it seems to be moving differently than it should.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Density of Presence

The cistern is home to approximately two hundred permanent residents, several dozen animals, and uncounted insects and vermin. All of them exist in a space smaller than most surface village squares. You cannot escape the presence of others. There is no horizon to gaze at, no empty field to walk into, no quiet corner that has not already been claimed by someone who will want to know why you are in their space.

For travelers accustomed to privacy, this can be overwhelming. The constant proximity of bodies, the impossibility of true solitude, the awareness that every word you speak might be heard by a dozen ears—you may find yourself craving space that does not exist.

**Mental preparation:**
- Accept before you descend that you will be observed. Your clothing, your face, your manner of speaking, your reactions to the environment—all will be noted, catalogued, discussed. You are a novelty in a community that sees few outsiders. This attention is not hostile, but it is relentless.
- If you need time away from people, your only options are departure or the deepest night hours (roughly the period between the moon's highest point and dawn, when even the cistern settles into its quietest state). Plan for this if you are staying overnight.
- The inhabitants have developed a culture of "noticing but not acknowledging"—a way of coexisting in close quarters that allows for a semblance of privacy. You will see people pass within arm's reach without greeting each other, hear conversations continue as someone walks directly through them, watch a dozen activities occur in overlapping space without collision. Learn this rhythm. Move through rather than around.

### The Vertical Orientation

The cistern is built up, not out. Platforms, bridges, ladders, and ledges create a three-dimensional living space that requires a different kind of spatial awareness than most surface dwellers possess.

**What this requires:**
- Comfort with heights. If you have any significant fear of falling, the cistern will be difficult for you. The walkways are narrow, the railings are minimal or absent, and the perspective of looking down forty feet to the sump floor can induce vertigo. The children who grow up here learn early not to fall. You did not grow up here.
- A mental map that includes elevation. Navigation in the cistern requires you to think in three dimensions constantly. "Where is the potter?" "Third platform up, western wall, below the goat enclosure." You will get lost. Everyone does on their first visit. The inhabitants will give directions that assume you know the landmarks; you will not know the landmarks. Ask for clarification. They are patient with genuine confusion.

### The Temporal Distortion

Several observers have noted that time seems to behave differently in the cistern. Whether this is a measurable phenomenon or a psychological effect of the light, the sound, the density, and the limited environmental cues is unknown. But you should be prepared for it.

**Reported effects:**
- Hours passing without your awareness. The lack of normal day/night cues (the light column moves, but the overall illumination changes little) and the constant activity can make time seem to smear.
- A sense that the past and present are less distinct than they should be. The accumulated layers of construction—platforms built on platforms, newer walls over older carvings—create a physical manifestation of time that you can see. Some travelers report a sensation of being in multiple times at once, of hearing conversations that might have happened years ago, of seeing movement in their peripheral vision that resolves into nothing when they look directly.
- Dreams in the cistern are said to be unusually vivid. If you sleep there, expect this. The inhabitants consider it normal. They have interpretations for common dreams; ask about them if you're curious, but do not assume their meanings are universal.

## Social Preparation

### The Economy of the Cistern

You will not be entering as a conqueror or a savior. You will be entering as a visitor who needs things—information, perhaps, or permission to stay, or simply the experience of having been there. The cistern operates on exchange. Nothing is free because nothing can be free in a closed system where every resource is claimed and every claim is defended.

**What you can offer:**
- Goods from the surface are the most obvious trade item. But consider carefully what you bring. The cistern does not need food (they produce their own, barely enough for their population); they do not need water (they have the sump, and they know how to purify it); they do not need clothing (they weave their own, and your garments will not fit their frames any better than theirs fit yours).
- What they value are tools and materials they cannot produce themselves: good metal (knives, needles, fishhooks), cordage of higher quality than they can make, glass vessels, paper (they have no source of it), possibly medicines if you have knowledge of them.
- Knowledge itself is a currency. If you can read, you will find those who want letters written or documents interpreted. If you know construction techniques, there are always repairs needed. If you have medical training, you will find patients. The cistern has its own healers, but their knowledge is traditional and limited.
- Labor is always welcome. If you have nothing else to offer, a day of carrying water, clearing waste, or helping with repairs will earn you a meal and a place to sleep.

### The Social Structure

Do not make assumptions about power in the cistern. There are no kings, no priests, no officials in the surface sense. There is only tenure and reputation.

**Key figures:**
- The council of elders are the oldest residents, those who remember the cistern as it was two generations ago. They settle disputes because they have the moral authority of memory—they know what was agreed, what was built, who inherited what from whom. Do not approach them directly for favors. Let them observe you. Let them send a messenger if they wish to speak.
- The families who control the upper platforms near the oculus are the closest thing to an aristocracy. Their tenure is longest, their construction most elaborate, their goods most valuable. They are not wealthy in any surface sense, but they are comfortable. They are the ones most likely to trade with outsiders—they have the most to gain from it and the most to lose from disruption.
- The lower levels, near the sump, are the domain of newer arrivals, those without established claims, those who have not yet earned (or have lost) the right to better space. These are not necessarily the poorest inhabitants—they may have skills that give them other forms of status—but their living conditions are the most difficult. They are also the most likely to approach you with offers of information or assistance, hoping to gain advantage through the connection.

### Taboos and Warnings

The cistern's culture has developed over centuries of enclosed coexistence. Its rules are not written; they are remembered. Violate them, and you will not be punished directly—you will simply find yourself increasingly isolated, increasingly unable to function in the community, until you leave or are expelled.

**Do not:**
- Steal. The penalty is exile, and exile in this region is close to a death sentence. The surface is not kind to those who have grown up below, and the cistern's inhabitants have ways of making sure an exiled thief cannot simply walk back in.
- Speak loudly unless you are warned that it is necessary. The acoustics punish noise. The community punishes those who add to the already overwhelming sound.
- Venture into the deep cracks in the rock, the places where the original carvers broke through into cavities below. The children are told that something lives there; the adults do not entirely dismiss this. There are sounds from below that do not match any known animal. Whether the danger is supernatural or simply physical (a fall, a collapse, an infection from unknown fungi), the prohibition is absolute.
- Insult the pigeons. This sounds absurd. It is not. The birds of the cistern are unusual—more intelligent, more patterned, more aware than surface pigeons. The old women who tend them say they remember, that they carry knowledge, that they can find their way back to a person years later. These claims are accurate. The pigeons are a communication network for those who know how to use them. They are also watching, in their way. Treat them with the respect you would give to any intelligence that can move freely through a space you are trapped in.

### The Question of Violence

Violence is rare in the cistern, but not because the inhabitants are pacifists. Violence draws attention. Attention draws the surface authorities. The surface authorities periodically try to clear the cistern, to seal it, to remove the squatters who have made their home in a structure they do not legally own. These attempts have never succeeded—the cistern's inhabitants have bolt-holes and tunnels that even the authorities do not know about—but they are disruptive and dangerous.

Therefore, the community polices itself. A violent person is a threat to everyone, not just their target. The penalty for violence is usually exile. In serious cases, the offender may simply... disappear. The cistern has ways of dealing with those who cannot be allowed to remain.

**What this means for you:**
- Do not be violent. This should be obvious. But understand that even defensive violence can be considered a threat to the community. If you are attacked, the expectation is that you will retreat, deflect, or incapacitate without causing lasting harm. The cistern does not have room for grudges, feuds, or ongoing conflicts. Resolve disputes quickly and finally, or let them go.
- Do not bring weapons that are obviously weapons. A knife is a tool; a sword is a weapon. The former is acceptable; the latter is a statement of intent that will make people nervous. Nervous people in a crowded space are dangerous.

## Practical Logistics

### Entering the Cistern

The entrance is through worn stone steps that descend from the surface. You will likely be observed as you approach—the cistern has lookouts, usually children who play near the entrance and report arrivals. By the time you reach the archway, the community will know you are coming.

**The descent:**
- Go slowly. The steps are uneven, worn by centuries of feet, and the transition from bright surface light to the dimness below will blind you temporarily.
- The sound will hit you before you understand what you're hearing. Do not stop to analyze it. Keep moving until you reach the main chamber, then find a wall to rest against while your senses adjust.
- You will be approached within minutes. This is a test of sorts—the cistern wants to know who you are, why you are here, and whether you are a threat. Have your answers prepared. Be honest; the community has ways of verifying information, and a lie discovered early will follow you throughout your visit.

### Where to Stay

If you are visiting briefly—a few hours, a single day—you do not need accommodations. But if you plan to stay overnight or longer, you will need to arrange space.

**Options:**
- The cistern has no inns, no guest rooms, no designated spaces for travelers. You will be sleeping in someone's home, on their platform, in space they could use for their own family. This is a gift, and it should be treated as one.
- Expect to pay, either in goods or in labor. The rate is not fixed; negotiate before you accept. Common arrangements: a small item of value for a single night, or a day's work for two nights and meals.
- The lower levels are cheaper (in terms of what you'll be expected to provide) but less comfortable. The dampness is more pronounced, the smell of the sump is stronger, the vermin are bolder. The upper levels are drier, brighter, and more expensive. Choose according to your resources and your tolerance for discomfort.
- If you have no goods and no skills to trade, you may be offered space in exchange for carrying messages or small items to the surface. The cistern's inhabitants make regular trips up for foraging and trade, but there are always tasks that require someone with no particular allegiance to the community.

### Eating and Drinking

The cistern can feed itself, barely. The goats provide milk, the chickens provide eggs, the pigeons occasionally provide meat, and there are stores of grain (vulnerable to rats, requiring constant vigilance). Foraging parties bring supplementary food from the surface. But there is no surplus.

**What this means:**
- If you stay for a meal, you are eating food that would have gone to someone else. Bring your own if possible, or plan to compensate your hosts generously.
- The cooking is simple—clay pots over small fires, ingredients combined without much refinement. The food is filling but not varied. If you have dietary restrictions, bring your own supplies.
- Do not drink from the sump unless you have no alternative. Even boiled, it is not clean by most standards. The water that collects in the upper crevices, where condensation is freshest, is safer—but it is also claimed, and you will need permission to access it.

### Leaving the Cistern

The departure is as disorienting as the arrival, but in reverse. The fading of the sound, the sudden brightness of surface light, the strange emptiness of open space—these can be jarring.

**What to expect:**
- Give yourself time to readjust before attempting any complex tasks. Your first few minutes on the surface will be a blur of sensory recalibration.
- The contrast between the enclosed, dense world below and the open, silent world above can cause a temporary melancholy. The cistern, for all its difficulties, is *alive* in a way that the surface often is not. Some travelers report a sense of loss upon leaving, a feeling that they are exiting somewhere real for somewhere thin.
- If you have formed connections during your stay, you will be expected to acknowledge them before you go. A simple farewell is sufficient for casual acquaintances; a gift or a promise of return is appropriate for those who have helped you significantly. Do not make promises you do not intend to keep. The cistern has a long memory.

## The Deeper Preparations

### What You Are Really Walking Into

The Cistern of Orami is not a tourist destination. It is not a curiosity to be visited and then left behind. It is a living system—a human ecology that has developed in isolation, that has solved the problems of existence in its own way, that will continue long after you have returned to whatever world you came from.

You cannot see the cistern without being changed by it. The density of life, the compression of space, the strange acoustics and the stranger light, the weight of accumulated time—these will leave marks. Most travelers report vivid dreams of the cistern for weeks after their visit. Some report a persistent awareness of enclosed spaces, a new appreciation for the vertical dimension of architecture. A few report hearing, in quiet moments, the echo of the cistern's sound—a distant murmur like a hive, like a heart.

### The Question of Purpose

Why are you going? What do you hope to find? The cistern will give you what it gives everyone: an experience of humanity compressed to its essence, of survival made communal, of a world built from nothing but stone and time and the stubborn insistence of people who had nowhere else to go.

If you are going to observe, observe respectfully. If you are going to learn, learn humbly. If you are going to take—artifacts, stories, knowledge—understand that you are taking from people who have little, and give something in return.

The cistern is not a museum. It is not a lesson. It is a home.

Treat it as you would any home you are privileged to enter: with respect for its occupants, with care for its fabric, with gratitude for the glimpse it offers into a way of being that you could not have imagined before you descended those worn stone steps and heard, for the first time, the great churn of sound that means you have arrived.
