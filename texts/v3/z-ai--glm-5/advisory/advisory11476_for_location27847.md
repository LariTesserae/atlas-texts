# Preparation Guide: The Plateau of Eternal Thunder

## What You Are Actually Walking Into

Before any practical preparation, you need to understand something fundamental about this place that no equipment list can address: you are visiting a location that does not need you, has never needed anyone, and will not notice your presence. This sounds like it could be said of many wild places across the multiverse. It cannot. Most wildernesses react to visitors in some way — tracks are left, brush is broken, animals flee. The Plateau of Eternal Thunder will not register your existence in any meaningful way. You will arrive as a vibration too small to measure against a vibration too large to comprehend. This is not humility. This is physics.

The sound is the defining fact of everything that follows. Not the geography, not the gardens, not the strange physics — the sound. Every decision you make about gear, route, duration, and mental preparation radiates outward from that single reality. The roar has been continuous for longer than your species has existed. It will not pause for you. It will not modulate for your comfort. It will not provide a single moment's silence for you to collect yourself, check your bearings, or hear your own thoughts. You must build every plan around the assumption that from the moment you step onto the plateau until the moment you leave it, you will be submerged in noise.

Understand this before you go further: the descriptions you have read are accurate but insufficient. Reading about a wall of white noise that you feel in your chest is not the same as standing in it. Your body will react before your mind does. Your breathing pattern will change. Your heartbeat will attempt to synchronize with frequencies it cannot quite identify. You will feel pressure behind your eyes, in your sinuses, in the joints of your jaw. This is not damage — it is your body attempting to process input that evolution did not prepare it for. The sensation passes for most travelers within the first few hours, replaced by a kind of numbness that itself takes getting used to. But the initial encounter can be genuinely disorienting, and if you are not prepared for it, you may panic. People have panicked. People have turned around within sight of their entry point and left without ever having actually arrived.

So the first preparation is this: decide whether you actually want to go. Not whether you want to have gone. Whether you want to go. These are different things, and on the plateau, the difference matters.

## Ear Protection: The Question You Cannot Avoid

You will want earplugs. Of course you will. Any ordinary human approaching a place described as deafening will reach for hearing protection as a reflex, and you should bring several varieties — foam, silicone, wax, over-ear protection, the best noise-canceling technology your origin universe provides.

Here is the problem: most of what the plateau offers is accessed through sound. The undertones, the patterns, the resonance structures in the ancient architecture, the alleged voices within the roar — all of this is auditory. If you block the sound, you are visiting a flat, damp, stone expanse with some plants in it. You could find that anywhere. The sound is the purpose. The sound is the place.

But prolonged exposure to sound at the volumes described will damage human hearing. This is not speculation. This is biology. The hair cells in your cochlea do not regenerate. Enough time in the roar, and you will experience permanent threshold shifts — hearing loss that does not recover. The plateau does not care about your cochlea.

The hermits who live there, the ones who have supposedly been there for centuries, have either adapted in ways that human physiology should not allow, or they have accepted hearing loss as the cost of admission. Possibly both. You are not a hermit. You are a traveler, and you presumably want to continue traveling to other universes with your senses intact.

The approach that experienced plateau travelers recommend, if you can find one willing to discuss it, is graduated exposure. Spend your first hours with high-quality ear protection, allowing your nervous system to acclimate to the tactile component of the sound — the chest vibration, the physical pressure — while reducing the auditory assault. Then remove protection for short, measured intervals. Ten minutes. Fifteen. Build tolerance slowly. You are not trying to become immune to the sound. You are training your body to stop panicking in its presence.

Bring multiple sets of earplugs. They will get wet. Everything on the plateau gets wet, and moisture degrades foam and silicone faster than you might expect. Bring a dry storage container for whichever pair you are not wearing. Accept that you will lose some to the environment. Bring more than you think you need.

If you are traveling with companions — and you should consider this carefully — establish a signal for hearing distress before you arrive. A hand sign that means "I need to put my plugs in now." A gesture that means "I can't hear you at all." You will not be able to communicate this verbally once you are on the plateau.

## The Moisture Problem

The condensation is the second defining fact, and it is in some ways more practically challenging than the sound. The plateau does not rain. It does not drizzle. It simply makes every surface damp, constantly, through a process that has no source and no direction. The moisture materializes on skin, on fabric, on stone, on metal, on every exposed surface simultaneously. You cannot take shelter from it because there is no weather to shelter from. You cannot dry off because there is no dry air to dry in.

This means that every piece of equipment you bring will be wet within your first hour on the plateau and will remain wet for the duration of your stay. Plan for this.

**Clothing.** Forget cotton. Forget anything that absorbs water and stays heavy. You need synthetic materials that wick moisture away from skin and retain insulating properties when wet. Multiple thin layers are better than single thick ones — you can adjust to the warmth, which is constant and significant, but the wetness makes temperature regulation deceptive. You may feel warm because the air is warm, but constant evaporation from wet clothing pulls heat from your body steadily, and hypothermia in warm conditions is a real risk if you are not attentive. Bring a vapor barrier — a thin, waterproof layer that goes between your base layer and your insulation — to trap your body heat against your skin rather than letting it evaporate into the plateau's atmosphere.

**Footwear.** This requires serious thought. The stone surface is smooth — river-stone smooth, eight hundred million years of erosion smooth — and it is perpetually wet. This is a slipping hazard that cannot be overstated. You will be walking for days across surfaces that offer the traction of a wet bathroom floor, except the bathroom floor extends to every horizon and you cannot stop walking because there is nowhere to stop. Boots with aggressive tread are a starting point, but tread fills with moisture and becomes ineffective. Consider rubber-soled footwear designed for wet rock — approach shoes, canyoneering boots, anything from your origin universe that maintains grip on saturated smooth surfaces. Bring multiple pairs. Bring drying agents that work without sunlight, because there will be no sunlight reliable enough to dry anything. Bring foot powder and use it at every rest stop, because constant moisture against skin produces maceration, then blistering, then infection, and you are days from any garden where you might find rest.

**Equipment protection.** Anything electronic, anything paper-based, anything that degrades with moisture exposure — all of it needs sealed waterproof storage. Not water-resistant. Waterproof. Sealed containers with gaskets. Dry bags within dry bags. The condensation finds gaps. It finds imperfect seals. It finds the one corner of your pack where moisture can sneak in, and it will. Assume your gear will be subjected to a constant, gentle, relentless dampness for every moment of your stay, and protect it accordingly.

**Fire.** You will not make fire on the plateau unless you are in a garden and have access to the dried plant material there. The stone is wet. The air is wet. Your fire-starting materials will be wet within minutes of exposure. If fire is essential to your survival strategy — for warmth, for water purification, for psychological comfort — you need to bring self-contained systems that do not require external fuel or external conditions. Chemical heaters. Waterproof matches stored in multiple sealed containers. A supply of dry tinder that you keep in vacuum-sealed packaging, opened only when you are in a garden with shelter from the wind. And even then, the moisture in the air will work against you. The hermits use friction-fire techniques, but they have had years to perfect them in these conditions. You will not.

## Navigation on a Featureless Expanse

The plateau is continent-sized. Continent-sized. Let this settle in your mind. You could walk for days in any direction and not reach an edge, not find a landmark, not encounter anything that distinguishes one section of smooth wet stone from another. The slight eastward tilt is your only consistent directional reference, and it is subtle — perceptible if you are paying attention, but easy to miss if you are distracted by the sound, the moisture, the strangeness.

**Maps.** Do not assume your mapping technology will function correctly here. The plateau's strange physics — the probability shifts, the atmospheric distortions, the suggestion that the sound affects the way light refracts and distance appears — mean that instruments may not give reliable readings. GPS systems, if they function at all in your universe's version of this location, may produce coordinates that do not match the physical landscape. Compass headings may drift. Do not rely on any single navigation method.

**Dead reckoning.** Learn it before you arrive. The ability to maintain a heading by counting steps and tracking direction without instruments is essential here, because your instruments may fail and the landscape will not help you. The eastward tilt gives you a rough directional sense — water flows east, if it flows at all, and the slight slope is detectable through your feet if you train yourself to feel it. Practice walking a straight line blindfolded before you arrive. You will need every navigational sense you have.

**Garden positions.** If you can obtain records of garden locations from previous travelers — the hermits, researchers, or the sparse academic literature that exists on the plateau — do so. The gardens are your waypoints, your water sources, your shelters, your only landmarks in a landscape that offers none. Knowing where the nearest garden lies, and in what direction, could mean the difference between survival and days of exposure on bare stone. But treat any garden positions as approximate. The moisture migrates. The gardens shift in their vitality. A lush garden on a previous visitor's map may be merely damp when you arrive.

**Marking your route.** You will not be able to leave visible markers — the stone is too hard, too smooth, too wet for cairns or scratches to persist reliably. Consider bringing biodegradable trail markers in bright colors — small flags, ribbons — that you can weigh down with stones at intervals. Understand that the plateau's wind patterns may move them. Understand that the condensation may make them unreadable. Understand that navigation here is an exercise in managing uncertainty, not eliminating it.

## Water and Food

**Water.** The condensation provides moisture, but collecting it in quantities sufficient for drinking requires technique and patience. The moisture materializes on surfaces — your clothing, your skin, the stone, your equipment. You can wring out clothing that has been exposed to the air. You can spread fabric across stone surfaces and collect what accumulates. You can channel the condensation that runs along the smooth granite into containers placed in natural grooves. But you will not collect enough water by passively waiting for condensation to bead on your skin. You need active collection methods.

Bring fabric designed for moisture harvesting — materials with hydrophilic surfaces that attract and hold water. Large sheets of such fabric, spread across stone surfaces and wrung out periodically, can produce surprising quantities. In the gardens, water collects more reliably in the depressions where ferns and mosses grow. Look for pools in the stone, for areas where the condensation has accumulated faster than it can evaporate. The water will be clean — it comes from the atmosphere, not from ground sources — but treat it anyway. You are a long way from medical care.

**Food.** You cannot carry enough food for a multi-day crossing of the plateau. You simply cannot. The distances are too great, the walking too slow on wet stone, the energy expenditure too high against the constant sensory assault. You need to supplement what you carry with what the plateau provides.

The gardens contain ferns and mosses. Some of these are edible. Research the specific species before you arrive, if records exist. The goats eat them, which suggests they are not toxic, but goat digestion and human digestion differ. Bring a universal field test for plant toxicity if your technology provides one. If not, use the standard progression: rub on skin, wait. Touch to lips, wait. Small taste, wait. Small portion, wait. At each stage, the wait should be longer than you think it needs to be. In an environment where the sound disrupts your ability to perceive your own body normally, you may not notice symptoms as quickly as you would elsewhere.

The goats themselves are a potential food source, but hunting them is ethically complicated and practically difficult. They are the only large fauna on the plateau. They do not vocalize. They have adapted to the sound in ways you have not. Killing one feels wrong in a way that is hard to articulate but important to feel. If you must, do it quickly, with respect, and use everything. But plan your food supply around not needing to.

Bring dense, high-calorie travel food — the most calorie-dense provisions your universe offers. Energy bars, concentrated pastes, dried foods that can be reconstituted with the water you collect from condensation. Seal them in waterproof containers. Plan for more days than you expect to stay. The plateau does not offer easy exit, and you may find that leaving is harder than arriving, especially if the sound has begun to affect your perception of time and direction.

## Communication

You will not speak on the plateau. Not to companions, not to yourself, not to anyone. Your voice will be swallowed by the roar before it travels a meter from your mouth. Shouting produces the same result as whispering — nothing. You must have alternative communication systems established before you arrive.

**Sign language.** If traveling with companions, develop a vocabulary of hand signs covering essential communication: directions, needs (water, food, rest, help), emotional states (fear, confusion, overwhelm), and decision points (stop, continue, turn, retreat). Practice this vocabulary until it is automatic. On the plateau, you will not have the cognitive bandwidth to remember improvised signs. The sound consumes attention. Make the communication instinctive before you need it.

**Writing.** Bring waterproof writing surfaces — slates, wax tablets, waterproof paper and pencils. Moisture will make ordinary paper useless within minutes. Even waterproof paper degrades over days of constant dampness. Have multiple options. Writing will be your primary method for complex communication, but it is slow and requires stopping, which means standing still on wet stone, which means exposing yourself to the full weight of the sound without the distraction of movement.

**Signal devices.** Consider simple visual signals — a brightly colored cloth on a pole, a mirror for flashing, a light if your technology provides one that functions in wet conditions. These are for emergencies, for situations where a companion is too far away for sign communication. Establish what each signal means before you arrive. A raised fist. A specific color displayed. A pattern of flashes. Make it simple, because in an emergency on the plateau, your thinking will be compromised by the sound and you will not remember complex protocols.

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section in this guide, and it is the hardest to write, because the plateau's psychological effects are not predictable. They vary by individual. They vary by duration. They vary in ways that the strange physics of the place seem to influence in ways that cannot be anticipated.

**The sound and the mind.** Constant, unvarying white noise at high volume produces predictable psychological effects in laboratory conditions: stress, irritability, cognitive impairment, sleep disruption, and in extreme cases, hallucination. The plateau is not a laboratory. The sound is not a recording. It has been described as feeling intentional, as having presence, as being something that is not merely heard but encountered. Your mind will attempt to make sense of it, to find patterns, to impose narrative on what may or may not be patterned noise. This is normal. This is what human minds do. But on the plateau, this normal cognitive process may produce abnormal results.

Travelers report that the sound becomes a presence after extended exposure — not a hallucination, but a shift in perception. The roar stops being background and starts being foreground, then becomes something more than either: a context, an environment, a state of being. This can be deeply disorienting. It can also be profoundly peaceful, in a way that is difficult to describe to anyone who has not experienced it. The sound becomes the silence within which everything else exists. This is what the hermits have found. This is why they stay.

**The emptiness.** The plateau is vast and featureless between the gardens. You will walk for hours, possibly days, seeing nothing but smooth gray stone stretching to every horizon, feeling nothing but the dampness and the vibration, hearing nothing but the roar. This is a specific kind of psychological challenge that most humans in most universes never encounter — not danger, not beauty, not strangeness, but pure, extended emptiness. The mind does not handle emptiness well. It populates the void with fears, with memories, with projections. You will find yourself thinking about things you have avoided thinking about for years. You will have conversations with people who are not there. You will make decisions about your life that you have been postponing, because the emptiness gives you no escape from yourself.

This is, for some travelers, the point. The plateau is not a tourist destination. It is a confrontation. It strips away everything external and leaves you with what you carry inside. If you are not prepared for that confrontation, it can damage you. If you are, it can change you in ways you may consider valuable. But you should not enter the plateau without understanding that this is what you are signing up for.

**The perception shifts.** The accounts of altered time perception, of probability anomalies, of the sound seeming to affect the very fabric of reality — take these seriously. Not because they are proven, but because multiple independent travelers report similar experiences, and the consistency of the reports suggests something real is occurring, even if that something is psychological rather than physical. Prepare for the possibility that your sense of time, distance, and causality will be affected. Maintain external timekeeping if you can — a mechanical watch, waterproof, that does not rely on signals that might be disrupted. Check it regularly. If you notice that hours have passed like minutes, or minutes like hours, note it. If you notice that you cannot remember how long you have been walking, stop. Sit down. Consult your instruments. Ground yourself in external reference points before continuing.

**The undertones.** The hermits say the sound contains patterns. Voices, they claim. Meaning encoded in the roar. If you stay long enough, you may begin to hear them. This is not necessarily a problem — many travelers report it as a profound and moving experience. But you should be aware that the boundary between "hearing patterns that exist in external sound" and "experiencing auditory hallucinations produced by a brain under prolonged sensory stress" is not clear, and the plateau's strange physics may make it impossible to determine which is occurring. If you begin hearing voices, pay attention to what they say. If they tell you to do things that would harm you or prevent you from leaving, recognize this as a danger signal. If they simply speak, simply communicate, simply exist — you are experiencing what the hermits experience. You are hearing what the plateau has been trying to say for millions of years. Listen, but do not lose yourself.

## Duration: How Long Should You Stay?

This depends entirely on your purpose. A day trip — arriving, experiencing the sound, visiting a garden, leaving — is possible if you enter from a point near a garden. You will get a sense of the place. You will not understand it. You will have been a tourist.

Three to five days gives you the experience of a full crossing between gardens, the experience of sleeping in the sound, the first stages of psychological adaptation. You will begin to notice the perception shifts. You will begin to develop the sign language fluency that extended travel requires. You will be exhausted, possibly hearing-impaired, and changed in ways you cannot yet identify. This is the minimum duration for a meaningful visit.

Two weeks puts you in the territory where the plateau begins to work on you. The sound is no longer something you hear — it is something you exist within. Your relationship to silence, to communication, to solitude, to your own thoughts will have shifted. You may not want to leave. This is the duration at which travelers begin to understand why hermits stay.

Beyond two weeks, you are not a traveler anymore. You are becoming something else. The plateau's strange physics, the probability shifts, the time distortion, the voices in the sound — these accumulate. You may find that leaving is difficult, not logistically but psychologically. You may find that the world outside the plateau feels wrong, too quiet, too empty in a different way. You may find that you have started to hear the undertones and cannot stop. You may find that you have aged differently than you should have, and that the question of whether you have been here for weeks or months or years has become impossible to answer.

Set a duration before you arrive. Tell someone — someone who is not coming with you — when you expect to leave. Give them instructions to come looking for you if you do not return by a certain date. Treat these instructions seriously. The plateau does not trap people, but it changes them, and changed people sometimes forget that they intended to leave.

## What Not to Bring

Do not bring expectations. The plateau will not meet them. Whatever you imagine the sound will be like, it will be different. Whatever you imagine the emptiness will feel like, it will be different. Whatever you imagine the gardens will offer, they will offer something else. Come empty.

Do not bring the need to accomplish something. The plateau does not reward achievement. It does not provide summit moments or photo opportunities or badge-worthy experiences. It provides immersion, and immersion is not an accomplishment. It is a state.

Do not bring the assumption that you will be the same person when you leave. You will not. The sound changes everyone. Sometimes the change is small — a new appreciation for silence, a slight hearing deficit, a story to tell. Sometimes it is large — a reordering of priorities, a loss of interest in the world you came from, a conviction that the plateau spoke to you and that what it said was important. You cannot control which outcome you receive. You can only prepare for the possibility that the visit will cost more than you expected.

Do not bring the belief that you understand what you are doing. No one understands what they are doing on the plateau. The hermits who have been there for decades do not understand. The researchers who have studied it for careers do not understand. The sound contains everything, which means it contains nothing that can be isolated and comprehended. You are stepping into a mystery that is older than your species and that will not be solved by your presence. The most you can hope for is to experience it. The least you can do is experience it honestly.

## Final Notes

The Plateau of Eternal Thunder is not dangerous in the way that many multiverse locations are dangerous. Nothing here will attack you. Nothing here will poison you or devour you or trap you in an alternate dimension. The goats are gentle. The insects are benign. The hermits, if you encounter them, are helpful in proportion to their interest in helping, which varies.

The danger is subtler. The danger is that you will go seeking something you cannot name, and you will find it, and it will not be what you wanted it to be. The danger is that the sound will strip away the things you use to define yourself — your language, your sense of time, your certainty about what is real — and leave you with something rawer and stranger than you knew you contained. The danger is that you will like what you find.

Pack carefully. Prepare thoroughly. Listen to your intuition about whether you should go. And if you go, understand that the plateau has been waiting for longer than anything has ever waited, and that it does not expect anything from that waiting. It simply resonates. You are the one who will be changed. It will not be.

Go if you must. Stay away if you can. Understand the difference before you decide.
