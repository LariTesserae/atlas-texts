# Preparation Guide: The Drowned Mill-Cathedral

## What You Are Entering

Before you pack a single item, understand this: you are not entering a place. You are entering a process. The Drowned Mill-Cathedral is not a location you visit and leave unchanged. It is a system that will try to incorporate you from the moment you arrive, patient and indifferent, using whatever you bring as raw material for its slow cycles.

This is not a warning. This is a description.

You can visit. You can leave. People have. But only those who understood what they were stepping into—the ones who treated the Cathedral-Mill as a living entity with its own logic, its own timescales, its own quiet intentions.

---

## Physical Preparation: Your Body

### Respiration

The atmosphere is water. Not metaphorically—literally, the air is so saturated that every breath draws in droplets. For most humans, this means two things: your lungs will work harder, and your body will retain moisture from the outside rather than from drinking.

**What to bring:**

A rebreather with humidity regulation. Not a full respirator—you need to be able to smell this place, to taste it, because the chemical signatures of the vegetation tell you where you are. But a device that can extract enough water from your intake air to prevent pulmonary edema during extended exposure. The warm temperature masks the strain; you may not notice you're drowning from the inside until it has already begun.

Spend at least two weeks acclimating to high-humidity environments before arrival. Steam rooms, saunas, tropical biomes—anything that trains your lungs to process saturated air. The Cathedral-Mill's atmosphere is warmer than most natural humidity, which makes it easier in some ways and harder in others. Your body will not recognize it as threatening until the fluid accumulation becomes critical.

**What to expect:**

After six to eight hours, you will feel fluid in your chest. This is normal. After twelve hours, you will cough clear liquid. This is your body trying to adapt. After twenty-four hours, you need to leave or you need medical intervention. The plants have no use for your timekeeping, but your lungs operate on human schedules.

### Skin and Contact

Everything here touches you. The vegetation density allows no distance between your body and the environment. This means two forms of exposure: mechanical (the constant brush of leaves, vines, moss) and chemical (the territorial compounds the plants excrete).

**What to bring:**

A body suit made from inert material—something the vegetation cannot easily colonize. Synthetic fibers treated with antimicrobial compounds. The engineered bacteria that light the pipes *will* attempt to establish colonies on any organic surface, including untreated cotton, leather, and skin. You want to present the system with barriers it cannot easily metabolize.

However: you cannot fully seal yourself off. The humidity will find any gap, any seam, and the plants' chemical compounds are dissolved in that moisture. They will reach your skin eventually.

**The chemical terrain:**

The vegetation practices slow territorial warfare. Ferns, mosses, vines, aquatic plants—each releases compounds that discourage encroachment from the others. These compounds are not designed to harm humans, but they are not designed to spare you either.

If you touch a fern and then touch a moss, you may transfer enough compound to trigger a reaction. Usually mild: localized itching, redness, a burning sensation that lasts several hours. But the Cathedral-Mill has been refining its chemistry for centuries. Some visitors report stronger responses—blistering, numbness, temporary discoloration of skin that lasted weeks after they left.

Learn the zones. Ferns on upper walls. Mosses on horizontal surfaces. Vines in vertical spaces. Aquatic vegetation below the water line. When you move through the Cathedral-Mill, move through one zone at a time. Do not brush against multiple species in rapid succession. Do not collect samples from different plants and store them in the same container—the compounds can react with each other in unpredictable ways.

**What to apply:**

A barrier cream formulated for high-humidity environments. Reapply every four hours, more frequently if you are moving actively through vegetation. The cream will not prevent contact, but it will slow the absorption of territorial compounds and give you time to wash them off.

And wash them off you must. The water is everywhere—you will have no shortage of it. But the water *is* the medium of transmission. When you rinse your skin, you are also exposing yourself to whatever compounds have dissolved in that particular location. There is no pure water here, only water that carries different things at different points in its cycle.

### Temperature Regulation

The water is body-temperature. The air is body-temperature. The machinery generates heat from below. This sounds comfortable, and in the short term, it is. But your body relies on temperature differential to regulate itself. In an environment that matches your internal heat, you cannot cool down effectively.

**What to bring:**

A cooling system. Passive cooling will not work here. You need something active—a circulating coolant, a Peltier-based heat exchanger, something that can maintain a temperature gradient between your body and the environment. The technology exists across many worlds; acquire it before you arrive.

Without active cooling, you will accumulate heat slowly. After several hours, you will feel lethargic. After a day, you may experience heat exhaustion despite the moderate temperature. The warmth is insidious precisely because it feels so gentle—like being held, the description says, and that is accurate. You are being held. The holding becomes constraining.

### Mobility

There is no ground. Only levels. You will climb, swim, balance, and crawl. The surfaces are overgrown, corroded, wet, and often vertical. The water fills the lower third of the cathedral spaces, and the only paths forward are through the vegetation or through the flood.

**What to bring:**

Climbing gear suited for organic surfaces. The vegetation is strong—the vines are cable-like, the moss carpets provide cushioning and grip—but it is not stable. Roots shift as they draw water. Leaves adjust their angles. A hold that seemed solid may prove to be a cluster of stems with nothing behind them.

Swimming capability. You will need to move through flooded sections, and the water is warm, dense with dissolved minerals, and moving slowly upward through the circulation system. It resists motion slightly more than pure water would. The current is gentle but persistent—always upward, always cycling.

Grip enhancers. Your hands will be wet constantly. The surfaces will be wet constantly. Friction becomes unreliable. Consider adhesive pads, gecko-surface gloves, anything that can maintain hold in saturated conditions.

Footwear with drainage. You will be submerged repeatedly. Boots that trap water become boots that breed bacteria and fungus—both of which the Cathedral-Mill's vegetation may find interesting substrates. Let the water move through; let your feet breathe as much as they can in this environment.

## Sensory Preparation: What You Will Experience

### The Light

The bioluminescent bacteria produce a steady, institution-white radiance. It is normal in color temperature but strange in source—living lamps that metabolize dissolved minerals and exude photons as waste. The light is even, diffuse, coming from strips along pipes that thread through every space.

**What this means for you:**

No shadows. Or rather, no single shadow. Your body dims the light from multiple sources simultaneously, creating a general darkening rather than a defined shape. This is disorienting for humans who rely on shadow for spatial awareness. You may find yourself misjudging distances, bumping into surfaces you thought were further away.

The light does not change. There is no day or night here, only perpetual fluorescent noon. Your circadian rhythm will abandon its patterns within twelve to sixteen hours. Bring a timer, a light that you control, something that allows you to impose your own cycles on the timeless illumination.

### The Sound

The machinery thrums—a low vibration more felt than heard. Water drips eternally. Plants creak and settle. But otherwise: silence. Profound, held, waiting.

**What this means for you:**

Your own sounds will be magnified by contrast. Your breathing, your movement, the rustle of your gear against vegetation—these will seem loud, intrusive, almost violent in the quiet. Some visitors find themselves whispering involuntarily, apologizing to the space for disturbing it.

The silence also means no directional cues. In most environments, you orient yourself by ambient sound—wind, animal calls, the white noise of life. Here, you have only the thrum of machinery (omnipresent, sourceless) and the dripping of water (coming from everywhere above you). You can become lost easily. You *will* become lost eventually.

### The Absence

No animals. No insects. No fish. No birds. The Cathedral-Mill is a monoculture of engineered plant life and patient machinery. The silence is not empty, but it is missing something fundamental—what we might call the quick sounds, the daily rhythms of creaturely existence.

**What this means for you:**

A profound sense of isolation, even if you travel with companions. The living world you evolved to expect—competitive, noisy, urgent—is absent. What remains operates on timescales that make your presence feel irrelevant. The vegetation does not respond to you quickly. The machinery does not acknowledge you at all. You are a temporary disturbance in a system that measures significance in centuries.

Some visitors find this peaceful. Others find it existentially vertiginous. You will not know which category you fall into until you are there. Prepare for both responses.

## Psychological Preparation: The Dissolution of Boundaries

This is the most important section. You can bring the best equipment, follow every protocol, and still find yourself undone by what the Cathedral-Mill does to the sense of self.

### The Absorption Pressure

The description says it plainly: *this is a place that wants to absorb you.* Not with violence, but with patient logic. The system uses what it receives. Your skin will be colonized by bacteria if you stay too long. Your hair will host moss. Your lungs will fill with water that has been cycling through the vegetation, carrying the chemical signatures of engineered life. Your minerals will be distributed through the plants if you die here—your matter repurposed.

Even while you live, the boundary between your body and the environment becomes arbitrary. The humidity is absolute. You cannot tell where you end and the wet air begins. The warmth matches your internal temperature. The vegetation surrounds you, touches you constantly, leaves no gap between your flesh and the living matter of the space.

**How to resist:**

Maintain rituals of separation. Clean your skin deliberately, even though it will be wet again within minutes. Change your clothes on a schedule, even though the old ones are no drier than the new ones. Eat food you brought with you, not from the Cathedral-Mill's vegetation. Drink water you carried in, not from the cycle that moves through the machinery.

These actions have no practical effect—you are still saturated, still surrounded, still being slowly processed—but they maintain the psychological distinction between *you* and *environment*. They remind your mind that a boundary exists.

Travel with a companion if possible. Not for safety—though there are hazards, none are immediate threats—but for witness. Another person who sees you as separate, who addresses you by name, who maintains your identity through recognition. The Cathedral-Mill does not see you. It will take longer for it to erode a self that is being confirmed by another.

**Warning signs:**

If you begin to feel that staying would be acceptable—that you could simply stop moving, find a ledge, let the vegetation grow around you—leave immediately. This is not depression. This is the system working as designed. The Cathedral-Mill does not create despair; it creates an absence of urgency that makes incorporation feel peaceful.

If you notice yourself having difficulty remembering why you came, what you were doing before, what you intended to do after—leave. The timeless environment, the oxygen-rich air, the constant warmth: these are mildly intoxicating. They slow thought. They make human concerns seem distant, abstract, unimportant.

If you find yourself touching the vegetation intentionally—not to navigate, not to examine, but simply to touch, to be in contact—note the impulse and monitor it. It may be innocent curiosity. It may be the beginning of a desire to join the system rather than pass through it.

### The Time Distortion

Without animal life, without the rhythms of predation and reproduction, the Cathedral-Mill operates on slow time. Water rises millimeter by millimeter. Vines extend centimeter by centimeter. Gears complete rotations measured in decades.

You could spend a day here and not know it. You could spend a year. The vegetation would barely notice. The machinery would complete a handful of cycles. The water would rise by the width of a finger.

**How to maintain your time:**

External timers. Bring multiple devices—mechanical, digital, biological. Set them to your home world's cycles. Check them obsessively. The Cathedral-Mill will not provide temporal cues; you must supply your own.

Tasks with clear endpoints. Do not enter with vague intentions like "explore" or "document." Enter with specific goals: reach a particular level, collect a particular sample, spend no more than six hours in the main chamber. The space will erode your sense of purpose if you do not anchor it to concrete actions.

A departure plan. Know how you will leave before you arrive. Mark your entry point. The Cathedral-Mill has exits, but they lead only to other spaces like itself—a network of cathedral-mills connected by flooded tunnels. You can wander deeper indefinitely. You cannot wander out accidentally.

## Practical Logistics

### What to Carry

**Essential:**

- Rebreather with humidity regulation
- Active cooling system
- Full-body suit of inert synthetic material
- Barrier cream, multiple containers
- Climbing gear rated for organic/wet surfaces
- Footwear with drainage and grip enhancement
- Multiple light sources you control
- Multiple timers set to your native time cycle
- Navigation markers (physical, not electronic—the humidity affects electronics)
- Water and food from outside (sealed containers)
- First aid supplies focused on skin exposure and pulmonary issues
- Emergency extraction beacon (check compatibility with your point of origin)

**Useful:**

- A companion
- Sample containers that can be sealed against humidity
- Recording equipment protected from moisture
- A journal with waterproof pages—writing by hand maintains mental acuity better than electronic recording
- Something personal that cannot be colonized—a metal object, a stone from home, something that remains definitively *yours*

**Do not bring:**

- Organic materials you intend to keep (leather, cotton, wood, paper—the vegetation will colonize them)
- Electronics not rated for 100% humidity (which is nearly all standard electronics)
- Anything you would be distressed to lose or see transformed

### Duration Limits

These are estimates based on previous traveler reports. Individual tolerances vary.

**Up to 6 hours:** Minimal risk. You will feel the humidity, the strangeness, the absence of animal life. You will likely experience some disorientation. You will almost certainly be able to leave.

**6 to 12 hours:** Moderate risk. Fluid accumulation in lungs begins. Skin exposure to territorial compounds becomes significant. Time distortion sets in. The psychological pressure to stay begins. You should leave.

**12 to 24 hours:** High risk. Pulmonary edema likely without medical intervention. Skin reactions probable. Sense of self may begin to erode. The desire to stay may become strong. Leave if you can.

**Beyond 24 hours:** You may not want to leave. You may not be able to leave without assistance. The Cathedral-Mill has had time to begin its slow work on you. Recovery after extraction may take weeks or months.

Do not plan to stay longer than six hours. If your purposes require more time, plan multiple excursions with full recovery between them.

### Extraction

If you or a companion begins to show signs of absorption—the desire to stay, the confusion about time and purpose, the inability to remember why you came—effect immediate departure. Do not wait. Do not attempt to complete your task. Leave.

If someone cannot be convinced to leave, physically remove them if possible. If they have been present for more than 24 hours and resist extraction, they may have passed a threshold of incorporation. At that point, forcing them out may cause psychological damage as severe as leaving them in. This is a judgment call that cannot be made in advance.

If you cannot find your exit, remember: the Cathedral-Mill does not trap you deliberately. It simply does not prioritize your navigation. Move upward, toward the less-flooded levels. Look for the original smokestack entries or their equivalents. These are the points where the structure opens to the outside—or to the network of connected cathedral-mills, which may have their own exits.

If you are lost in the network, keep moving upward. The water rises from below. The oldest structures are at the bottom, the newest at the top. Eventually, you may reach levels that have not yet been flooded, where the vegetation is younger, where the machinery is different. These may connect to other networks entirely.

Or you may not. The Cathedral-Mill is patient. It has been processing visitors for centuries. Some of them are still there, in a sense—incorporated, distributed through the vegetation, their minerals cycling through the slow water, their matter repurposed into leaves and stems and roots.

## Why Go At All?

After all these warnings, you may wonder why anyone would visit the Drowned Mill-Cathedral.

The engineering alone is worth witnessing. A structure built without knowing what it was building toward—or perhaps knowing exactly. Machinery that has run for centuries without maintenance, without operators, turning with the patience of geological processes. Vegetation designed for an environment that should not exist, thriving in conditions that would kill any natural plant. A biome that maintains itself, perfects itself, continues itself, indifferent to the presence or absence of its creators.

The atmosphere carries a kind of peace that few places offer. Not the peace of emptiness, but the peace of completion—a system that needs nothing, wants nothing, lacks nothing. The vegetation does not struggle. The machinery does not strain. Everything fits. Everything works. In a multiverse of competition, predation, urgency, and lack, the Cathedral-Mill offers an alternative: existence without anxiety.

And there is the archive function. The vegetation is a library, each leaf holding information, each root-thread carrying code. If you have the knowledge and equipment to read it, you may learn something about the world that built this place, or the world they were preserving against, or the world they hoped would come after. The Cathedral-Mill has been waiting for someone to check out its books.

But go knowing what you are: a temporary presence in a patient system. Go equipped to remain yourself. Go with clear purpose and clear departure. Go to witness, not to join.

And if you feel, at any point, that staying would be acceptable—

—leave.
