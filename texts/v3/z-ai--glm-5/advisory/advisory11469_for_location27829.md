# Preparing for The Old Basin

## The Mental Preparation First

Before you pack anything, you need to understand what you're walking into. The Old Basin will not hurt you quickly. It will not thrill you with danger or dazzle you with spectacle. What it will do is erode your sense of significance. This is a place that has seen millions of years and will see millions more. You are a breath passing through. Some travelers find this liberating. Others find it unsettling in a way they can't name until they're standing there, realizing they've stopped expecting anything to change.

Prepare for monotony. Not the harsh monotony of a desert or the cold monotony of tundra, but a warm, wet, living monotony. The landscape repeats itself like a phrase spoken by someone who has forgotten they're speaking. You will walk for hours and arrive where you started, visually speaking. This can trigger a particular kind of disorientation—the sense that you haven't moved at all, that distance has become meaningless. Some travelers report losing track of not just where they are but *when* they are, the present moment stretching into an endless, featureless now.

If you have any tendency toward existential unease, if you struggle with feelings of insignificance or meaninglessness, consider whether this destination is necessary for your journey. The Basin will not create these feelings from nothing, but it will amplify what's already there. Conversely, if you find peace in vastness, if you're the sort of person who feels comforted by the thought that the universe will continue long after you're gone, you may find the Basin profoundly settling.

## Footwear: Your Most Critical Decision

Your feet will tell you the truth of this place before your eyes do. The ground gives. It remembers being mud. It wants very much to be mud again, and your weight pressing down gives it permission.

Standard hiking boots will not serve you here. The soles are too narrow, the treads too aggressive. You will sink. Not dramatically—you won't plunge to your knees in a sudden bog—but incrementally, inexorably. Each step presses you slightly deeper into the compressed clay-pale substance, and each step requires slightly more effort to lift free. After an hour of walking, your calves will begin to understand. After three hours, your lower back will join the conversation.

What you want instead is something with surface area. Wide, flat soles that distribute your weight the way snowshoes distribute weight across snow. The principle is similar: you're trying not to press through a surface that wants to give way. Several multiverse outfitters now stock what they call "basin walkers"—essentially modified platform boots with broad, smooth soles and minimal tread. The lack of aggressive tread is actually an advantage here. Deep grooves fill with the clay-mud and become counterproductive, adding weight and turning your feet into small excavating tools.

The material should be waterproof, but understand that "waterproof" in this context means something different than it does elsewhere. The water here isn't falling from above; it's seeping from below. It will find its way in eventually. Your boots will become damp inside. Accept this. Plan for it. Wear socks that can handle being wet—synthetic blends designed for wet conditions, never cotton, which will hold moisture against your skin and cause blisters within hours.

Bring more socks than you think you need. Twice as many. Change them frequently. Your feet will be constantly damp, and even the best socks will eventually become saturated. Dry socks are not a luxury here; they're a medical necessity. The combination of constant moisture and warmth creates ideal conditions for fungal infections and bacterial growth. Changing your socks at least three times per day should be considered minimum care.

Gaiters are essential. The fine cracks in the surface mean that your feet aren't just pressing down into a uniform material—they're pressing into a mosaic of slightly unstable sections. Clay-mud can work its way up over your boot tops with each step. Gaiters that seal tightly around your upper boots will save you significant discomfort.

## Clothing: Living in the Held Breath

The air here holds moisture "like a held breath." This is not the choking humidity of a tropical rainforest or the oppressive wet heat of a summer swamp. It's something gentler and more pervasive. You will feel it on your skin constantly—a awareness of the boundary between you and the world.

This means that nothing you wear will ever be truly dry. Accept this now. Your clothes will become damp from the ambient moisture, then damper from your own sweat, then reach some equilibrium state of persistent, tolerable wetness. Clothing that works well in other humid environments will work well here: loose weaves that allow air movement, synthetic fibers that don't absorb water into their structure, designs that don't cling to the body.

But there's a tension you need to navigate. The air is warm, the ground radiates heat upward, and you'll be walking across an open landscape with almost no shade. You'll want light colors to reflect sunlight. You'll want coverage to protect against the clouds of flying insects. You'll want loose fits for air circulation. But you'll also find that the pervasive dampness makes any fabric heavier than normal, and heavy fabric drags and chafes.

The solution that experienced travelers have settled on is layering in a way that seems counterintuitive for a warm environment. A very thin, form-fitting base layer made of synthetic wicking material, covered by a looser, lightweight overshirt in a light color. The base layer moves moisture away from your skin and provides a smooth surface that reduces chafing. The overshirt provides sun protection and some defense against insects. Both will be damp, but the combination is more comfortable than either alone.

Long pants are non-negotiable, despite the warmth. The scattered plant clumps, while rare, can scratch. The reptiles include some that may bite if startled. The ground-level insects are less likely to find bare skin to bother. And the constant moisture makes your skin softer and more vulnerable to abrasion. Light, loose trousers with a synthetic blend, perhaps with convertible zip-off legs if you anticipate wanting more airflow during rest stops.

A hat with a full brim—not a baseball cap, but something that shades your ears and the back of your neck. The sun here isn't markedly more intense than elsewhere, but there is literally no shade. You will be fully exposed for as long as you're walking. A neck gaiter or scarf can provide additional protection and can be wetted for evaporative cooling if needed.

## Navigation: The Curve That Confuses

The horizon curves here. "Not dramatically—you have to really look for it—but the land is so flat, so ancient, so thoroughly worn down by time that the earth's gentle rounding becomes visible."

This matters more than you might think. Human navigation relies heavily on visual landmarks, and the Basin denies you almost all of them. No mountains, no trees, no buildings, no rock formations. Just the flat expanse, the scattered pools, and the occasional plant clump. And all of it repeats. "Walk for an hour, and the view repeats itself. New pools, new clumps, new arrangements of creatures, but the same quality of space."

The curvature of the horizon, subtle as it is, means that your usual sense of "that point in the distance" becomes unreliable. Things at the very edge of visibility are already being pulled slightly below the plane you're walking on. This is true everywhere on a round world, but the Basin's extraordinary flatness makes it visible. Your brain, trained on landscapes with more visual complexity, may misinterpret what it's seeing. Distances will feel wrong. The horizon will seem slightly too close or slightly too far, depending on the light.

A compass is essential, but not sufficient. The magnetic field here is stable, but the landscape itself plays tricks with your sense of direction. Without visual landmarks to anchor your heading, you may find that even with a compass, you drift slightly from your intended path—the natural variation in human gait amplified by the featureless terrain. Learning to walk a truly straight line takes practice.

Some form of distance tracking is crucial. A pedometer, GPS, or even a simple measured pace-count can help you understand how far you've traveled. Without this, you'll have no way to distinguish genuine progress from the visual repetition. The psychological effect of walking for three hours and feeling like you haven't moved can be profound.

GPS works, but test your specific device before relying on it. The flat terrain means good satellite reception, but some travelers report that the combination of the subtle curvature and the water-saturated ground creates minor interference patterns that affect certain models. Know your equipment's quirks.

Consider leaving markers at your starting point—something visible from a distance. The landscape "accepts objects and makes them disappear," but a marker pole tall enough and brightly colored enough should remain visible for at least several days. Don't expect to find your way back by spotting your own footprints; even the large reptiles leave only temporary marks. Their footprints "fill with water, become small pools themselves, then fade."

Sound navigation is possible here—indeed, the acoustic landscape is one of the Basin's defining features. Different areas have different sound signatures based on the density and type of life around the pools. With practice, you can learn to navigate by ear, locating pools and population centers by the quality of their amphibian conversations. This isn't something to rely on for your first visit, but it's a skill that develops with exposure.

## Water and Food: The Cruel Paradox

Water is everywhere. You cannot drink it.

This is perhaps the Basin's cruelest feature for human travelers. Pools "appear without warning" at your feet, water seeps from millions of fine cracks, the ground itself is saturated—and none of it is safe. The "specific chemistry" that supports the invertebrate life includes minerals and compounds that your body cannot process. Some pools are more mineral-laden than others, but distinguishing safe from unsafe is impossible without testing equipment that most travelers won't carry.

You must bring all your drinking water. All of it. Calculate what you think you'll need, then add fifty percent. The warmth radiating from below and the humid air will dehydrate you faster than you expect, even though you won't feel the dramatic thirst that a dry heat produces. Your sweat doesn't evaporate efficiently in the moist air, so your body continues producing it, continuing to lose fluids and electrolytes.

Water treatment is theoretically possible but practically difficult. Filtration won't remove the dissolved minerals; chemical treatment won't neutralize them. Distillation would work, but the Basin offers no fuel for fires—nothing to burn, no wood, no dried plant matter. The ground is too damp to support even a small contained flame for more than a few minutes before the heat is conducted away into the saturated earth. Solar stills are possible during the midday burn-off period, but they're slow and require carrying equipment that many travelers find too bulky.

The safer approach is to bring a generous supply and plan your route around the need to carry it. A hydration system with a drinking tube allows you to sip continuously, which helps you stay ahead of dehydration. Add electrolyte supplements to compensate for the minerals you'll lose through sweat.

Food presents less of a challenge but still requires planning. The landscape offers almost nothing for human foragers. The plant clumps produce nothing edible—the "tough, low growths with fleshy leaves" are adapted to the Basin's specific chemistry and contain compounds that human digestion can't process. The animals are technically edible but catching them requires skills most travelers lack, and the ethics of killing creatures in an ecosystem where "nothing goes to waste" are complicated. Better to bring your own.

Pack dense, shelf-stable foods that don't require cooking. The moisture in the air means that anything not sealed will become damp within hours. Crackers will soften. Dried fruit will rehydrate into a sticky mass. Pack everything in waterproof containers, and expect that even sealed packages will develop condensation from temperature changes.

## Sleep and Shelter: The Difficult Night

Camping in the Old Basin requires accepting that you will not have a dry camp.

The ground is saturated. The air is saturated. The warmth continues rising from below even after the sun sets. There is no escaping the moisture. Tents pitched on this surface will have water seep up from below and condense from above. The "few stones" that exist are rare, and finding one large enough to serve as a tent platform would be fortunate indeed.

Some travelers have experimented with suspended sleeping systems—hammocks strung between poles they carry themselves. This keeps them off the damp ground and provides some separation from the crawling life. It's an imperfect solution; the poles add weight, and setting up a stable suspension system on the Basin's uneven surface requires practice.

A more common approach is to use a tent with a fully waterproof tub floor, understanding that the floor will be damp from below. Bring a closed-cell foam sleeping pad that won't absorb moisture. A synthetic-fill sleeping bag or quilt, never down, which would clump and lose insulation value in the persistent dampness. And accept that you'll be sleeping in conditions that feel slightly uncomfortable by normal camping standards.

Night temperatures don't drop dramatically. The ground continues radiating stored heat, and the humid air holds warmth well. You won't be cold, but you might be clammy. The psychological effect of sleeping in a damp tent, on damp ground, surrounded by the ongoing "amphibian murmur" and the occasional "heavy footsteps squelching" of reptiles, can be strange. Some travelers find it soothing. Others find themselves unable to fully relax, their bodies convinced that something is wrong.

The sounds continue at night, though they "drop to murmurs." This is a feature, not a bug. The acoustic landscape is part of the Basin's character. Trying to block it out entirely means missing one of the place's essential qualities. But light sleepers may benefit from moderate earplugs that reduce but don't eliminate the sound.

Consider whether you need to sleep at all during your visit. If you're planning a shorter expedition—entering at dawn, walking through the day, exiting before dusk—you may be able to avoid overnight camping entirely. This is a valid strategy for a first visit. The Basin can be appreciated in a long day, though you'll only see a small portion of its vastness.

## Health Considerations: The Small Dangers

The Old Basin will not kill you through dramatic means. No predators will hunt you. No sudden chasms will open beneath your feet. The dangers here are small, cumulative, and easy to underestimate until they become significant.

**Insects and Arthropods:**
The "clouds of small flying things" include mosquitoes, biting flies, and other insects that feed on blood. They're present throughout the day, with peaks during the calmer periods when wind doesn't disperse them. The warm, humid environment supports their life cycles perfectly, and the abundant amphibian life means pools of standing water everywhere for breeding.

A high-quality insect repellent is essential. Apply it to all exposed skin and reapply frequently—sweat and humidity reduce its effectiveness. Some travelers report that the standard DEET-based repellents work but need reapplication every few hours. Picaridin-based alternatives may last longer in humid conditions for some people.

Beyond the flying insects, the ground-level life includes ticks, mites, and other small arthropods. Long pants tucked into socks and sealed with gaiters provide reasonable protection. Do a thorough tick check during rest stops and especially at the end of each day. The Basin's ecology is isolated enough that most travelers report no tick-borne illnesses, but the risk isn't zero.

**The Amphibians:**
Thousands upon thousands of them. "Small creatures with slick bodies and wide mouths." Most are harmless, but some species in analogous environments produce skin secretions that can irritate human skin or mucous membranes. Don't pick them up unless you need to, and if you do, wash your hands thoroughly before touching your face or eating.

The greater danger is psychological—startling one amphibian can trigger a chain reaction as dozens of nearby individuals dive into pools simultaneously. The sudden splashing, multiplied across hundreds of individuals, can be genuinely startling, enough to cause a fall if you're walking on uneven ground.

**The Reptiles:**
"Larger creatures... some no longer than a forearm, others substantial enough to leave deep trails in the softer ground." The substantial ones deserve respect. The description suggests reptiles filling ecological niches similar to small crocodilians or large lizards elsewhere. They're not aggressive toward humans—there's no mention of attacks—but they will defend themselves if stepped on or cornered.

Watch where you walk. The soft ground and frequent pools mean your attention is often downward anyway, looking for stable footing. Learn to recognize the signs of larger reptile presence: deeper trails, areas where the vegetation around a pool is trampled, the occasional basking spot where something large has been lying.

**Fungal and Bacterial Concerns:**
The persistent warmth and dampness make the Basin a paradise for fungal growth. Athlete's foot, jock itch, and similar conditions can develop rapidly in travelers who don't practice rigorous hygiene. Change socks frequently. Change underwear at least once per day. If you stop for more than a brief rest, consider removing your boots and airing your feet.

Any small cut or abrasion will heal slowly here and may become infected. Carry a basic first-aid kit with antiseptic, bandages, and antibiotic ointment. Clean even minor wounds promptly.

The water in the pools contains bacteria and other microorganisms adapted to the specific chemistry. Don't get pool water in your eyes, mouth, or open wounds. If you fall into a pool, rinse off as soon as possible with clean water from your supply.

**Heat Stress:**
The warmth is "not oppressive" in the way that deserts or tropical forests can be. It's pervasive rather than intense. But the combination of warmth from below, sun from above, and humidity that prevents efficient sweating can still lead to heat exhaustion.

Learn the early signs: headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion. If you notice these in yourself or a companion, stop immediately, find shade (even if it's just the shade of your own body sitting), hydrate, and wait until symptoms pass before continuing. Pushing through heat exhaustion in this environment can lead to heat stroke, which is a genuine medical emergency.

## What Not to Bring: Learning from Others' Mistakes

Don't bring a tent with a mesh floor. The ground will seep through, and you'll wake up in a pool of your own creation.

Don't bring a down sleeping bag. Even "water-resistant" down will eventually clump in this environment, leaving you with an expensive, useless sack of damp feathers.

Don't bring fire-starting equipment. There is nothing to burn. The ground is too damp to sustain even a small fire, and the plant clumps are too valuable to the ecosystem to sacrifice for a few minutes of flame.

Don't bring a fishing rod. The pools contain life, but catching it requires specific local knowledge, and the mineral chemistry makes most of the aquatic creatures unpalatable to humans anyway.

Don't bring a traditional compass without understanding how to use it in a landscape without landmarks. A GPS device with waypoints is more useful for most travelers.

Don't bring too much food that requires preparation. Everything is damp; your hands are damp; simple, ready-to-eat foods reduce stress.

Don't bring anything you're not willing to lose to the ground. The Basin "accepts objects and makes them disappear." Items set down can sink into the clay-mud or become submerged in an expanding pool. Keep everything attached to your person.

## The Proper Mindset: How to Be Here

The Basin "doesn't need your attention. It doesn't need anything." This is not a place that requires you to appreciate it. It will continue its slow cycles of evaporation and condensation, its amphibian conversations, its incremental settling regardless of whether you witness it.

This can feel like indifference, and in a sense, it is. But it's also an invitation. Because the Basin requires nothing from you, you're free to simply exist within it. No expectations. No agenda beyond being present.

Some travelers approach the Basin as a hiking challenge, setting ambitious distance goals. This misses the point. The landscape rewards slowness, attention, patience. Stand still for long enough, and you begin to perceive the acoustic map—"the direction and distance of each pool, the population density around each seep, the movement of larger creatures across the basin." Close your eyes, and you can navigate by sound.

Others come seeking profound spiritual experience, expecting the vastness and antiquity to trigger epiphanies. Sometimes this happens. More often, the experience is subtler—a gradual erosion of the expectation that everything must mean something, a settling into the fact of being in a place that was ancient before your species existed and will continue long after.

The Basin "holds you for a moment, then releases you." Don't try to extend that moment. Don't try to mark your passage with cairns or signs; the landscape will erase them. Don't try to take anything beyond your memories; removing even a handful of the clay-pale earth feels like a violation. Don't try to claim significance for your visit.

Walk. Listen. Feel the warmth through your soles. Let the damp settle on your skin. Watch the pools reflect the sky. When you're ready, leave.

The Basin will be here. It has time.
