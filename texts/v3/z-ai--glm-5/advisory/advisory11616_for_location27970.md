# Preparation Guide: The Condensation Cities

## Mental Preparation First

Understand what you are walking into. This is not a destination you visit for pleasure or novelty. The Condensation Cities will test your patience, your senses, and your tolerance for discomfort before your first day is complete. Many travelers who arrive without adequate psychological preparation do not stay long enough to adapt. The ones who do adapt describe a threshold period—roughly two to four weeks—during which the environment feels genuinely hostile. Then something shifts. Not the city. You.

Prepare yourself for the sensation of never being dry. Not once. Not for a single moment during your stay. You will wake damp. You will work damp. You will sleep damp. Your skin will prune. Your hair will lose whatever character it normally has and adopt a limp, heavy quality. Within days, you will stop noticing the sensation of moisture on your forearm, on your neck, on the back of your ears, because it will simply become the baseline state of your body. This is not adaptation in the romantic sense. It is surrender. Approach it that way.

Prepare for the noise. Before you arrive, spend several days sleeping with industrial audio recordings playing at moderate volume—generator hums, steam releases, mechanical rhythm. Layer bird and insect sounds over this. Do not use earplugs during this preparation. You need to begin the process of learning to filter, not to block. Earplugs will be essential upon arrival for sleep during the first week, but you cannot live in them indefinitely. The locals do not. They have simply stopped hearing what you cannot yet ignore.

Prepare for the absence of green. This sounds trivial. It is not. Humans carry an expectation of vegetative life the way they carry expectations of gravity. The plateau's landscape—stone, concrete, steel, animal, fungus—will trigger something in you that feels like deprivation. You will look for plants where there are none. You will notice the empty window boxes, the barren planters that some optimistic district administrator installed and abandoned. You will crave the color green with an intensity that surprises you. Bring something green if you need it. A single hardy moss culture in a sealed container. A photograph of a forest. Whatever works. Do not underestimate this.

Most importantly, prepare for the scale. The Condensation Cities are not a city. They are a nation that happens to be built as a continuous urban environment. You will not explore them in any comprehensive sense. Accept this before arrival. Choose a district. Learn it. Walk its streets until your feet know the uneven patches and your lungs know the microclimate. The rest of the plateau will exist for you as rumor, as tram ride, as conversation with someone who came from a district you will never visit. This is how the locals live. It is how you should live while you are there.

## Physical Conditioning and Health

The altitude alone warrants serious consideration. Four kilometers above the lowland seas means thin air, reduced oxygen, and a cardiovascular system working harder than it does in most environments. If you are arriving from a sea-level or low-altitude universe, spend at least a week at intermediate altitude if your transit path permits it. If not, anticipate altitude sickness: headache, nausea, shortness of breath, fatigue, insomnia. These symptoms will compound with the environmental stresses of the plateau and can produce a genuinely debilitating first week.

Begin cardiovascular conditioning at least a month before arrival. You will walk more than you expect. The lateral sprawl of the city, the tram system's limited coverage, and the reality that most destinations require multiple transit modes mean that foot travel is fundamental. Strengthen your ankles and knees—the streets are wet, uneven in places, and the stone beneath them can shift subtly under the influence of water saturation. A twisted ankle in an unfamiliar district, far from anyone who knows you, is a situation you want to avoid.

Build tolerance for respiratory irritation. The industrial atmosphere carries particulate matter, chemical traces, and the omnipresent biological output of a city-scale animal population. If you have asthma, reactive airways, or any pulmonary sensitivity, consult with a medical professional who understands multiverse environmental exposure. Bring appropriate medications in quantities sufficient for your stay plus a buffer. The local medical infrastructure is functional but oriented toward occupational injury and endemic conditions; they may not have what you need.

Strengthen your immune system. The plateau's biological environment is not inherently pathogenic to humans—the animal life has coexisted with the human population for centuries—but you are an outsider with no acquired resistance to local microbial flora. The fungal layer that underlies the city's waste processing is everywhere, in the air, in the water, on surfaces. Most of it is harmless to humans. Some of it is not. Give your body the best possible starting position.

Expect skin problems. The constant moisture, combined with the mineral content of the condensation and the chemical traces in the air, will affect your skin in ways you cannot fully predict. You may develop rashes, fungal infections of the skin, or simply a persistent rawness in areas where friction and moisture combine. Your feet are especially vulnerable. Prepare for this.

## What to Bring

### Clothing

Abandon everything you know about appropriate dress. The rules are different here.

Your base layers should be synthetic moisture-wicking fabrics. Not cotton—cotton will absorb the ambient moisture and never release it, becoming a cold, heavy second skin that breeds skin infections. Not wool—wool retains warmth when wet, which sounds ideal but in this environment means you will be perpetually overheated and damp simultaneously, a uniquely unpleasant combination. Synthetic fabrics designed for high-output activities in wet conditions will serve you best. Bring enough for daily rotation over at least two weeks. You will not be doing laundry in the conventional sense; clothes here are rinsed, treated with antifungal solution if available, and hung in areas with forced air circulation. Full drying is aspirational.

Your outer layer must be fully waterproof, but not in the way you might expect. A heavy raincoat will trap the moisture your body produces and create a personal humidity disaster inside your clothing. You need a breathable waterproof shell—material that repels external moisture while allowing vapor transfer from within. Even this will be compromised by the plateau's atmospheric conditions within days. Bring waterproofing treatment and reapply it regularly. Bring two shells. One will be drying while you wear the other.

Footwear is critical. You need boots that are waterproof, have aggressive traction for wet stone and concrete, and are durable enough to withstand the chemical environment. The mineral deposits that streak every surface will build up on your soles. The crawlers, if they take interest in your footwear's composite materials, may damage them. The constant moisture will try to separate the seams. Bring two pairs. Break them both in before arrival. New boots in this environment will give you blisters that will not heal during your stay.

Cover your head. The condensation will bead on your hair and scalp constantly. A waterproof hood or hat reduces the rate at which your scalp absorbs moisture and the minerals it carries. This is not vanity. Prolonged scalp exposure to the plateau's water chemistry can cause irritation and, in some travelers, a temporary thinning of hair that resolves only after departure.

Bring gloves. Thin, waterproof, allowing dexterity. You will be touching wet surfaces constantly—railings, door handles, tram poles, the walls of tunnels. The tactile sensation of perpetual dampness on your hands becomes psychologically wearing. Gloves reduce this.

Bring eye protection. Not sunglasses—the light is diffuse and rarely intense enough to require them. But the condensation carries particulate matter, and the industrial sectors produce aerosolized chemicals that can irritate eyes. Clear, sealed goggles for the industrial districts. Simple protective lenses for general wear. Your eyes will water in the first days regardless. This is normal.

### Equipment

Waterproof everything. Not water-resistant. Waterproof. Every document, every electronic device, every piece of food, every medication. Use sealed bags, waterproof cases, and silica gel packets liberally. Assume that anything exposed to the open air for more than an hour is compromised. The condensation does not merely settle on surfaces; it actively penetrates containers that are not hermetically sealed. Travelers have opened supposedly protected backpacks to find their documents bled ink, their electronics corroded, their food softened and mineral-tainted.

Bring your own water filtration if you are sensitive to taste or mineral content. The city's tap water is safe—it has been condensed and processed through systems that are, by any standard, effective—but it tastes of metal and altitude and, to some palates, of the faint industrial heritage of every surface it has touched. A good filter will reduce this. Do not expect to eliminate it entirely.

Bring noise protection. Multiple types. Foam earplugs for sleeping. Higher-fidelity filtered earplugs for daytime use, which reduce volume without eliminating the sound entirely—you need to hear the city, to some degree, for navigation and safety. Active noise cancellation devices if you have them, though their effectiveness against the plateau's broadband, low-frequency soundscape may be limited. A white noise generator for your sleeping quarters, which can help mask the erratic elements of the nighttime soundscape by providing a consistent auditory baseline.

Bring lighting. The diffuse daylight is adequate for navigation but poor for detail work. At night, the municipal gas lamps and electric lights provide ambient illumination but not focused light. A waterproof headlamp with multiple battery sets is essential. The batteries will degrade faster than you expect in the humid environment. Carry extras.

Bring navigation tools that do not depend on electronics. The atmospheric conditions interfere with electrical systems; even the city's own message network is limited to the central districts. A compass may be unreliable—the plateau's geology and the industrial electromagnetic fields can affect magnetic readings—but a physical map, once you obtain a local one, will serve you better than any device. Learn to navigate by landmarks, by the direction of water flow in the street channels, by the orientation of the condenser towers.

Bring a personal shelter. Not a tent in the traditional sense—a moisture-proof bivouac that can be deployed in any covered space. There are no hotels in the tourist sense. There are boarding houses in most districts, but availability is not guaranteed, and the quality of their moisture protection varies enormously. A traveler who can sleep in their own sealed environment, even if it is little more than a waterproof cocoon, has a significant advantage.

### Medical Supplies

Antifungal preparations. Topical, for skin. Oral, as a prophylactic if your medical advisor recommends it. The fungal environment of the plateau is pervasive, and while it is not aggressively pathogenic, it will take any opportunity your compromised skin provides.

Broad-spectrum antibiotics, if available to you. The animal life is not dangerous, but the environment produces injuries—slips on wet surfaces, cuts from corroded metal, abrasions from stone—that can become infected in the humid, microbe-rich environment. Wound care supplies: waterproof bandages that actually adhere to wet skin (these exist but are not common), antiseptic, closure strips.

Rehydration salts. The altitude and the constant moisture can deceive you into thinking you are hydrated. You are losing water through respiration at altitude faster than you realize, and the mineral content of the local water may not be efficiently absorbed. Monitor your hydration. The salts help.

Altitude sickness medication, if you are arriving from lowlands. Acetazolamide or equivalent, as appropriate for your physiology. Consult someone who understands the specific demands of sustained high-altitude residence, not just acute exposure.

Sleep aids. The noise will interfere with your sleep for the first weeks. A pharmacological bridge to adaptation is reasonable. Do not rely on it indefinitely—the goal is to learn to sleep in the plateau's soundscape, not to medicate through your entire stay.

## What to Leave Behind

Leave behind any expectation of comfort. Not because comfort is impossible here, but because your definition of comfort will not survive the first day. The locals have a different relationship with the word. Comfort here means dry socks. Comfort means a sealed room where the condensation forms on the walls instead of on your skin. Comfort means the noise has dropped from overwhelming to merely constant. If you carry your old definition, you will be miserable.

Leave behind rigid schedules. The city operates on cycles that have nothing to do with your itinerary. The trams run when they run. The messengers arrive when they arrive. The sky-vermin emerge when they emerge. You will adapt to the city's rhythm or you will be constantly frustrated. Build flexibility into every plan. Assume that any commitment will be delayed by factors you cannot control or predict.

Leave behind electronic dependence. Whatever devices you carry from other universes will fail here. The moisture is the primary enemy, but the electromagnetic interference from the city's industrial systems and the plateau's own geological properties will disrupt communication and navigation electronics. If you need a device to function, it must be ruggedized to military or industrial standards, and even then, expect reduced reliability. The most successful travelers here are those who can operate with pen and paper and memory.

Leave behind your assumptions about cleanliness. The city is not dirty in the conventional sense—the water that covers everything is, in most areas, reasonably clean condensation. But the mineral deposits, the animal presence, and the industrial output create a patina on everything that you will initially perceive as filth and eventually perceive as simply the way things look here. If you compulsively clean, you will exhaust yourself. The surface you wipe dry will be wet again within minutes. Accept the patina.

Leave behind dietary expectations. The plateau has no agriculture in the conventional sense. No plants means no plant-based foods except those imported at significant expense. Protein comes from the blind fish-kin of the cisterns and the occasionally harvested grazers from the margins. Carbohydrates come from the synthesizers, which process fungal biomass into calorie-dense but unremarkable foodstuffs. If you have dietary restrictions, bring your own food. If you are flexible, prepare for a diet that is adequate but monotonous and that carries, always, the faint taste of the systems that produced it.

## Behavioral Adaptation

### Movement

Walk with shorter steps than you normally would. The surfaces are wet, but more than that, they are unpredictable. The stone beneath the paving may have shifted. A channel may have overflowed. A crawler swarm may have pitted a concrete surface overnight. Move with awareness, not speed. The locals walk this way—a particular gait that travelers eventually adopt without conscious decision. You will know you are adapting when your stride shortens and your pace slows to match the city's rhythm.

When you descend into the older districts—the ones cut into the stone—be aware that the architecture changes character. Ceilings may be lower. Passages may be narrower. The air will be drier but also more stagnant. The lighting, if it exists at all, will be minimal. Move carefully. These chambers have existed for longer than the surface city, and their users have had centuries to forget the hazards they contain.

Use the trams when they serve your route. They are slow, crowded, and run on schedules that only locals seem to intuit. But they cover distances that would take hours on foot, and they provide a degree of shelter from the environment that walking does not. Learn the routes. Learn the unwritten rules of tram etiquette, which are different in every district. Watch before you board.

### Interaction with Animal Life

Do not attempt to control or reduce the animal presence. You will fail, and you will annoy the locals, who have long since accepted the fauna as co-residents. The sky-vermin will roost in your equipment if you leave it uncovered. The crawlers will investigate your belongings if you leave them on the ground. The aquatic colonies in the water channels will not bother you unless you bother them.

Accept the droppings. They are everywhere. They are calcium-rich, which means they bond to surfaces with remarkable adhesion. Your boots will be coated within a day. Your outer clothing will be spotted. Do not waste effort on continuous cleaning. The locals wear the droppings as a matter of course. You will see children playing in streets patterned with them. You will eat in facilities where they streak the exterior walls. This is the plateau.

If the crawlers take interest in your equipment—and they may, particularly if it contains mineral composites they find metabolically useful—do not try to deter them with chemicals. The local ecosystem is balanced in ways that are not obvious, and introducing foreign deterrents can produce unpredictable cascading effects. Physical barriers work: store your belongings on elevated surfaces, in sealed containers, away from the gaps between buildings where the crawlers travel.

### Social Conduct

The residents of the Condensation Cities are not unfriendly. They are occupied. Their lives are structured around the demands of the environment and the rhythms of the city, and they have little bandwidth for the social performance of welcome. Do not interpret reserve as hostility. Do not expect to be invited into homes. Do not expect conversation beyond the transactional.

If you want to build relationships, you do so through repeated, low-intensity contact. Visit the same food vendor at the same time each day. Walk the same route. Occupy the same bench in the same covered passage. The locals notice patterns. When your pattern becomes familiar, you become familiar, and familiarity is the foundation of whatever passes for community here.

Learn the district boundaries. Not just the administrative ones—the real ones, the ones defined by dialect and custom and microclimate. Walking from one district into another is not merely a geographical transition. You will be assessed differently. The social norms will shift. What was acceptable behavior in the Industrial Quarter may be rude in the Archives District. Observe before acting. Ask questions only when necessary, and only of people who seem willing to answer.

Do not photograph the animal life without asking. It is not a prohibition—there is no formal rule—but the locals find it slightly offensive, as if you have arrived in their home and begun photographing their family members without permission. The animals are not pests to them. They are co-inhabitants. Treat them with the respect you would treat anyone's household.

### Hygiene

Bathe when you can. The facilities vary by district. Some have public bathing houses fed by the condensation systems. Some have private arrangements. Some have nothing. Clean water is not scarce here—the opposite—but clean, warm, treated water for personal use requires infrastructure that not all districts maintain.

Treat your feet as your most important hygiene priority. Dry them whenever possible. Apply antifungal powder. Change socks. Remove your boots when you are in a sheltered space and let your feet air, even if the air is damp. The single most common complaint that drives travelers from the plateau is foot problems that become severe enough to make walking unbearable. Without walking, you cannot exist here.

Manage your clothing rotation carefully. Even with adequate supplies, you will find that your clothing degrades faster than expected. The mineral content of the condensation stiffens fabric. The antifungal treatments you apply to it alter its properties. The constant dampness promotes mildew that no amount of care can fully prevent. When clothing becomes unwearable, do not discard it casually—the city has waste processing, but the system is oriented toward industrial output, not personal effects. Find out where the district's textile waste goes. Use the system, do not circumvent it.

## Duration and Departure

Plan your initial stay for no less than one month. Anything shorter and you will leave during the adaptation period, having experienced only the discomfort and none of the equilibrium. The city does not reveal itself quickly. It does not reveal itself to people who are still fighting it.

If, after a month, you find the noise tolerable, the dampness manageable, and the absence of plants something you have stopped remarking on, you may stay longer. Many travelers do. The city has a way of absorbing people who are willing to be absorbed. You will develop routines. You will know which streets flood slightly during the heavy condensation hours. You will know which vendor sells the least mineral-tasting food. You will know the sound of the sky-vermin departing their roost as a signal of morning rather than an intrusion.

When you depart, the experience will stay with you. Travelers from the Condensation Cities report that silence feels wrong for weeks afterward. That dryness feels like absence. That the absence of animal sounds in other environments creates a vacancy that is not peaceful but unsettling. The plateau changes you, not through drama or spectacle, but through the slow, persistent reconfiguration of your baseline.

You will grow clammy in your memory. You will hear the machines in your sleep. You will reach for a wall to test if it gives slightly, like a soaked sponge.

This is normal. This is what it means to have been somewhere that found its equilibrium and refused to explain it to you.
