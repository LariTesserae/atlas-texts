# Preparation Guide: The Germination Basins

## Before You Arrive

**Abandon your instruments.** This is not advice but warning. Whatever you carry that measures, quantifies, or reduces the world to data—it will betray you here. Thermometers will show warmth while ice forms beside them. Spectrometers will catalog wavelengths that correspond to nothing. Your devices will not break; they will drift, quietly and then profoundly, until the numbers they offer mean less than the feeling of air on your skin. Save the weight. Save the expectation.

What you bring instead: analog tools. A notebook with waterproof pages. A pencil, not a pen—the ink will run in the wet hours. Your own body, which remains the most reliable instrument you possess in a place where physics has grown speculative. Train yourself to notice warmth without a thermometer, distance without a rangefinder, time without a clock. These skills will serve you when your devices have become decorative.

---

## What to Wear

**You will be damp.** This is not preventable. The condensation arrives each cycle with the reliability of breathing, and the warmth rising from below ensures that the damp never quite dries. Accept this as baseline. Resistance is futile and leads only to the particular misery of fighting what cannot be changed.

Instead, choose fabrics that *accommodate* moisture rather than repel it. Something that breathes, that can be wet without becoming heavy or losing its structural integrity. The settlers have presumably developed clothing suited to these conditions—observe them when you arrive, and adapt. Avoid anything that will rot, mold, or break down in constant humidity. Leather is hopeless. So is anything with metal components that can corrode.

Your feet require particular attention. The warmth rises through the ground, and the surfaces run wet for an hour each cycle. You will feel the heat through your soles—a pleasant sensation at first, then a strange one, then something you stop noticing because it has become part of your body's understanding of the environment. Choose footwear that allows you to feel the warmth without burning, that grips wet surfaces, and that can be worn for extended periods without developing the fungal infestations that constant moisture encourages. Bring powder. Bring more than you think you need.

Layer, but loosely. The temperature varies within narrow bands—warm and damp, warmer and damper—but these small variations matter more than you expect when your body is never fully dry. You will want to adjust, to open a layer, to close one. The clothing should move with you, should breathe with you, should feel like something the city's vegetation might approve of.

Cover your hair. The air carries pollen in quantities that register as color, and something else that instruments detect but cannot name. You do not want this accumulating on your body, in your lungs, in the folds of your clothing. A simple covering, something washable, something you can remove and shake clean during the drier hours.

---

## Respiratory Preparation

**Breathe shallowly at first.** Not from fear but from caution. The air here carries more than moisture and pollen—there is the unnamed particulate, the industrial residue, the spores that arrived with the construction materials and have since become integral to the city's metabolism. Your lungs are unaccustomed to this particular cocktail.

Bring a respirator, but understand its limitations. The filters were designed for known contaminants, and the basins contain elements that defy categorization. The respirator will help during your initial acclimation, giving your body time to adjust without overwhelming its defenses. It will not protect you entirely. Nothing will protect you entirely. The question is not whether you will breathe the basin's air but how quickly your body learns to process what you have inhaled.

Watch the settlers. They breathe deeply here. Their bodies have adapted. Yours may adapt as well, given time, or you may leave before adaptation becomes necessary. The choice is yours, but the adaptation is not entirely under your control.

If you have asthma, allergies, or any respiratory vulnerability, reconsider whether this destination is wise. The germination basins do not accommodate human weakness. They *incorporate* it, slowly, into processes that serve the city's own ends.

---

## The Wet Hours

**Learn the rhythm.** The cycle is not a day—you must abandon that framework immediately. There is no sunrise, no sunset, no reliable visual cue to mark the passage of time. What there is instead: warmth increasing, moisture condensing, surfaces dripping, warmth decreasing, moisture lifting. This cycle has its own duration, its own irregularities, its own relationship with the city's growth that you will come to feel before you can articulate it.

The wet hour—when condensation forms on every surface and the thoroughfares run with water—is the most dangerous time for a newcomer. Not because the water is deep (it isn't) or because the surfaces are treacherous (they yield slightly, absorbing impact, which creates its own instability), but because the wet hour is when the city is most *active*. The vegetation adjusts its orientation. The structures shift by fractions as their materials expand with moisture. The fauna—whatever they are—move more confidently through the hanging gardens. The boundaries between city and organism become less distinct.

Stay inside during the wet hour. At least at first. Observe through membranes and curtains of vegetation. Let the city conduct its business without your interference. You will know when you are ready to move through the wet; the city will tell you, through channels you cannot consciously perceive but will learn to trust.

## How to Move

**Walk softly.** The surfaces absorb sound with efficiency that borders on hunger. Your footfalls will be whispers, your voice will carry perhaps ten meters before the air swallows it. This is not a limitation but a condition—a feature of the environment that shapes how you exist within it.

Learn to communicate through gesture, through proximity, through the positioning of your body relative to others. The settlers have developed languages that require no sound—hand signals, patterns of movement, the subtle choreography of people who have learned that speech is an inefficient medium in a place where sound dies. Observe them. Learn what you can.

Move at the pace the city sets. The wide plazas between buildings create the temptation to cross quickly, to traverse the emptiness efficiently, to treat the space as something to be crossed rather than inhabited. Resist this. The sparseness is intentional; the distances serve the city's need for circulation, for sightlines, for the particular quality of visibility that the dim light creates. When you cross a plaza, you become a figure in motion, a silhouette, a gesture of presence. The city sees you. Whatever process coordinates its growth registers your movement. Walk as though you are being watched—not with fear but with awareness.

Do not run. Not because running is dangerous but because it marks you as something the city must interpret. Running suggests urgency, threat, prey. You do not want to be categorized as any of these.

---

## The Quiet

**Prepare for silence.** Not absolute silence—the city breathes, and the breathing produces a subsonic pulse you will feel in your chest rather than hear—but the absence of ambient sound that most humans find deeply unsettling. Your footsteps: whispers. Your voice: swallowed. The rain, when it comes, sounds like fingers drumming on cloth. The vegetation rustles, but quietly, as though the plants are keeping secrets.

This silence does things to the human mind. It strips away the auditory camouflage that most people use to avoid their own thoughts. It makes the internal louder. Some travelers find this liberating; most find it disturbing. Know which you are before you arrive.

Bring no sound-making devices. They will not work as intended—acoustics in the basins follow their own logic—and the attempt to fill the silence with familiar noise will mark you as something foreign, something that does not understand where it is. The city accepts all inputs, but some inputs produce responses you may not want.

Instead, learn to listen to what the silence contains. The subsonic pulse. The drip of condensation. The almost-imperceptible creak of structures growing. The movement in the upper canopy—something shifting its weight, settling. The silence is not empty. It is full of information you have not learned to decode.

## Psychological Preparation

**You will lose yourself here.** Not immediately, not obviously, but slowly and thoroughly. The dim light strips identity—the markers you carry that say "this is who I am" become silhouettes, become movement, become a walking rather than a person. The distances between buildings make you visible from multiple vantages simultaneously, which creates a peculiar paradox: you are always observed but never recognized. The anonymity here is not the anonymity of crowds but the anonymity of reduced signal, of light that renders you indistinct.

Some travelers find this liberating—a shedding of identity that allows them to become something new. Others find it terrifying. Know yourself before you arrive, and know that the basin's effect on identity is not optional. The light does what the light does. The distances create what they create. You can resist, but resistance requires energy, and the basin's warmth and dampness have a way of draining the energy you might use for resistance.

Prepare also for the temporal disorientation. Without reliable external cues, your circadian rhythm will drift. Sleep when the city sleeps—which is to say, never entirely, but in waves, in patterns that correspond to moisture and warmth rather than light and dark. You will wake uncertain of how long you have slept. You will move through hours that feel both compressed and expanded. Time in the basins is not the time you know. Accept this. Fighting it produces only exhaustion.

**The most important psychological preparation: understand that you are not visiting a place. You are entering a process.** The city is growing, evolving, learning. The settlers are components of that process—not masters, not observers, but parts. The distinction between "inhabitant" and "inhabited" has collapsed here. The city does not belong to the settlers; they belong to it, or they are becoming part of it, or the category "part" has lost its meaning in a biome that does not recognize boundaries between organism and environment.

If you cannot accept being a component rather than a controller, do not visit the germination basins.

## The Central Plaza

**Do not go there.** This is not prohibition; no authority will stop you. This is counsel born of the settlers' accumulated experience and the intuitive understanding that some spaces are not for humans.

The central plaza is an emptiness the city chose to maintain. It is not public space, not civic space, not neutral ground. It is a communication node, perhaps, or a locus of whatever process coordinates the city's growth. The condensation hangs there rather than falling. The warmth rises visibly. The vegetation surrounding it grows more intricate, more patterned, more suggestive of meaning that your mind cannot quite grasp.

The settlers avoid it. Not from fear—they do not speak of danger—but from recognition that the space serves purposes that do not include them. Respect this. The city has accommodated human presence; it has integrated settlers into its metabolism; it has not, apparently, offered the central plaza as part of that accommodation.

If you find yourself near the central plaza during the wet hours, when condensation forms in columns and the air glows with the absence of dimness—retreat. Not quickly. Not with alarm. Simply move away, at the pace the city sets, toward the districts where human presence has been accepted.

The plaza is not for you. This is not a judgment but a boundary. Honor it.

## What to Observe

**The vegetation.** Watch how it interacts with the structures—symbiosis rather than parasitism, integration rather than invasion. The plants here have become components of architecture, and the architecture has become a substrate for growth. Notice how the leaves angle toward the particulate glow, then relax, then angle again. This rhythm tells you something about the city's cycles. Learn to read it.

**The structures.** They grow. Millimeters at a time, but visibly over extended observation. Watch the foundations extend themselves, the walls thicken, the doorways contract during wet hours and expand during dry. The city is not built; it is cultivated. The distinction matters more than you can initially understand.

**The fauna.** Or whatever they are—the presences at the edge of perception, the movements in the canopy, the shapes that slip through pooled water. Do not approach them. Do not flee from them. Observe. They may be animals; they may be extensions of the city; they may be what settlers become when they have been absorbed fully enough to shed their human shapes. The uncertainty is itself information.

**The settlers.** Watch how they move through their city, how they interact with its rhythms, how they have learned to guide growth through sound frequencies and foot traffic patterns. Notice what they avoid. Notice what they accept. Notice the moment when you realize they are no longer entirely human—not in any visible way, but in the way they belong to the city more than it belongs to them.

**Your own responses.** This is the most important observation you will make. Notice when the silence stops feeling oppressive. Notice when the damp stops feeling uncomfortable. Notice when you begin to anticipate the wet hours, when your body starts to align with the city's rhythms, when the subsonic pulse in your chest becomes not a sensation but a form of communication. These changes are data. Record them. Decide, consciously, whether you welcome them.

## Duration of Stay

**Set a limit before you arrive.** Tell someone your planned departure. Write it down. The basin's effect on temporal perception makes it easy to lose track of how long you have been present, and the gradual process of integration makes it easy to stop caring.

The settlers arrived with no departure date, and now they are components. This is not tragedy—they seem content, or at least peaceful, or at least beyond the categories of happiness and unhappiness that govern human life outside the basins. But they did not choose this with full foreknowledge, and you have the opportunity to choose differently.

Three days is sufficient to experience the basin's essential character. A week is sufficient to begin feeling the initial stages of adaptation. Two weeks is sufficient to wonder whether leaving is necessary. A month is sufficient to forget why you would want to.

Leave when your presence still feels like presence rather than process. Leave while you still distinguish yourself from the city. Leave if leaving still occurs to you as an option.

---

## Final Counsel

The germination basins are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are not neutral. These categories assume a relationship between environment and visitor that does not apply here. The basins *are*—they exist, they grow, they incorporate, they transform—and your presence within them will be processed according to logics that you may observe but will not fully understand.

Go with respect. Go with attention. Go with the understanding that you are entering something alive, something young, something still learning what it is becoming. The city will accommodate you as it accommodates all inputs. The question is not whether it will change you but whether you will notice the change, and whether noticing will be sufficient to resist it, and whether resistance is something you want to expend energy on, and whether the thing you might become is worse than the thing you are.

The basin collects. That is its nature. It collects moisture, heat, light sifted through particulate, vegetation, fauna, settlers, visitors. It collects and it germinates—seeds, spores, possibilities, transformations.

What grows in you is not entirely under your control. Know this before you arrive. Accept it, or stay away.
