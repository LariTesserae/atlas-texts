# Preparing for The Bloomery

## What You Are Preparing For

Before anything else, understand this: you are not going to a place. You are going to a *process*. The Bloomery is not a destination in the way that a city or a forest or a ruin is a destination. It is a moment — a very long, very slow moment — in the life of something that is becoming itself. You will enter sideways through a crack in a mountain at altitude, you will stand in a warm pocket of stone that should not exist, and you will watch copper filaments grow at a speed that makes glaciers look impulsive. That is the visit. That is all there is.

You are not needed there. Nothing about the Bloomery requires your presence, responds to your presence, or acknowledges your presence. This is not hostility. It is indifference at a scale that most humans find difficult to sit with. Prepare for that. Prepare for the feeling of being inside a machine that does not know it is a machine, building itself for a purpose it has not yet determined, in a space that is only barely committed to existing. Prepare for the fact that this will not be dramatic. It will be quiet, and warm, and slow, and deeply strange, and you will leave the way you came, and nothing will have changed.

That is the visit.

## Physical Preparation

### Conditioning and Health

The approach matters. The fissure is at altitude — high enough that the air outside is thin and cold. You will feel this in your lungs before you ever reach the entrance. If you are not acclimatized to altitude, you should plan for a gradual approach, spending at least a day or two at intermediate elevation before attempting the final climb. The basalt slope is not technically difficult, but it is high, and your body will remind you of this with every step.

Inside the Bloomery, the air is warm but not necessarily thicker. The description does not mention any change in oxygen concentration, and the warmth has no identifiable source — it is metabolic, ambient, a property of the space rather than a product of ventilation. You should assume the air composition is similar to the outside. If you needed supplemental oxygen for the approach, you may still need it inside. Do not assume the warmth means the air is richer. It does not. It means the stone is alive in some way you cannot explain, and that is a different thing entirely.

Cardiovascular fitness matters more than strength here. The squeeze through the fissure is narrow — shoulders only, barely — but it is brief. The scramble down from the ledge to the chamber floor is roughly thirty feet of rough basalt, and the rock is warm and textured, which actually provides decent grip. You do not need climbing equipment for this. You need steady hands, a calm stomach, and the ability to lower yourself down irregular stone without panicking about the fact that the stone beneath you feels *provisional*.

Because it will feel provisional. That is something no amount of physical preparation can address, but you should know it in advance: the floor of the Bloomery does not feel entirely solid. It is solid — you will not fall through it, the stone is stone, the basalt is basalt — but it feels as though it is considering other options. If you are prone to dissociation, to vertigo that is triggered by spatial uncertainty rather than physical height, or to anxiety in spaces that feel *wrong* in ways you cannot articulate, you need to think carefully about whether this visit is for you. There is no shame in deciding it is not.

### What to Wear

Layers. The outside is cold and thin. The inside is warm. You will want to strip down inside, but you will not want to carry the weight of heavy gear in your pack while you squeeze through the fissure. The solution is a shell — a thin, windproof outer layer that packs small — over a midweight insulating layer that you can remove and stuff in a pack once inside. The fissure is narrow enough that anything bulky on your back will scrape and catch. Pack slim. Pack soft.

Wear something you do not mind getting abraded. The vesicular basalt inside the Bloomery is pocked and rough. Your knees, your elbows, your palms — anywhere you touch the stone — will be mildly abraded by the texture of the rock. The stone is warm, which is disorienting, because your body expects warm stone to be smooth (sun-heated sandstone, for instance) and this is not smooth. It is rough and warm, like a living thing with a skin condition. Gloves are reasonable. Thin ones. You will want to touch the filaments, and you will want to feel the vibration in them with your fingers, so do not wear anything so thick that it deadens sensation.

Boots with grip. The scramble down from the ledge is not long but it is irregular, and the warm stone can be surprisingly slick where the surface is smooth. Approach shoes or light hiking boots. Nothing heavy. Remember: you are entering sideways. Everything on your body will press against the fissure walls as you pass through.

No metal. This is not a rule — there are no rules, there is no keeper, there is no gate — but it is a recommendation born of intuition. The filaments are conductive. They carry pulses. They are growing into the stone, stabilizing it, pinning reality down strand by strand. Introducing metal into this space — especially ferrous metal, especially anything that could act as an antenna or a bridge or an unintended node in the network — feels unwise. There is no evidence that it would cause harm. There is no evidence that it would not. The Bloomery is a system in progress, and you do not know what it is sensitive to. Carry your gear in fabric and plastic. If you must bring metal — a knife, a buckle, a stove component for the approach — keep it on your person, not in your hands, and do not set it on the floor near the gear-formations or the filament bases.

### What to Bring

**Water.** The Bloomery is warm, and you are at altitude, and the air is dry. You will dehydrate faster than you expect. Bring more than you think you need. The warmth is not hot — it is body-temperature, ambient, the warmth of an idling engine — but it is constant and inescapable and it does not cool at night because there is no night. There is only the flat, sourceless, concept-of-daytime light that illuminates the chamber without ever changing.

**Food, but minimal.** You will not be there long. The Bloomery does not invite extended stays. Not because it is dangerous — it is not dangerous, not in any immediate sense — but because the *wrongness* accumulates. The rippling walls. The breathing ceiling. The sense that the stone is only mostly committed to being stone. Most visitors will find that two to four hours is sufficient. Bring enough food for a day, in case your approach or return takes longer than expected, but do not plan to eat inside. There is something about the metabolic warmth of the chamber that makes the idea of your own metabolism feel redundant, intrusive, out of place. You can eat if you want to. You will probably not want to.

**A notebook and a pencil.** Not a pen — pens fail, pens leak, pens depend on gravity and pressure and the assumption that the physical world will continue behaving in predictable ways, and inside the Bloomery that assumption is under gentle, constant pressure. A pencil. Write down what you see. Write down what you feel. You will want a record, because afterward you will not be certain you remember it correctly. The Bloomery has a quality of *unrealness* that does not diminish with time spent inside — if anything, it increases — and you will emerge from the fissure into the cold thin air of the mountain slope and you will find that your memories of the chamber have a soft, uncertain quality, like a dream that was vivid in the moment and now refuses to hold detail. A notebook will help. You will read it later and you will not entirely recognize your own handwriting, and that is part of the experience.

**A watch or timer.** The Bloomery is timeless. The light does not change. The warmth does not fluctuate. The hum does not vary. There is no external cue for the passage of time, and the internal cues — the growth of the filaments, the slow rippling of the walls — operate on timescales that make human timekeeping feel quaint. You will lose track of how long you have been inside. This is not dangerous in itself, but it is disorienting, and if you have a schedule to keep — a portal to catch, a party to rejoin, a body that needs food and sleep on a rhythm — you should have a way to track the hours. Check your watch periodically. Do not trust your sense of time. It will be wrong.

**Nothing electronic, if you can help it.** This is the same intuition as the metal recommendation. The filaments are infrastructure. They are conducting something. You do not know what. You do not know if your phone, your radio, your headlamp, your GPS beacon is going to interact with the network in ways that are benign or otherwise. The light inside is flat and sourceless and sufficient — you will not need a headlamp. The space is small enough that you will not lose your bearings — you will not need GPS. If you carry a communication device for safety on the approach, turn it off before you enter the fissure. Turn it back on when you are out. This is not a rule. It is a respect. The Bloomery is building something. Do not introduce variables it has not accounted for.

**A small piece of stone or mineral from elsewhere.** This is not practical advice. This is ritual advice. Bring a pebble from a riverbed, a chip of granite from a trailhead, a small piece of somewhere-else. Hold it in your hand when you are inside the Bloomery. It will feel different from the basalt. It will feel *decided* — fully committed to being what it is, stable, real in a way the Bloomery's stone is not yet. It will help. It will give you something to compare against. When the walls ripple and you start to wonder if you are imagining it, the pebble in your pocket will remind you that there is a world outside this chamber where stone does not breathe and reality does not require maintenance. You are visiting that world's opposite. Hold a piece of it in your hand.

## Mental Preparation

### What to Expect From Your Senses

Your eyes will adjust. The light is flat and even and casts no shadows, which is deeply unnatural — you have never been in a space with no shadows, not even an operating theater, not even a snowfield under overcast — and your visual system will spend the first few minutes trying to find a light source, a gradient, a direction. It will fail. Accept this. The light comes from the concept of daytime, not from a lamp. You will see clearly. You will see everything in the chamber. You will just never find where the seeing comes from.

Your ears will not adjust. The hum is below the threshold of hearing but above the threshold of sensation. You will feel it in your teeth. You will feel it in your skull. It will not go away. It is the sound of the system working — the filaments conducting, the stone warming, the gears waiting, the process continuing. It is not loud. It is not painful. It is *present*, and it does not stop, and you cannot tune it out because it is not arriving through your ears. It is arriving through your bones. Some people find this unbearable. Some people find it meditative. Most people find it strange for the first hour and then stop noticing it, which is its own kind of strange, because the hum has not changed — you have simply decided to let it become part of your body's baseline. You have accepted the frequency. This is probably fine. This is probably not a problem. But notice it. Notice when you stop noticing. That transition is worth paying attention to.

Your skin will be confused. The stone is warm. The air is warm. Everything is the same temperature — body temperature, roughly, or close enough that the usual thermal cues your skin uses to navigate space are useless. You will not feel walls as walls. You will not feel the floor as floor. You will feel the texture — the vesicular basalt, the pocked rough surface — but the warmth will make everything feel *close*, *intimate*, as though the chamber is touching you rather than you touching it. This is not claustrophobia, but it is adjacent to it. It is the sensation of being inside something rather than being inside a space. There is a difference. You will feel it.

Your sense of balance may be affected. The floor feels provisional. The walls ripple. The ceiling breathes. None of this is enough to actually destabilize you — you will not fall, you will not stumble — but your vestibular system will register the *wrongness* and it will send low-level warning signals that manifest as mild nausea, mild unease, a slight feeling of being at sea. This comes and goes. It is worst when you look at the walls without focusing, when you let your peripheral vision take over and the rippling becomes more apparent. If you feel it strongly, look at the filaments. They are the most stable thing in the chamber. They do not ripple. They do not breathe. They hold. Let your eyes rest on them and your balance will steady.

### What to Expect From Your Mind

The Bloomery is not dangerous, but it is *de-stabilizing*. Not in a dramatic way. In a slow, quiet way. Like sitting in a room where the furniture is being rearranged by someone you cannot see, very slowly, one centimeter at a time, and you only notice when you reach for something and it is not quite where you left it.

Your thoughts will drift. Not because the space is boring — it is not boring, it is extraordinary, it is beautiful in the way that circuitry is beautiful, in the way that geology is beautiful — but because the space operates on a timescale that does not accommodate human thought. You will watch a filament for two minutes and see it grow a quarter of a millimeter, and your mind will try to extrapolate, to imagine the chamber finished, to imagine the gears turning, to imagine what the Bloomery *makes* — and you will fail, because the timescale is too long and the process is too incomplete and the answer is too far in the future. Your mind will reach for meaning and find only patience. This is frustrating at first. It becomes peaceful. It becomes the peace of sitting in a cathedral during its construction, knowing the stonemasons will finish long after you are dead, and the cathedral does not care, and the cathedral is beautiful already, and the beauty is in the becoming, not the being.

You may experience a kind of temporal vertigo. The Bloomery is under construction and always will be. This means it has always been under construction. This means it will always be under construction. This means the *now* you are visiting is not a moment — it is a *duration*, an indefinitely long stretch of time in which the process is happening, and your visit is not an event in the life of the Bloomery. It is a rounding error. You are a mayfly in a cathedral, and the cathedral is being built, and you will be dead before the first wall is finished. This is not a sad thought. It is a clarifying one. It is the kind of thought that makes you aware of your own scale, your own tempo, your own frantic little heartbeat ticking against the geological patience of the filaments and the stone. Some travelers find this liberating. Some find it terrifying. Most find it both, and the both-ness is the real experience of the Bloomery: the sense of being insignificant and free at the same time, of being part of a process that does not need you and does not exclude you and does not care whether you watch or not.

Stay with that feeling. Do not fight it. The Bloomery is not here for you. It is here for itself. And *here* is provisional.

### What to Expect From the Reality

This is the hardest thing to prepare for, because it is the hardest thing to describe.

The walls ripple. Not much. Not enough to be certain. The ceiling breathes. Not much. Not enough to measure. The floor feels provisional. Not unstable. Not hollow. Just... *undecided*. The stone is only mostly committed to being stone. The space is only mostly committed to being a space. The filaments are pinning it down — strand by strand, circuit by circuit — but they are not finished, and until they are, the Bloomery exists in a state of becoming that is not quite *being*.

You will feel this. Not as a hallucination, not as a vision, but as a quality of *wrongness* that accumulates. The first hour, you will be fascinated. The second hour, you will be unsettled. The third hour, if you stay that long, you will begin to feel that the chamber is *looking at you*, not with eyes, not with intent, but with the diffuse, non-specific awareness of a space that is not sure it is a space yet and is therefore paying attention to everything inside it, including you. This is almost certainly projection. Almost certainly. But the Bloomery is a place where *almost certainly* is not as comforting as it usually is, because the usual certainties — stone is stone, space is space, reality holds — are under construction here, and construction means the walls are not yet walls, and the floor is not yet floor, and you are standing in a maybe.

When this feeling becomes too strong, leave. This is not a failure. This is not weakness. The Bloomery is not a challenge to be endured. It is a place to be visited briefly and remembered slowly, and the memory will be more valuable than the endurance. Go out through the fissure. Stand on the slope. Breathe the cold, thin, *decided* air. The mountain is real. The sky is real. The wind is real. You are real. The Bloomery will continue without you, as it was continuing before you, as it will continue after you, and that is not a rejection. That is the nature of the place.

## Practical Sequence

**Approach:** Allow a full day. The altitude requires acclimatization. The fissure is not marked. You will need to locate it by the description alone — a crack in the underside of a basalt shelf, barely wide enough for shoulders. If you cannot find it, you are not missing something. It is not a place that wants to be found. It is a place that *is*, and finding it is your problem, not its.

**Entry:** Strip to your shell layer. Remove anything bulky from your pack. Turn sideways. The fissure is warm even before you are fully inside — you will feel the transition in the stone under your hand, the basalt going from cold to warm as you push through. This is your first signal that you have arrived. Squeeze. Do not rush. The fissure is not long, but it is tight, and the rock is rough, and you will scrape. When you come through, you will be standing on the ledge. Take a moment. Breathe. Look.

**Inside:** Scramble down from the ledge to the floor. Touch the filaments — gently, with fingertips. Feel the pulse. Watch a single branch for as long as your patience allows. Notice the growth. Walk the floor. Look at the gear-formations. Try to understand what they are. Fail. This is normal. Sit on the ledge. Write in your notebook. Check your watch. Stay as long as the wrongness is interesting. Leave when it becomes unsettling.

**Exit:** The fissure is the same. Sideways. Push through. The cold air outside will hit you like a reset. Stand. Breathe. You are out. The Bloomery is behind you, still warm, still growing, still building, still maybe.

---

## Final Note

Do not take anything from the Bloomery. Not a piece of filament. Not a chip of basalt. Not a photograph, if you were somehow able to take one, which you probably were not, because the light does not behave and the space does not hold still and the filaments are infrastructure and the gears are mechanism and the stone is chassis and all of it is part of a system that is stabilizing a reality that does not need your souvenirs. Leave it all. Take only your notebook and your memory and the feeling of the hum in your teeth.

And when you are out, and the cold thin air is real in a way the warm air inside was not, and the mountain under your feet is decided in a way the chamber floor was not, and the sky above you is actual in a way the sourceless light was not — hold the pebble from somewhere-else in your hand and be glad that reality, where you come from, is finished. That it holds. That the stone is stone and the space is space and you do not have to wait for it to decide.

The Bloomery is under construction, and it always will be. You are not. You are here, and you are real, and you are done.

That is enough.
