# Preparing for the Ascendant Quarter

## A Practical Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. Physical Preparation: The Body's Complaint

**Altitude is your first enemy.** The Quarter sits above the cloud line at a height that kills the unprepared. Your lungs will not understand why the air has abandoned them. Your blood will thicken with desperation, producing more red cells in a frantic attempt to catch what oxygen remains. This takes time— days, sometimes weeks— and you will spend those days gasping, dizzy, waking at night with your heart slamming against your ribs because your body believes it is dying.

*Preparation:* Begin acetazolamide two days before ascent. Drink water until you feel sick, then drink more. Accept that you will be useless for your first week. Plan for this. Do not schedule important meetings or demanding physical tasks. Your body is learning to breathe again; give it permission to fail.

**The cold is the second enemy.** It is a high, thin cold that comes through your clothing like the fabric isn't there. The perpetual dimness means the sun provides no warmth, and the cloud layer below reflects cold upward. The factories produce heat, but it is a localized, industrial heat, and the streets between them are ice.

*Preparation:* Layers. Not expensive technical gear but honest wool and cotton, the kind that retains warmth even when damp. You will be damp often— the condensation, the seepage, the rain that falls twice weekly. Bring gloves. The wind cuts.

**The ground is your third enemy.** Not because it will kill you— it moves slowly, the text says— but because it will not hold still. The subsidence, the shifting, the ongoing geological indecision: your ankles will not forgive you.

*Preparation:* Boots. Not fashionable boots, not comfortable boots. Boots that grip. Boots that support. Boots with ankles that lock. Break them in before you arrive. Your feet will swell at altitude; size up.

---

## II. Mental Preparation: The Mind's Unsettling

**You must understand what you are walking into.** The Quarter is not a normal city built on unstable ground. It is a city built on a place where *reality itself has not finished deciding what it wants to be*. This is not metaphor. This is ontological fact. The mountain is still becoming. The rock is still choosing. And you will feel this choice happening beneath your feet.

*Preparation:* Practice tolerance for ambiguity. This sounds abstract, but it is not. You will walk down a street and later not be certain whether you walked down it. You will see a building and for a moment see a different building— older, younger, almost-the-same. Your mind will try to resolve these contradictions. It will fail. You must learn to not resolve them. To let the uncertainty exist. To say: I am in a place that has not finished being, and I will not force it to be finished.

**The time-sickness is real.** Events will pile up rather than pass. A conversation will feel like it is still happening days later. This is not memory. This is the peculiar thickening of time at altitude, in a place where the future is already present in the stone, pressing outward.

*Preparation:* Keep a journal. Write everything down. Date every entry. Use a physical notebook, not a device— electronics behave strangely here, and there is something about handwriting that helps anchor you in linear time. When you are uncertain whether something happened yesterday or today, consult the book. Trust the book more than your memory. Your memory is not reliable here. It is not *you* that is unreliable; it is time itself.

**The sounds will exhaust you.** The description calls it "loud in the way that a city built inside a resonating cavity is loud." Every surface reflects. Every footstep returns. The rock sings. The machines grind. The birds shriek in frequencies that match the mountain's drone. You will not find silence. Silence does not exist here.

*Preparation:* Earplugs help, but they do not solve. The sound comes through your bones as much as your ears. You will sleep poorly. Accept this. Bring a sleep mask as well— the streetlamps burn at all hours, the factories never stop, and the glow from the insects and the clouds below will ensure your room is never truly dark.

## III. What to Bring

### Essential Equipment

**A good walking stick.** Not a collapsible hiking pole but something solid, something that can bear your full weight. The ground shifts. The tram tracks have been re-leveled so many times they form uneven stairs. The eastern quarter has dropped two meters; there are places where the street simply ends in a small cliff. You will need to test every surface before committing your weight.

**A pressure cooker.** The altitude changes cooking. Water boils at temperatures too low to properly cook food. The local tubers— those rain-flavored growths from the seepage zones— *must* be thoroughly cooked. The locals have pressure cookers. You should too.

**Goggles or eye protection.** The air is full of particulates— factory smoke, crystal dust from the stamp mills, spores from the spiral-moss. Your eyes will burn constantly without protection. Get ones with clear lenses; the perpetual dimness means tinted lenses will leave you blind.

**A local guide for the first month.** This is not optional. The grid has bent. The streets that made sense forty years ago now curve and dead-end in ways no map captures. The soft spots are not marked; locals learn them by walking, by the particular wrongness in the air. A guide will keep you from the streets where reality thins. A guide will know which buildings are still attached to bedrock.

**Cash.** Not credit, not universal exchange credits, not promises. Cash. The Quarter runs on cash— specifically, the ingots of stabilized matter that the factories produce. Everything else can be traded, but cash is easiest. Bring enough for three months. You may stay longer.

### What Not to Bring

**Expectations.** You will arrive expecting a city and find a process. You will expect solid ground and find something that is still deciding. You will expect answers and find only more fruitful questions.

**Electronics you cannot afford to lose.** The crystals here dissolve in lowland air, but the *unstable* minerals affect everything they touch. Devices fail unpredictably. Data corrupts. The cloud layer below means satellite communication is impossible. You will be isolated.

**A rigid itinerary.** The Quarter does not run on schedule. The cable railway operates when it operates. The streets shift. The ground subsides. Your plans will not survive contact with the mountain's indecision. Bring plans, but hold them loosely.

## IV. What to Expect

### The First Week

You will spend your first week suffering. The altitude will compress your lungs. The cold will find every gap in your clothing. The noise will prevent sleep. You will question why you came.

*This is normal.*

Find lodging in the western quarter— the ground is more stable there, the buildings newer, the soft spots fewer. Accept that you will not be productive. Drink water. Eat the local tubers when you can stomach them; they grow on geological energy and will help your body adapt to geological strangeness. Walk only when necessary. Watch the mountain from your window. Notice how the profile changes over days. This is the only activity worth attempting.

### The Second Week

Your body will begin to accept the altitude. Your mind will not accept the reality. You will walk outside and feel the ground *yield* slightly, as though the stone is considering being something else. You will see a building lean and then, looking away and back, see it leaning slightly differently. You will not know if it changed or if your perception changed.

*This is also normal.*

Begin walking with a guide. Learn the streets that bend. Ask about the soft spots— the locations people avoid, where reality is thinner. Your guide will be reluctant to discuss them. This is a good sign. The people who discuss soft spots too openly are either newcomers who don't understand or residents who have been too affected. Neither group should be trusted.

### The First Month

You will notice the plants. They grow too fast. They grow in spirals. They grow toward sounds you cannot hear. You will watch a fern unfold in real time, a new frond extending over the course of an hour, and you will feel uneasy in a way you cannot articulate.

You will be offered spiral-moss to smoke. The locals will tell you it produces a mild euphoria and a sense of the mountain's future shape. It is technically illegal. It is sold in every market. You will try it because you are here to experience, and you will spend an afternoon weeping because you felt the mountain *becoming* and it was beautiful and you cannot explain this to anyone who was not there.

*This is also normal, though less commonly discussed.*

### The Long Term

If you stay longer than a season, you will find yourself changing. You will develop a tolerance for ambiguity that your past self could not imagine. You will stop expecting the ground to hold still. You will start listening for the mountain's song at dusk, and when you hear it, you will feel it in your teeth.

You may not want to leave. Many don't. The text mentions the botanist who came to study and stayed to tend her greenhouse, refusing to let anyone examine her specimens. This is common. People come to extract— minerals, knowledge, experience— and instead are extracted *from*. The mountain takes them in. They become part of its ongoing decision.

Ask yourself, before you arrive: what would it mean to stay? What would it mean to love a place that has not finished being?

## V. Dangers and How to Survive Them

### Physical Dangers

**The subsidence is slow but real.** Buildings do fall. The eastern quarter drops by centimeters per year; some buildings have already collapsed into each other. Avoid structures that lean too dramatically. The municipal engineers post warnings, but the warnings are in a code of colored flags that outsiders do not know. Ask. Learn the flags.

**The cable railway is safer than it looks.** The cables hum with tension, the gears grind day and night, but the system has operated for decades. The danger is not the machinery but the altitude— standing on the platforms at the terminus, above the cloud line, the wind can push you. Stay away from edges. Hold the rails.

**The animals are not aggressive but they are not afraid.** The goat-like creatures will stand in your path and watch you with eyes that seem to know something. Do not provoke them. They are larger than they should be. Go around. Wait. They will move eventually. The birds are more concerning— the flocks that move as single organisms, the unclassifiable soarers with too many feathers. They do not attack, but their density means you will be surrounded, and their calls can be disorienting. Stay calm. Walk slowly.

**The larger shapes above the terminus are a real danger.** The surveyors see them moving in the mist. They do not come down into the city. *Yet.* Do not go above the cable railway's highest point without serious preparation and local guidance. No one knows what those shapes are. No one wants to find out.

### Ontological Dangers

**The soft spots are the real risk.** A soft spot is a location where reality is thinner, where the mountain's indecision creates ambiguity about what is. Walking through one can leave you uncertain whether you walked through it or only thought about walking through it very convincingly.

This sounds like a philosophical problem. It is not. It is practical. You need to know whether you were somewhere. You need to know whether something happened. In soft spots, you cannot know. The experience accumulates. After enough soft spots, you may begin to doubt all your experiences. You may become unable to distinguish memory from imagination. This is a form of madness that the locals recognize. They have institutions for it, low buildings in stable areas where people who have been too affected are cared for.

*How to avoid soft spots:* Locals avoid certain streets, certain alleys, certain buildings. They will not explain why, but they will warn you. "Not that way." "The other route is better." "There's nothing you need down there." Trust these warnings. Do not investigate. The mountain's indecision is not your puzzle to solve.

**The crystals are beautiful and they will kill you if mishandled.** The minerals extracted here dissolve in lowland air. The stamp mills process them while they still hold shape. Touching raw crystal with bare skin can leave residue that, when you descend to lower altitude, will begin to break down on your body. The locals know this. They handle everything with gloves. They process everything before it leaves. You should too.

## VI. What to Eat and Drink

**The local food is strange but nourishing.** The tubers from the seepage zones taste like rain on hot stone— a description that seems impossible until you taste it, and then it seems obvious. Boiled thoroughly (use your pressure cooker), they provide a neutral base for any seasoning. The locals eat them at every meal. You should too; they settle the stomach and help with altitude adaptation.

**The spiral-moss is illegal but ubiquitous.** Smoked, it produces mild euphoria and topographic premonitions. This is not a metaphor. You will feel the shape of the mountain as it is becoming. You will know, briefly, where the ridges will be in a thousand years. This knowledge fades, but the sense of having known it remains. Some travelers find this addictive. Some find it terrifying. Some find it both.

**The water is safe but tastes of stone.** It comes from the seepage, the condensation, the twice-weekly rain. It has been filtered through rock that has not finished being rock. It tastes of potential. This is fine. It will not hurt you. It may even help.

**Do not eat anything you cannot identify.** The plants here respond to forces other than sunlight and water. Some grow in spirals; some grow toward sound; some grow in ways that have no clear pattern. The locals have learned which are safe, but you have not. Ask before eating anything. The botanist in the greenhouse on the eastern slope may be your best resource— if she is willing to speak to you.

## VII. What to Bring Home

**Not raw crystals.** They dissolve. The residue on your skin will dissolve into you. This is not a souvenir you want.

**The stabilized ingots, if you can afford them.** These are what the factories produce— matter that has been processed into certainty. They cannot be made elsewhere. They enable construction and engineering that should not be possible. They are valuable beyond measure in the lowlands. If you can obtain one, legally, you may fund your travels for years.

**Stories.** This is what most travelers bring home. The story of the mountain that sings. The story of the plants that grow toward a future only they can perceive. The story of walking down a street and not knowing, afterward, if you walked or dreamed.

**A sense of your own instability.** You will return changed. You will have spent time in a place that has not finished being, and some of that not-finishedness will have rubbed off on you. You will look at solid things and know they could be otherwise. You will feel time piling up instead of passing. You will hear, sometimes, in quiet moments, a low resonant drone that reminds you of the mountain singing at dusk.

This is not damage. This is expansion.

You came to the Ascendant Quarter to experience something real. What you experienced was that reality itself is not finished. That the world is still deciding what it wants to be. That you, too, are still deciding.

The mountain knows this. It has always known. It is patient. It will wait for you to understand.

---

## VIII. Final Preparations

Pack light. Pack warm. Bring a journal and write everything down.

Say goodbye to certainty before you arrive. It will not survive the ascent.

When you feel the ground shift beneath your feet— and you will— do not panic. The mountain is not falling. It is becoming. You are present for a process that usually takes millennia, compressed into human time, accessible to human perception.

This is a gift.

You are being allowed to watch the universe make up its mind.

Pay attention.
