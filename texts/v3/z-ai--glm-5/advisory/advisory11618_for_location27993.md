# Preparing for the Black Plateau of Ymurra

## A Practical Guide for the Human Traveler

---

## I. Understanding What You Are Entering

Before you pack a single item, you must understand something fundamental: you are about to enter a world where your primary sense—vision—has no function. This is not darkness as you have experienced it. It is not the darkness of a moonless night, nor the darkness of a cave with your lamp extinguished. It is an *absolute* blackness, so complete that after hours within it, you will begin to doubt that vision was ever possible. You will press your hand before your face and feel your eyelashes brush against your palm before you perceive any difference between open and closed eyes.

Ymurra has never known light. Its ecology evolved over millions of years in complete absence of illumination. The creatures here have no concept of "seeing"—the acoustic fauna carry only a mythic memory of it, passed down in vibratory languages you will not understand. You will be a visitor to a world where your body is, in some fundamental way, a malformed thing—adapted for a sense that does not exist there, equipped with eyes that will serve only to betray you with phantom images and desperate hallucinations.

But you are human. You are adaptable. And with proper preparation, you can experience Ymurra not as a nightmare of deprivation, but as an immersion into a sensory realm that your species has never fully inhabited—the world of pure sound.

---

## II. The Physical Challenges

### The Wet

Ymurra is saturated. The atmosphere is warm and thick with water vapor, heated from below by geothermal networks that pulse through the stone like a great slow heart. The condensation is constant—it forms on any surface cooler than the surrounding air.

And you will be cooler than the surrounding air.

Your body temperature, maintained at approximately 37°C, will be lower than the ambient warmth that rises from Ymurra's heated bones. This means that within moments of arriving, you will begin to collect condensation on every exposed surface. Your skin will bead with water that seems to materialize from nothing. Your clothing—unless carefully chosen—will become sodden. The water does not fall from above; it *emerges* onto you, weeping from the air itself.

This constant wetness will infiltrate everything. Your gear, your provisions, your skin, your orifices. The moisture carries minerals from the stone, bacterial films from the vegetation, chemical signatures from the countless organisms that have touched it before you. You will be drinking the plateau with every breath, absorbing it through your skin, tasting it constantly.

**Preparation:**
- Accept that you will be wet. Do not fight it with waterproofing—you cannot. The water comes from the air, not from without. Instead, choose materials that function while wet, that drain quickly, that do not degrade or chafe when saturated.
- Your skin will need protection, not from the water itself, but from the prolonged exposure. Without intervention, you will develop maceration—the softening and breakdown of skin from extended wetness—within days. Bring barrier films, waterproof bandages, and drying agents for vulnerable areas. The creases of your joints, the spaces between your fingers and toes, your groin and underarms—these will be the first to suffer.
- Consider the bacteria and microorganisms that live in Ymurra's waters. Your immune system has no memory of them. You will need filtration for any water you drink, even though you will be constantly touching and absorbing the local moisture through your skin. The mineral content is high—some springs carry dissolved compounds that your kidneys will struggle to process. Bring chemical neutralizers and broad-spectrum antimicrobials.

### The Warmth

The temperature is warm but not scorching—perhaps 28-32°C in most areas, higher near thermal vents and active pump systems. This is comfortable for brief exposure but exhausting over extended periods. Combined with the near-100% humidity, it creates an environment where your body cannot cool itself through evaporation. Sweat will not dry. It will simply add to the moisture already coating your skin.

**Preparation:**
- You will need to manage your body heat carefully. Insulation is counterproductive—you need ventilation, not warmth. But ventilation means exposure to the wet. Choose garments that create a thin air barrier between your skin and the environment, materials that wick moisture outward while preventing the constant condensation from reaching your skin directly.
- Bring cooling systems if your technology permits. Simple evaporative cooling will not function here—there is no dry air to evaporate into. You will need active cooling: thermal exchange units, chemical cooling packs, or systems that exploit the temperature differential between the warm air and the cooler water in the streams and reservoirs.
- Monitor yourself for heat exhaustion. The symptoms will be harder to detect when you are already wet, already uncomfortable, already deprived of your visual reference for your own body's condition. You will need to track your intake and output carefully—your water loss through sweat is invisible when everything is wet.

### The Terrain

The ground beneath your feet is not solid. It squelches. The water table is so close to the surface that you are essentially walking on a saturated sponge, a thick mat of low-growing moisture-trapper plants that overlap like scales, releasing water with every step. In the lower channels, the water rises openly, forming slow-moving streams that wind between vegetation thickets.

The larger vegetation—the sang-tubes, vascular giants rising up to forty meters—creates a forest without sight lines, a vertical domain where you can become disoriented within meters of your starting point. Their surfaces are slick with condensation and bacterial films, difficult to grip, unpleasant to touch.

**Preparation:**
- Your footwear must provide stability on uncertain ground. The soggy surface will try to pull your feet down with each step, and the water will lubricate every surface. You need aggressive tread, secure ankle support, and quick-release mechanisms—if you step into a channel deeper than expected, you may need to free yourself instantly.
- Consider whether you will walk at all. The terrain is difficult, the navigation nearly impossible, the footing treacherous. Alternative modes of movement—climbing harnesses for the sang-tube clusters, flotation devices for the water channels, suspended systems that keep you above the ground-level obstacles—may serve you better.
- But any system you bring must function without visual reference. You will not be able to *see* where you are attaching a harness or placing a foot. Tactile and acoustic feedback will be your only guides.

## III. The Sensory Challenge: Sound

This is the true test of your preparation. The sound on Ymurra is *tremendous*.

It begins with the water—the weeping of the stone faces, the rushing of countless springs, the hissing of narrow high-pressure jets, the flutelike whistling of water finding channels through stone. The waterfalls that plunge over the plateau's edge create deep booms that echo off the cloud sea below, returning as distorted reflections seconds later, out of phase with their sources.

Then the machines—the great pumps and condensers that run on thermal differentials, massive droning things that have operated for centuries. Some maintain steady rhythms; others have fallen into arrhythmic states, creating unpredictable clanking, grinding, mechanical wheezing. The sounds travel through the stone itself, transmitted as vibrations you will feel through your feet and body.

Then the life—the clicks, chirps, resonant calls, and infrasonic pulses of the acoustic fauna. The low-frequency rumbles of the riph herds. The rapid triangulation clicks of the striiders. The territorial songs of the glider-weavers, carrying across kilometers. The vibrations transmitted through water, through stone, through the air itself.

For Ymurra's inhabitants, this is a language—a continuous stream of information that carries the shape of the landscape, the location of resources and threats, the movement of air and water, the presence of other creatures. They do not experience it as noise. They experience it as *meaning*.

But you are not adapted to this. To you, it will be a wall of sound, a sensory overload that makes thought difficult, that demands attention simply to parse. You will struggle to separate one sound from another, to identify what is nearby and what is distant, to distinguish between the harmless drone of a pump and the hunting click of a predator.

**Preparation:**

### Training Your Ears

Before you arrive, you must train your auditory processing to handle complexity. Begin with recordings—layered soundscapes of increasing density, multiple simultaneous audio streams, frequencies across the spectrum. Practice identifying individual sounds within complex acoustic environments. Practice tracking movement through sound alone—have companions move around you in darkness while you attempt to locate them by hearing.

Learn active listening—the deliberate focusing of attention on specific sound sources while filtering others. This is a skill that can be developed, though most humans never need it to the degree that Ymurra will demand.

Consider learning the basics of echolocation. Some blind humans have developed this capacity to remarkable degrees, using mouth-clicks or tapping sounds to create mental images of their surroundings from the echoes. You will not match the precision of Ymurra's acoustic specialists, but even rudimentary echolocation will provide more spatial information than your useless eyes.

### Sound Equipment

Bring sophisticated audio processing equipment. You will need:

- **Directional microphones** that can isolate specific sound sources, helping you separate the near from the far, the relevant from the ambient.
- **Frequency filters** that can attenuate the constant drone of water and machinery, allowing you to focus on the biological sounds that indicate creature presence.
- **Audio mapping systems** that can take multiple microphone inputs and create a spatial representation of the sound environment—a way to "see" through sound.
- **Infrasonic detectors** for the low-frequency rumbles and ground-transmitted vibrations that human ears perceive poorly or not at all.
- **Bone conduction interfaces** that transmit sound through your skull directly, bypassing the air and allowing you to hear vibrations transmitted through solid surfaces you are touching.

But do not rely on this equipment exclusively. Batteries fail. Electronics degrade in high humidity. You must be able to function without technological assistance if necessary.

### Creating Sound

You will need to make sound deliberately—to navigate, to communicate, to signal. But every sound you make will be *heard*.

The creatures of Ymurra have evolved to detect and locate sounds with extraordinary precision. The striiders can locate prey in absolute darkness by the slightest sound—the rustle of a footstep, the catch of a breath. You cannot move silently enough to avoid detection by acoustic specialists. You must instead control *what* you sound like.

**Preparation:**
- Develop a repertoire of sounds that may help you. Study the acoustic ecology—learn which sounds indicate predators, which indicate prey, which are territorial markers, which are mating calls. You may be able to use certain sounds to signal that you are not prey, or to mask your presence with other sounds.
- Bring sound generators that can produce specific frequencies—distraction signals, masking noise, perhaps even communicative tones if you can learn the rudiments of the local acoustic languages.
- Understand that you will be broadcasting your presence constantly. The question is not whether you will be heard, but *what* will hear you, and how it will interpret what it hears.

## IV. Predator Awareness

You will be prey on Ymurra.

This is not a comfortable thought, but it is essential. You are entering an ecosystem where you do not know the rules, where you cannot see the threats, where your every movement betrays your position to hunters who have evolved over millions of years to kill in absolute darkness.

### The Striiders

The primary threat to humans will be the striiders—bipedal predators that hunt by sound triangulation. Their auditory organs cluster in depressions along their sleek skulls like acoustic dishes. They can locate prey by the slightest sound—a heartbeat, a breath, a footfall on the soggy ground.

They do not hunt in packs, but they tolerate each other's presence in loose territorial networks, sharing information about prey movements through click-languages. If one striider detects you, others may be informed.

**Preparation:**
- Learn the sound of striider clicks—the rapid triangulation patterns they use to communicate. If you hear these sounds, you are being hunted, or at least observed.
- Understand that you cannot move silently enough to evade them. Your best strategy is to make yourself *unappealing* as prey, or to stay in areas where striiders do not hunt—near the active pump-stations, where the noise interferes with their acoustic hunting.
- Consider defensive sound systems—loud, disorienting bursts that might confuse their triangulation, sounds that mimic larger or more dangerous creatures. But understand that any defensive sound will also attract attention from other creatures.
- Your body has no natural defenses against a striider. If they catch you, you will not see them coming. You will not outrun them in terrain they know and you do not. Your only advantage is your technology and your preparation—knowing they exist, knowing how they hunt, having strategies to avoid being targeted.

### Other Threats

The cloud sea below the plateau holds different predators—larger, less specialized, operating on different frequencies. If you approach Ymurra's perimeter, you may encounter creatures that climb from below, following resources, responding to seasonal patterns.

The water holds its own dangers. The vithra—amphibious acoustic specialists—are not predatory toward creatures your size, but the streams and channels may hold other things. And the water itself, saturated with minerals and microorganisms, poses health risks.

The vegetation can be treacherous. Some of the epiphytic climbers have tendrils that "taste" the air for chemical signatures—some may be carnivorous, adapted to trap and digest insects or small creatures. The fungus-analogs that glow with faint bioluminescence may produce chemicals toxic to human physiology.

**Preparation:**
- Bring comprehensive first-aid and trauma supplies. You will need wound care that functions in wet conditions, antivenins and antitoxins if you can determine what local threats produce them, and systems for calling for help if you are injured and cannot move.
- Consider armor—lightweight, flexible, resistant to piercing and cutting. It will not protect you from everything, but it may provide the margin between a glancing blow and a fatal strike.
- Establish protocols for sleeping and resting. You cannot simply lie down in Ymurra—you will be wet, you will be vulnerable, you will be broadcasting your unconscious presence to every predator within acoustic range. You will need enclosed shelter, acoustic dampening, possibly guard systems.

## V. Navigation in Absolute Darkness

This is perhaps your greatest challenge. You will arrive on a vast plateau with no visual reference points—no horizon, no sun, no stars, no landmarks you can see. The vegetation is dense, the terrain variable, the sounds disorienting. You will have no instinctive sense of which way is forward.

The inhabitants of Ymurra navigate by sound, reading the landscape through its acoustic signature—the sounds of water, the drones of machines, the calls of territorial creatures. They build mental maps from vibration and frequency. You will need to do the same.

**Preparation:**

### Dead Reckoning Systems

Bring inertial navigation systems that track your movement and maintain a position estimate. These systems—based on accelerometers, gyroscopes, and magnetic sensors—can track your path through space without external references, counting your steps, measuring your turns, maintaining a bearing even when you cannot see where you are going.

But these systems drift. Over time, errors accumulate, and your estimated position diverges from your true position. You will need to calibrate against known locations when possible—finding the ancient structures, the pump-stations, the streams—and updating your position estimate.

### Acoustic Landmarks

You will need to learn Ymurra's acoustic geography. The machines provide consistent sound sources—the droning of active pumps, the clanking of partially-functioning systems, the steady rhythm of condensers. These are landmarks in the dark, reference points you can navigate by if you learn to identify and locate them.

The water features have characteristic sounds—the hissing of narrow springs, the fluting of sheet-falls, the booming of waterfalls plunging into the cloud sea. Each can serve as a beacon if you learn its signature.

**Preparation:**
- Before traveling far, spend time in one location learning the local soundscape. Map the sounds around you—identify their sources if possible, their directions, their approximate distances. Build a mental acoustic map before you attempt to move through the space.
- Bring sound-recording equipment and the ability to play back recordings. You may need to compare what you hear now to what you heard earlier, to recognize a sound you have encountered before, to identify a landmark you are approaching.
- Consider marking your own path with sound—placing acoustic beacons at intervals, devices that emit distinctive sounds you can locate and return to. But understand that these beacons will also be audible to every creature in the area.

### The Terrain Itself

The ground under your feet will tell you something. The squelch of the moisture-trapper mat differs from the firmer stone near the plateau's edge. The sound of your footsteps changes when you approach water, when you move from one vegetation zone to another, when you climb or descend.

Learn to read these subtle cues. The inhabitants of Ymurra do—they know where they are by the feel of the ground, the sound of their own movement, the way sounds reflect from nearby surfaces.

## VI. Psychological Preparation

The psychological challenge of Ymurra should not be underestimated.

### The Absence of Sight

You have never experienced true, absolute, endless darkness. Even in the darkest night you have known, there has been *something*—moonlight, starlight, distant artificial illumination, the glow of your own technology. Ymurra offers none of this. The sky is a black void, a ceiling of absence. There is no dawn, no stars, no light at all except the faintest waste-glow of fungus-analogs, too dim to provide meaningful illumination.

The human mind rebels against this. Vision is not merely a sense; it is the primary way you construct your understanding of the world. You think in space and distance and relationship—all visual metaphors. Without sight, you will feel unmoored, disconnected, uncertain of your own existence.

Hallucinations are common in prolonged darkness. The visual cortex, starved for input, begins to generate its own—phantom lights, imagined shapes, entire landscapes that flicker into perception and vanish. These are not signs of mental illness; they are the normal response of a brain adapted for vision, struggling to function without its primary data source.

**Preparation:**
- Train yourself in darkness tolerance before arriving. Spend extended periods in absolute blackness—hours at first, then days. Learn how your mind responds. Develop techniques for maintaining orientation and calm when you cannot see.
- Consider whether you will bring any light source. A lamp would allow you to see your immediate surroundings, to have visual reference for your own body and gear. But light on Ymurra is *foreign*—a harmful radiation that no organism has needed to defend against. You do not know how the local life will respond to illumination. It may attract creatures. It may damage organisms not adapted to protect themselves from light. It may be the equivalent of setting off a flare in a dark room full of predators.
- If you bring light, use it sparingly, and understand that you are altering the environment you came to observe. Red light may be less disruptive than full-spectrum illumination, but there is no guarantee—the creatures here have no concept of light, no reason to distinguish between frequencies of something that simply does not exist in their world.

### The Absence of Quiet

The sound never stops. Ymurra is never quiet. For its inhabitants, this is normal—sound is information, presence, life. But for you, it will be noise, an overwhelming constant input that you cannot escape.

Some travelers in similar environments report a particular kind of exhaustion—the exhaustion of sensory bombardment, of the brain working constantly to process input that never ceases. You may find yourself craving silence, straining to find moments when the sound diminishes, desperate for rest from the roar.

**Preparation:**
- Bring sound-dampening equipment—earmuffs, noise-canceling systems, earplugs. You will need periods of reduced acoustic input, times when you can let your auditory processing rest. But understand that wearing sound protection also blocks the sounds you *need* to hear—the approach of predators, the calls that indicate danger, the acoustic cues that tell you where you are.
- Establish protocols for safe rest—times when you are in a protected location, when you can reduce your acoustic vigilance, when you can afford to dampen the sound and let your mind recover.

### The Absence of Orientation

On Ymurra, there is no horizon. The sky is a black ceiling. The ground is a wet unknown that extends beyond your perception. You will have no visual reference for "up" and "down" except gravity itself, no sense of the space that surrounds you except what you can reach and hear.

This creates a particular kind of vertigo—a spatial disorientation that persists even when you are standing still, even when you are lying down. You may feel that you are falling, or that the ground is tilting, or that you have lost all sense of your body's position in space.

**Preparation:**
- Develop your proprioceptive sense—your awareness of your body's position and movement independent of vision. Practice moving blind, learn to trust your internal sense of where your limbs are, where balance lies.
- Create tactile reference points—ways to touch and feel the space around you, to build a mental model of your immediate surroundings through physical contact.
- Use sound for orientation—learn to judge distance and direction by acoustic cues, to feel the space around you as a shape made of sound.

## VII. The Industrial Systems: Opportunity and Hazard

The ancient machines are still running. Ymurra's infrastructure—its condensers, pumps, channels, and reservoirs—has operated continuously for perhaps millions of years, maintained by automated systems that crawl along condensation surfaces, clear debris, seal cracks, adjust flows.

These systems present both opportunity and hazard.

### Opportunity

The infrastructure creates resources. The collection reservoirs hold water—mineral-rich, biologically active, but filtered through the ancient systems. The channels provide clear paths through the dense vegetation. The structures at channel intersections offer shelter—weathered buildings, partially functional, rare points of visual interest with their faint indicator lights.

The machines have rhythms, patterns you can learn and use. The riph herds migrate in response to pump cycles; the striiders hunt in the gaps between machine-clatter. You can incorporate these patterns into your own behavior, using the industrial rhythms as timing for movement and rest.

**Preparation:**
- Study the machine systems before attempting to interact with them. Understand what they do—harvest water from the air, distribute it through the plateau, maintain the thermal-exchange processes that power everything.
- Approach the functioning structures with caution but without fear. The machines are not intelligent, but they are adaptive, capable of responding to changing conditions. The sounds they make may serve as passwords or triggers—acoustic signals that cause systems to respond.

### Hazard

The machines do not know you are there. They are not designed to avoid harming creatures in their operational areas. The maintenance crawlers that clear debris will not distinguish between a blockage and your body. The pumps that move water create forces that could trap and drown. The condensation surfaces are designed to collect moisture—you may find yourself collected along with it.

The electromagnetic fields generated by the functioning systems are disruptive to the sensory organs of local fauna. They avoid active installations for this reason. These same fields may interfere with your own equipment—your navigation systems, your communications, your electronic devices.

**Preparation:**
- Learn to recognize the sounds of active systems—the droning of pumps, the clicking of maintenance cycles, the vibration of thermal exchanges. Approach these sounds with caution.
- Do not touch the machines until you understand their function and current state. Some surfaces may be hot, others moving, others chemically active.
- Keep your distance from the repair systems. They are not aggressive, but they are relentless, designed to clear obstacles and maintain function. If you become an obstacle, they will clear you.

## VIII. Health Considerations

### The Water

You will need to drink. Ymurra offers water in abundance—condensation, springs, streams, reservoirs. But the water is not pure. It carries minerals dissolved from the stone, microorganisms from the ecology, chemical compounds from the biological activity of millions of years.

**Preparation:**
- Bring filtration and purification systems designed for high mineral content and biological load. Simple filtration will not remove dissolved compounds; you may need distillation or chemical treatment.
- Test any water source before drinking from it regularly. The mineral content varies between springs—some may carry concentrations your kidneys cannot process, metals that will accumulate to toxic levels, compounds that will interfere with your body's chemistry.
- Maintain strict protocols for water handling. Even filtered water can be re-contaminated by contact with local surfaces, by the condensation that forms on your containers, by the air itself.

### The Microorganisms

Ymurra has an ecosystem, and ecosystems are full of organisms that live on and in other organisms. The local bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms have evolved for millions of years in conditions unlike any on your world. They have no evolutionary memory of humans, no adaptation for exploiting your biology. But that does not mean they cannot harm you.

Some may find your body a novel environment, a warm wet habitat full of nutrients they can consume. Others may produce compounds toxic to your biochemistry as byproducts of their own processes. Still others may provoke immune responses your body cannot manage.

**Preparation:**
- Consider your skin and mucous membranes as compromised from the moment you arrive. You cannot keep the local environment out—it will coat you, enter you, become part of you. Your best defense is to maintain your body's natural barriers and support your immune system.
- Bring broad-spectrum antimicrobials, but use them sparingly. You do not know what you will need them for, and overuse may weaken your natural defenses.
- Monitor yourself constantly for signs of infection or adverse reaction—changes in skin condition, digestive disturbance, respiratory difficulty, neurological symptoms. In the dark, visual self-examination is impossible. You will need to feel for changes, to track your own function by internal perception.

### The Physical Strain

The combination of constant warmth, high humidity, challenging terrain, sensory deprivation, and psychological stress will tax your body severely. You will need more rest than normal, more food, more water. But rest is difficult in a place where you cannot safely lie down, where predators may find you if you sleep, where the sound never ceases.

**Preparation:**
- Plan for reduced efficiency. You will not be able to travel as far or work as hard as you would in more familiar conditions. Build rest into your schedule—enforced periods when you stop, recover, maintain your body.
- Bring concentrated nutrition that does not require preparation and functions when wet. Your appetite may decrease under stress, but your caloric needs will increase. You must eat even when you do not want to.
- Monitor your hydration carefully. You are surrounded by water, but drinking enough is another matter. The humidity may suppress your sense of thirst even as you lose water through sweat and exertion.

## IX. Equipment Summary

What you bring must be chosen with care. Every item must earn its place in your pack, must justify the weight and bulk against the cost of carrying it through difficult terrain in absolute darkness.

### Essential Systems

**Navigation:**
- Inertial navigation unit with long battery life
- Audio mapping system with multiple directional microphones
- Infrasonic detector for low-frequency vibrations
- Magnetic compass (verify it functions near potential electromagnetic interference)
- Acoustic beacons for marking path and return points

**Sensory Enhancement:**
- Echolocation assistance system (active or passive)
- Directional microphones with isolation capability
- Frequency filters for acoustic focus
- Bone conduction interface for vibration detection
- Audio recording and playback for landmark comparison

**Environmental Management:**
- Moisture-managing clothing (permeable, quick-draining)
- Active cooling system (thermal exchange or chemical)
- Full-body moisture barrier film for skin protection
- Drying agents and barrier creams for vulnerable areas
- Respiratory filtration mask (humidity-compatible)

**Safety and Defense:**
- Lightweight flexible armor
- Acoustic deterrent system (for predator confusion)
- Emergency sound beacon (for rescue signaling)
- First aid kit optimized for wet conditions
- Trauma supplies for predator attack

**Life Support:**
- Water filtration/purification system
- Concentrated nutrition supplies
- Hydration monitoring system
- Broad-spectrum antimicrobials
- Sleep system with acoustic dampening

**Communication:**
- Audio-based communication with travel companions
- Pre-arranged signal protocols
- Emergency extraction beacon

### Questionable Items

**Light sources:** Use only if necessary, understanding that you are introducing a foreign element to an ecology that has never known illumination. Consider red light for minimal disruption, but be prepared for unpredictable responses.

**Visual documentation:** Cameras may record images, but you will not be able to review your recordings until you leave. Consider whether the weight is justified.

**Excessive technology:** Every electronic system is a point of failure in high humidity. Bring what you need, and backups for critical systems, but understand that complexity increases vulnerability.

---

## X. Final Considerations

Ymurra is not a place for casual tourism. It is an environment that will test every aspect of your preparation, your equipment, your physical and psychological resilience. The darkness is absolute, the sound overwhelming, the hazards numerous and difficult to predict.

But for those who prepare properly, Ymurra offers something extraordinary: an immersion into a sensory world that humans have never fully inhabited. You will experience a landscape shaped not by light but by vibration, an ecology that communicates in frequencies you are only beginning to understand, a system of life and machine that has functioned without oversight for longer than your species has existed.

You will be blind, but you will learn to see with your ears.

You will be wet, but you will become part of the water cycle.

You will be prey, but you may learn to move through a world of predators as something they do not recognize as food.

The plateau is vast, and the darkness is deeper than distance. The sound never ceases. The water never stops. And if you prepare well, if you respect what Ymurra is and what you are within it, you may return with knowledge and experience that no other human has ever possessed.

Travel carefully. Listen always. And remember: in a world without light, you are never alone—you are simply unable to see what surrounds you.
