# Preparing for the Exhalation Plains

## Before You Step Through

**Do not arrive unannounced.** This is not a place you can simply walk into and orient yourself. The Exhalation Plains will begin processing you the moment you arrive—the landscape absorbs heat, moisture, carbon, and presence itself. You need preparation that assumes you are *permeable*.

Spend time in cold, wet environments before your visit. Not for acclimatization—there is no acclimatizing to this place—but to learn your own responses to prolonged wet cold. You will need to recognize hypothermia in yourself before your judgment becomes unreliable. The Plains do not kill quickly; they incorporate slowly, and the danger is that you will not notice yourself becoming part of the process.

---

## Physical Protection

### The Body

You will need a fully enclosed suit with positive pressure. Not for contamination—the biologicals here are not pathogenic in any way we understand—but because the moisture here *permeates*. It enters. The condensation beads on skin, seeps into clothing, works its way through layered fabrics until everything is damp, then wet, then part of the landscape's water cycle.

The suit must be:

- **Impermeable to liquid water** but breathable enough to prevent your own condensation from drowning you from inside. This is difficult. Test your suit in a sauna before departure.
- **Thermally insulated** with particular attention to joints and extremities. The cold here is penetrating—it finds the small gaps, the thin places, the seams where materials meet.
- **Neutral in color**—grey, blue-grey, anything that does not contrast with the monochromatic light. You do not want to be visible. You do not want to be *distinct*.

**Gloves are essential.** The ground gives way when you walk, and you will need to touch surfaces to navigate. Your hands will be the first part of you that the landscape processes. The mineral-fungal structures have surfaces that range from slick to abrasive, and you need dexterity without exposure. Thin thermal liners under waterproof outer gloves—a layered approach, because nothing stays dry here forever.

**Footwear must be chosen carefully.** The ground is not solid but not liquid—it yields, then resists, then yields again. Tall boots with flexible soles that can sense the surface beneath you. Rigid soles will cause you to misread the ground, to place weight incorrectly on something that might shift. You need to feel what you're stepping on, because what you're stepping on may be a processing structure, a channel, a form that responds to pressure.

**Eye protection** serves two purposes. The moisture in the air will bead on any surface, including your eyes. Extended exposure causes irritation, then inflammation, then a condition that resembles early-stage blindness—not permanent, but disorienting in a place where you cannot afford to be disoriented. Additionally, the light in breakdown zones behaves incorrectly. It can pool, move in wrong directions, produce frequencies that the eye registers but cannot process. Shaded, sealed goggles with anti-fog treatment are not optional.

**Ear protection is complicated.** The sound here is constant and layered, and you will be tempted to block it out. Do not fully block it. You need to hear the rhythm changes—the industrial processes cycle through phases, and certain sounds indicate where you are in the cycle, whether a region is in intake, transformation, or output mode. This information is navigational. But the aggregate roar is also damaging. Wear filtered protection that reduces volume without eliminating frequency information. Learn to listen through the protection, to attend to layers rather than experience the full assault.

## Navigation and Orientation

### The Problem of Horizons

You will not be able to see where you are going. Visibility extends perhaps fifty meters in optimal conditions—conditions that shift as the processing cycles change and the herds move through the fog. The horizon is a gradient, not a line. You will not be able to orient by sight alone.

**Magnetic compasses are unreliable here.** The mineral-fungal structures contain significant iron deposits in their silicate matrices. You may get readings, but they will be local, not planetary. Do not trust them.

**Inertial navigation is your best option.** A device that tracks your movement, that builds a map based on where you have been rather than where external references tell you to go. You will need to mark your arrival point clearly—leave something that transmits, that you can find again. The landscape does not build structures, but it does process materials; be prepared for your marker to be incorporated, covered, absorbed into the production cycles. Bring multiple markers, different types, and do not rely on any single one.

**Learn to read the landscape's rhythms before you need to navigate them.** The industrial processes have phases—intake, transformation, output, rest. These are observable in the sound patterns, in the movement of herds, in the flow of water across surfaces. Each region cycles differently. You can navigate by rhythm, by moving between regions at optimal points in their cycles. But this takes time to learn. If you cannot observe and learn, move slowly and never commit to a direction without confirming it multiple times through different sensory channels.

**The fauna are navigational aids.** The grazers move in patterns that respond to processing cycles. They know where production peaks, where rest phases make certain regions more traversable. You can follow herds, at a distance, to find paths through the density. But do not approach closely. Do not let them incorporate you into their patterns. You are observing, not joining.

## Temporal Stability

### The Hidden Danger

The strangest and most critical preparation concerns time itself. The Exhalation Plains produce temporal effects as byproducts of their industrial processes. This is not obvious. It does not announce itself. But it is the danger that most travelers do not anticipate, because we assume time is the medium we move through, not something that can be manufactured.

**Wear a mechanical timekeeping device.** Something analog, with gears and springs—something that exists in physical space rather than digital time. Digital clocks may drift, synchronize to signals that do not exist, or begin to measure time differently than your subjective experience. An analog watch gives you a reference point, something you can hold and watch and confirm is still operating as expected.

**Check it constantly.** Note the time when you arrive. Note it again at regular intervals. If the watch begins to run fast or slow, if the intervals between checks feel longer or shorter than the measured time, you are in a breakdown zone. Move. Do not stay to observe. The reality-thin places will layer time, and you can lose hours—or gain them—in moments that cannot be accounted for.

**Do not bring anything that depends on precise timing.** Medications that must be taken at specific intervals. Processes that require synchronization. The Plains will desynchronize you. Plan for flexibility. If you have medical needs, bring extra supplies and accept that your schedule will not hold.

**Be aware of memory gaps.** In breakdown zones, time can fold back on itself. You may find yourself standing in a place you do not remember walking to, or you may walk for what feels like hours only to find that no time has passed at all. Carry a voice recorder or a notebook, and use it constantly. Speak your observations. Note where you are, where you were, where you intend to go. If you find an entry you do not remember writing, or a gap in the record where there should be one, you have passed through a temporal anomaly. Mark it. Do not return to that location.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Sound That Does Not Stop

You cannot prepare for the sound. You can only know that it will be there, and that it will affect you.

The roar is constant. It is layered—industrial grinding, animal calls, water movement, and something else, something that may be reality grinding against itself. Your brain will adapt, will begin to filter it, to treat it as silence. This adaptation is useful but also dangerous. You stop perceiving informational content because you stop perceiving sound at all.

**Listen deliberately.** Every few minutes, consciously attend to the soundscape. Pick out a layer—the grazers, the processing hum, the water—and follow it for a moment. This keeps your auditory processing active. It prevents the strange deafness that comes from constant input, where you hear nothing because you hear everything.

**Do not try to find silence.** There is no silence here. Even if you could somehow dampen all external sound, you would hear your own body—your pulse, your breath, the movement of fluid in your ears. And beneath that, you might hear something else. The landscape may produce sounds that exist without cause, that come from times adjacent to your present, that speak in frequencies that your brain interprets as meaning without being able to decode it. Do not listen for those. Do not try to understand them. They are meaningless outputs—products of a process that has outlived its purpose.

**Sleep will be difficult.** If you must rest, do so in short intervals, never more than two hours at a time, and never alone. Have a companion wake you, or set an alarm that uses vibration rather than sound. Your brain will try to incorporate the roar into your dreams, and you may wake more tired than when you slept. This is normal for this place. Accept it.

---

### Being the Only Built Thing

You will be out of place here—not unwelcome, because the landscape does not reject, but *categorically distinct*. Everything here has grown, evolved, emerged. You have been built. You have intentions, linear plans, purposes that exist outside the processes around you.

This creates a psychological strain that is difficult to anticipate. You will feel it in the gap between your logic and the landscape's logic. You will want to understand, to find the design, the intention, the meaning. There is none. The industrial processes have evolved toward efficiency, not purpose. The herds follow patterns that have no shepherd. The breakdown zones produce effects without causes.

**Do not look for meaning.** This is the most important psychological preparation. You will be surrounded by production without product, process without purpose, presence without form. Your mind will try to impose narrative, to find the story that explains why this exists. There is no story. The Exhalation Plains have always been here. They will always be here. They produce because they produce, and the production has become unmoored from any intent.

If you find yourself searching for meaning, stop. Observe something specific. Attend to the immediate. The search for narrative will only exhaust you, and exhaustion in this landscape leads to errors, and errors lead to incorporation.

## Interaction Protocols

### The Fauna

The animals here are not hostile, but they are not safe. They have evolved to feed on industrial processes, and you are an anomaly—a source of heat, moisture, carbon, and something else, some trace of intentionality that the landscape does not otherwise contain.

**Do not approach the grazers.** They move in herds through the processing zones, scraping secretions from mineral surfaces. They are large, slow, and seemingly gentle, but they move in rhythms that respond to cycles you cannot perceive. If you find yourself in the path of a herd, do not run. Do not make sudden movements. Stand still. Let them flow around you. They will incorporate you into their movement if you let them—if you try to move against them, you will be pressurized, displaced, potentially crushed by the density of bodies.

**Be aware of interruption predators.** They do not kill; they disrupt. They locate moments of energy exchange—grazers feeding, processing structures at peak output—and absorb the released energy, the heat, the chemical byproducts. You are a continuous source of disruption. You are warm where the landscape is cold. You are metabolizing where everything else is being processed. The interruption predators may find you interesting. They may approach you, circle you, attempt to feed on your presence. They will not attack, but they will hover. Their proximity causes unease—subjects report a sense of energy draining, of coldness that exceeds the ambient temperature, of time passing strangely. If a predator begins to circle you, move toward a processing structure. The structures are better targets. Let the predator find richer feeding.

**The symbiont-forms will ignore you.** They are small, numerous, and occupied with the maintenance of industrial processes. They clean surfaces, move materials, facilitate the flow. You may not notice them until you see the landscape through close inspection—until you realize that every surface is in motion, covered in tiny forms performing tiny functions. They are not dangerous. They are also not communicative. They have no interest in you. Accept this. Do not try to interact.

---

### The Processing Structures

Do not touch the processing surfaces unless you must. They are intake zones, transformation chambers, output apertures—they are *working*. Your contact can disrupt a cycle, cause a backup in the production flow, create a small zone of turbulence where the process attempts to incorporate an unexpected input.

If you must touch, touch briefly. Use your gloved hands. Do not press. Some surfaces absorb rapidly—samples have disappeared into transformation chambers, incorporated so quickly that no sound was made, no resistance offered. The structures are not aggressive; they simply *process*, and you are material.

**Recognize the phases of processing.**

- **Intake:** Surfaces glisten, moisture accumulates, the structure seems to pull at the surrounding atmosphere. This is when absorption is most active. Avoid contact.
- **Transformation:** The structure hums, clicks, produces internal sounds of chemical reaction. It is occupied with its own processes. Contact is less likely to cause absorption, but the heat generated can be intense.
- **Output:** Apertures open, gases and liquids are released, crystallized residues are extruded. This is when the structure is most visible, most active, most obvious. The release can be forceful. Maintain distance.
- **Rest:** The structure settles, quiets, seems dormant. This is the safest time for close observation, but rest phases are brief and unpredictable. A structure in rest may shift to intake without warning.

### The Breakdown Zones

This is where reality thins. Where the industrial process has begun to produce meaningless outputs—temporal artifacts, probability clouds, forms that exist but have no history of formation.

**You will know you are approaching a breakdown zone before you see it.**

The sound changes—layers separate, become distinct, then begin to overlap incorrectly, as if multiple moments are sounding at once. The light behaves strangely, pooling in places that should not hold light, moving in directions that do not match the source. Your skin may prickle. Your analog watch may stutter.

**Stop.** Do not proceed.

Observe the zone from a distance—fifty meters at minimum, more if you have the option. Note what you see. The fauna approach these zones, ingest something, move away changed. This does not mean you can do the same. You are not adapted to this landscape. You do not know what ingestion would do to you.

If you must enter a breakdown zone—there is rarely a reason to do so—enter slowly. Do not go deep. Note the boundary where light begins to pool incorrectly, where sound begins to fold, and do not cross it. Observe from the threshold. The zones produce *meaningless outputs*. There is nothing to find. There is only the production of things that exist only to exist.

**Do not bring anything electronic into a breakdown zone.** Devices fail, behave unpredictably, sometimes begin to function in ways that were not intended. Your analog watch is your timekeeping. Your voice recorder is your memory. If these are affected, leave immediately. Do not attempt to document the effect from within it.

## Duration and Return

**Do not stay long.** The Exhalation Plains incorporate visitors slowly, through absorption of heat and moisture and presence. The longer you remain, the more you become part of the landscape's water cycle, its carbon cycle, its industrial processes. You will not notice this happening. You will simply find, after hours or days, that you are colder than you should be, that your thoughts are slower, that the sound has become a kind of silence.

Set a maximum duration before you arrive. Four hours for reconnaissance. Eight hours for extended observation. Never more than twenty-four hours in a single visit. If you must return, return after you have restored yourself—warm, dry, away from the sound, away from the constant presence of water and processing and production.

**The return is disorienting.** When you step back through to your origin point, the silence will be deafening. You will feel the absence of sound as a pressure, a weight, something that must be filled. This passes. Give yourself time in a quiet space to reacclimate. Do not seek loud environments to compensate. Your auditory system needs to recalibrate gradually.

**You may dream of the Plains.** The sound, the moisture, the sense of being surrounded by dense presence—these can return in sleep, particularly in the first nights after a visit. This is normal. It will fade. If it does not fade, if the dreams persist for more than a week, seek support from someone who understands exposure to high-weirdness environments.

---

## Final Note

The Exhalation Plains are not hostile. They are not predatory. They are *processes*—immense, ancient, ongoing processes that have outlived any purpose they might once have served. They will not harm you intentionally. They will simply incorporate you, process you, continue their production with you as one more input among the countless inputs that have fed them across geological time.

Your preparation is not defense against an enemy. It is maintenance of your distinction. You are a built thing in an unbuilt landscape, an intentional presence in a place where intention has no meaning. You are there to observe, to learn, to document—and then to leave.

The Plains were there before you. They will be there after you. The honor of visiting is to witness something that has no need of being witnessed, to experience a place that will not remember you, to stand briefly in a process that will continue for geological ages after your presence has been absorbed and processed and released back into the atmosphere.

Walk carefully. Listen deliberately. Do not seek meaning in meaningless output.

And when the sound becomes silence—when you no longer hear the roar because you have become part of it—move toward your return point immediately. That adaptation is the first sign of incorporation.

The Plains are patient. They have nowhere else to be.
