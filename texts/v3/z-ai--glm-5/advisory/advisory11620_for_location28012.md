# Preparing for the Drowned Plateau

## What You Are Walking Into

You are not going to a place. You are going into a *condition* — a state of being that water and land have entered together and cannot leave. The Drowned Plateau will not greet you. It will not notice you. It will tolerate you the way it tolerates everything: with patience, with density, and with the absolute certainty that you are temporary. Prepare accordingly, or do not go at all.

---

## The Body You Bring

### Lungs and Air

First, and with absolute clarity: **you cannot breathe this water.** The description says the water is "reluctant to enter lungs that had not earned their place," and you should treat that not as poetry but as warning. The medium here is thick, warm, and seemingly gentle, but it is not yours. It belongs to the things that evolved within it. Your lungs are not gills. Your alveoli cannot extract oxygen from a substance that operates by rules your biology never encountered.

Bring a closed-circuit rebreather, not standard scuba. You will be at altitude — the surface of this water sits where clouds should pass below — and the atmospheric pressure above is reduced. Standard dive tables will lie to you. Decompression sickness operates differently when the water you're surfacing from sits at two or three thousand meters of elevation. The Navy tables were not written for a lake on a mountain. Calculate your decompression schedules from first principles if you must, or stay shallow. Fortunately, shallow is all there is.

The water's thickness — described as "warm as breath" — suggests viscosity higher than seawater. Your breathing regulator may need adjustment. Test it in warm, viscous fluid before you travel. Honey-warm saline is a rough approximation. If your equipment cannot cycle properly through a medium that resists flow, you will notice too late, at the bottom of fifteen meters of water that does not want you, with a mouthful of nothing.

Consider bringing a full-face mask rather than a mouthpiece. The temporal distortion described at the plateau — the "thickness" of time, the overlapping of morning light with afternoon warmth — may affect your perception of duration. You might lose track of how long you've been under. A mouthpiece can be spit out in moments of disorientation. A mask stays on.

### Skin and Touch

The water is warm. This is deceptive. Prolonged immersion in water even a few degrees below body temperature will slowly extract heat, and the plateau's warmth is not uniform — it shifts, it stratifies, it pools. The warm regions the plants follow are not everywhere. Between them lie cooler volumes, layers that belong to older water, water that remembers the deep ocean or the ancient rain. You will swim through warmth and then through a membrane that resists, and on the other side of that membrane the temperature may drop several degrees without warning.

Wear a thin wetsuit — 2mm, perhaps 3mm. Not for the warmth you expect, but for the warmth you will unexpectedly lose when you cross from one stratum to another. The suit also protects against the microorganisms. Every cubic meter contains thousands of presences, the description says. Filaments so fine the water seems structured. Your skin is permeable. Your pores are openings. You do not know what these microorganisms can do to human biochemistry, and neither does anyone else. A wetsuit is a boundary between you and a book that has been written since the inundation began. Do not let it write on you.

Cover all exposed skin if possible. Thin neoprene gloves. A hood. The water's density of life is absolute — every centimeter claimed, every photon intercepted. Your uncovered skin is surface area that something will attempt to colonize, not from malice but from the sheer momentum of a system that fills every niche. You are a niche. Close yourself off.

### Movement and Gravity

The text says gravity may not apply "with ordinary force" here, and the floating canopy can be walked upon "if one had feet, if gravity still applied here with ordinary force." This is ambiguous. It might mean the plateau's elevation reduces gravity slightly (it does not — at a few kilometers up, gravitational acceleration is effectively unchanged). It might mean the dense medium provides buoyancy that alters your relationship with weight. It might mean something stranger.

Assume you will be buoyant but not weightless. The water is deeper than a human is tall — fifteen meters, sometimes twenty — and thick enough to slow your descent. You will float somewhere in the middle of the column unless you weight yourself. Bring adjustable buoyancy compensators and weight belts. You want to be able to descend to the floor and ascend to the canopy at will, not be trapped at some neutral buoyancy layer, spinning slowly, unable to reach either the sharp rocks below or the floating vegetation above.

But the weight belt is also a danger. The strata boundaries resist passage. If you are weighted for the lower regions and you cannot push back through the membrane between layers, you are trapped beneath an iridescent ceiling, looking up at warmth and light you cannot reach. Test the boundaries gently. Press against them with your hands before you commit your whole body. If they do not yield, do not force. The layers exchange materials at their boundaries through "deliberate diffusion." You are not a material they have agreed to exchange.

### Eyes and Sight

The light here is layered. Bright and filtered in the upper five meters, then yellowing, then greening, then a blue "so deep it hums at the back of the eyes." Bring a dive mask with clear, untinted glass. You will need to see the strata boundaries — they shimmer iridescent at certain angles. You will need to see the sharp rocks on the floor. You will need to see the drifting spore-clouds in the upper water, thick enough to obscure, dense enough to breathe if you removed your regulator (do not remove your regulator).

Your eyes will adjust slowly to the quality of light here. The plants grow toward warmth, not light — which means the warm regions may not coincide with the bright ones. You might find yourself in a zone of deep blue that is paradoxically warm, your eyes telling you one story while your skin tells another. This dissonance accumulates. After hours in the water, you may stop trusting your vision. This is not madness. This is the correct response to a place where light and warmth have divorced each other.

At night — and there is night here — the darkness "accumulates" rather than falling. Light drains downward, pooling in the lower strata, and the boundaries between layers glow faintly. Bring a dive light, but use it sparingly. The sound of your presence already travels for kilometers; the light of your lamp will travel further, and you do not know what it might attract or disturb. The fauna may be rare, but rare is not absent. The "single large creature" that passes through in a day, leaving a wake that takes hours to resettle — you do not want to be the disturbance it investigates.

## The Mind You Bring

### Silence and Sound

This is perhaps the most difficult preparation, because it is the one no equipment can manage for you.

**You must be quiet.** Not merely still — quiet. Sound travels for kilometers through this medium. A cough ripples outward until it reaches the plateau's walls and returns. The snap of a stem rings for hours. Your exhalations through a regulator, the clink of a weight belt, the creak of neoprene — all of this will write through the water, through the structured filaments of microorganisms, through millions of relationships that have existed in continuous silence since the inundation began. You will be a sound no one asked for in a place that has been learning to speak in whispers for millennia.

Minimize everything. Tape your buckles. Coat your metal fittings in silicone. Breathe slowly and evenly. Do not kick aggressively; use your hands when possible, moving with deliberate slowness through a medium that rewards patience and punishes haste. The plants grow in near-silence — a frond unfolding takes three days. Match their pace. When you move through this water, you are moving through a book. Move as if you are reading it, not tearing pages.

The silence will affect you more than the cold or the strangeness. Humans are not built for silence this complete. Your brain will generate phantom sounds — clicks, tones, voices — as it struggles to process the absence of input. The saturation of "possibility of sound" will press against your eardrums like a held breath. You will feel that something is about to happen, is always about to happen, and it never does. This is not anxiety. This is the plateau's temporality. Learn to sit inside the pause without reaching for its resolution.

### Time and Thickness

The hours overlap here. Morning's light mingles with afternoon's warmth, and both persist in the same volume. You may surface from a dive convinced you have been under for twenty minutes and find that three hours have passed — or that seven minutes have passed, and the afternoon light has somehow invaded your memory of the morning descent. Your watch will not help. Your watch measures a kind of time that the plateau has politely declined to participate in.

Bring a physical timer — a mechanical watch with a bezel, the kind that ticks. Not for accuracy, but for anchoring. When the thickness of time begins to disorient you, the tick-tick-tick of the escapement mechanism will remind you that sequential causality still exists somewhere, even if the plateau has suspended it locally. Hold the watch to your ear if you must. Let the rhythm reassemble your sense of before and after.

Do not make time-sensitive plans. Do not tell yourself you will surface by a certain hour. The plateau does not negotiate with schedules. You will leave when the place permits you to leave, when your nervous system has accepted enough of the temporal distortion to function normally again, or when you have recognized that "normally" is not something you will be returning to for a while. Give yourself extra decompression time — not for nitrogen, but for the mind.

### The Ecological Weight

You will feel it. The description is explicit: "the pressure of all that presences," the ecological density that is absolute, the sense that every centimeter is claimed, every photon intercepted, every degree of warmth captured. Humans evolved in environments with margins — empty spaces, distances between organisms, room to breathe. The plateau has no margins. You will feel crowded by things you cannot see, pressed upon by a living system that has no gap for you to occupy.

This is not claustrophobia in the usual sense. The water around you is open, the volume visible from surface to floor. But the density is existential rather than spatial. You will feel like an intruder at a gathering that was complete before you arrived, where every seat is taken, every conversation engaged, every role filled. You are excess. You are the thing the system did not account for and does not need.

Sit with this feeling. Do not flee it. The plateau's ecosystem has resolved its competitions into "a dance rather than a war." The arrangement is one of accommodation, persistence, and the wisdom that comes from having existed for long enough to learn that destruction is expensive. You will not be destroyed here unless you destroy yourself through carelessness. You will simply be — out of place, out of time, out of ecosystem. That is bearable. That is what being a visitor means.

### The Bones

You will see them on the floor, white against dark silt, scattered among the sharp stone fingers. They do not decay. The water preserves them, or nothing here has learned to consume them yet. They are offerings, the text says — or arrangements that might be deliberate if anything here had hands.

Some of these bones may be human. Other travelers may have come before you, unprepared, and found that drowning here is slow, that the water is reluctant, that you sink through the strata and come to rest on the rocks and become part of the sediment over centuries. Do not dwell on this. Do not look too long at any single set of bones, or you will begin to see the logic of staying, the comfort of becoming a thin white line in the accumulating record, of joining a system that does not need you but would, perhaps, wrap around you if you let it.

Look at the bones, note them, and look away. You are a visitor. This is not your grave unless you choose to make it so.

## Practical Logistics

### How to Arrive

The plateau ends in walls, not slopes. Vertical cliffs, sheared upward along faults, plunging through the water column and emerging into empty air above. You cannot walk to the Drowned Plateau. You must approach from above — by aircraft, by parachute, by some conveyance that can deposit you at the water's surface at an altitude where clouds should pass beneath.

This means your first encounter with the plateau will be the moment you enter the water from above. You will fall through thin air — the altitude will make your breath short even before you touch the surface — and then you will meet the warm, thick medium, and it will resist your entry, because it resists all intrusion, and then it will accept you, because it accepts everything eventually, with patience.

Time your arrival for mid-morning if possible. The light is bright in the upper five meters, the warmth distributed through the upper strata, and the canopy is visible from above — a broken mat of floating vegetation that you will need to avoid or navigate through. Do not land on the canopy. It is severed from its roots, drifting, tangled enough to walk upon "if gravity still applied here with ordinary force" — which means it may not support you, or it may support you unpredictably, or you may break through into the water column tangled in vines that have been growing toward warmth for longer than your species has existed.

Land in a gap. The canopy has openings — float-beds rot and sink, creating spaces that are filled "before they have fully emptied." Find a gap that is still open. Enter the water cleanly.

### How to Move

Slowly. Slower than that. Match the pace of the drifting grazers — "drops of flesh given just enough purpose to seek the next meal." They have survived here. Their slowness is not weakness; it is the correct velocity for this medium.

Use your hands more than your fins. The water is thick, and finning will create turbulence that ripples outward for kilometers, disturbing the strata, the microorganisms, the filaments of structured life. Pull yourself through the water gently, as if swimming through a library. Because you are.

When you encounter a stratum boundary — that iridescent shimmer, that resistance that presses back against you with something more than viscosity — approach it slowly. Test it. Press your palm against it. Some boundaries will yield with effort. Others will not yield at all, and you must find another path. Do not force your way through. The boundaries exchange materials by "deliberate diffusion" — a process that implies some form of selection, some criterion of admission. You may not meet it. Accept this and navigate around.

If you must ascend or descend through multiple strata, rest between each crossing. The pressure differentials may be small, but the resistance of the boundaries takes effort, and the temperature and light quality change with each layer. Your body and your mind need time to adjust. Do not push through three boundaries in rapid succession. The plateau does not reward urgency.

### How to See

The floor is fifteen to twenty meters down. The water is clear. You can see the limits. This is a gift that most underwater environments do not provide — comprehensible scale, visible boundaries, a sense of where you are.

But look also at what is between you and the floor. The water column is not empty space. It is structured, inhabited, layered with life and chemical memory. The spore-clouds in the upper five meters. The yellowing light, then the greening, then the deep humming blue. The boundaries that shimmer. The drifting grazer you might mistake for a drop of water until it moves. The "single large creature" you might see once, passing through, leaving a wake.

Train yourself to look at the medium, not through it. The Drowned Plateau is not a place with water in it. The water is the place. Everything that matters here is happening in the water — in its layers, its memories, its refusal to merge what should not be merged. If you look only at the floor and the surface, you will miss the plateau entirely.

### How to Leave

The same way you arrived: through the air above. The plateau's walls are cliffs. You cannot climb them from the water, and you cannot swim down through the water to the ocean far below — the walls extend downward into darkness, and the water stops at those walls, bounded by them. You must exit from the surface.

But the surface is covered in floating canopy, tangled and drifting, and the temporal distortion may make it difficult to coordinate an extraction. If you are traveling with support — a boat, an aircraft — arrange signals in advance. Flares may not be visible through the canopy. Radio communication may not penetrate the water. Agree on a timing protocol: if you do not surface within a certain window, they should assume you are in difficulty.

When you surface, do so slowly, through the thinnest part of the canopy you can find. The vegetation has been adapting for longer than you can comprehend. It may have adapted to entangle. It may have adapted to capture. Or it may simply be growing where it grows, and your struggle is your own. Move gently. If you become tangled, do not thrash. Thrashing writes sound through the water. Thrashing writes desperation through the water. The plateau does not respond to desperation. It responds to patience.

Breathe the thin air at altitude. Let your body recalibrate to the ordinary world of sequential time and unstructured space. The disorientation will last for hours, possibly days. You will feel the absence of the plateau's density — the margins and emptiness of the ordinary world will seem wrong, exposed, lacking. This is normal. This is what it feels like to leave a place that is full and return to a place that is not.

## What to Carry

- **Closed-circuit rebreather** with altitude-adjusted decompression calculations
- **Full-face dive mask** — prevents loss of regulator during temporal disorientation
- **Thin wetsuit (2-3mm)** with hood and gloves — biological barrier and thermal insurance
- **Adjustable buoyancy compensator and weight belt** — you must control your position in the column
- **Mechanical dive watch** — not for precision, but for the anchor of sequential ticking
- **Dive light** — small, low-lumen; for night navigation only; use sparingly
- **Dive knife** — not for defense (there is nothing to fight) but for entanglement in the canopy
- **Silicone tape and lubricant** — silence every buckle, cushion every metal edge
- **Waterproof writing slate** — for observations, for messages, for grounding yourself when time thickens
- **First aid kit** with broad-spectrum antibiotics — you do not know what the microorganisms here can do
- **Emergency surface marker buoy** — bright, visible from air, for extraction coordination
- **A physical object from home** — something you can hold, something that exists in ordinary time and space, something that reminds you what "normal" feels like when the plateau has convinced you that normal was always a lie

---

## What Not to Bring

- **Cameras** — the act of recording presumes that what you are seeing can be captured, and it cannot. The plateau's strangeness is not visual; it is temporal, ecological, existential. A photograph of the Drowned Plateau will look like a photograph of water. You will show it to people and they will see nothing remarkable, and you will be unable to explain what is missing from the image, which is everything.
- **Weapons** — there is nothing to fight. The large creatures are rare and uninterested. The microorganisms cannot be stabbed. The water cannot be threatened. You are not in danger from violence here. You are in danger from belonging nowhere, and a knife does not solve that.
- **Expectations** — the plateau does not explain itself. It does not resolve its contradictions. It holds them. If you arrive expecting answers — why the water stratifies without gradient, why the bones do not decay, why the fauna are absent, why time moves thick — you will leave with nothing. If you arrive expecting to witness a place that holds its oppositions without resolution, you will leave with exactly that, which is enough.

## What to Understand Before You Go

The Drowned Plateau does not need you. This is not hostility. This is not indifference in the human sense. It is the simple truth of a system that achieved completeness before you arrived, or that is still achieving completeness through processes that do not require your participation, or that operates by rules that make your category of being — mobile, air-breathing, time-linear — simply irrelevant.

You will feel this irrelevance. It will press against you from every direction, from every cubic centimeter of water that is claimed, every photon that is intercepted, every stratum boundary that resists your passage. You will feel like a question that no one is asking, a sound that no one is waiting to hear.

But you will also feel the patience. The plateau is not hostile. It is not even unwelcoming. It is *accommodating* — in the deepest sense of that word. It has learned, through millennia of slow competition resolved into slow cooperation, that persistence is more effective than destruction. The vine and the stalk have an arrangement. The strata exchange materials at their boundaries. Nothing here wants to destroy. Everything here wants to persist, and persistence has taught the wisdom of accommodation.

You can persist here, too, if you are patient. If you move slowly. If you respect the boundaries and the silences and the thickness of time. If you accept that you will never earn your place in this ecosystem, but you can visit it without breaking it, and it will allow you to pass through like a sound that takes hours to fade, leaving behind a faint disturbance in the filaments of life, a memory of your passage recorded in the chemical gradients of the water, in the positions of the plants that leaned slightly away from your warmth, in the silt that settled differently where you disturbed it.

This is what you are: a disturbance that the plateau will remember, in its way, for a while. Not because it chose to remember you, but because it remembers everything. The water holds the signature of its origin — every drop, every stratum, every layer that does not merge because it has not forgiven the others their differences. The water will hold the signature of you, too, once you have passed through it. The warmth of your body. The chemistry of your skin. The sound of your breathing, rippling outward through millions of relationships, a cough in a cathedral that rings for hours before fading.

Go, if you are going. Be quiet. Be slow. Be patient. Watch the light drain downward at evening and the strata glow at their boundaries. Listen to the silence that is not empty but full — saturated with the possibility of sound, with the possibility of your sound, which you have chosen not to make. Lie on the floor among the bones and the sharp young rocks and look upward through fifteen meters of structured water, through the canopy and the spore-clouds and the drifting grazers, toward a surface that sits where clouds should pass beneath, and understand that you are in a place that has been holding its breath since before your species learned to breathe.

And then leave. Before the patience becomes comfortable. Before the silence becomes your language. Before the thickness of time makes the thin sequential hours of the ordinary world seem unbearably fast and empty and wrong. Leave while you still remember that you are a visitor. Leave before the plateau decides to keep you, slowly, patiently, in the accumulating record of its silt, a thin white line among the bones that do not decay.

Leave. The water will hold your memory. It holds everything.
