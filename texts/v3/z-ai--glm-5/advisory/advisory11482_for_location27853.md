# Preparing for the Plateau of Standing Mist

A practical guide for the multiverse traveler, compiled from accounts of those who have visited and returned — and careful study of those who did not.

---

## The Ascent: Physical Preparation

**The climb itself demands respect.** The paths cut into the cliff face were not made for casual visitors. They were made by people who had lived their entire lives on the plateau, whose bodies had adapted to thin air and whose minds had absorbed the patient rhythm of a place where hurry means nothing. Assume you do not have these advantages.

**Rope.** Two hundred feet minimum, preferably three hundred. The paths narrow without warning, and the mist obscures edges until you are already at them. Carabiners rated for stone wear — the cliff rock does not erode, which means it remains jagged, unforgiving, eager to catch and hold fabric and flesh. Gloves with reinforced palms. The climb takes longer than the distance suggests, and your hands will suffer first.

**Footwear.** The plateau's stone retains its sharpness. Edges that would have been smoothed by centuries of wind and water elsewhere remain here like broken glass, like the teeth of something that has been waiting. Boots with thick soles, preferably layered leather rather than synthetic — the dampness penetrates synthetics within hours, and wet feet mean cold feet, and cold feet on sharp stone mean injury that will not heal at the normal rate. Bring extra socks. Bring more than you think you need. The dampness finds everything.

**Layers upon layers.** The plateau steals warmth with methodical efficiency. Not violently — this is not a place of violent weather — but persistently, the coolness settling into bone and staying. Wool closest to the skin, then more wool, then something waterproof but breathable. The mist is too fine to be called rain, too present to be ignored. It collects on every surface. After three hours on the plateau, you will be damp everywhere, and the dampness will not leave until you do. Plan for this. Accept it. Fight it and you will spend your energy on something you cannot change.

**Head covering.** Essential. The diffuse light comes from everywhere, which sounds pleasant until you realize it means there is no shade, no relief, just the same sourceless glow from the moment you arrive until the moment you descend. A wide brim keeps the moisture from collecting on your face, in your eyes, running down the back of your neck. Some travelers report headaches after extended exposure — not from brightness, but from the strangeness of light without source, shadow without direction.

## The Temporal Question: Mental Preparation

**This is the preparation most travelers neglect, and the one that matters most.**

The plateau exists in suspension. Time moves differently here — not stopped, never quite stopped, but slowed, thickened, made to pool in place rather than flow forward. You will feel this within your first hour. A moment stretches. A conversation takes on weight. You watch a bird circle a tower and realize you cannot remember how long you have been watching, only that you started watching, and now you are still watching, and the bird is still circling.

**Before you ascend, decide how long you will stay.** Write it down. Tell someone who is not making the climb. The plateau makes such decisions difficult once you are within its influence. Days become ambiguous. Nights blend into the diffused half-light. You may intend to stay for an afternoon and find yourself unable to say, three days later, when that afternoon ended. The people who live here do not track time the way you do. They do not need to. Their aging is slow, their memories perfect, their days measured in patterns rather than hours.

**Set an external marker.** Something you must return for — a meeting, a departure, a promise. Make it concrete. The plateau's peace is seductive precisely because it offers relief from urgency, and urgency is what reminds us to leave. Without urgency, without the pressure of time passing, the plateau becomes comfortable. It becomes home. It becomes the place you stay.

**Practice memory before you arrive.** The plateau preserves more than stone. Experiences here remain present, accessible, as though they are still happening. This sounds like a gift — who would not want to remember perfectly? — but perfect memory means perfect retention of everything. The argument you had with your partner before you left. The fear you felt on the climb. The grief you thought you had processed years ago. All of it remains present on the plateau, preserved in the mist, accessible as though it happened moments ago. Travelers report the experience differently: some find it healing, a chance to sit with old pain until it transforms; others find it overwhelming, a flood of unprocessed experience they cannot escape. Know yourself before you arrive. Know what you carry.

## Navigation: Moving Through Suspension

**The paths deceive.** Distance works strangely on the plateau. A route that takes ten minutes one day may take thirty the next, not because you walked slower but because the path itself expanded, the stone stretching like something alive to accommodate your passage. The locals know which routes compress and which extend, but they cannot always explain the logic. They feel it. They have walked these paths since childhood. You have not.

**Hire a guide if you can.** Not for the climb — the climb is physical, manageable with preparation — but for the plateau itself. The people who live here understand the spatial distortions instinctively. They know that walking clockwise around the eastern tower leads to the counting room in half the expected steps, while walking counter-clockwise can leave you wandering for hours. They know which hollows to avoid after dark, which paths grow longer when you walk them alone.

**If you cannot hire a guide, navigate by the gardens.** The vegetation islands do not move, whatever else shifts. Find the largest garden — the central hollow where communal gatherings happen — and orient yourself by its position. From there, the city organizes itself: food preparation structures to the north, tool-making buildings to the west, living quarters scattered between. The stone expanses between gardens feel like empty space, but they are not empty. They are connective tissue. They hold the city together. Walk them quickly. Do not linger on bare stone if you can help it.

**Sound will not save you.** The mist swallows it. Shout for help and watch your voice die within arm's reach, absorbed by the dampness, transformed into something like the memory of sound. Whistle, and hear nothing but the faint suggestion of a whistle, arriving at your own ears as though from very far away. The plateau's acoustic isolation means you must navigate visually, and the mist limits visibility to perhaps a hundred feet on clear days, far less in the deeper hollows.

**Mark your path.** Bring something you can leave behind — strips of bright cloth, small stacks of stones, anything distinct and visible against the pale rock. Do not rely on mental mapping. The plateau does not want you to leave, and the navigation distortions are part of this wanting. Mark your route from the moment you reach the top. Mark it more heavily than seems necessary. The marks will not erode; they will remain exactly where you place them. Follow them back when it is time to go, even if the path they mark seems wrong, seems longer than you remember, seems to be leading you somewhere other than where you started.

## The Wildlife: Biological Preparation

**The animals here have adapted to suspension.** This makes them strange. Not dangerous, necessarily — the plateau has predators, but they hunt in the gardens, not on the connecting stone — but strange in ways that can unsettle the unprepared traveler.

**The deer-things** move in herds of exact size. Watch them long enough and you will notice: the number never varies. A birth occurs, and within days, a death follows, restoring the equilibrium. They do not fear humans the way deer elsewhere fear humans. They have no reason to. Hunting them would be pointless — the meat does not preserve, even on the plateau, and the herd would simply produce another animal to replace whatever you took. They regard travelers with something like patience, like tolerance, like the understanding that you will move through their territory and then leave, and they will still be here, unchanged, when you are gone.

**The small predators** — the pale cats, the silent hunters — ignore humans almost entirely. They hunt the birds that nest in the fern thickets, the small mammals that live in the garden undergrowth. They have no interest in you. Do not interest them. Do not interfere with their territories, which follow geometric patterns the animals seem to know instinctively. Walk through a predator's marked space and you may feel the pressure of its attention, the weight of being observed by something that has not yet decided what you are.

**The insects** stay in their hollows. This is a mercy. They swarm thickly enough in some gardens to make breathing difficult, but they do not cross the bare stone. Stand at the edge of a depression and watch the insects fill the air with their vibrating bodies, their noiseless flight, and know that you can step back onto the connecting rock and leave them behind instantly. They will not follow. They cannot. Something about the bare stone repels them, or simply fails to attract them, and so the gardens exist as islands of insect life surrounded by zones of relative emptiness.

**The deep hollows** are different. The mist pools there, thick as fog, visibility dropping to feet rather than yards. The locals avoid these places after dark and caution visitors to do the same. Something lives in the deepest gullies — something large, rarely seen, felt more than observed. The sounds it makes register as silence, as the absence of sound, as the space where sound should be. Travelers who have ventured into the deep hollows report pressure, the sense of being watched by something patient and vast. None have been harmed, but none have remained long enough to learn what watched them. The plateau preserves life, but it does not guarantee safety. It does not guarantee anything. It simply holds.

## The Social Landscape: Cultural Preparation

**The people who live here have been here for a very long time.** They remember every visitor. They remember every trade, every conversation, every word spoken in passing. Their memories are preserved by the same force that preserves the stone — not frozen, but accessible, present, as though each experience continues to exist in the moment it occurred.

**Do not lie to them.** They will know. Not because they have superior perception, but because they remember everything you say, and any inconsistency will be preserved forever in their recollection, accessible for comparison whenever they choose to examine it. They do not confront deception with anger. They simply note it. They add it to the accumulation of facts they hold about you. And they never forget.

**They do not haggle.** This confuses travelers from places where bargaining is expected. The plateau's inhabitants name their prices fairly, and the prices are always the same, and there is no mechanism for negotiation because negotiation implies that the outcome matters in some future context. On the plateau, there is only the present transaction, and the present transaction has no urgency. Pay what they ask or do not buy. They will not lower the price. They will not raise it for the next traveler. They will simply wait, in their doorway, with their basket of goods, for as long as it takes.

**Gifts are meaningful in ways you may not expect.** The plateau imports almost nothing. Its inhabitants have limited use for outside goods. But they understand gesture. They understand the symbolic weight of giving something that cannot be obtained where they live. Spices, salt, small metal implements — these fetch reasonable prices in trade, but a gift of something unique, something that carries no obligation, something offered simply because you thought they might appreciate it — this is remembered differently. This becomes part of the story they tell about you, preserved in their perfect recall, added to the accumulated narrative of every traveler who has ever passed through.

**Learn a few words of their language.** It has changed slowly, preserved by the static atmosphere, but it is not identical to any tongue spoken in the lower lands. The sounds are softer here — consonants rounded, vowels extended, everything shaped by the need to penetrate the dampness that swallows sharper speech. A greeting, a phrase of thanks, a word for farewell. These small efforts register. The people note them. They remember them. They incorporate them into their understanding of who you are.

## The Architecture: Spatial Preparation

**The buildings here are older than they appear.** Not worn, not weathered — the plateau does not permit erosion — but old in the way that means accumulated presence. The stone walls have stood since the plateau rose, and they will stand until something changes the fundamental nature of the place. They do not need maintenance. They receive it anyway.

**Do not assume interior dimensions match exterior ones.** The carved structures — those cut directly into the plateau's outcrops — contain spaces that are larger inside than they should be. This is not magic, exactly, but it is not not magic either. It is the plateau expressing itself, extending itself, making room where room should not exist. The locals know which rooms to use for storage, which passages lead somewhere useful, which corridors turn back on themselves in ways that suggest the stone is thinking. Ask before you enter any carved structure. Ask specifically about the room's properties. Some of them stretch time the way the paths do — enter one, spend what feels like minutes, emerge to find hours have passed outside.

**The counting room** is worth visiting, if the locals permit it. Deep in one of the carved complexes, past corridors that seem longer than the building's exterior could contain, the walls are covered in marks made over centuries. Lines, dots, spirals — a record of something, though no one remembers what. The current inhabitants add their own marks sometimes, feeling that the accumulation matters. Stand in this room. Feel the weight of all that counting, all those generations making their small additions to a record no one understands. Some travelers report a sense of connection to the past here, a feeling that the marks are trying to communicate something. Others report only a pleasant blankness, a quieting of thought. The room affects different people differently. It is safe, as far as anyone knows. It simply is.

**Sleep in the designated buildings.** The locals maintain structures specifically for visitors, set apart from their own living quarters, positioned in the less strange parts of the city's geography. These buildings have normal interior dimensions, normal temporal flow, no unusual properties beyond the plateau's general suspension. They are warm enough, dry enough, safe enough. Do not sleep on the bare stone between buildings. Travelers who have done so report dreams that follow them back to waking, a sense of having been observed by something in the mist, a difficulty returning to normal sleep patterns after they leave. The stone is not dangerous, exactly, but it is not neutral either. It holds things. It may hold you.

## The Departure: Leaving the Suspension

**This is where travelers struggle most.** The plateau does not want you to leave. Not through malice — there is no malice here, no malevolence — but through the simple physics of suspension. The place holds everything. The mist beads on your skin, and you become part of what the plateau preserves, and departing means tearing yourself away from a place that has begun to incorporate you into its patterns.

**Prepare for resistance.** The paths down seem longer than the paths up. The cliffs seem steeper. The mist thickens near the edges, obscuring the drop, making each step feel like a step into uncertainty. This is normal. This is the plateau expressing its nature. Push through. Keep moving. The difficulty is not a warning — it is simply what happens when something held by suspension tries to leave.

**Say goodbye properly.** The people here remember everything, and they will remember if you leave without acknowledgment. A word of thanks to those who hosted you, a gift if you can manage one, a moment of genuine connection before you descend. They do not expect this, exactly — they have hosted many travelers who left without ceremony — but they remember it when it happens. They incorporate it into their understanding of the world beyond the plateau. And perhaps, in some small way, you will become part of their story, part of the accumulated narrative that they preserve without effort, without decay, without end.

**Do not look back at the bottom.** This is superstition, perhaps, but it is also practical. The plateau will still be there when you look, rising two thousand feet above you, its mist-laden summit obscuring the city you just left. But the looking can be difficult. Travelers report a sense of loss, a sudden awareness of everything they are leaving behind — the peace, the suspension, the strange comfort of a place where time pools instead of flows. Feel this loss, if it comes, but keep walking. The lower lands have their own rhythms, their own demands, their own flow of time that will not wait for you to adjust. The plateau will be there if you need to return. The plateau will always be there, unchanged, waiting, holding its mist and its stone and its slow, suspended present.

---

## A Final Note

The Plateau of Standing Mist offers something rare: a place where nothing is lost. Where everything persists. Where the sharp edges of the world remain sharp, the clear memories remain clear, the present remains present in a way that admits no erosion, no decay, no forgetting.

This can be a gift. It can be a rest. It can be exactly what you need — a pause in the rush of multiverse travel, a moment that stretches into as many moments as you require.

It can also be a trap. Not through malice, never through malice, but through the simple nature of suspension. The plateau holds. That is what it does. It holds stone and mist and life and time, and it holds them gently, persistently, without ever quite letting go.

Visit if you need to. Stay as long as you choose. But make that choice before you arrive, and hold onto it while you are there, and remember, when the paths stretch and the mist thickens and the peace settles into your bones, that there are other places waiting for you beyond the cliffs — places where time flows, where things change, where the future remains unwritten.

The plateau will understand. It understands everything, eventually. It simply does not change its understanding, does not update its beliefs, does not move forward from the position it has held since it rose from the earth in a single sustained groan, forever ago, or perhaps just this morning.

Time, on the plateau, is difficult to measure. But the departure is always possible, for those who remember to make it.
