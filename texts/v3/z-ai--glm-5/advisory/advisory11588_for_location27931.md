# Preparation Guide: The Exudation Tiers

## The First Thing You Must Understand

You are not coming here to visit a city. You are coming to enter an organism that has grown beyond anyone's comprehension. The Exudation Tiers will receive you the way a body receives a splinter—with irritation, with pressure, with an immune response. Your job is to become small enough, quiet enough, and compliant enough that the city tolerates your presence for the duration of your stay.

This place changes people. Not metaphorically. Physiologically. Psychologically. The travelers who treat this like any other wet climate destination are the ones who leave with permanent hearing damage, respiratory infections that follow them home, and a tremble in their hands that never quite stops. The ones who prepare properly leave with memories they cannot fully explain and a craving for humidity that haunts them in dry places.

What follows is what I have learned, and what I wish someone had told me.

## Respiratory Preparation: Training for Waterlogged Air

The air in the Exudation Tiers holds more water than not. This is not a poetic phrasing—it is a measurable fact. The humidity levels in the lower tiers can reach saturation points where the air is essentially a fine suspension of water droplets. Breathing there requires effort. Each breath pulls moisture into your lungs, and each exhale fails to clear it entirely.

You will need to train your lungs before arrival.

### The Humidity Acclimation Protocol

Begin at least six weeks before your planned departure. You will need access to a humid environment—a steam room, a sauna, or a bathroom with the hot water running. The goal is progressive exposure.

**Week One and Two:** Spend thirty minutes daily in the highest humidity you can tolerate. Do not exert yourself. Sit. Breathe. Learn the sensation of air that fights entry. Pay attention to where your chest tightens and where your throat catches. These are the places that will suffer first when you arrive.

**Week Three and Four:** Increase to sixty minutes daily. Add mild exertion—slow walking, gentle stretching. Your body needs to learn how to demand oxygen when the air resists. You will feel panic in these sessions. Your lungs will protest. Your heart rate will spike. This is normal, and you must push through it. The panic is the training.

**Week Five and Six:** Increase to two hours daily if you can manage it. Add conversation to your exertion—speak aloud, read aloud, practice the shapes of words in wet air. The Exudation Tiers are loud, and you will need to speak to be heard. Your throat will need to be ready.

### Equipment Considerations

Do not bring a standard rebreather or filtration mask. The moisture in the Exudation Tiers will clog any conventional filter within hours. The residents do not use filters—they use either no protection at all, having adapted over generations, or they use simple mesh barriers that catch the largest particulates while allowing water to pass through.

I recommend the latter approach for short visits. A fine mesh face covering, made from hydrophobic material, will reduce the intake of insect life and fungal spores without attempting to filter the unfilterable water from the air. Pack at least twenty such coverings. They will grow foul quickly, and you will need fresh ones.

### The Secondary Respiratory Risk: Infection

The air is not merely wet. It is alive. Bacterial colonies drift in the condensation. Fungal spores ride the updrafts. The warm, wet environment is ideal for microbial growth, and your respiratory system is a foreign landscape where nothing has evolved to compete.

Begin a prophylactic antifungal regimen one week before arrival and continue it for two weeks after departure. Consult with a xenobiologist or a physician familiar with non-standard biospheres. The specific organisms in the Exudation Tiers have adapted to human hosts over millennia of cohabitation—they are not generally fatal to prepared travelers, but they can establish persistent colonies in unadapted lungs.

I also recommend carrying a broad-spectrum antibiotic, though you should use it only if symptoms develop. The resident population has their own pharmacopeia, developed over generations, and if you fall ill, seek out a local healer before resorting to your own supplies. Their medicines know this place better than yours do.

## Hearing Protection: The Noise Is Not Background

Let me be absolutely clear about this: the noise levels in the Exudation Tiers cause permanent hearing damage within hours of unprotected exposure. This is not an exaggeration. The sound pressure levels measure between 95 and 115 decibels in most inhabited areas, with peaks exceeding 120 decibels near mechanical infrastructure or during fauna population events.

The noise comes from everywhere. The fluid circulation systems. The fauna. The settling of ancient structures. The ongoing construction. The updrafts and downdrafts moving through tight architectural spaces. You cannot escape it by moving to a quieter area. There are no quieter areas. The city generates sound as a byproduct of its own existence, and that sound concentrates in the basin like water in a bowl.

### What Standard Protection Will Not Do

Foam earplugs are insufficient. The frequencies present in the Exudation Tiers include extremely low-frequency vibrations that travel through bone conduction. You can block your ear canals entirely and still feel the sound in your teeth, in your skull, in the fluids of your inner ear.

Noise-canceling headphones are similarly inadequate. The sound profile shifts constantly, and the wavelengths present exceed the operating range of most consumer devices.

### What You Actually Need

You need industrial-grade hearing protection designed for continuous exposure. Look for the following:

**Active Noise Cancellation with Extended Range:** Devices that can detect and counter frequencies down to 20 Hz and up to 20,000 Hz. The lower end is critical—much of the most damaging sound in the Exudation Tiers operates below the range of normal human speech.

**Bone Conduction Dampening:** Some form of headband or casing that reduces vibration transmission through the skull. This is not standard in most hearing protection and may require specialty equipment.

**Sealed Canal Design:** The protection should form a complete seal with your ear canal. Moisture will be present on everything, and any gap will allow water to seep in, compromising the seal and introducing bacteria.

**Extended Battery Life or Rechargeability:** You will not be able to purchase replacement batteries in the Exudation Tiers. The resident population does not use electronic hearing protection—they have adapted biologically. Bring enough power to last your entire stay, plus a safety margin.

### The Adaptation Strategy

Even with excellent protection, you will feel the sound. It will press against your skin. It will vibrate in your chest. You cannot fully insulate yourself from physical sound pressure without also insulating yourself from the environment.

Plan for rest cycles. Find accommodation in the mid-tier ranges, where the noise is somewhat diffused by distance from both the deep machinery and the upper construction zones. Sleep in shifts if necessary. The sound does not stop, and your brain will not adjust to it the way it adjusts to constant background noise in other environments. The Exudation Tiers do not offer true background—everything here is foreground.

### Communication Considerations

The residents communicate through a combination of sound, gesture, and vibration. Their spoken language operates at higher frequencies than most standard human speech, which cuts through the ambient noise more effectively. They also use a form of signed language developed in the crowded, wet spaces where speech fails.

Learn the basics of this signed language before arrival. Even a few dozen signs will help you navigate. The sound environment makes verbal communication difficult even for the adapted residents, and they will appreciate the effort to meet them in their preferred modality.

## Thermal Regulation: Living in a Body-Temperature World

Nothing here ever goes cold. The heat rises from below, radiates from walls, and saturates the air. The water that weeps from the architecture is always at least body temperature, and often warmer. The city holds heat like a living thing holds warmth, and you are inside that thing.

This poses challenges that are opposite to what most travelers expect. You do not need to pack for warmth. You need to pack for heat dissipation in an environment where heat dissipation is nearly impossible.

### The Humidity-Temperature Compound Effect

The danger is not merely the heat. It is the combination of heat and humidity. In dry environments, sweat evaporates and cools the body. In the Exudation Tiers, sweat does not evaporate. It beads on your skin and joins the general moisture, leaving you wetter and no cooler. This is how heat exhaustion and heat stroke occur in places that feel no hotter than a warm bath.

### Clothing Recommendations

Pack light, hydrophobic, loose-weave fabrics. The goal is to minimize fabric that retains water while maximizing airflow across the skin. Cotton is a poor choice—it holds moisture and becomes heavy, warm, and restrictive. Synthetic quick-dry materials are better. The best options are fabrics designed for tropical diving environments, which resist water absorption while remaining flexible.

Cover your skin despite the heat. The insect life here will land on any exposed surface and drink the moisture they find there. Their mouthparts are adapted for piercing bacterial mats and mineral accretions, and they will pierce your skin without hesitation. Light, full-coverage clothing is essential.

Consider a cooling system if you plan to stay more than a few days. There are compact, wearable heat-exchange units designed for high-humidity environments. They work by circulating cooled fluid through tubes woven into a vest or shirt. They require power and add weight, but they may be the difference between endurance and collapse.

### The Acclimation Period

Your first three days in the Exudation Tiers will be difficult. Your body will attempt to regulate temperature through mechanisms that do not work here. You will sweat profusely. You will feel lethargic. You may experience headaches, nausea, and confusion.

Plan for this. Do not schedule demanding activities during your acclimation period. Drink water constantly, even though the air is full of it—you need internal hydration to support your body's failing cooling systems. Rest when you feel the need. Pushing through heat exhaustion in the Exudation Tiers can lead to collapse in a place where collapsed bodies are not easily recovered.

## Navigation: Finding Your Way in the Infinite Layered Maze

The architecture of the Exudation Tiers defies conventional navigation. You will not be able to maintain a mental map of your surroundings. The city is too dense, too layered, too inconsistent. Sections that appear connected may not be. Paths that existed yesterday may be flooded, blocked, or rebuilt today. The city is never not under construction, and that construction changes the layout without notice.

### Why Maps Fail

Maps assume a static environment. The Exudation Tiers are not static. They are a living process. What was a thoroughfare last month may be a construction zone this month and a flooded chamber next month. The residents do not use maps. They use accumulated knowledge, learned routes, and social networks that share information about current conditions.

Additionally, the city's structure is not standardized. Different eras of construction used different organizational principles. One section may follow a grid pattern that made sense to builders whose aesthetic and practical philosophies are entirely lost. Another section may be organized radially around nodes whose purpose is unknown. A third section may have no apparent organization at all, having grown through accretion rather than planning.

The oldest sections, deep below, follow rules that predate any extant culture. Some travelers report sections that seem to reconfigure themselves, though I cannot confirm this personally. What I can confirm is that no map remains accurate for long.

### Navigation Strategies That Actually Work

**Hire a Local Guide.** This is the single most important preparation you can make. A resident of the Exudation Tiers knows the current state of the areas they frequent. They know which passages are flooded, which sections are unstable, which zones are contested between resident groups. They can negotiate with other residents on your behalf. They can interpret the social signals that you will not recognize.

Do not attempt to hire a guide after arrival. Arrange this in advance through whatever interdimensional networks facilitated your travel. The best guides are booked well ahead of time, and the ones available on short notice are often desperate, inexperienced, or both.

**Learn the Tier System.** The Exudation Tiers are organized approximately vertically, with lower numbers toward the depths and higher numbers toward the upper construction. This numbering system is inconsistent—different sections number differently, and some sections use entirely different organizational schemas—but it provides a rough framework.

The middle tiers, roughly 400 through 600, are the most accessible to visitors. They are not so deep that the heat and pressure become overwhelming, and not so high that the construction activity makes navigation impossible. Most visitor infrastructure, such as it is, exists in this range.

**Carry a Depth Gauge.** Not an altitude meter—a depth gauge. You need to know how far below the rim you are at any given moment. This information will help you orient yourself when all other navigational cues fail. A simple pressure-based gauge will work, as the atmospheric pressure increases predictably with depth.

**Mark Your Path.** The environment works against permanent marking. Water washes away most substances. The mineral accretions grow over anything left too long. The construction activity removes whatever it encounters.

But temporary marking is possible. Chalks designed for underwater use can mark surfaces for several hours before the water degrades them. Physical markers—weighted objects left at junctions—can help you identify where you have been. Do not use anything electronic. The environment is not kind to devices, and the noise and moisture will rapidly compromise their function.

### The Risk of Getting Lost

This is not a trivial risk. Travelers have become lost in the Exudation Tiers and never been found. The city is too vast, too dense, too changing. If you lose your way in the deep tiers, you may wander for days without encountering anyone who can help you. If you lose your way in the flooded sections, you may not emerge at all.

Set hard limits on your exploration. Decide in advance how far you will stray from known areas, and do not exceed those limits regardless of what you see. The city offers endless fascination, and that fascination is a trap. There is always another level, another passage, another glowing mineral formation that promises to be more beautiful than the last. Resist the pull. Turn back when your limits say to turn back.

## Biological Hazards: The Fauna and the Not-Plants

The fauna abundance index is high. This is a polite way of saying that the Exudation Tiers are swarming with life that has evolved to exploit every available niche—including you.

### The Insect-Analogs

The most immediately challenging fauna are the things that fill the insect niche. They are not precisely insects—they follow different evolutionary lineages—but they occupy similar ecological roles, and they exist in similar overwhelming numbers.

They swarm. They form living curtains in doorways. They land on any surface and drink the moisture beading there. That surface includes you.

Most of these creatures are not dangerous individually. Their mouthparts are designed for piercing thin biological films and mineral accretions, not for penetrating thick skin. But their saliva contains enzymes that begin digesting their food source before they drink it. These enzymes can cause localized skin reactions—itching, swelling, and in sensitive individuals, more serious inflammatory responses.

When millions of these creatures land on you over the course of a day, the cumulative effect becomes significant. You will itch everywhere. You may develop a diffuse rash. The open skin creates an entry point for the bacterial and fungal life that surrounds you.

**Protection:** Wear full-coverage clothing. Treat your clothing with a hydrophobic sealant that discourages landing. Move slowly through swarm areas—rapid movement attracts attention. Do not attempt to brush swarms away with exposed hands; this merely exposes more skin.

### The Larger Fauna

The hairless cat-like creatures that skitter along vertical surfaces are generally harmless. They have evolved to eat the smaller insect-analogs and the bacterial mats. They will ignore you unless you threaten them. If you threaten them, they will bite, and their bites are designed to create wounds that fester in the humid environment. Do not threaten them.

The bird-like creatures that never land present no direct danger. They occupy airspace too high for normal interaction. However, their droppings are acidic and fall in a constant rain in certain areas. The residents know where these areas are and avoid them. Your guide should know as well.

The eyeless reptilian shapes that swim through the flooded lower levels are more concerning. They are adapted for an environment where sight is useless, and they navigate by vibration and chemical sensing. They are not aggressive, but they are large—some specimens exceed two meters—and they are strong. If you venture into flooded sections, move slowly and smoothly to avoid attracting their attention. They cannot see you, but they can feel you, and they will investigate interesting vibrations.

**Do not swim in the flooded sections.** The reptilian creatures are not the only things that live in those waters. Bacterial colonies thrive in the warm, still areas. Fungal mats grow in floating masses. And the reptiles themselves may mistake you for food—not because they want to eat you, but because they cannot see what you are.

### The Mineral Growths and Fungal Mats

The not-plants present their own hazards. The crystalline growths can be sharp and will cut exposed skin. The slime molds in yellow and grey produce spores that can colonize human lung tissue. The bacterial mats create iridescent films across standing water that are beautiful and dangerous; disturbing them releases clouds of bacteria into the air.

**Do not touch the colorful mats.** Do not step in the iridescent pools. Do not brush against the crystalline growths. The residents have developed some degree of immunity to the local microbiome. You have not.

Carry a basic antiseptic. Clean any cut immediately, no matter how small. The bacterial environment here is designed to exploit organic material, and an open wound is organic material.

## Psychological Preparation: What This Place Does to the Mind

The Exudation Tiers affect visitors in ways that extend beyond the physical. The combination of constant noise, constant moisture, constant warmth, and constant visual stimulation creates a psychological load that many travelers do not anticipate.

### Sensory Overload

Your brain is not designed to process this much input continuously. The sound alone creates cognitive fatigue. Add the humidity on your skin, the heat radiating from every surface, the visual complexity of the layered architecture, the movement of the teeming fauna, and the strange luminescence of the mineral growths—and you have an environment that overwhelms the sensory processing systems.

You will tire more quickly than you expect. You will make mistakes in navigation and judgment. You may experience confusion, irritability, and difficulty concentrating.

Plan for this. Limit your active hours. Rest frequently in the quietest accommodation you can find. Do not push through mental fatigue the way you might in other environments. The Exudation Tiers will not reward endurance with adaptation. They will punish overexertion with collapse.

### The Sensation of Being Inside a Living Thing

Many travelers report a persistent feeling that the city is aware of them. Not in a paranoid sense—though paranoia is common—but in a structural sense. The walls pulse. The water circulates. The heat radiates. The sounds emerge as if from respiration. The city behaves like a body, and being inside it feels like being inside a body.

This sensation does not fade with time. It may intensify. You may find yourself speaking to the walls, or apologizing when you brush against surfaces, or feeling watched by mineral accretions that cannot possibly see you.

This is normal. Or rather, this is a normal response to an environment that genuinely does share characteristics with living systems. The city does process matter. It does maintain itself. It does respond to damage and intrusion. Your brain is correctly identifying patterns that exist. The disorientation comes from the scale—you are not accustomed to being the microbe inside the organism.

### The Unknowable Technology

The city operates through mechanisms that no one fully understands. You will see things that should not work. Doors that open without visible mechanisms. Light that emerges from walls without apparent power sources. Water that circulates without pumps. Structures that repair themselves.

The residents use these systems without comprehending them. They inherit knowledge about which switches to press and which valves to turn. They do not know why the switches work or what the valves control.

This can create a feeling of unreality. You may begin to doubt your own perceptions. You may feel that you are in a dream, or a simulation, or a story that someone else is telling. This feeling is also common, and also a reasonable response to an unreasonable environment.

Accept that you will not understand what you see. Accept that the residents do not understand it either. Accept that the city was built by processes that exceed current knowledge, and that its operation is inherited rather than designed. You are not failing to comprehend something comprehensible. You are encountering something that exceeds comprehension.

## Social Navigation: The Human Element

The Exudation Tiers are inhabited. The residents are descendants of the original builders and migrants who came later, drawn by the strange fertility of this wet mineral world. They are adapted to their environment in ways you are not. They have reduced auditory canals or alternative sensory mechanisms. They have immunity to local microbiomes. They have tolerance for the heat and the humidity and the noise that you cannot match.

They also have their own social structures, their own politics, their own conflicts, and their own expectations about visitors.

### Respect the Adaptation

The residents are not disadvantaged by their adaptations. They are optimized for their environment. Do not pity them for their reduced hearing or their different sensory modalities. Do not assume that your baseline human experience is superior to theirs. In the Exudation Tiers, they are the baseline. You are the deviation.

Show respect for their knowledge. They know things about this place that no external visitor will ever learn. They can navigate sections that would trap you forever. They can read the weather patterns that the city generates. They can predict fauna population events and avoid the worst swarms.

Do not condescend. Do not assume that their lack of theoretical understanding indicates a lack of practical knowledge. They keep the city running through accumulated practice. That practice is worth more than any theoretical framework you might bring.

### The Economics of the Tiers

The residents do not use currency in the way most visitors expect. They trade in knowledge, in access, in maintenance labor, and in the products of the city's biological and mineral output. The crystalline growths have value. The bacterial mats, properly processed, have value. The water itself, filtered through specific systems, has value.

Bring trade goods if you intend to stay any length of time. Small, durable, useful items—sealants, precision tools, medical supplies. Do not bring currency unless you have confirmed in advance that the specific residents you will interact with accept it.

### The Politics of Space

The city is crowded. Every cubic meter is claimed. Space is the primary resource and the primary source of conflict. Different groups control different sections, and passage through controlled space requires negotiation.

Your guide should handle these negotiations. Do not attempt to negotiate for yourself unless you are very confident in your understanding of local protocols. You may inadvertently commit to obligations you do not understand, or offend groups whose territory you are traversing.

### The Unwritten Rules

I cannot document all of the social norms you will encounter. They vary by tier, by section, by the history of the specific inhabitants. But some general principles apply:

- Do not block passages. The crowded architecture means that stopping in a thoroughfare is a serious imposition.
- Do not touch water sources without permission. The water circulation is life, and contamination is a grave offense.
- Do not disturb the mineral accretions or fungal mats without permission. Some of these growths are cultivated.
- Do not assume that the absence of plants means the absence of agriculture. The residents farm bacterial mats, fungal colonies, and certain mineral formations.
- Do not photograph residents without explicit consent. The sound of your equipment will be audible, and the act will be noticed.

I have mentioned many items throughout this guide. Here, I compile them into a single inventory.

### Respiratory
- Twenty or more hydrophobic mesh face coverings
- Prophylactic antifungal medication (begin one week before arrival)
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (for emergency use only)

### Auditory
- Industrial-grade hearing protection with extended frequency range
- Bone conduction dampening headband or casing
- Sufficient battery capacity for your entire stay

### Thermal
- Light, hydrophobic, loose-weave full-coverage clothing (multiple changes)
- Wearable cooling system for extended stays
- Hydrophobic sealant treatment for clothing

### Navigation
- Funds and arrangements for a local guide
- Pressure-based depth gauge
- Underwater-use chalks for path marking
- Physical markers for junction identification

### Biological Protection
- Full-coverage clothing treated with hydrophobic sealant

### General
- Trade goods appropriate to your length of stay
- A signed language guide for the local communication system
- Documentation of your planned routes and limits
- Contact information for emergency extraction, if such services are available

## Final Preparation: Accepting What You Cannot Control

The Exudation Tiers are not a destination you conquer. They are an environment you survive. The distinction matters.

You can prepare perfectly and still encounter something unexpected—a construction event that changes the layout of your planned route, a fauna population surge that makes certain areas impassable, a social conflict that traps you in a section longer than intended. The city operates by rules that are not designed around your comfort or your schedule.

Accept this before you arrive. Accept that you will be wet for the duration of your stay. Accept that you will be hot. Accept that you will be unable to hear clearly and unable to see clearly and unable to move freely. Accept that the city will press against you from every direction, that it will not pause for you, that it will not adjust for you.

If you can accept these things, you may find what I found: a strange, overwhelming, haunting beauty that changes your understanding of what a city can be. The light fracturing through crystalline growths. The iridescent bacterial mats shifting across standing water. The architecture of ten thousand years of building, all of it wet, all of it warm, all of it alive with the movement of water and creatures and people who have made this place their own.

The Exudation Tiers are not for everyone. But for those who prepare properly, they offer something that cannot be found anywhere else in the known multiverse: the experience of being inside a living process, a city that is more organism than structure, a world that generates itself continuously and will continue generating long after you have left.

Go prepared. Go humble. Go knowing that you are a visitor in a body that does not need you.

And when you return—if you return—know that you will carry this place with you. The humidity in your lungs. The sound in your bones. The memory of water weeping from walls that pulse with their own circulation.

You will not forget the Exudation Tiers. They will not let you.
