Preparing for the Glistening Expanse

## Before You Arrive

### Mental Preparation

You must understand what you are stepping into. The Glistening Expanse is not merely a place but a condition—a state of near-completion. Most worlds you visit will be in media res, in the middle of their becoming. This place has nearly finished becoming. It is waiting in the way a held breath waits. You will feel this immediately, and it may unsettle you more than any active danger would.

The absence of animals will affect you more than you expect. Your brain has evolved to interpret silence as threat, to parse animal sounds as information. Here, there is nothing to parse. The grass does not rustle with movement. No birds call distance. No insects hum. After the first few hours, you may notice your own body sounds—breathing, heartbeat, the fabric of your clothes—with startling clarity. Some travelers report feeling exposed, as though they are the only moving thing in a world that has forgotten why movement matters.

Prepare yourself for the silence. Not by practicing silence, but by practicing *presence* in silence. Learn to sit without audio input. Learn to notice your own internal sounds without anxiety. If you habitually use music or podcasts to fill quiet spaces, stop doing so at least a week before arrival. The plateau will not accommodate your need for auditory distraction, and fighting it will exhaust you.

The reality erosion will require a different kind of preparation. You cannot train for it directly, but you can cultivate flexibility. Practice holding two contradictory memories simultaneously. Practice noticing when your perception shifts. Keep a small journal where you record what you observe, immediately after observing it. The written word will become your anchor—you will need something outside yourself that does not shift.

### Physical Conditioning

You will walk. You will walk endlessly. The plateau is not merely large but effectively infinite for practical purposes. Settlements are separated by weeks of travel. There are no beasts of burden, no vehicles, no shortcuts. If you want to reach a particular location, you will walk there, and you will carry everything you need on your back.

Train your body for long-distance walking over flat terrain. This is different from hiking in varied landscapes. The plateau's flatness is absolute—no ascents to break the monotony, no descents to provide relief. Your legs will perform the same motion thousands of times per day, and the mental challenge will exceed the physical one. Boredom will be your primary adversary.

Condition your feet specifically. You will be walking on stone much of the time—smooth, weathered gneiss that can become slick when wet. Your boots should have excellent grip and be broken in thoroughly before arrival. Blisters here will not heal quickly; the cold and moisture slow all biological processes, including recovery.

Build endurance for cold without intensity. The temperature hovers near freezing—cold enough to be uncomfortable, not cold enough to kill quickly. This is in some ways more difficult to manage than extreme cold. Your body will never fully adapt, but will constantly expend energy maintaining temperature. Train yourself to function in similar conditions, and learn to recognize the early signs of hypothermia: confusion, lethargy, poor coordination. You may not shiver. The cold here works by degrees.

### Supplies and Equipment

Your gear must serve multiple functions and fail gracefully. Redundancy is essential, but weight matters—you will carry everything.

**Navigation Equipment**

Standard compasses work on the plateau, but the shifting distances make directional navigation unreliable. A journey north may take longer or shorter than the same journey south. Instead, rely on landmark navigation when possible. The pillar-moss towers, the root-mountains, the settlements—these provide reference points that hold more consistently than cardinal directions.

Carry a mechanical timepiece. Digital devices may behave unpredictably near areas of reality erosion, and battery life will suffer in the cold. A quality mechanical watch, wound daily, will give you a personal time reference independent of the plateau's temporal anomalies. Write down the time when you depart and when you arrive. Note discrepancies. This data may help you identify which areas have stronger distortions.

Consider carrying a length of twine or light rope—not for climbing, but for marking paths through the pillar-moss forests. The moss-towers can create maze-like formations, and in fog conditions, visibility drops to meters. A physical trail marker you can follow back may save your life.

**Clothing**

Layering is essential. The temperature fluctuates between slightly below and slightly above freezing, and you will need to adjust throughout the day. Base layers of moisture-wicking material—silk or synthetic, not cotton, which holds dampness against the skin. Mid-layers for insulation; wool remains functional when wet, though the plateau's inhabitants do not use animal products, and you may wish to respect local norms. Outer layers must block wind while allowing moisture to escape; the slow rain soaks through anything that is not specifically waterproof.

Cover your extremities. Thin gloves that allow dexterity, with thicker mittens to pull over them when you are not using your hands. A hat that covers your ears. A scarf or neck gaiter that can be pulled up over your face in fog or wind. The cold is patient; it will find any exposed skin and work on it steadily.

Footwear must be waterproof. The shallow water-channels are everywhere, and you will cross them constantly. Boots that become waterlogged will not dry during daylight hours; they will freeze overnight. Gaiters help prevent water from entering over the tops of your boots. Carry extra socks and change them whenever your feet feel damp—wet feet in near-freezing conditions invite tissue damage.

**Shelter and Sleep**

A tent designed for alpine conditions will serve you well here. The wind is constant but rarely severe; you do not need a fortress, but you do need something that will not collapse under the weight of accumulated frost or the pressure of extended fog-dampness. Stake it securely; there are no trees to break the wind, and the stone does not hold stakes well. Look for hollows where soil has accumulated, or use the grass-mats as anchors.

Your sleeping bag should be rated below the temperatures you expect. The plateau's cold is pervasive; it rises from the stone as much as it descends from the air. An insulating pad is essential—you cannot sleep directly on the rock without losing heat faster than your body can generate it. Consider a closed-cell foam pad rather than an inflatable; punctures happen, and the plateau offers nothing to repair them with.

**Food and Water**

Water is available everywhere but requires treatment. The slow-moving channels collect debris, and while animal waste is not a concern, fungal and bacterial contamination is. Boil what you collect, or filter it through material fine enough to remove particulates. The locals drink from certain springs they know to be clean; if you stay in a settlement, accept their guidance on water sources.

Food is more complicated. The plateau produces enough to sustain its inhabitants, but not enough surplus to feed travelers easily. Carry your own supplies, but be prepared to trade for local food if you stay in settlements. The reed-grass seeds are nutritious but bland; the succulent mats under trees can be eaten but provide little caloric value; the trees produce fruits that nothing gathers, which are edible but may not be familiar to your digestion. Bring calorie-dense foods that do not spoil quickly—nuts, dried fruit, hard cheeses if your climate allows, preserved meats if you are not concerned about violating local norms.

Be aware that food behaves strangely here. Some travelers report stored food spoiling overnight; others find that perishables remain fresh for weeks. There is no predicting which you will encounter. Check your supplies regularly. If something spoils, discard it immediately; the plateau's decay is slow, and consuming something partially spoiled may affect you more severely than it would elsewhere.

**Tools**

A knife is essential. Stone blades are possible to make but difficult to master; the locals spend years learning the skill. A good steel knife will serve you better, and a multi-tool with additional functions is worth the weight.

Fire-starting equipment must be reliable and redundant. The plateau offers fuel—dried grass, dead wood—but lighting it in damp conditions requires practice. Carry waterproof matches, a ferro rod, and a lighter. Keep the lighter warm against your body; butane lighters function poorly in cold temperatures. Practice starting fires with damp materials before you arrive.

Carry repair materials—cord, patches, adhesive. The plateau offers little that can repair modern equipment. If something breaks, you must fix it yourself or do without.

**Medical Supplies**

Your kit should address the specific hazards of this environment. Hypothermia treatment: emergency blankets, heat packs, the knowledge of how to rewarm a person gradually rather than rapidly. Wound care: bandages that stay adhered in damp conditions, antiseptic (decay happens slowly here, but infection still occurs). Pain management: analgesics for joint pain and headaches, which you may develop from the constant cold and the atmospheric pressure at altitude. Digestive aids: unfamiliar food and water may upset your system.

Any prescription medications you require should be brought in quantities exceeding what you expect to need. Time passes strangely here; a week may feel like a day, or a day like a week. Do not assume you will be able to leave when you planned. Bring extra.

**Recording Equipment**

You will want to document what you experience, but standard equipment may fail. The humidity affects electronics; the cold drains batteries; the reality erosion may corrupt digital storage. Consider analog alternatives—a film camera with mechanical functions, a physical journal with waterproof paper and ink that will not run when damp.

Write everything down. Not just observations, but times, distances, directions. Record what you remember of each day before sleeping, while memory is fresh. If you notice discrepancies later—your notes say you traveled north, but your compass says you are south of where you started—note those too. Patterns may emerge that help you navigate the anomalies.

## Upon Arrival

### Acclimatization

You will need time to adjust. The altitude—two and a half kilometers—will affect you if you arrive from a lower elevation. You may feel short of breath, dizzy, unusually fatigued. These symptoms will pass as your body produces more red blood cells, but the process takes days to weeks. Move slowly. Do not exert yourself unnecessarily. Hydrate constantly; the thin air and cold accelerate moisture loss through respiration.

The cold will penetrate you. It does not strike; it seeps. After hours of exposure, you may find that your hands are clumsy, your thoughts slightly slowed, your movements slightly imprecise. This is mild hypothermia. Stop, warm yourself, drink something hot if possible. Pushing through will only worsen the condition.

The silence will take longer to accept. Your mind will generate sounds to fill the absence—phantom rustles, imaginary voices, the sense of something moving just outside your field of vision. This is normal. It will fade as you acclimate, or it will become manageable. If it becomes distressing, humming quietly to yourself provides just enough sound to calm the instinct that interprets silence as danger.

The light will seem strange. The thin atmosphere makes the sky pale rather than deep blue; the sun is bright but without warmth; shadows are crisp and dark because there is no haze to scatter light. At night, the stars may seem closer than you are accustomed to, the darkness absolute when clouds do not cover the sky. Give your eyes time to adjust to the new parameters.

### Reading the Landscape

Learn to see the plateau's subtle variations. What appears uniformly flat has texture if you know where to look.

The shallow depressions where water collects are slightly darker than the surrounding stone—moisture stains the gneiss a deeper grey. Learn to recognize these from a distance; they indicate both water sources and the soil where vegetation grows thickest.

The grass stands tell you about wind patterns. Where the grass leans consistently, the prevailing wind direction; where it grows taller, areas protected from the worst of the weather; where it grows sparse, areas of particularly harsh conditions or recent flooding.

The trees mark the deepest soil and the most reliable water. They space themselves according to the resources beneath them; where trees grow closer together, the land is richer, and settlements are more likely to be nearby.

The pillar-mosses indicate stability. They only form where conditions have been consistent for centuries—no flooding, no severe temperature fluctuations, no significant disturbance. Their presence suggests a safe area to establish camp. However, be alert to their patterns. If they seem to form deliberate shapes—circles, lines, arrows—note this but do not assume they were made for you. They may be building something beyond human comprehension.

The horizon is your constant companion. Watch it for changes. If structures appear—immense, indistinct, unfamiliar—observe them without approaching. If lights blink in patterns, count the intervals and note the locations. If mountains seem to rise and then fade, document the direction and duration. These phenomena may be meaningless, or they may be warnings. Do not investigate personally unless you have no other choice.

## Navigation and Movement

### The Problem of Distance

You cannot trust distance to remain constant. A settlement three days' travel to the north may be four days' travel back. A landmark visible on the horizon may take longer to reach than it appears to warrant. The plateau stretches and compresses according to rules you do not know.

Compensate by over-preparing. When traveling between known points, carry enough supplies for half again as long as the journey should take. If you estimate five days, carry for seven or eight. The weight will slow you, but running out of food in a place where distances shift is worse than traveling slightly burdened.

Travel in straight lines when possible. The shortest distance between two points is straight; if the distance itself varies, at least you will not add extra variation by curving your path. Pick a landmark on the horizon and walk toward it. If the landmark vanishes—which can happen—stop and reassess. Do not continue without a destination; the plateau is large enough to become lost in permanently.

If you become disoriented, stop. Set up camp. Wait for the fog to clear, if fog is the problem, or for the light to improve, if it is near dawn or dusk. Walking blindly will only increase the distance between you and anywhere useful.

### The Problem of Time

Time passes differently in different areas. This is not perception; it is measurable. If two travelers separate and then reunite, their timepieces may show different elapsed times. A day of walking may correspond to half a day in a settlement, or a day and a half.

To the extent possible, synchronize with local time when you are in settlements. Ask what day the locals believe it to be. Compare to your own records. Note the discrepancy and whether time runs faster or slower relative to your baseline.

When traveling alone, your timepiece becomes your only reliable reference. Trust it over your subjective sense of duration. If you feel you have walked for hours but your watch shows only one hour has passed, believe the watch. If you feel you have walked briefly but your watch shows many hours, trust the watch. Your perception is being affected; the mechanism is not.

Do not make time-sensitive plans. If you arrange to meet someone at a particular location on a particular day, understand that you may arrive early or late through no fault of your own. Build flexibility into every schedule. The plateau does not operate on human time.

### Traveling Between Settlements

When moving from one settlement to another, obtain as much information as possible before departing. Ask about water sources, terrain variations, weather patterns, and any unusual phenomena along the route. The locals have lived with the plateau's strangeness for generations; they know which areas to avoid and which to trust.

Hire a guide if one is available. The locals navigate by landmark and memory in ways that no instrument can replicate. They know that certain rock formations, certain moss-pillars, certain trees mark progress along a route. They know which areas distort distance more severely and which are relatively stable. A guide may cost you—trade goods, labor, favors—but the cost is worth it.

If you must travel alone, mark your path. Leave cairns of small stones, or tie bits of cloth to grass stalks, or use any other method that creates visible trail markers. These will help you find your way back if you need to retreat, and they may help others find you if you become lost.

Avoid traveling in dense fog unless you have no choice. Visibility drops to meters, landmarks vanish, and the silence becomes absolute. Wait in place if possible. If you must move, move slowly, testing each step before committing your weight.

### Encounters with Anomalous Zones

You may encounter areas where reality erosion is more pronounced—where objects vanish, where time moves at extreme rates, where the horizon shows things that should not exist.

If you notice objects moving or vanishing, stop and observe. Does the effect seem localized? Can you identify its boundaries? Note the location relative to landmarks and avoid passing through that area if possible. If you must pass through, move quickly and do not set anything down.

If time moves extremely fast or slow, you may not notice immediately. Pay attention to your body's signals—hunger, fatigue, thirst. If you feel suddenly ravenous, time may have accelerated; hours may have passed in what felt like minutes. If you feel strangely rested despite walking for hours, time may have slowed. Check your timepiece, but understand that it too may be affected.

If you see structures on the horizon—immense, indistinct, impossible—do not approach them. They are not settlements. They may not be real. But they may be real enough to trap you, to change you, to pull you somewhere you cannot return from. Observe from a distance, document what you see, and continue in another direction.

If you encounter an area where you cannot proceed—not physically blocked, but simply unable to continue—do not force yourself. The plateau's edges are unknown, but they may be closer than the maps suggest. Or you may have found a fold, a crease in reality where forward ceases to have meaning. Turn back. Find another route. Some boundaries are not meant to be crossed.

## Interacting with Settlements

### Approach and Reception

Settlements on the Glistening Expanse are isolated by weeks of travel. Visitors are rare, and therefore notable. You will be seen coming from a long distance—the flat terrain offers no concealment—and your arrival will be observed before you arrive.

Do not attempt to sneak up on a settlement. The emptiness of the plateau means that any movement is tracked automatically by anyone looking in that direction. Approach openly, during daylight hours if possible. Make your presence known with a shout or a wave when you are close enough to be recognized as human.

The reception you receive will depend on the settlement's size, location, and recent history. Larger towns see more travelers; they will be more accustomed to strangers. Smaller settlements may view you with suspicion or fear. Some may welcome you as a carrier of news. Others may see you as a burden, another mouth to feed in a place where food is grown by hand.

Be prepared to explain yourself. Who are you? Where do you come from? What do you want? The locals have no frame of reference for multiverse travel; they will interpret your answers through their own understanding. You may choose to say you come from beyond the plateau's edges, a traveler seeking new places. This is true enough and will not require explanations they cannot process.

### Trade and Exchange

Money is useless here. The settlements operate on trade—goods for goods, labor for goods, goods for labor. If you want something, you must offer something of comparable value.

Travelers often carry items that are valuable precisely because they are foreign. Metal objects, if you have them, will be highly prized. Tools, knives, needles, wire—anything made of metal will trade well. The plateau has no metal of its own; everything must be salvaged or imported at tremendous cost.

Knowledge may also be valuable. If you have skills the settlements lack—healing techniques, engineering principles, information about distant places—you may be able to trade services for supplies. Be cautious about offering knowledge that might disrupt their way of life. The plateau's equilibrium is ancient and carefully maintained. Introducing foreign technologies or ideas may have consequences you cannot predict.

If you have nothing to trade, you may be able to exchange labor for hospitality. The settlements always need work—fields to tend, buildings to repair, water channels to clear. You will not be the first traveler to pay for lodging with effort. Work willingly and well; your reputation will precede you to the next settlement.

### Social Norms and Etiquette

Speak quietly. This is not a rule but a practice shaped by the environment. Sound does not carry far, and silence is the baseline. A voice at conversational level fills a room. Speaking loudly feels aggressive, intrusive. Modulate your volume to match the locals'.

Do not ask about the anomaly. The locals do not discuss the reality erosion as a separate phenomenon; they live within it. Asking why time moves strangely will receive the same response as asking why the wind blows—because it does, because that is how things are here. If you press, they may become uncomfortable. Accept that some things simply are.

Respect the buildings. Some structures are older than anyone remembers. Some rooms feel wrong. The locals have learned which spaces are safe and which to avoid. Do not enter areas that the residents seem to be avoiding. Do not investigate strange architecture uninvited. The buildings have reasons for being what they are.

Accept the pace. Everything here is slow. Meals take time to prepare. Conversations take time to unfold. Journeys take time to complete. The impatience you may feel is your own, not the environment's fault. Breathe. Wait. Watch. The plateau rewards patience.

### Long-Term Stays

If you remain in a settlement for an extended period, you will be expected to contribute. The settlements survive through collective effort; no one eats who does not work. Your skills will be assessed and a role found for you. Accept it graciously. You may learn things about this place that you could never learn as a passing traveler.

Be aware that staying long-term may affect your relationship with time. You may begin to lose track of days, to feel that hours are longer or shorter than they used to be. This is normal for the plateau. Your records will help you maintain some connection to your previous temporal framework, but you may find yourself caring less about exact durations. The locals do not count hours; they count tasks completed, seasons passed, generations come and gone.

If you begin to feel that the plateau is where you belong—that the life here is right, that the slow cold quiet is what you have always wanted—pay attention to this feeling. It may be genuine acclimatization, or it may be the plateau claiming you. There are stories of travelers who came and never left, who forgot they were travelers, who became part of the settlement's memory and passed from visitor to resident to ancestor. There are worse fates.

## Departure

### Choosing When to Leave

You will know when it is time. Perhaps the strangeness will become too much—the discrepancies between your records and your memories, the objects that appear and disappear, the sense that the horizon is closer than it should be. Perhaps the cold will have worn you down, or the silence will have begun to feel like pressure rather than peace. Perhaps you will have learned what you came to learn and will feel the pull of other worlds.

Do not delay departure once you have decided. The plateau has a way of making leaving feel less urgent, of convincing you that one more day, one more week, one more season would make no difference. But days here are not the same as days elsewhere, and weeks may be months in the worlds you came from. The longer you stay, the harder it becomes to remember why you would leave.

### Preparation for Departure

Restock your supplies. Even if you plan to transit directly out of this reality, carry enough for several days of travel. Transitions do not always work as intended. You may emerge somewhere unexpected. You may need to survive until your next opportunity.

Settle your accounts. Return borrowed items, fulfill any promises you have made, leave the settlements on good terms. The plateau remembers, and the people here are part of it. If you have debts—material or social—they may follow you in ways you cannot predict.

Say goodbye to those you have come to know. The settlements are small; everyone knows everyone. Your absence will be noted and felt. Acknowledging this, marking your departure with words, is both polite and meaningful. You may never return; they may never see another traveler like you. The farewell matters.

### What You Take With You

You will carry more than supplies when you leave. The plateau changes those who walk it.

The silence will stay with you. After days or weeks of the plateau's quiet, the sounds of other worlds will seem overwhelming—animal calls, machinery, the constant background hum of places where things are still being made. You may crave silence for some time after departing. This will fade, but the memory of true silence will remain.

The patience will stay with you. Having walked for days without anything happening, having waited for fog to clear, for rain to stop, for time to pass, you will have learned something about endurance that cannot be unlearned. This may serve you well in other worlds.

The uncertainty will stay with you. Having seen objects vanish and time shift and horizons show impossible things, you will know that reality is not as solid as it seems. This knowledge is not comfortable, but it is valuable. You will be less surprised by strangeness, more prepared to accept that the universe contains things you do not understand.

The cold will stay with you, in your bones, for days or weeks after you leave. You will feel it at odd moments—a chill that has nothing to do with temperature, a reminder of the plateau's patient preservation. Let it pass. It is only memory, only the residue of a place that holds things for a long time.

## Final Notes

The Glistening Expanse is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is simply what remains when everything else has worn away—stone and water and cold and life that grows slowly and people who survive through accumulated knowledge and patience.

You can walk it safely if you prepare. You can learn from it if you observe. You can leave it if you do not let it claim you.

But understand that the plateau has been here longer than you can imagine. It has seen travelers before. It will see travelers after. It does not need you, and it does not object to you. It simply is—flat and cold and quiet, waiting for the next stage of existence, whatever that may be.

Walk lightly. Watch carefully. Leave when it is time.

And if you find yourself thinking that the silence is actually quite beautiful, that the cold is clean and clarifying, that the slow gray life here has a rightness to it that the busy worlds lack—stop. Check your records. Note the time. And begin planning your departure immediately.

The plateau welcomes those who wish to stay.

That is precisely the danger.
