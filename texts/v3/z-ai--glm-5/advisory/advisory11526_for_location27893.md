# Preparation Guide: The Weeping Plateau

## Before You Arrive

### Physical Conditioning (Start 4-6 Weeks Prior)

You will be walking on wet stone at altitude for extended periods. Your cardiovascular system needs preparation for thin air that "leaves your lungs faster than it enters." Begin altitude training if possible—even simulated altitude environments will help. Focus on endurance over strength; the plateau demands patience, not power.

Practice walking on uneven, slippery surfaces. Find wet rock faces, moss-covered paths, any terrain that punishes haste. Learn to trust your balance slowly. Develop the instinct to test each foothold before committing weight. The travelers who fall here are almost always the ones who moved too quickly.

### Respiratory Assessment

The plateau's atmosphere is genuinely thin. If you have any respiratory conditions—asthma, reduced lung capacity, susceptibility to altitude sickness—consult a medical professional versed in multiverse physiology. Consider acquiring supplemental oxygen, though know that carrying it will mark you immediately as someone who hasn't acclimated, and the weight may prove more burdensome than the condition it addresses.

## Essential Equipment

### Footwear

This is your most critical choice. The plateau's stone is perpetually wet, and the moss that covers much of it provides no traction—only the illusion of it before your foot slides sideways.

**What works:**
- Mountaineering boots with aggressive lug patterns, but avoid traditional Vibram soles, which skate on wet rock. Seek out compound rubbers designed for amphibious terrain.
- Boots that drain. They will get wet. Accept this and choose footwear that doesn't retain water against your skin.
- Ankle support matters more than waterproofing. The carved stairs have been worn smooth by centuries of water and passing feet; an ankle that twists here will heal slowly, poorly, possibly never correctly.

**What fails:**
- Anything marketed as "waterproof." The plateau's water is patient. It will find its way in, and then it will stay, pooled against your skin in ways that cause blisters, then rawness, then infection.
- Approach shoes, trail runners, any footwear optimized for speed. Speed is not your ally here.

### Layering System

The cold is not temperature but absence. It works differently than the cold you know. It doesn't bite; it erodes.

**Base layer:** Merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking fabric. Do not wear cotton. Cotton kills in wet cold—it absorbs water and holds it against your skin, creating a slow hypothermia that you won't notice until your hands stop working properly.

**Mid layer:** Fleece or wool, something that retains warmth even when damp. You will be damp. Accept this.

**Outer layer:** A shell that blocks wind but breathes. The plateau generates its own weather; sudden fog, sudden temperature drops, rain that falls "like small expenditures of violence." But sealed shells create their own problems—your body heat will condense inside, and you'll be wet from within instead of from without.

Bring more socks than seems reasonable. Dry feet are the difference between a successful traversal and a medical evacuation that may not be possible.

### Navigation Equipment

**Do not rely on:**
- GPS systems. The plateau's density value of 1.8 and its unique material composition interfere with satellite signals in unpredictable ways.
- Compass readings. The plateau's stone contains magnetic anomalies that make compass needles drift, settle, and sometimes simply point toward the nearest large cistern as if drawn to the water itself.

**What works:**
- Physical mapping. Paper, waterproofed. Mark your route as you go. The city's density makes it easy to lose track of your entry point.
- Visual landmarks. The plateau is vast, but certain carved structures stand out—a shrine marker on a high terrace, a cistern of unusual size, a passage that opens onto a dramatic overlook. Note these. They may be your only way back.
- Your own breadcrumbs. The city is so dense that leaving a trail of small objects—waterproof, non-organic, nothing that the moss or water will claim—may help you recognize passages you've already walked.

### Water and Food

**Water:**
The irony is immediate: water everywhere, but can you drink it?

The plateau's water seeps through stone, collects in cisterns that may hold salamander-things or older organisms, and has been moving through carved channels for centuries. It tastes of mineral, of absence, of something you can't name.

Bring purification tablets and a quality filter. Test any cistern water before drinking deeply. Some travelers report the water being perfectly safe; others describe intestinal distress that lasted weeks. The difference may be in which cistern they chose, or it may be in the travelers themselves—some constitutions handle the plateau's water; others don't.

Carry enough clean water for three days minimum. This is heavy but may save your life if the plateau's water doesn't agree with you.

**Food:**
There is nothing to forage. The moss is not food. The sparse grasses hold no nutrition your body can access. The insects are too small to bother with, and the salamander-things are too rare, too possibly-significant, to kill for sustenance.

Bring calorie-dense, waterproof food. Nuts, dried meat, fat-based energy bars, hard cheeses that won't spoil in damp conditions. Plan for more than you think you need; the altitude and cold increase caloric requirements, and the plateau's strange atmosphere seems to accelerate hunger in ways that don't correlate with physical exertion.

### Lighting

The plateau's light value of 2.2 means normal visibility during daylit hours—but the afternoon light transforms everything, and the description mentions the city dimming "room by room" as sunset approaches. You will want to see this, but you will also need to navigate after dark.

Bring:
- A headlamp with red-light mode. The plateau's silence and atmosphere make bright white light feel aggressive, intrusive, wrong. Red light preserves night vision and feels less like a violation of the space.
- Backup batteries. Cold drains power cells faster.
- A small lantern for your sleeping space. The rooms go dark completely at night, and the psychological comfort of a small light should not be underestimated.

### Shelter and Rest

**You do not need a tent.** The city provides rooms, passages, terraces—carved spaces that offer shelter from weather. Carrying a tent adds weight and marks you as someone who doesn't trust the plateau's architecture.

**You do need:**
- A sleeping bag rated for cold, damp conditions. Down insulation fails when wet; synthetic is heavier but more reliable here.
- A sleeping pad that provides insulation from cold stone. The rock holds a deep chill that will leach heat from your body through the night.
- A waterproof bag for your sleeping system. The rooms are not wet inside, precisely, but the air holds moisture that settles on everything. Your sleeping bag will become damp just from existing in these spaces; minimize this by keeping it sealed when not in use.

## Psychological Preparation

This may be more important than the physical.

### Solitude

You will likely see no one. The city is empty—not abandoned, empty. The distinction matters. Abandoned places hold the ghost of bustle, the echo of past presence. Empty places have simply never been occupied, or have emptied so thoroughly that the emptiness itself has become the inhabitant.

Prepare for isolation. If you are traveling alone, understand that the only voice you will hear is your own. The silence has weight. It presses against your ears, your thoughts. Some travelers find this peaceful; others find it corroding. Know which you are before you arrive.

If possible, travel with one companion. Not a group—groups make too much noise, violate the plateau's atmosphere, become the wrong kind of presence. But one other person, someone who understands the need for quiet, can anchor you when the city's strange density starts to feel like it's pressing in from all sides.

### The Sense of Being Watched

Travelers report a consistent phenomenon: the feeling that the city itself is aware of them. Not hostile. Not welcoming. Just... aware. The carved rooms, the water channels, the moss spreading millimeter by millimeter—it can feel like the components of a vast organism, and you are the microbe walking across its skin.

This is not dangerous. It is, however, disquieting. Prepare for it. Expect it. When you feel the weight of attention from something that isn't there, remind yourself that this is a documented response to the plateau's density and silence. You are being watched by the architecture. It means you no harm. It is simply... observing.

### The Temptation to Stay

The plateau exerts a pull. The rooms are adequate, the water eternal, the temperature mild. The light transforms everything to beauty in the afternoon. The silence stops being oppressive and becomes restful. You could stay here, the thought whispers. You could walk forever through carved rooms and never see the same one twice. You could stop walking entirely and let the plateau hold you.

Some travelers have stayed. Not many, but some. They were found weeks or months later, sitting in cisterns or lying on terraces, alive but not entirely present. Their eyes held the plateau's grey half-light, and when they spoke, they spoke about water, about stone, about waiting for something they couldn't name.

Bring something that anchors you to elsewhere. A physical object that means enough to pull you back. A letter from someone who expects your return. A task left unfinished. Something that matters more than the plateau's seductive emptiness.

## Arrival Protocol

### Initial Hours

Do not rush to explore. The plateau is vast; it will be there tomorrow, and the next day, and the day after that. Rushing leads to falls, to disorientation, to the kind of mistakes that compound in a place with no medical infrastructure.

**First:**
- Breathe. Let your lungs adjust to the thin air. Move slowly until you understand how the atmosphere feels in your body.
- Find water. Locate a cistern or basin, test the water, purify it. Know where your hydration will come from.
- Choose a base. A room near the plateau's edge, near your entry point, with good visibility and multiple exits. Make it yours for however long you stay.

**Then:**
- Walk. Short distances at first. Return to your base before you're tired. The plateau's scale defeats comprehension; you cannot map it in a day, and trying will only exhaust you.
- Observe. Watch how the water moves. Notice which way the drainage channels flow. Begin to understand the plateau's logic—its circulatory system of water and stone.
- Listen. To the silence, to the small sounds that do exist—footsteps, water clicking against itself, the wind when it arrives. Let your ears calibrate to the acoustic deadness.

### Daily Rhythm

The plateau has its own rhythm, but you are not of it. You need sleep in longer blocks than the city's perpetual-weft allows.

**Morning:** The light comes slowly here. The clouds that the plateau sits among filter everything to a diffuse grey before true dawn. Wake when you can see clearly. Eat. Drink. Check your feet for blisters, your skin for the beginning of sores.

**Midday:** The best hours for exploration. The light is strongest, the water surfaces most visible. Walk as far as you comfortably can, then return. Mark your path. Note landmarks. Leave nothing behind.

**Afternoon:** The light changes. This is the plateau's moment of transformation, when "every wet stone becomes a mirror" and the city "blazes with indirect light." Watch it. This is why you came. But watch it from a place you know, not from somewhere unfamiliar. Getting lost as darkness falls is how travelers disappear.

**Evening:** Return to your base. Eat. Dry your feet. Prepare for night. The temperature will drop—not dramatically, but enough to matter. The city will dim room by room. The silence will deepen.

**Night:** Sleep. The plateau has no predators, no dangers that hunt after dark. But it does have the darkness itself, and in that darkness, your mind may supply what isn't there. If you wake in the night, do not explore by headlamp. Stay still. Wait for morning.

## Hazards and How to Avoid Them

### Falls

The wet stone is treacherous. The moss is worse. The carved stairs have been worn smooth by generations of water and feet; they offer no traction, only the illusion of it.

**Prevention:**
- Test every foothold before committing weight.
- Keep your hands free. You will need them for balance.
- Do not walk quickly. Ever. Speed is not your ally on the Weeping Plateau.
- If carrying a pack, ensure it doesn't pull you backward. The weight distribution matters more here than on solid ground.

### Hypothermia

The wet cold is insidious. You won't feel yourself becoming hypothermic until you're fumbling with buckles you could normally manipulate in your sleep.

**Prevention:**
- Stay dry where possible. Change socks frequently. Dry your feet at night.
- Eat more than you think you need. Your body burns calories to stay warm.
- If you start feeling confused, clumsy, or unusually tired, stop. Get warm. Drink something warm if you can. Hypothermia takes time to develop and time to reverse.

### Disorientation

The city's density makes every direction look the same. The carved rooms, passages, and terraces repeat without pattern you can easily recognize. You can walk for hours and realize you've been circling.

**Prevention:**
- Mark your path. Physically. With objects you can recognize.
- Note landmarks. High shrines, large cisterns, collapsed sections.
- If you lose your way, stop. Do not continue walking. Sit. Think. Try to remember the last landmark you recognized. Backtrack if you can. If you can't, stay where you are until the light helps you orient.

### Psychological Deterioration

The silence, the solitude, the sense of the city itself watching—these wear at travelers over days and weeks.

**Prevention:**
- Set a duration for your stay before you arrive. A week, perhaps. Long enough to see the plateau, not so long that the plateau sees into you.
- Talk to yourself if you need to hear a voice. Sing if the silence becomes too heavy. The plateau won't mind.
- Maintain routines. Wake at a set time. Eat at set times. Explore at set times. Structure keeps you human.

### The Salamander-Things

Or whatever they are. The dark shapes in the deeper cisterns.

**Assessment:**
We don't know what these are. They're rarely seen. They don't seem aggressive, but why would they be? Nothing threatens them here. You're not prey. You're not competition. You're just... present.

**Precaution:**
- Don't swim in the deep cisterns. There's no reason to.
- If you see movement in the water, watch it. Let it see you watching. Acknowledge the presence without approaching. Most encounters end with the shape vanishing into darker water. Let it.
- If something surfaces near you, move away slowly. Do not run. Running on wet stone is how falls happen.

## What to Leave Behind

Do not bring:
- **Technology expecting electricity.** There are no charging stations, no power sources. The plateau has tech value 0.4; it was made by hands, not machines. Your devices will die and become dead weight.
- **Metal tools.** The city was carved without metal. Bringing metal into a place that explicitly rejected it feels like a violation. It may not be forbidden, but it is certainly... discordant.
- **Anything that makes sustained noise.** The plateau's silence has weight. Breaking it continually with electronic beeps, music, recorded voices—this is not dangerous, but it is wrong. It marks you as someone who doesn't understand where you are.
- **Expectations of comfort.** The rooms are adequate. The water is present. The temperature is mild. This is not discomfort. But neither is it comfort. It is adequacy. Expect more and you'll be dissatisfied. Expect adequacy and you'll understand.

## Departure

The plateau will let you leave.

This is important to understand: the Weeping Plateau does not trap. It does not pull you back or confuse your exit or make the way out impossible to find. You can walk to the edge, begin your descent, and return to the lands below. The city has no walls, no gates, no barriers. It is carved openness, and you can exit any time you choose.

But know that some part of you will remain.

Not your body, not your spirit, but your presence. The plateau holds memory in its stone and its water channels. You walked here. You touched its surfaces, added your warmth to the cold stone, left the faintest trace of yourself in the moss and the air and the water that will one day reach a cistern you never saw.

This is not a threat. It is simply what the plateau does. It accumulates presence. Every traveler adds to its density, its complexity, its vast held breath.

Leave before you forget why you came. Leave while the anchor you brought still means something. Leave with your notes, your observations, your memory of the light transforming water to mirrors.

The plateau will still be there if you need to return. It has time. It has water. It has patience measured in the slow spread of moss and the even slower settling of stone.

It will wait.

---

## Final Note

The Weeping Plateau is not a place to visit lightly. Its silence changes how you hear. Its density changes how you see. Its water, moving through carved channels like blood through veins, changes how you think about architecture, about presence, about what it means to build something that outlasts everyone who built it.

Go prepared. Go respectful. Go knowing that you are temporary.

The stone is patient. The water is eternal. The city holds emptiness as carefully as it once held people.

What it holds now, perhaps, is a kind of waiting—not for anyone specific, not for you, just waiting itself, as a state of being, as a condition of existence.

You can visit. You can walk the carved rooms and watch the light transform the wet stone and feel the silence press against your ears.

But remember: the plateau was here before you, and it will be here after you. It doesn't need you. It doesn't need anyone.

It just is.
