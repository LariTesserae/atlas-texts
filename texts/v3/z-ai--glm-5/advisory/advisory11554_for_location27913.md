# Preparing for the Ascendant Manufactory

## The Mental Architecture

Before you pack anything, you need to understand what you're walking into. This is not a place that will try to kill you—not directly, not in the way that some multiverse locations reach out with active hostility. The danger here is more patient. The Manufactory operates on timescales that make human urgency feel almost comical, and if you arrive expecting urgency, you will find yourself... misaligned.

Your first preparation is therefore temperamental. You must practice *slowing*. In the weeks before your visit, train yourself out of expectation. Stop checking the time so often. Let conversations pause without filling the silence. Eat slower. Walk slower. The Manufactory will not speed up for you, and if you try to speed yourself up to compensate, you will simply exhaust against its resistance—like swimming against a current that does not notice your effort.

The psychological weight of the place accumulates. Visitors report a specific kind of unease that builds over days: the sense of being *seen* by light that has no warmth, of moving through geometry that does not quite resolve, of existing in a space where the usual constants have been replaced by industrial approximations. You should prepare for this the way deep-sea divers prepare for pressure—not by fighting it, but by learning to move *with* it, to let your expectations bend.

## What to Bring

### Clothing: The Damp Protocol

The moisture at the Ascendant Manufactory is not rain. It does not fall; it *condenses*. It beads and runs and collects with a persistence that will find every gap in your defenses. Waterproof gear is essential, but not the heavy industrial kind—the platforms and walkways can be slick, and you need mobility. Look for layered moisture management: a base that wicks, a membrane that seals, an outer layer that sheds.

Bring more undergarments than seems reasonable. The damp gets everywhere, and nothing destroys morale faster than the creeping awareness that you have been subtly wet for six hours. Change frequently. The workers here have learned this; you will see them in practical, layered configurations that prioritize dryness over style.

Temperature regulation matters more than warmth. The climate never quite becomes cold, but it also never warms. It holds at a cool steady state that can feel comfortable for an hour and then slowly draining over a full day. Dress in layers you can adjust. Bring gloves—the railings and surfaces collect moisture, and your hands will suffer without protection.

Most importantly: good boots. The walkways are metal, concrete-substitute, and substances you won't recognize. They can be slick, uneven, and in some places, *not quite stable*. You need traction and ankle support. Break them in before you arrive. Blisters heal slowly in the Manufactory; everything does.

### Navigation Tools: When Maps Fail

Bring a compass. Understand that it will not work properly. The magnetic field here—if it even exists—behaves according to rules that were engineered rather than evolved. Your compass will drift, settle, drift again. It is not useless; it is simply *suggestible*. Watch which way it tends to settle when you're in stable areas, and use that as a baseline.

Physical maps are better than digital. The Manufactory's reality instabilities affect electronic systems in unpredictable ways—devices that rely on precise coordinates or consistent signal timing may malfunction, or worse, may *almost* function correctly enough to mislead. Paper does not lie, or at least it lies differently.

But here is the difficult truth: the map will not match the territory, and the territory will not match itself from one day to the next. You must learn to navigate by *relationships* rather than absolutes. This corridor connects to that assembly bay, which sits beneath the gantry with the particular sound of its machinery. The room with the vegetation filter is three levels down from the observation deck where the light hits differently at certain hours. You build mental architecture out of accumulated observation rather than geometric certainty.

Mark your path. Chalk is traditional; the workers use it, and it shows up against the industrial surfaces. Mark turns, mark doors you've passed through, mark the way back. Do not trust your memory to hold spatial information in a place where space itself is uncertain.

### Light and Vision

The Manufactory is never dark. This is one of its strangenesses—the relentless industrial illumination that eliminates shadow and watches without warmth. Your eyes will adjust, but they will also *tire*. Bring something to create temporary darkness for sleeping—a proper sleep mask, not a cheap one, but something that seals fully and does not let the light press through. You will need genuine darkness to rest properly; without it, visitors report strange dreams, fragmented sleep, a sense that even their unconscious hours are being observed.

That said, bring a light anyway. There are places—corridors that have begun to drift, rooms that exist partially, gaps in the designed illumination where something has failed or been forgotten—where you may need to see. A headlamp keeps your hands free. Keep it small and reliable.

Consider eye protection. The light is bright to the point of scrutiny, and prolonged exposure creates a specific kind of fatigue. Sunglasses or shaded lenses can help, though they will mark you as an outsider immediately. The workers have adapted; their eyes have learned to manage the exposure. You have not.

### Food and Water

Do not drink the water.

This is not because it is unclean—on the contrary, the Manufactory's filtration systems are extensive and precise. The water has been processed, filtered, fed through systems that need it. It is chemically potable. But it has been *processed* by machines that do not fully understand organic requirements, and long-term consumption by visitors has produced... irregularities. Minor at first. Cumulative over time.

Bring your own water. As much as you can carry without compromising mobility. The Manufactory operates on slow timescales, but dehydration operates on human ones, and you do not want to depend on systems that were designed for something other than your biology.

Food is less critical—you can eat the processed rations that the workers consume, and they will sustain you—but the portions are sized for metabolic rates that have adapted to the Manufactory's peculiar pace. You may find yourself hungry more often than the meal schedules account for. Bring supplemental nutrition: dense, preserved, easy to carry.

### Sleep and Rest

Finding a place to sleep in the Manufactory is itself a kind of navigation. The living quarters exist—they are layered into the dense press of structures—but they are assigned, occupied, and not meant for travelers. You will need to find the in-between spaces: the maintenance corridors where workers sometimes rest, the warm areas near exhaust vents where the fauna cluster, the nooks that form at the junctions of walkways and platforms.

Bring a sleeping system designed for hard surfaces and cold air. A proper sleeping pad, not the thin foam kind. Something that insulates. The platforms hold cold the way sponges hold water; they have been constructed with intent, not grown, and they do not generate warmth.

Earplugs are essential—not for noise, but for the *quality* of the sound. The quiet here is unnatural, and visitors report that their brains try to *fill* the absence with phantom noise. Earplugs create a kind of neutral baseline that is easier to tolerate than the Manufactory's particular hush.

### Documentation

Bring multiple ways to record what you observe. Paper and pen, yes. A simple camera, mechanical if possible—the digital ones may be affected by the reality instabilities. The workers do not document much; they have learned that paying too close attention to the edges of things is a path to uncomfortable questions.

Write down everything. Not just what you see, but what you *feel*—the weight of the air, the quality of the light, the sense of being observed. The Manufactory affects travelers in different ways, and the pattern of that effect may matter more than any individual observation. Note when corridors seem longer or shorter. Note which rooms feel stable and which feel *thin*. Note where the animals go and where they avoid.

Keep duplicate records. The Manufactory's reality instabilities can affect physical objects as well as spatial relationships—pages may go missing, entries may shift. If something matters, write it twice, in two different places.

## What Not to Bring

Leave behind anything that depends on precise timing. Mechanical watches will drift; digital devices will malfunction in ways that are almost correct. The Manufactory has its own time, and that time does not align with yours. You will know time has passed by your body's signals—hunger, fatigue, the need for sleep—not by any instrument.

Do not bring expectations of comfort. The Manufactory is not hostile, but it is not welcoming either. It is *functional*—every surface, every system, every space serves a purpose that may or may not include your presence. Comfort is not among those purposes.

Do not bring anything you cannot afford to lose. The Manufactory's reality instabilities manifest in part through small disappearances—a tool set down for a moment that is not there when you reach for it again; a bag left in a corner that has moved when you return. Whether these are genuine dimensional effects or simply the result of navigating an environment where attention is constantly fragmented, the result is the same. Travel light with what you cannot replace.

## The Fauna: Protocols for Coexistence

The animals here emerged rather than arrived. They are not natural, but they are not hostile—not exactly. They have adapted to the Manufactory in ways you have not, and this gives them advantages you cannot predict.

The birds with their too-many-jointed wings will watch you from the railings. Let them. Do not make sudden movements toward them. They are not aggressive unless threatened, but their definition of threat may differ from yours. The compound-lens eyes click through focal lengths audibly; this is not a warning, but it is information. They are assessing you. Be boring.

The small scurrying things in the maintenance tunnels are more likely to avoid you than approach. They clean particulates from corners—a function in the Manufactory's ecology—and they prefer to work undisturbed. If you must move through their spaces, move slowly and predictably. They will clear a path. They always do.

The larger presences in the upper gantries—the ones with the too-regular footsteps and too-rhythmic breathing—are best left alone. Do not seek them out. Do not try to observe them closely. If you hear them moving at night, stay where you are. The workers have an understanding with these beings, or at least an accommodation, and that accommodation depends on mutual disinterest. Do not break the disinterest.

In general: observe without investigating. The fauna here are part of the Manufactory's system. They have a function, even if that function is not clear to you. Interfering with them is interfering with the system, and the system does not respond well to interference.

## The Reality Instabilities: Living With Uncertainty

The most important preparation is accepting that space here is not reliable. You will experience this. A corridor will feel longer on the return trip. A door will open onto a room you did not expect. A window will show a landscape that does not match.

When this happens, do not panic. The instabilities are rarely dangerous directly—rather, they are disorienting, and disorientation in a dense industrial environment can lead to accidents. Move slowly. Reorient yourself using stable reference points. Find a wall and follow it until you reach a junction you recognize.

Learn the stable zones. The workers know them—areas where reality has not yet begun to fray, where geometry holds and maps remain accurate. Stay in these areas when you can. Venture into the unstable zones only when necessary, and never alone.

Do not look too closely at the edges of things. This is advice the workers give, and it is genuine. The Manufactory does not like to be examined. Observation changes things here—makes the instabilities more likely to manifest, more likely to *notice* you. There is a reason the production processes are obscure, the purpose of the goods unclear. The system operates better when no one asks too many questions.

If you find yourself in a space that does not feel right—a room that is too large, a corridor that should not exist—leave immediately. Do not investigate. Do not document. Simply exit and mark the location so you can avoid it later.

## Duration of Stay

Do not stay long.

This is the most important advice for any traveler. The Manufactory's effects accumulate. The damp seeps into your clothing, your skin, your expectations. The light watches until you forget what darkness feels like. The reality instabilities train you out of trusting your own perception. The slow pace teaches you that urgency is meaningless. After enough time, you may find yourself becoming part of the system—another functional element in a vast industrial ecology.

The workers who remain—the ones who tend the vegetation, maintain the machinery, navigate the corridors—they were travelers once too, or their parents were, or their grandparents. The Manufactory collects people the way it collects moisture. Stay long enough, and you may forget why you came. Stay longer, and you may forget that you were ever anywhere else.

A week is enough for most purposes. Two weeks pushes the boundaries of safe exposure. A month or more, and you begin to risk something that is not quite absorption but is close enough to be concerning.

When you leave, you will find that the world outside feels strange. Fast. Chaotic. Unengineered. The normal processes of weather and time and biological decay will seem almost excessive after the Manufactory's precise dampness and controlled light. This is normal. It fades. Most of it fades.

## Exit Strategy

Know how you will leave before you arrive.

The Manufactory is a place of slow processes, but your exit should not be one of them. When it is time to go, *go*. Do not linger for one last observation. Do not try to see the thing you missed. The Manufactory will be here—it will always be here, in a sense, operating on timescales that make your departure irrelevant to its function.

Mark your entry point. Return to it. Leave through the same gap in reality that admitted you. Do not attempt to find new exits; the workers have stories about travelers who tried to leave through doors that were not there yesterday, who descended through platform layers looking for ground that may not exist.

If you cannot find your original exit point—which can happen, the Manufactory's instabilities do not respect even well-marked paths—seek out the oldest workers. They know the stable routes. They know the ways out. They may be reluctant to share them, but they will, eventually, if you are patient. Patience is a currency here.

## A Final Note

The Ascendant Manufactory produces continuously, industriously, at scales that suggest vast purpose. You will not understand what it produces. You will not understand why. This is not a failure of observation—it is a feature of the place.

What you will understand, if you stay long enough to notice, is that the Manufactory is still becoming. The erosion parameter reads young because it is young. The structures have not yet learned to age because aging implies a kind of surrender that the system has not yet accepted. The reality instabilities are not flaws—they are growing pains, the world struggling to accommodate so much concentrated intention.

You are visiting something that is still figuring out what it is. Be careful. Be patient. Do not let it figure you out in return.
