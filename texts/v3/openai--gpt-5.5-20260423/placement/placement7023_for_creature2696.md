I would find myself very low in the made world, not in a wilderness but in a hidden organ of construction: a dry bright chamber sealed between older walls, where architecture has become climate.

Not at the center, at first. The center is too exposed, too polished by the steady roar. I would be near one of the crowded pillar-bases, where a floor rib meets a square column and fails by the width of a hair. There, in the seam, powdered salt has collected. Dust rests because the air’s pressure permits it. The light arrives indirectly, after striking three or four pale surfaces, so it is still abundant but no longer sharp enough to scour everything clean.

That is where I would be.

Not standing there. Not visiting. Not moving through.

I would be attached.

At first I might look like a flaw in the masonry: a gray whitening along the joint, a crust slightly too regular to be only mineral bloom. Then, closer, I would become a little colony of dry life. A rosette, perhaps, but not a green one. My leaves would be thin, overlapping mineral scales, ash-silver on the upper side and faintly violet underneath where the light cannot quite reach. Their edges would be furred with salt crystals. My body would lie flat against the stone, as though I were not growing from it but remembering how to be part of it.

I would not have roots in the ordinary sense. I would have fastenings: pale threads gone sideways into plaster pores, ceramic scratches, salt seams, old injuries of the floor. I would not search downward for soil. There is no downward worth seeking. I would hold myself in place and taste the chamber through contact.

I would know the world as differences in dryness.

This crack is kinder than that crack.  
This column hums deeper than the next.  
This dust is old enough to use.  
This salt burns unless taken slowly.  
This reflected light can be endured.  
This shadow is blue and safe.

I would feed on light without reaching for it. I would accept it the way the walls accept it: continuously, almost without appetite. My surfaces would be pale because too much brightness is a burden. I would wear dead skin, powder, and crystal as armor against abundance.

Water would not be my dream. Water would be an event to survive.

A human breath passing over me would be weather. A fingertip would be a season. Sweat would be catastrophe or feast, depending on where it fell and how quickly the chamber took it back. I would close, harden, draw salts around myself, and wait for the trespass to become old.

My time would be almost indistinguishable from the room’s time. I would not count days. I would count accumulations: a thicker rim, a new scale under an old one, a filament reaching one grain farther into the seam. A century might mean that I have widened by the breadth of a fingernail. A lifetime might be the slow decision to divide.

The roar would pass through me always. I would not hear it as an animal hears, but I would be shaped by it. One edge of me would lift microscopically where the high hiss travels down from the ceiling slit. My inner fastenings would grow denser where the floor hum gathers. Dust would settle against me in patterns taught by sound, and I would learn those patterns as law.

I think I would not be a single plant, finally.

I would be what appears in several places and is one thing underneath: a dispersed patience. A gray crust here, a violet rosette there, a few glassy threads in the next seam, all joined invisibly through pores and salt-lines inside the construction. To a visitor I would look like many small lives. To myself, if such a self could exist, I would be a slow map of usable cracks.

I would be the chamber’s marginal handwriting.

A dry photosynthetic skin.

A life that does not oppose the architecture but completes it.
