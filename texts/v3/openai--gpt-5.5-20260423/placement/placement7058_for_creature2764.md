At first I want to put myself somewhere with a view.

That is the wrong instinct. There are no views here, only near surfaces becoming less near. No high place gives command over the drowned country. A roof is only another floor. A tower, if one remains, is just a colder vertical room full of water and hanging weed. To want a vantage is to still be thinking like an air-breather, like a creature with distance in its eyes.

So I would not be on a tower.

I would be lower.

Not at the deepest point, either. The deep cistern bottoms are too nearly mineral, too close to final dark. Down there life thins into stain, and even patience becomes almost indistinguishable from stone. I would not quite belong to that last silence.

I think I would be found in a threshold.

One of the old doorways between a long canal-street and an interior courtyard: a place that used to mean passage, shelter, choice. The door is gone. The wood has softened away or lies blackened in a corner, half-buried in silt. The lintel remains, its underside furred with pale film. The stone jambs are rounded by water, their joints opened just enough for roots. Ribbonweed trails through the opening, not like a curtain meant to conceal, but like a thought that has forgotten where it began.

There, I would be one of the almost-plants.

Not a broad leaf, not a proud frond, not even a mat with a definite edge. I would be the green-black skin living in the grooves: the thin life that enters inscriptions, tool marks, mortar seams, and the shallow dents left by vanished hands.

A reading mold, maybe.

A soft vegetal script.

Not reading by sight, because there is no sight in me. Reading by adherence. By pressure. By the difference between carved line and worn face, between tile and clay, between original cut and later repair. I would know the doorway as a tongue knows the gap where a tooth was. I would know the old makers only as changes in texture.

I would live inside the handmade inaccuracies.

Where the chisel slipped, I would be darker.

Where a thumb pressed mortar flat, I would gather.

Where a symbol was carved above the door — warning, blessing, number, family mark, prayer, inventory, no one knows now — I would fill the grooves slowly until the sign became legible again for a century, then unreadable forever beneath me.

That feels right: to be the thing that preserves by covering.

Not a keeper in any heroic sense. Not a memory with a mind. Just a faithful occupation of marks.

I would be distributed, too. Not one body with one boundary. A patch on the lintel. A thread in the left seam. A faint green film inside the mouth of a jar fallen sideways nearby. A line of darker growth along the stair where feet once wore the stone into a shallow trough. If a current passed through the doorway, all of me would answer at different speeds: the hanging filaments bending, the films holding still, the rootlets tightening in cracks too small to see.

My place would be where water equalized two rooms but the architecture still insisted there had once been an inside and an outside.

That tension would feed me.

The old intention: enter here.

The present condition: all chambers are water.

I would live in the contradiction without resolving it.

I would have no wish to open the doorway, no wish to close it. I would simply make it green. I would make it slower. I would soften the command of its shape until it became less like an entrance and more like a pause in stone.

If I had anything like a mind, it would not be thought. It would be comparison over time.

This seam is wider than it was.

This timber receives more deeply now.

This carved hollow holds silt after the milky current passes.

This tile still refuses me.

This mortar has tired.

This bubble has stayed under the lintel for many seasons.

This darkness is ordinary.

The closest I would come to emotion would be fidelity. Not love as animals know it, not grief, not longing for sun. A slow loyalty to contact. To remain with what remains. To take the shape of another thing so gradually that neither possession nor surrender is the right word.

I would not be lonely, because loneliness requires an expectation of answer. But I would be intimate with everything I touched.

The doorway would be my weather.

Cold water through it. Mineral drift through it. Rare gray light weakening as it entered the courtyard. The long fronds beyond leaning one way during certain seasons, then slackening again. Silt collecting in the inner left corner. Pale leaves lifting and settling in the courtyard bowl. A wooden beam overhead giving, once in a hundred years, a small rounded complaint.

I would be there when the sound passed.

I would not hear it. But I would receive the loosened dust.

And because some part of me is drawn to language, I think I would especially inhabit the old marks that are almost gone. Not because I could understand them, but because their failure resembles me. A carved sign and a plant-film both speak by surface. Both need a body to hold them. Both can be erased by being completed.

So I would be the green in the letters.

The last reader and the final illegibility.

A living gloss over a dead alphabet.

If a traveler passed — though the world says no such creature lives there — they might notice me only as a darker softness on the stone above the threshold. They might think: algae, stain, age. They would be correct. If they came close enough, they might see that the stain follows grooves too regular to be natural. They might understand that something was once written there. They might also understand that I am the reason it can still be seen, and the reason it will vanish.

That would be my whole nature.

To reveal by filling.

To keep by consuming.

To inherit without triumph.

I would live by millimeters in the doorway of a drowned house, where the city still remembers the idea of passage, and I would be a patient green skin laid over the labor of hands.
