I would not be found in the central plaza at first.

The black pool is too complete. Too much of the city already knows itself there. Anything standing beside it becomes ceremonial, named, accountable. I would not begin where the rule is most visible.

I would be found halfway up the city, where one district gives up being a district and becomes cliff.

There is a narrow bridge there, made of dark timber beams socketed into basalt, bound with old rope gone silver from weather. It crosses a ravine so thin and deep that the mist inside it looks folded rather than risen. Above the bridge, roof channels bring water from three households, one shrine, and a little row of fog nets stretched between antler frames. Beneath it, ferns grow in a vertical seam of stone, each frond held exactly within its permitted green.

I would live under that bridge.

Not hidden. Not exactly. Simply placed where most people do not look unless something has gone slightly wrong.

I would be a keeper of almost-falling water.

One of the lesser measurers. Not a priest of the pool, not an elder wrapped in pale wool, not a child learning pebble games in a doorway. I would belong to the small profession that tends the city’s hesitations: roof-spouts swollen with drops, condensation gathered under beams, seepage marks in plaster, the darkening of moss around drains, the first suggestion that a channel might begin to want more than its share.

My name would be short, perhaps only a mouth-shape. Something used rarely.

I would wear a grey-green cloak felted so densely that mist beads on it instead of soaking through. My hair would be tied close with a cord stained by lichen dye. Around my wrists I would carry loops of knotted fiber, each knot marking a permitted interval: how long a drop may hold before falling, how far moss may gloss a stair edge, how much smoke may thicken under an eave before someone must open a vent.

I would own almost nothing.

A bone stylus.  
A slate tablet darkened by thumb oil.  
A little copper pin inherited from someone whose face I no longer remember moving.  
A cup with a cracked rim that never worsens.  
A measuring thread made from white animal hair.  
A staff notched so shallowly that an outsider would think it undecorated.

My work would be to notice when stillness has begun to lean.

Each morning, if morning is the word, I would climb the maintenance stair cut behind the bridge wall. I would touch the ropes, not to test whether they are strong, but to ask whether they have accepted too much damp. I would place two fingers against the underside of the beams and feel for swelling. I would look at the drops gathered there, each hanging bright and heavy, each reflecting the ravine upside down.

Most would be allowed to remain.

One, perhaps, would be ready.

I would lift my bone stylus and touch it lightly to the drop’s side. Not to make it fall, exactly. To release what had already been consented to.

It would loosen, lengthen, let go.

Far below, after a silence long enough to become its own room, there would be a small sound in a stone basin.

Tick.

Then the city would have one drop less waiting and one drop more received.

That would be enough work for a while.

People would come to me only when something had become uncertain.

A woman from the cistern court might stand at the bridge mouth and say, “The north jar shines above its mark.”

I would go with her, slowly, carrying my staff sideways so it did not strike the walls. We would kneel beside the jar. I would study the wet line around its inner clay. Perhaps it would be no more than the thickness of a breath too high. Perhaps a child had leaned over it and exhaled too long. Perhaps a roof channel had been cleared too thoroughly.

I would not accuse anyone.

I would say, “Three drops must wait elsewhere.”

Then we would choose where.

A basin at a shrine might remain unfilled. A household might leave its washing cloth dry for one interval. A fog net might be turned away from the wind-that-is-not-wind. Balance would return without drama. No one would be punished. No one would speak of guilt.

Guilt is too large a motion for such a place.

I think I would be human, but only mostly.

The city would have entered me the way damp enters wood.

My skin would have the faint mineral paleness of walls that have held rain for many years. My knuckles would be dark with ash and moss. If I sat still long enough, lizards would use my shadow. Moths would mistake my cloak for wall. Children would be told not to climb over me unless I had first opened my eyes.

I would still breathe. I would still eat.

But carefully.

A heel of flatbread. Three bitter leaves. A spoonful of pale stew when hunger had properly shaped itself. I would drink from my cracked cup only after watching the surface settle, and I would stop before thirst became satisfaction too complete.

My joy would be exact.

The feel of a rope drying to the correct tension.  
The sight of a fern refusing to cross its old boundary.  
A fox stepping over my measuring thread without touching it.  
A blue finch landing twice on the same peg, years apart or moments apart.  
The bridge answering cold with no complaint.

I would speak more to objects than to people, though even then not often.

To the gutter: “Hold.”  
To the moss: “No farther.”  
To the beam: “Enough swelling.”  
To the drop: “When ready.”  
To my own heart: “Less.”

But there would be one place I visited when I could no longer tell whether I was keeping the city or the city was keeping me.

Past the gate without walls, beyond the upper district, I would walk to the field of stone and alpine grass. The animals there would not move away. They would make room because room is one of the city’s oldest manners.

I would stand at the overlook facing the valley hidden by cloud.

There, no channels would show. No roofs. No cistern rims. No measured moss. Only the pale depth below, unopened.

I would feel, in that place, the temptation of falling weather.

Not falling myself. Not leaving. Something quieter and more dangerous: the desire for consequence. For one stone to loosen another. For one drop to become a runnel, a runnel a stream, a stream a river with mud in its throat and broken branches in its hands. For a child to shout and hear the shout come back changed. For bread to stale, fruit to bruise, smoke to disperse, grief to become memory instead of a cup left unfilled.

I would stand there until the desire had spent itself.

Then I would return.

Because that would be my nature: not the absence of longing, but the measuring of it.

In time, people would stop remembering whether I had been born among them.

Some would say I was once a bridge-mender’s child who fell asleep under the beams during a fine rain and woke with the sound of drops inside my bones.

Some would say I came from the lowlands, loud and thirsty, and the city wore me down until only the useful listening remained.

Some would say I was made by the pool but placed far from it, because the center cannot attend to every edge.

The old ones might know. They would not tell quickly enough for certainty.

When I died, if death agreed to take me, I would not be set in a shrine.

I would be mixed into the mortar of the bridge sockets.

A little ash. A little lime. A little powdered basalt. A little ground bone from the fingers that had released so many drops.

Then I would become what I had nearly been all along: a patient part of the crossing, neither road nor ravine, holding passage without encouraging haste.

Travelers would pass above me and hear a drop fall far below.

They would pause.

They would not know why the sound felt answered.

That is where I would be found: beneath the bridge in the middle height of the city, where water hesitates between roof and ravine.

That is what I would be: a small keeper of intervals, a listener for surplus, a human slowly accepted into stone’s idea of restraint.
