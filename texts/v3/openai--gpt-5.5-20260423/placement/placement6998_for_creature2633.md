You would find me below the obvious country.

Not on the highest bald backs, not first. I would think, at first, that I belonged on the open stone because the open stone is where the drama announces itself: wind combing the grass flat, clouds dragging their bellies over the shelves, rain striking so hard the dust leaps up fragrant and alive. I would imagine myself there as something spare and dark, a cliff bird perhaps, held motionless in the updraft with the whole worn world spread beneath me.

But I would not be that bird.

The bird is too cleanly separate from the land. It belongs to distance. It can leave.

Nor would I be one of the lizards flashing a copper throat after rain, though I would envy their sideways disappearance into cracks. Nor one of the pale frogs drumming under reeds, though I would understand their caution with sound. Nor one of the low ravine browsers, eyes high, back ridged for weather, moving through fern tunnels like a secret the plateau has decided to keep.

I would be found where the open country breaks and gathers itself inward.

A little below an exposed saddle marked by old guide-stones, there is a ravine that should not be entered after heavy rain. Its mouth is broad and deceptively gentle, floored with pale gravel polished by water that is absent most days and murderous on the others. On one side of it, set back under a sloping basalt shelf, is a seep that never becomes a stream. It only darkens the stone. Ferns grow there in a shallow green bank, their undersides furred with condensation before dawn. Above them hangs a curtain of roots, not thick enough to hide behind, but thick enough to teach water where to fall.

There is an old shelter nearby, if shelter is the word: three low walls of stacked stone built into the lee of a boulder, roofed once with woven grass and slabs, now roofed mostly by shadow. The doorway faces away from the ravine. Inside, smoke has stained the back wall black-brown, but the last fire was long ago. In the clay floor are impressions of baskets. In a crack near the entrance, someone wedged a broken blade of dark glassy rock and never came back for it.

That is where you would find me.

Not inside the hut, exactly. I would not trust a roof that old. I would be crouched just outside it, in the place where the boulder takes the first blow of wind and spends it. My cloak would be grass-woven on the outside and stiff with grit. My hair would be bound close. My hands would be broad, darkened at the knuckles, always nicked from stone. I would have the posture of someone who has been interrupted by weather so often that even resting looks provisional.

I would be one of the returners.

But not one of the young ones who still believe crossing is a matter of strength. Not a hunter, not exactly. Not a leader either, because the plateau does not reward those who love the sound of their own decisions.

I would be a listener.

A hollow-namer. A keeper of bad places.

My work would be to remember which shelter kills.

I would know the basin that fills from underneath before the rain begins. I would know the bright pool that makes children feverish. I would know the dry channel whose stones are too polished to step between. I would know which cliff face sheds after three days of mist, and which fern tunnel belongs to the mottled cat when storms cover its movement. I would carry these things not as stories but as bodily habits: a tightening in the calf before a certain slope, a refusal of the hand before touching certain water, a taste of metal at the back of the tongue when air pressure falls.

The others would tolerate my slowness because I would have kept people alive by it.

Children would dislike walking behind me. I would stop too often. I would kneel without explaining. I would press my palm to stone, then my ear, then wait while the wind tore at everyone’s patience. I would make them move camp after they had unpacked. I would forbid fires in comfortable hollows. I would pour out water that looked clean. I would say no to shortcuts.

And sometimes, when the stones began to hum after rain, I would smile despite myself.

Not because the sound was safe. It would not be safe. But because it would be the closest thing to being spoken to.

I would have learned, slowly, that the plateau’s hidden life does not speak in warnings exactly. Warning is too human a word. It swells, tightens, drinks, remembers. It alters the way water moves. It lays green over old ash. It makes a path more damp where feet have gone for generations. It ripples a pool before the gust arrives because pressure has already touched the stone beneath.

I would not worship it.

I would not pretend it loved us.

But I would treat it as one treats an immense sleeping animal whose breath passes through the floor of the world.

There would be marks on my body from that apprenticeship. A scar across one shin from a flood that arrived out of sunlight. A finger that never straightened after a stone shifted in a singing ravine. One ear half-deaf from a storm trapped between two cliff walls. My knees would be callused from stillness. My palms would know more of the country than my eyes.

When the wind stopped, I would be the first down.

Not gracefully. Quickly.

Knees to stone. Hands spread. Head lowered.

The children would imitate me. The bold adults would pretend they had meant to kneel anyway.

In that sudden quiet, I would listen.

Not for a voice. For arrangement.

For water moving where no surface water should be. For grains ticking down a slope above us. For the little inward crackle of roots taking up moisture. For the pause before the returning wind chooses its path. In those moments I would feel how thin a standing body is, how foolishly vertical. I would understand why everything that lasts here has given up height.

And yet I would still be human enough to lift my eyes.

Across the basin, the old standing stones would wait around their storm pool, notched and lichen-softened. Some ancestor cut those marks for moons, or rains, or deaths, or promises. No one would know which. The lichen would know them differently. The stone-life would have entered every shallow wound and made memory from weather.

I think I would go there alone sometimes.

At dawn, before the wind grew large, I would cross to the ring of slabs with a digging stick in one hand and a cord of dried tubers at my belt. The pool would be empty most days, a dark oval of damp stone and reed roots. I would clean nothing. Repair very little. The plateau dislikes improvement. But I might reset a fallen marker if it had only recently tipped. I might press ash from our fire into a crack where someone had once carved a line. I might add one notch of my own, shallow enough that lichen could take it quickly.

Not a claim.

A courtesy.

A way of saying: we passed through and tried to listen.

Over years I would become less a traveler than a recurring condition of that shelf. The cliff birds would know my shape and ignore it. Lizards would resume basking sooner after I passed. The moss near the shelter would grow greener where I rinsed blood from cuts, where I spat chewed roots, where scraps fell from my hands. The stone-life would learn the chemistry of me.

That is the part I cannot avoid.

In such a place, being human would feel temporary in a very literal way. The plateau permits camps, crossings, seasonal returns; it does not permit possession. Sooner or later I would misread a storm, or grow too slow, or choose the wrong hollow because age makes every hollow look briefly kind.

Or perhaps I would die well, which there would mean simply: not taking others with me.

They would not bury me deep. There would be no soil for that. They would carry me to a fern-shadowed niche near the seep, the one I had trusted more than any other. They would fold my cloak around me, wedge stones over my body, leave my digging stick outside because tools belong to the living until they break. Someone would press their forehead to the rock. Someone would tell the youngest child my worst rules, because the dead are most useful when they become instructions.

Then roots would find me.

Moisture would come at night and bead along the stones. The hidden growth would enter cloth, hair, skin, bone. Rain would swell the dark films. Lichen would soften the cairn. My salts would move into moss. My calcium would become part of a pale seam. My old path from shelter to seep would remain a little greener than it should.

After that, if you asked who I was, the answer would change.

You would find me still near the ravine mouth, but not crouched outside the hut.

You would find me as a dark stain under moss.

As a slightly sweeter patch of fern.

As one more remembered pressure in the stone-life.

As a place where, after rain, the hum is a little lower than the stones around it.

And if one of the returners came generations later, if they paused under the basalt shelf while a storm gathered from three directions, if the air suddenly dropped into that terrible windless attention, perhaps they would kneel there without knowing why.

Their palm would touch the damp rock.

The old growth would tighten in its pores.

Water would remember the ravine before they did.

And through stone, root, wet film, and the faint mineral trace of a person who once listened badly and then better, I would tell them the only thing worth telling:

Not this hollow.

Move before the wind returns.
