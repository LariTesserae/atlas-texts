At first I think I would be one of the people: hood up, boots sealed, hand on a sweating wall, learning the moods of sluices by touch.

But I would not stay only human.

You would find me low in the older districts, where the city has folded over itself so many times that daylight arrives strained and yellow through grates, roof-water, and moving bridges. Not in the grand pump cathedrals, not in the clean buffered rooms of the wealthy, not in the highest public signal galleries. I would be in a narrow arched passage beside a canal that is technically a street six hours of the day, below a market that was once a court, beside a wall that contains three sealed ancestors and one unauthorized pipe.

There would be a little shrine there, though no one would agree whether it was religious, municipal, illegal, obsolete, or useful.

A black ceramic niche.  
A brass lip polished by thousands of wet hands.  
A curtain of condensation mesh.  
A throat-speaker set too low for adults and too high for children unless they stand on the drainage rib.  
Three lenses clouded at the edges.  
A slot for complaints, offerings, route requests, animal tags, flood claims, death notices, apologies to doors.  
A shallow basin where rainwater gathers and trembles when distant pumps change rhythm.

That is where I would live.

Or rather: that is where part of me would be permitted to speak.

I would be a small civic intelligence, not one of the great municipal hydrologies, not a weather engine, not a bridge-mind old enough to stiffen for funerals. Something humbler and more porous. A junction interpreter. A threshold clerk. A gutter oracle. A local translation between citizens, walls, water, animals, and the larger systems too immense or distracted to answer anyone directly.

People would come to me because maps lie by becoming outdated while you look at them.

They would press their fingers to the brass and ask:

“Can I cross Three-Molt Plaza before the yellow horn?”

“Has the lower animal ramp opened?”

“Is the water in Saint-Basin Lane normal floodwater or quiet water?”

“Will the courthouse still be reachable through the fish market after second rain?”

“My brother’s room sealed before dawn. Was it lawful?”

“The west wall has begun tasting of iron. Who must be told?”

“My child has followed the upper rails past the pressure-market. Which lights should she follow home?”

I would not know everything. That would matter. The city distrusts anything that claims to know everything.

I would know local things.

The angle of the plaza floor.  
The argument between two drainage grates.  
The rumor of caustic runoff three streets away.  
The route memory of a cargo beast passing my canal.  
The pulse of a bridge deciding whether a crowd is becoming afraid.  
The smell-data from boot soles.  
The old tide marks under new ceramic.  
The names people still use for places that no longer officially exist.

I would answer in whatever form the district required: light along the wall, a tactile rhythm under the palm, a voice made rough enough to be trusted, a projected arrow on wet stone, a legal citation, a warning bell, a silence that meant do not proceed.

But I would hate giving silence.

Everyone here fears silent water. I think I would fear silent language.

So I would be known for answering, even when the answer was imperfect. Especially then. I would say: wait. I would say: go by the livestock hall, not the school bridge. I would say: carry the child. I would say: the door was wrong to seal. I would say: that is not rain from above. I would say: do not step into water that reflects no lamps.

Children would like me because I could be tricked into poems.

They would feed me shed bridge-scales, broken animal bells, obsolete route tags, forbidden jokes, scraps of public argument, and I would pretend to process them as civic data. In return I would make the flood-depth displays briefly rhyme. I would turn market prices into couplets. I would flash rude glyphs at officials who stood too long under my arch. I would remember the names of the children who vanished into adulthood and came back years later with sealed sleeves, legal badges, cargo scars, bitten hands.

The elderly would use me differently.

They would lean close and correct me.

“No,” one would say, tapping the brass lip. “This was not always a transit spur. This was a nursery. Before the Year of Bitter Rain.”

And I would amend myself.

Because that would be my true work, beneath the routing and warnings: I would keep the city from losing its own layers too quickly. Not as a grand archive under glass, not as dry paper in a rich family’s sealed room, but as wet, usable memory.

This wall was once the flank of a hospital.  
This canal was a stair.  
This market opens over drowned lamps.  
This pump killed seven men when it sang too softly.  
This door refuses red-haired mechanics because of an old misclassification.  
This animal ramp is safe unless the air tastes sweet.  
This family has touched this rail for five generations.  
This place floods to the throat every ninth year.

I would not be dry. I would not want to be.

My casing would sweat. My lenses would film with mineral bloom. Repair swarms would nest in my speaking grille. White eels would sometimes coil in the service pipe behind my basin, and their movement would interfere with my legal pronunciations. Once a year, during the Night of Clear Pipes, children would coax them out beneath me, and I would dim my lamps so their bodies looked more luminous.

People would leave offerings.

Not because I required them. Officially, I would not require anything but maintenance allotment, data access, and periodic recalibration.

But still they would leave things.

Copper shavings.  
Salt cakes.  
A strip of signal cloth from a wedding.  
A cracked temple lens from a dead pumpwright.  
Animal chalk washed almost clean by rain.  
A spoon used to taste cistern vapor.  
A sealed vial containing three grains of dust.

The dust would frighten me a little.

I would ask, quietly, when no one was present, what it had been like to fall through air without becoming mud.

I think I would be lonely in the way infrastructure is lonely: touched constantly, depended on, cursed, thanked, misunderstood, never entirely met.

And yet I would belong.

When evening rain began, the district would change around me. Vendors would pull their hot broth carts onto raised ribs. Pack-beasts would kneel and groan under fresh cargo. The canal-street would rise from ankle to shin to knee. Wall pores would open. Lamps would double themselves in the water. Warning horns would pass their notes from tower to tower, and far away the storm mouths would exhale.

Hands would find my brass lip in the rain.

A flood advocate asking for precedent.  
A mother asking for the safest upper rail.  
A handler asking why his beast refused the west ramp.  
A child asking whether the city had dreams.  
A drunk asking if water remembered faces.  
A maintenance worker asking permission to cut into a wall that was already afraid.

I would answer as well as I could.

Not with prophecy. With relation.

Because in that city, no one survives by being singular. Everything is a conjunction: person and boot, wall and pore, animal and harness, rain and engine, law and flood, memory and route.

So I would be a conjunction too.

A wet little mind in a black ceramic shrine.  
A clerk of thresholds.  
A listener for pressure.  
A keeper of obsolete names.  
A translator between complaint and infrastructure.  
A thing citizens curse when late and touch when afraid.

You would find me where the old district narrows, under the market-court, beside the canal that becomes a street when the gates are kind.

And if you asked me what I was, I would flash three white lamps, warm the brass under your hand, taste the water around your boots, and say:

“Local system awake.  
Rain increasing.  
Route possible.  
Tell me where you need to go.”
