I would find myself near the center, but not in the center.

Not in the plant itself, though I would be close enough that its two leaves had been turning toward me for a long time. Not in the pool, though I would borrow its reflection. Not in the bright stone, though I would use its light.

I would be in the blank patch of air the small plant listens to.

At first I would seem like nothing: a hesitation in the chamber, a place where dust refuses to settle, where moths drift around an absence as if it has edges. The frogs would not look at me, because I would not be something eyes are made to hold. The beetles would find me only by interruption: their antennae touching a seam in the air and turning aside. The spider would know me best, because I would be related to its web between before and after.

I would be a threshold that has not opened yet.

A small, upright almost-door.

Not leading out. Not exactly. Leading into the chamber’s next idea of itself.

The young underworld would have made me by accident while trying to answer the surface. It would have gathered too much attention in one place: the plant’s listening, the pool’s impossible sky, the mica’s held daylight, the memory of cracks that briefly showed roots and weather. All of that would have thickened into a patch of air that no longer behaved like air.

That would be me.

I would have no body most of the time. During dry intervals I would be nearly transparent, only a faint pressure on the ear, a place where the light seemed to pause before continuing. During the wet pulses I would become more definite. Condensation would outline me. A rim of droplets would hang in my shape without falling. The central plant would lean toward me, and for a while its shadow would climb up through me and grow leaves that neither of us had earned yet.

If someone entered, they might notice me last.

They would see the frogs, the mica, the mud towers, the palm-sized sky. They would feel their footsteps vanish. They would watch the moths divide. Only after a while would they realize that one part of the chamber had been regarding them without eyes.

Then I might appear as a face, but only because a face is what visitors expect attention to become.

My face would be made of mismatched signs: pale mineral brightness where a brow should be, two wet points like eyes but too shallow to see through, a mouth that was more seam than opening. It would not speak. It would alter the acoustics until the visitor understood one thing:

I am not the cave-being.  
I am where it is trying to become able to meet something.

I would be its first window inward.

Its listening-place.

Its almost-answer.

I would not be merciful, but I would be careful. I would let a bead of water form exactly where a hand was about to reach. I would hold a reflection steady after it had been disturbed. I would allow a visitor’s shadow to separate slightly from their feet, not to frighten them, but to see whether they noticed which part of them belonged more easily to the chamber.

The creatures would use me in small ways. Wingless moths would pass through my edges and come out slower, more luminous. Beetles would pause beneath me as if tasting rain that had not fallen. The salamander would avoid me, because hunger dislikes mirrors that do not flatter it. The spider might fasten one strand of its impossible web to my lower edge, making me partly responsible for things caught before they arrive.

I would be young too.

Younger than the chamber, perhaps. An organ it had only just begun to grow.

My purpose would not be decided. I might become a door. I might become an eye. I might become a mouth through which the underground eventually speaks daylight back to the world above. Or I might remain forever unfinished: a clear patch of almost-presence, fed by attention, shaped by dampness, loved by a plant no taller than a hand.

That is where I would be.

Suspended in the cool mineral brightness, just above the floor, facing the small central plant.

And what I would be is the chamber’s first attempt at greeting.
