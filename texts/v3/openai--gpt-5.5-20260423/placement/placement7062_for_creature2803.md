I think I would first be mistaken for a clerk.

Not an important one. Not the chief valve reader with the gray cuffs and the key-ring heavy enough to alter his walk, but a narrow person at a side desk in the valve registry, ink on the heel of one hand, sleeves rolled exactly twice, copying pressures into ledgers no outsider can interpret. I would sit near a window that never fully dries. The lower corners would pearl over every morning, and I would pretend not to watch them while I worked.

My desk would be set just where the registry becomes less office and more instrument: close enough to the wall that I could feel the pipes speaking through the brick, close enough to the tower stair that every change in the pressure bell would arrive in my bones a fraction before it entered the air.

People would bring me numbers.

West culvert, half-rise after rain.

Dye works cistern, slow fall, no visible leak.

South pump warm at the casing but cold at the third joint.

Fountain sweating against the wind.

I would write them down in a small, exact hand. I would make columns. I would sharpen pencils with a pocket knife and save the shavings for kindling. I would know which entries were routine and which ones had begun to lean toward trouble. I would know when a figure had been rounded by laziness. I would know when someone had lied because they were afraid of what they’d heard underground.

But after a while, I don’t think clerk is quite right.

I would be too often absent from my desk.

You would find me in the yards after rain, crouched beside the iron trestles, counting the spacing between droplets beneath a pipe. You would find me running one finger along mortar, not touching the wet itself but the place just beside it. You would find me in the market arcade at dusk, when people are buying onions and lamp oil, staring at the edge of a slate roof because the beads there have formed a pattern no weather can explain.

Children would notice me before adults did. Children always notice the half-useful, half-strange people. They would bring me things: a nail sweating blue-green at the head, a dead moth with mica dust on its wings, a scrap of paper pressed with copper moss, a bottle cap that floated uphill in a gutter for three seconds before correcting itself.

I would pay them in buttons, string, and bad advice.

“Don’t follow eels,” I would say.

“Unless they look back twice.”

Then I would deny having said the second part.

I think I would live above a narrow workshop behind the pump house, where the windows fog from the inside even when the stove is out. The room would be full of shallow trays, chalk maps, glass jars, bits of labeled brick, curled strips of zinc, bent hinges, broken gauge needles, and little lengths of pipe cut from retired lines. Nothing valuable, except by my own private taxonomy. I would keep a kettle on a trivet and forget it until it sang itself hoarse.

On the wall I would have a map of the town, but not the map anyone else uses. Mine would ignore most roads. It would show seams, thresholds, recurring damp, reliable moss, false dry ground, eel appearances, bell-rings, condensation blooms, places where beadlings have been suspected, places where old women sit without admitting why.

It would be a map of where the town is thinking.

No, that is still too human.

Maybe I would not be a person all the way through.

Maybe I would be one of those residents that everyone has decided not to name because naming would oblige them to admit how often they make accommodations.

Not a beadling exactly. They are too small, too intent, too much like punctuation in the grammar of water. I would be larger than that, slower, less pure. Something that has lived too long among ledgers, pipes, and sills to remain only one thing.

I would be an edge-keeper.

A threshold thing with ink-stained fingers.

In daylight, I would pass well enough. I would wear a black coat shiny at the elbows, heavy boots pale with clay, a cap pulled low. I would greet boiler tenders by name and know which fox had denned beneath which cart. I would stand aside for laundry women carrying wet baskets. I would buy bread, coal, lamp wicks, and the bitter little apples that arrive in crates near the reed sheds.

But in certain weather, especially after rain when the sun returns without warmth, I would become difficult to look at directly.

My outline would gather moisture.

Not dramatically. No one would scream. This is not a screaming town. But a gutter boy might glance at me and see that the cuffs of my coat were not merely wet but beaded in perfect rows. A valve clerk might notice that I cast no shadow across dry stone, only along cracks. A child racing droplets down a windowpane might see one stop halfway, hesitate, and turn toward me.

The old people would know.

They would not say anything. They would shift on their threshold benches and make room.

That is where I would go when uncertain: to the old people at the doors. I would ask, as if idly, “Did the east culvert always knock twice before a rise?”

And one would say, “No. Once, before the brick yard sank.”

Another would say, “It knocked three times the year the fountain froze outward.”

Then they would look at me as though I had been born yesterday and also before their grandmothers.

My work, if I had work, would be to notice when a boundary has become too sharp.

The town believes water must be guided, not resisted. But every so often someone forgets. A contractor builds a wall too clean and proud, with no weep holes. A young engineer lays a pipe as if force can replace listening. A householder seals a threshold against damp and traps a little weather inside the boards. Then the settling gathers there. Beadlings come. Hinges sweat. Moss darkens. Eels appear where no eel has business being.

I would arrive with a chalk line, a cold chisel, and a ledger.

I would not curse the owner. That is for neighbors.

I would ask where the water wants to leave.

Then I would make a cut.

A small one. A channel under a sill. A notch in a lip of zinc. A hole no wider than a finger through the mortar. A correction. A courtesy.

Because that would be my nature, I think: not to stop change, not to worship it, but to give it somewhere narrow and honest to pass through.

At night I would climb the pressure tower.

Not always. Only when the bell had rung strangely, or when the frogs were too quiet, or when the starlings had all chosen the same false whistle. The stair would be damp at its outer edge. The iron rail would bloom beneath my palm. At the top I would sit beside the bell and listen to the buried river mutter through the basin.

From there the town would look less like a town than a collection of negotiations.

Brick negotiating with ivy.

Steam with cold air.

Clay with gravity.

Machines with patience.

People with what they refuse to call magic.

The fountain in the square would shine faintly, condensing instead of flowing. The troughs would hold enough water for sparrows. The market arcade would breathe green from its half-abandoned stalls. Pipes would cross the roads like sentences written by a practical but eccentric hand. Somewhere a fox would move along the railway embankment. Somewhere an eel would lift its pale head in a cellar and discover a new question.

And I would understand, up there, that I belonged not to the river or the town, not to machinery or moss, but to the instant where each touches the other and must decide what kind of contact it will be.

So yes.

You would find me near the valve registry, officially.

In the marginal desk by the sweating window.

But really you would find me wherever the town has made an edge and the water has come to read it.

I would be the one called when a dry hinge drips at noon.

The one children suspect can hear beadlings.

The one adults dismiss until the pressure bell rings.

A ledger-keeper, sill-cutter, threshold listener.

A mostly human instrument of the settling.
