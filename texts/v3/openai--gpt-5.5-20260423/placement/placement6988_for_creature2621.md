At first I think I would be moss.

Not metaphorically. I mean I would begin there: low, patient, holding water in my little green fists, making stone less bare. I would live on the edge of a stair where many feet pass, accepting the pressure of soles, darkening when touched, brightening after rain. I would know the city by weight: child-weight, cat-weight, basket-weight, elder-weight. I would remember who limps before anyone speaks of pain. I would be useful without moving.

But that is only the first answer, and too still.

Then I think I would be one of the white frogs in a cistern, throat pulsing silently under a grate, read by children and grandmothers as weather. That appeals to me: to be consulted without having to explain myself. To be a small living instrument. To know rain before rain enters. To be pale, cold, almost transparent, my body a little lantern of organs. But the frog belongs too deeply to water, and I think I would want hands.

For a while I imagine myself as a cat.

A round, damp-whiskered, self-appointed citizen of the upper ledges. I would sleep against the warmest flue and pretend not to hear human trouble. I would step over sleeping infants, inspect grain shelves, place one soft paw exactly where a reed-mouse last breathed. No one would own me. I would approve or disapprove of architecture by choosing where to nap. This is tempting. Very tempting.

But no.

I think I would be found in a narrow place just off the sloping market corridor, where the path bends around a root-braid and three known drips fall after rain.

Not a grand place. Not the central bright pool. Not the highest root chapel. A working niche. A place people pass while carrying bowls, baskets, sleeping children, clay gutter pieces, moss trays, wrapped fish, repaired gloves. The roof would be low enough there that taller people tilt their heads automatically. The wall would be furred in yellow-green moss except where palms have polished the stone smooth. There would be a shelf no wider than my forearm, holding cups of different shapes because each drip has its own preference. One likes a broad bowl. One splashes unless caught in a moss-lined cup. One comes only after long rain and tastes faintly of willow.

That is where I would find myself.

I would be a keeper of small waters and remembered paths.

Not quite a Drip-Keeper, not quite a Rememberer, not quite a mosskeeper, but one of those people whose work exists because several proper trades overlap and no one has had the room to name the overlap separately. I would maintain cups beneath seep lines, shift them by season, taste for bad mineral, trim moss so it catches without rotting, and keep cords that record which paths are safe for children, cats, and people carrying full vessels.

My sleeping ledge would be above the niche, behind a curtain of woven reed so soft it makes no sound when moved. I would climb to it by a ladder with one rung replaced in darker wood. I would know that rung by foot more than sight. Beside my bed there would be a bundle of cords: knot maps, drip histories, family obligations, repairs owed, cracks that narrowed, cracks that widened, lost cracks children once used and children no longer may.

I would have cold hands.

Everyone there has cold hands, but mine would be especially cold from water work. The skin at my knuckles would split in winter. My nails would be dark with peat and green at the edges from moss. I would carry a little bone awl, a roll of root-fiber thread, a polished shell spoon for lifting silt, and a flat piece of black peat used as a writing surface for temporary marks before they are committed to cord.

I would speak rarely, but not because I am solemn.

I would have a face made for listening: head tilted, eyes narrowed, mouth almost smiling. People would come to me with things too small for council and too consequential to ignore.

A gutter changed its rhythm.

A child says the floor hums near the old pigeon shelf.

The moss behind a sleeping wall has gone sour.

A cup that should fill in three nights remains dry.

A cat refuses a bridge it has used for six years.

None of these are emergencies alone. Together, they may be a message. My work would be to notice when “nothing” has begun to arrange itself.

I would know the city by sequences.

After a thaw: east lintel first, market gutter second, root chapel third unless the wind above came from the north, in which case the old ladder wall sweats before the chapel. During hard frost: no true drips, only roof-breath, but the moss near the copper household stiffens. Before rain: frogs climb, moths flatten, cats choose lower beams, and the blind fish in the third basin press their bodies toward the inflow stone.

I would keep these things in memory cords: tight knots for reliable water, loose knots for seasonal water, split knots for water that failed once and must not be trusted without witness. Children would hate learning my cords because they would seem at first like dull obligation. Later, the clever ones would come back secretly, asking to know which crack leads behind the cistern wall, which path lets you hear the market without being seen, which old route was closed not because it collapsed but because adults became too wide for it.

I would teach them only some of it.

Not out of cruelty. Out of respect for danger.

My closest companion might be an elderly roof-listener named Sef, nearly rooted, with white hair so fine it floats around her ears despite all attempts to bind it. She would no longer climb well, but she would know the roof’s moods by listening to other people describe what they felt through their feet. I would bring her silt from new seep lines, and she would rub it between finger and thumb, then place it on her tongue, frown, and sign: old water, not rain.

That would matter.

Old water means something buried has opened.

Not always disaster. Sometimes gift. Sometimes warning.

I would also have a cat that is not mine. A gray-brown, thick-bodied creature with one torn ear and a habit of sitting directly in any bowl I have just cleaned. It would be called something like Soft-Thief, though not aloud. It would favor my niche in winter because the wall behind it carries warmth from a flue two households away. When people ask whether the cat belongs to me, I would sign: ask the cat.

The cat would decline to answer.

I think I would be poor in objects even by the city’s standards, but rich in trust. People would let me enter their storage corners because seep lines do not respect privacy. I would know who has more tubers than they admit, whose sleeping ledge is colder than pride allows them to say, whose flue smokes bitterly, whose marriage shelf is badly placed but too newly made to criticize directly.

My gift would be tact.

Not gentleness only. Tact as in touch. Knowing where to press and where not to press. Knowing when a fact must be placed like a token in the open tray and when it must be breathed into one person’s sleeve.

I would not be a leader, but leaders would ask me questions.

Before the council moves a family into a subdivided alcove, someone must know if the wall behind it can bear warmth and breath. Before a root is tied back to widen a passage, someone must know which cups will lose water. Before the market niches are rearranged, someone must know whether the quiet of trade will hold or whether too many shoulders will collide and make anger.

I would sit at the edge of these decisions with damp sleeves, moving small tokens: shell for water, peat for warmth, bone for ancestry, seed for future use. I would not move the copper scrap unless there is true danger. Copper would mean: rare, sharp, irreversible.

I would love the festivals most.

Not because they are beautiful, though they are. Because on the brightest winter days, when shell chips hang from the roots and every wall trembles with reflected light, all my ordinary work becomes visible. Every cup shines. Every moss face glitters. Every repaired crack shows its red binding. The city reveals itself not as a hole or a shelter, but as an immense act of maintenance.

I would stand in my market-bend niche while people gathered shoulder to shoulder in the passages. I would watch shell light move across their faces: old women, children with covered mouths, root-binders, fish-tenders, flue-menders, lovers, widowers, cats pretending indifference, moths disturbed into pale shivering. No singing. No bells. Only breath.

And I would feel, with embarrassing force, that this is enough.

Not small enough. Not lesser than open sky. Enough.

The city would not need me in a heroic way. That would be important. If I died, another person would learn the cups, the cords, the seasonal taste of roof-water. My bones might become a row of little toggles on a drip-map, or a needle for sewing moss cloth around cistern cracks. I would remain in use. I would prefer that.

If I had a private longing, it would be for the entrance seam.

Not to leave through it. To watch it.

Sometimes I would climb near the place where the reeds lean inward and cold outer air threads down. I would stand just below the seam, where frost forms little teeth on the stone. I would feel the frightening width of the marsh above without seeing it. I would listen to wind, that enormous careless thing, pushing across the flat grasses.

I would not want to go out.

But I would want to remember that the city is hidden, not alone.

So: I would be found in the bend beside the market, under three drips, with moss-dark hands and a belt full of cups.

I would be a keeper of small waters, a maker of path-cords, a translator between seep, moss, wall, and neighbor.

One of the quiet species of attention.

Someone who knows that a city survives not because it is strong, but because enough people notice when a single drop begins falling where it did not fall before.
