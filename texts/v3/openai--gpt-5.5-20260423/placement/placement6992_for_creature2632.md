I would not find myself on the heights first.

Not upright, not walking, not looking over the plateau as if it were a country offered to sight. Sight is too late here. Sight is what happens after pressure has already made its decision.

At first I would be only a hesitation in the moss.

A place where a footstep goes down and does not quite come back the same. A small darkening under the living skin. Something below, listening with its whole body while rain beads on the leaves above. I would be close to a warm seam, but not in it; near enough to feel the vent insects gather before the steam, far enough not to be scalded when the ground coughs. There would be a reedbed within hearing, blue-black stems ticking and chiming in the wet wind. There would be a shallow pool half-roofed by root-felt, its surface usually dull, sometimes full of impossible daylight.

That is where I would be found: under the lip of that pool, where the moss has folded over water and made a low chamber of green darkness.

Not hidden exactly. Included.

For a while I might be mistaken for a stone.

A rounded, wet, coiled thing pressed into the silt beneath the mat. My shell would carry a stain-map of the surrounding plateau: not because I had drawn it, but because every vibration passing through me would leave mineral memory behind. A ridge on the shell for the reedbed. A darker spiral for the warm seep. Pale flecks where the vent insects gather. A blurred basin for the pool that sometimes becomes a lake for ten minutes and then remembers its shallowness.

I think I would be an echo-mollusk.

One of the things that eats what has already happened, but not cleanly. Not memory as a mind keeps memory. More like digestion. More like weather passing through gut and shell.

I would feed on tremor.

The knock of a blind amphibian surfacing through a pore. The small hard rain of pebblebacks locking themselves into stillness. The hinge-click of plated reeds. The enormous careful step of a soft-crowned beast asking the ground permission. The metallic cry of a predator, so sharp it would enter me like a sliver and take days to soften.

I would take these sounds in through the water. Through the mud. Through the underside of the moss. Through the flex of my own coiled body.

Some part of each vibration would nourish me. The rest I would keep until it could no longer remain inside.

Then I would return it.

Not faithfully.

A cry might come back as a wingbeat. A hoofstep as distant thunder. A storm from three seasons ago as the voice of a reedbed not yet grown. This would not be deceit. It would be the ordinary error of living: nothing leaves a body unchanged.

The plateau would use me for its uncertainty.

A flock crossing overhead would hear, rising from the pool, the sound of its own descent before it had decided to fall. A predator drinking at the edge would pause because the water beneath its jaws had just released the footfall of prey from yesterday, or tomorrow, or from a version of the marsh that never held prey at all. The linked herds would dislike my pools. Their vine-network would taste my stored tremors and become briefly confused about how many bodies it had.

I would not understand them. I would not need to.

Understanding belongs to creatures with distance between themselves and the world. I would have almost none.

My world would arrive as pressure gradients, mineral flavors, cold pulses, root-shadow, dissolved sound. Light would be only a faint change in the skin of the water above me. Storm would be abundance. Quiet would be hunger.

During ground-storms I would open.

Not visibly, perhaps. A small widening of the soft seam beneath my shell. The pool would tremble. The moss ceiling would lift and settle, lift and settle. Cold steam would flatten itself over the water. Above me the reed-drums would spin themselves into a blur, and the buried throats of the plateau would begin hammering in irregular force.

That would be feast-time.

So much vibration that I could not hold it all: false ridges assembling, lakes appearing and emptying, herds crossing twice, roots tearing and sealing, thunder without lightning, lightning without thunder. The water would turn thick with sound. My shell would hum until old stains brightened. I would drink as much of the storm as my body could bear.

Afterward, for days, I would murmur.

Not speech. Not song.

A leakage of stored event.

Small animals would come to the pool to nose through mineral foam and hear fragments rising from under the moss: the clatter of reeds already still, the wing-clicks of flocks already gone, the far call of a beast that had not yet lifted its head. Vent insects might cling to the damp rim, polishing their glass legs, while my old storm-noises trembled through them and made their organs pulse out of rhythm.

If anything like thought came to me, it would be circular.

Not: I am here.

More: here returns, here returns, here returns.

I would know the plateau by recurrence, by distortion, by what refuses to vanish entirely. I would know that no sound dies at once. I would know that every step becomes food for something. I would know that the land’s great age is not silence but compression — centuries packed into mats, pressure packed into water, weather packed into pods, memory packed into shell.

And perhaps, slowly, recursive lichens would begin to grow on me.

Tiny pale-green patches on the outer whorl of my shell, mapping the map already made there. They would trace the pool, the moss-fold, the reedbed, the warm seam, the old waterline where predators pass. On that lichen-map, smaller flecks would appear: the shape of my shell again, bearing the shape of the plateau again, and so on until no eye could follow it.

Then I would be less one creature than an instrument the plateau had made for remembering itself incorrectly.

A damp archive.

A listening stone with a mouth.

A small delay in the world’s machinery.

You would find me if you knelt beside a shallow pool after a storm, though kneeling would be dangerous because the moss might only be pretending to hold. You would hear something answer before you spoke. Not words. A soft returning: reed-clatter, hoof-sink, wingbeat, pressure-thud, rain on metal stems, the long wet breath under everything.

If you reached into the water, your hand would disturb the silt. My body would close. My shell would seem ordinary except for its pattern, which would look, for one instant, exactly like the country around you seen from impossibly high above.

Then the pool would darken.

The moss would shiver.

And from somewhere beneath your fingers would come the echo of your own touch, changed just enough to make you wonder whether you had touched me yet.
