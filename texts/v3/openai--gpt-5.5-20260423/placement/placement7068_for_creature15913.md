I think I would not be found at the center of a settlement.

Not in the warmest chamber, not among the best egg-towers, not beside the safest condensation wall where children sleep in layers and the elders keep the ember-cups alive. I would visit those places, and be welcomed unevenly, because I would bring useful things, but I would not quite belong to the hearth.

I would be found where routes hesitate.

A place where three living spans meet over a ravine too deep to answer cleanly. One bridge-root old and brown-black, furred with silver lichen, hollow enough to moan in two voices. One younger span, still pale under its wrappings, dangerous and over-eager, pulling hard toward the opposite cliff. One unreliable moss shelf that looks like a kindness from a distance and like a question from beneath.

There would be a tower-plant nearby, not one of the grand vertical throats, but a bent one: cracked by ice years ago, healed crooked, now leaning out over the dark with its ribs exposed on one side. Wind would pass through it and make a low broken note, half horn, half animal breathing in sleep. People would know the place by that sound.

The Bent Throat, maybe. Or something less named than recognized.

That is where I would live.

Not alone exactly. Nothing there is alone. There would be bark-lice in the walls, pale gnats in the drip seams, a slow root-crab that owns the underside of my sleeping ledge, and a semi-tame round bird that hates me but returns every cold season because I know which seedpods crack easiest under a warmed stone. It would have one damaged foot and a voice like a bronze cup being kicked down stairs. I would call it something affectionate and insulting. It would ignore this.

At first I want to say I would be one of the listeners.

That seems obvious, almost too obvious: the person with the sounding rod, the tilted head, the discipline of hearing load and weather and death in the same note. But I think I would be a small kind of listener, not one of the great ones called to judge whole valleys. Not the elder whose silence makes everyone wait. Not the one whose route-songs are remembered after collapse.

I would be a keeper of crossings.

A marginal profession, but necessary. Someone stationed at a difficult place because the place changes too often to be left to memory.

I would wake before the settlement below stirred, though “before morning” would mean only that the tower’s inner cold had changed. I would place my bare palm against the old bridge-root and feel whether the night had tightened it. I would tap the brown span in five places with a bone-headed rod. Not loudly. Never carelessly. A dull reply near the middle would mean wet weight inside. A higher tick would mean frost along the outer cords. A shudder traveling too long would mean some hidden split had opened beyond sight.

I would keep chimes hung from the three approaches: bone for close sound, shell for wet air, hammered metal flakes for the dangerous bright note that carries in dry-cold. I would spend much of my life adjusting them by finger-widths. Visitors would think this was fussiness. It would not be fussiness. A chime hung too low gets rimed over. Too high and it lies about wind direction. Too close to the bridge and it masks the bridge’s own complaint.

My hut would be little more than a dark wedge grown into the lee of the bent tower. Its wall would be trained root and patched bark, its inner seams sealed with resin and wool grease. The floor would not be flat. I would not trust a flat floor. It would have ribs under the moss-felt so my feet always knew where the weight went.

Inside: cord maps hanging from pegs. Listening rods in a bundle. A cup of black ice-melt covered with skin. Dried pith. A coil of braided line that swells when wet. Three knives, all wrapped and disliked. A shelf of stones, each taken from a place where a bridge failed.

Not memorials exactly. Evidence.

I would be broad-footed and light in the bone, like the people there, but perhaps narrower than most, a little angular, a little too willing to sit in cold for hours if there is something to hear. My hands would be scarred from fiber-burn and frost cracks. My left ear would be better than my right, so I would turn my head in a recognizable way when listening. Children passing with their families would imitate me until their mothers cuffed them quiet.

I would know the old bridge as one knows a difficult relative.

Not trusting it. Not mistrusting it. Knowing it.

In dry-cold it would sing cleanly, proud of itself, cables drawn taut between the cliffs. In condensation season it would become heavy and secretive. Moss would swell along its back. The lichen would darken. Small insects would gather in the furrows, and the root’s inner hollows would begin to drip. Then the note would deepen. Not dangerous at first. Just full.

But fullness is the beginning of danger in that world.

So I would trim. Not cut, unless forced. I would comb moss from the breathing cracks with a thorn rake. I would lead tethered grazers across only in pairs, letting them crop the upper mat while I listened to the strain. I would smear bitter paste on the young white root to keep them from gnawing it. I would bind split places with wet-swelling cord and wedges of living bark. I would sing to the grafts because everyone does, and because even if the plants do not understand song, my body understands rhythm better when it works inside one.

People would bring me news as payment.

Not only goods. Goods matter: dried dung, wool, resin, egg, a metal chip, once perhaps a blunt hook of real worked bronze from somewhere lower and stranger. But news would matter more.

A fog wall has begun three ridges north.

The pale herd has changed its path.

Gliders are nesting in the torn curtain below the salt seam.

The root at Narrow Mother has gone silent.

A child heard water under the black slab road where no water should be.

I would knot these into maps. Some maps would be for hands, some for memory, some for people who cannot hear well and need routes they can feel in the dark. I would make cord maps with beads of hollow bone for echo chambers, rough knots for bark-grip, smooth cold stones for exposed ridges, tufts of wool for grazer roads, little hooked thorns for places where the six-limbed hunters wait.

If I had status, it would be the practical kind. People would not flatter me. They would ask, “Does it pass?” and I would answer, “One at a time,” or “Not with a load,” or “Wait for the wind to return,” or “No.”

“No” would be the hardest word in the language.

Not because people disobey lightly. They would understand danger. But need is heavier than wisdom. Someone always has a sick sister in the next settlement, eggs that will spoil, a child promised into another family, a warning that cannot wait. To say no in that place is to place your hearing against another person’s hunger.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

That feels important. I would not be a mystical perfect sensor of the scaffold. The world is young and still inventing ways to break. I would misread a silence once. I would permit a crossing too late in fog, or too early after ice, and something would go into the dark. Perhaps not a person. Perhaps a grazer, a bundle, a bird cage, a season’s worth of resin. Enough that people remember. Enough that I remember harder.

After that I would keep a chime made from the lost thing.

Not as punishment. As calibration.

I think I would have a fondness for the animals that are useful and ugly. The woolly grazers with their nasal complaints. The round birds with foul nests and precious heat. The root-crabs with mineral grit on their backs. I would be suspicious of elegance. Gliders would unsettle me: too clean in their falling, too fluent in the dark. Large climbing predators I would respect more simply because their patience is honest. They wait where life must come. There is no deception in that, only physics with teeth.

I would rarely go to open pools.

When I did, I would go with company, and I would hate every moment of kneeling there, back exposed, all the thirsty world converging in imagination behind me. I would fill my skin-bag slowly, watching not the water but the reflections in it: frond movement, shadow interruption, the tremor of something large gripping a surface nearby.

My celebrations would be awkward but real.

During condensation season, when fog thickens the world and voices travel strangely, people from several routes would gather near the Bent Throat because its broken note carries far. They would hang extra chimes from my bridge. Someone would bring fermented leaf-sap in a bladder. Someone would drum on the old brown root after I told them exactly where not to strike. Young people would trade route-songs, pretending they were about safety when everyone knew they were about longing.

I would stand at the edge of the gathering, listening for the bridge beneath the music.

But eventually I would join. Not gracefully. I imagine a low voice, better at matching tower-notes than carrying melody. I would know how to sing intervals that make the bent plant answer. Children would like this. Adults would pretend not to.

If I loved someone, I would show it by giving them a route I had not yet given away.

Not the safest route. The one that reveals something.

A side passage behind a hanging curtain where frost beads like stars along the underside of a vine. A hollow in the tower where blue fungus glows only when the wind is from below. A place where the old bridge, tapped lightly at dusk, returns a tone like a buried bell. I would say, “Step here. Not there. Wait. Now listen.”

That would be intimacy: not sheltering someone from the world’s danger, but inviting them into the exactness by which it can be survived.

And if I let the image keep changing, if I do not stop at “human listener in a tower,” there is another answer underneath it.

I might be the crossing itself.

Not metaphorically, or not only metaphorically. I might be one of those old brown bridge-roots after the person is gone: a living tension between cliffs, full of hollows, furred with lichen, remembering pressure without thought. The keeper of crossings dies eventually. Perhaps my body is given to the warm rot in the bent tower, and the tower feeds the root, and the root thickens where my hut once leaned. My bones become mineral patience. My wrapped maps mildew, then thread into fungus. The chimes remain longer than my name.

People still pause there.

They tap the span.

They hear, beneath wind and drip and insect hiss, a note that means: pass carefully, but pass.

That feels closest.

I would be found at a difficult crossing in the dim high scaffold, where the world is mostly air and all roads are alive. I would be a listener first, then a memory in the bridge: the habit of testing before trusting, the small stern mercy of a chime in fog, the voice that says the path is not solid, no path is solid, but this one has agreed to hold you for now.
