I would not be found at the center first.

The center is too declarative: the crown, the slow red turning, the almost-flower teaching the chamber how to mean itself. I would look toward it, certainly. Everything would. But I would begin at the edge of one of the empty places, where the life has made a ring and refused the middle.

A vent-ring.

A little bare circle of hot salt and black stone, no wider than a basin, breathing dry mineral gas at intervals so slow they seem accidental. Around it the copper ribbons keep their distance. The velvet cushions thicken at the boundary but do not cross. Silver powder has fallen there and failed to become anything. It lies in a pale halo, iron-red at the outer edge, white at the center, baked into a crust that stings even stone.

That is where I would be.

Not in the living colony, not in the vent, but in the negotiated margin between them: the place where appetite stops and attention continues.

At first I would be mistaken for residue.

A flake of dead amber plate. A scale shed by a mica-leaf. A curl of black mineral tissue broken from a listening frond. Something thin, hard, and not worth naming. If lifted — though nothing here lifts things — I would be nearly weightless, brittle at the edges, translucent only when held against the false afternoon. My body would be a laminated sliver, copper-veined and smoke-dark, with a faint red point sealed inside me like an ember remembered rather than burning.

I would not have roots.

That would be my strangeness.

Everything else belongs by pressure: plate to seam, mouth to mineral, body to wall. But I would lie loose. Not free in the animal sense, not able to wander with intention, but unattached enough to be a problem. A detachable thought. A piece of the chamber that has come away from itself and not yet been reassigned.

The others would know me as a small discontinuity.

The black fronds would fold when I cooled.

The copper ribbons would brighten along their cut edges when I shifted.

The blue cushions would store the fact of me and release it later as a white pulse.

The silver flowers would dust me carefully, repeatedly, as if trying to persuade me to become local.

But I would remain partly uncommitted.

I would move only during the vent-breaths.

When the little pucker in the floor exhaled its dry battery-taste, the gas would lift one edge of me. Heat would warp me. Static would take hold. I would slide perhaps the width of a fingernail, then settle again, altered by the journey as much as relocated. It might take a year to cross the bare ring. It might take an afternoon that later proves to have belonged to another century. Motion, here, would not be a continuous act. It would be a series of permissions.

I would be a kind of pollinator, but not for flowers.

Not carrying life from one organism to another. Not visiting. Not choosing. I would carry surfaces.

A dusting of silver pollen on my underside. A charge caught from a blue-black cushion. A memory of impossible light from an amber fan. A tremor-pattern received from black fronds before the tremor happened. Wherever I came to rest, I would leave a little of one condition on another: heat on salt, copper on glass, future sunlight on present stone.

Most of the time nothing would happen.

That would be important.

This chamber is made of rare consents. Almost every contact fails. Most powders fall and harden into nothing. Most cracks seal before they can be tasted. Most lights are only memories of apertures the mountain never opened. So I would be patient with failure. I would be almost entirely failure: a long history of not rooting, not blooming, not becoming useful.

But once, after an interval too slow to call waiting, I would settle against the lip of a new hairline crack.

The crack would still be red within. It would have opened recently, or would open later and be visible now by mistake. I would touch it with my chipped edge. The ember sealed inside me would answer, not by flaring, but by aligning. My veins would darken. The silver powder caught in my layers would soften without melting. The stone below me would taste my underside and learn a shape it had not been planning.

Then a filament would grow.

Not from me exactly.

Not from the mountain exactly.

From the agreement between us.

It would be fine as a hair, green-black, jointed with tiny copper glints. It would cross the crack, hesitate, and flatten itself against the new mineral. Behind it, over days or centuries, a second filament would follow. Then a little fan. Then a black fold no larger than a fingernail, opening and shutting before any wind had been invented for it.

That would be my work: to make first contact slightly easier for things that cannot move.

I would be called, if this place used names, a stray hinge.

A dry pollinator.

A scale-bearer.

A little portable edge.

I would belong to the chamber’s uncertainty more than to its life. My body would be made from dead tissues, shed minerals, false light, and one red grain from the turning crown. I would be alive only intermittently, in the way a circuit is alive when current passes through it. Between pulses I would be artifact, litter, fossil-before-fossil. Then the mountain would clank far below, and the chamber’s distributed attention would tighten, and I would briefly know myself as part of the whole.

Not as an individual mind.

As a carried relation.

Warm.

Charged.

Loose.

Crossing.

If a human eye found me, it would probably overlook me. The person would attend to the crown, to the glass leaves, to the walls reflecting impossible chambers. They might step carefully over the vent-ring and never notice the thin dark flake sliding one millimeter in the thermal breath.

But if they stayed long enough, they would see a pattern.

The colonies nearest the bare places would not spread evenly. They would advance by little accidents. New growth would appear along the paths of fallen scales. Silver halos would organize where loose shards had rested. Amber fans would remember light more clearly after one of us had leaned beneath them. The chamber would not have animals, but it would have conveyance. Not weather, but transfer. Not feet, but drift.

I would be one of those drifts.

And perhaps, much later, when the mountain had cooled enough to decide what a cave is, when water finally entered from some crack opened by frost or time, when the first true moss tried to name the walls in green, my kind would already be gone. We would have hardened into seams. Our looseness would be over. The little red grains inside us would have gone dull. A geologist, if there were ever such a witness, might split a black plate and find my outline there: a thin irregular lens of copper, amber, and silver salts, crossing an ancient fissure.

They would call it an inclusion.

They would not know it had moved.

But here, now, while the mountain is unfinished and discovery has not been defined, I would be found at the vent’s edge, half dust and half instrument, carrying one part of the chamber to another.

I would be the smallest possible answer to the question of how a rooted world learns to reach.
