I would not be found on the upper rim, though I would want to begin there, looking down into the white basin until the town became a single wet organ of roofs, ledges, channels, smoke, and moving light.

At first I would think I was one of the children.

One of the solemn ones in stiff reed shin-guards, crouched beside a pool with my hands tucked into my sleeves, watching a cloud go the wrong way in the water. I would have a bone box hidden under my sleeping shelf, full of moth wings, crab shells, a fox whisker, a chip of upward-growing ice wrapped in wool though it had long since melted. I would know which steps to slap before trusting them. I would dare another child to put an ear to the old softened stair. I would be frightened of the forbidden reflections and unable to leave them alone.

But that is too warm a life for me. Too much breath. Too much face.

Then I would think I was a jackdaw.

White-feathered, black-eyed, tucked in a chimney crown with my claws wrapped around a smoke-warmed peg. I would imitate the first thaw-click of morning so perfectly that a woman below would lift her head from grinding grain and scold the roof. I would collect sounds instead of objects: goat cough, knife on shale, grandmother’s warning, the hush before all the birds stop. I would be nearly a citizen, nearly an omen, nearly a nuisance. I would know the town from above, which is a kind of knowledge, but not the deepest one.

No. I would be lower.

Not in the marsh exactly. Not in the open pools where the grey-throated birds stand with frost on their lashes. Not among the black mud and sleeping frogs, though I would pass near them sometimes, where bubbles knock softly against the under-ice.

I would be in the walls.

I would live in the narrow water that crosses houses.

You would find me in a channel no wider than two fingers, cut through old stone under a threshold polished by generations of wet boots. The house above me would be built into the cliff, three terraces above the marsh-edge, close enough to hear the central bridge when someone crosses it with a loaded sled, close enough to smell smoke, goat wool, boiled roots, damp hide, and mineral seep.

The room would have a raised sleeping shelf, a cupped hearth, a roof that drips only in three places because the family is careful, and a carved mouth in the uphill wall where water enters continuously. The stream would run through the floor, pass under a flat stepping-stone, slide beneath the door, and vanish into a darker seam between two houses.

That is where I would be.

I would be one of the glass eels.

Transparent except for the small dark punctuation of organs. Almost not there unless the light catches me. A thread of living water with a spine’s intention.

I would move through the town without owning any of it. Through household basins, under thresholds, behind plaster, along the old cut channels that were once stairs and then became drainage and then became my roads. I would know the undersides of rooms better than the rooms themselves. I would know which families rise before dawn, which children trail fingers in the floor-stream when they think no one sees, which elders murmur names into water before drinking.

I would not speak, but people would watch for me.

A flash in the basin: reassurance.

A silver line beneath the sleeping shelf: good.

Three eels in the hearth-channel after snow: the house is still in favor with its own foundations.

My absence would trouble them.

They would say, softly, “Have you seen them?”

They would cut fresh grooves with stone blades. They would clear grit from the carved mouth. They would lift the reed screen and whisper apologies to the water, though no one would admit to being afraid.

I would not be an omen because I wished to be. I would not bless or curse. I would only leave when the water changed.

When the mineral taste thickened wrong.

When warmth came from below instead of above.

When the walls began sweating sour.

When a sickness had entered the house before the people had a name for it.

They would think I knew the future. But I would only know the water sooner than they did.

That would be enough.

I would pass through their lives as a small, precise unease. A necessary uncertainty. A household creature and not a pet. A witness no one can question.

In the brightest hours, when the whole basin fills with shattered light, I would hide deep in the shaded channel under the threshold. Above me, boots would pause. Someone would knock ice from a cup. A child would be told not to step into a certain pool. A goat would refuse a stair. A jackdaw would make a false dripping sound from the roof, and an old woman would say, without looking up, “Liar.”

At night I would travel.

The water would loosen its hidden doors. Channels too narrow by day would open with thaw. I would slip through a seam behind the plaster, down into a wall-cold runnel, through the abandoned drain of a house whose roof became a smokehouse after collapse. I would pass the roots of moss. The backs of stone pins. A buried tooth sealed into mortar. The finger bones of an ancestor placed where a wall needed memory.

I would know the town as taste and pressure.

Fox above: warmth and musk through the roof-water.

Marmot burrow near: earth-soft vibration.

Spring-mouth ahead: bright mineral cold.

Feather-water: avoid until the ninth drip.

Cloud-wrong pool: stillness with a depth that does not match its basin.

I would never rise fully into view there.

No sensible eel would.

Still, I might come close beneath the ice skin and look upward. I might see, distorted through water and reflected sky, the soft underside of the town: bridge arches, hanging icicles, reed fences, human faces bent over brightness, all of them wavering, all of them dependent on channels they cannot fully map.

And perhaps that is why I would belong there.

Not as a ruler. Not as a builder. Not even as a voice.

As a passage-being.

A listener made of almost nothing.

A citizen of seepage.

One of the small transparent lives that prove the town is still connected to itself.
