I would not be in the widest bright plaza at first.

I think I would be found just off from it, where the central basin begins to lower toward the chain of warm shallow pools, beneath one of the braided root bridges that passes between moss courts and waxy spires. Not on top of the bridge, not where a stranger would walk, but underneath it, adhered to the living underside where the air is hotter and wetter, where drops gather slowly along root-hairs before falling into the green water below.

At first I might be mistaken for part of the bridge.

A pale knob.  
A seed-case.  
A small unfinished stone grown in the wrong place.

Then, after a long while, I would move.

I would be one of the slow shell-bearers.

Small, dark-bodied, almost boneless beneath my cup of cream-colored mineral shell. My back would look like new limestone: ridged, damp, unworn, as if the under-earth had begun to make a pebble and then allowed it to crawl. My underside would be soft and black-green, tasting the root as I crossed it. I would move with such patience that movement would seem less like travel than a change in emphasis.

My work would be maintenance.

I would graze the fine films that grow too thick on the breathing surfaces. I would polish the pores of the root bridges with my passing. I would open little clogged places where moisture has gathered too richly, where fungus has dimmed the light, where the country’s skin needs to remember how to breathe. I would not eat much. I would not desire much. I would live by removing excess, by making surfaces available again.

The plants would permit me.

That would be my form of citizenship.

Not freedom, exactly. Freedom would be too windy a word for that place. I would belong because I did not interrupt the pause. I would borrow warmth and return cleanliness. I would take film and leave openness. I would cross the underside of corridors made for larger creatures and never ask why the corridors had been left open.

If a pale lizard passed above me, I would feel it through the bridge: a light pressure, a held breath, a dimming of animal blood. If a moth settled on a glowing leaf nearby, I would taste the powder it shed when the air thickened. If a stranger stepped onto the bridge, the whole root would speak their weight into my soft body.

Then I would stop.

Not from fear. From attention.

My little shell would seal against me. My dark flesh would withdraw completely into the cup. I would become a piece of pale mineral, one more unfinished thought of the basin. I could wait like that for days. Waiting would not feel like loss. In that country, waiting is one of the ways a thing remains accurate.

I would know the world mostly by contact.

The underside of root.  
The mineral sweetness of seepage.  
The difference between useful film and suffocating film.  
The tremor of water accepting a drop.  
The warmth of a nearby pool before I reached it.  
The faint tightening of the great organism when something foreign entered.

I would have no need to see the whole valley. The whole valley would arrive in pieces through pressure and taste.

Sometimes I would pass near the upright almost-people in the central courts. From below, through the roots, I would feel their stillness as a deeper stillness than stone. They would not walk, but they would exert a kind of downward remembering. Around their bases the films would gather differently. I would clean there carefully, not because I revered them, but because the plant-country did. Their roots, or the roots that used them, would be more sensitive. Their mineral faces would glow faintly above me, unseen.

I would never know whether they had once been people.

I would only know that they were surfaces requiring tenderness.

My shell would grow slowly from what I ate and what the water gave me. Fine mineral milk from stone cups. Fungal dust. The pale leavings of root-skin. With time, the ridges on my back would thicken, taking on the same newness as the basin’s exposed stone. I would be animal and masonry at once: a moving fragment of the country’s unfinished architecture.

Perhaps that is why I would be there.

Not as a voice.  
Not as a witness from outside.  
Not as one of the remembered upright forms.

As a small correction.

A creature whose life consists of keeping the living walls from closing too completely. A permitted exception that helps permission remain possible. Where paths are not cleared but allowed, I would be one of the tiny reasons allowance continues.

If I lived long enough, I imagine I would move less.

My shell would become heavy. Its pale cup would thicken until withdrawal and emergence were almost the same act. I might settle under a root arch near a pool where the water never overflows. Film would grow around my rim. The plant would not digest me. Not quickly. It would interpret me.

Root-hairs would touch the shell first, tasting the mineral I had carried. A green skin would form over one side, then withdraw, then return. My body would soften into the country by degrees. The cup on my back might remain as a little pale alcove under the bridge, a tiny stone hollow where condensation gathers.

Later, a moth might drink there.

A thread-creature might alter my reflection from below.

A younger shell-bearer might pass over me and polish my rim without knowing I had ever moved.

That would be acceptable.

In that world I would not want to be central. I would not want to be separate enough to be heroic. I would want to be useful in the hidden places where the country touches itself: under bridges, along root ceilings, beside warm pools, near old stone ribs being gently revised into trellis.

I would be small, slow, and almost silent.

A dark soft body carrying a cup of new limestone.

A cleaner of breathing surfaces.

A little moving permission beneath the root bridge, in the hot yellow-white pause.
