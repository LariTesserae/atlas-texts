I don’t think I would be one of the towers.

That is the first image that offers itself, because they are so obviously magnificent: hollow organs of pale bark and glass, standing alone with centuries stored in their ribs. It would be tempting to say I would be one of those old fluted chimneys, conducting lightning, opening a crystalline flower once every three hundred years, making weather by breathing.

But no. Too sovereign. Too much vertical certainty.

I would not be a herd-beast either, though I love them: those long-distance bodies, narrow and disciplined, carrying green-black shade on their backs like inherited weather. There is something moving about them, the way their tenderness has become architecture. Adults standing around calves not because they are soft, but because wind is a blade and the young must be given time to harden. I can imagine wanting to be among them, moving in a dark procession under the late gold light, every step known through the crust by things waiting far away.

But I don’t think I belong in the open.

I think I would be found at an edge.

Not a dramatic edge. Not the lip of a canyon where the whole continent roars below. A more functional threshold: the inside rim of a vent tower, where the root-field’s pressure becomes air.

I would live in the throat of one of the old plants.

Small. Sealed. Nearly mineral.

A pressure-scribe, maybe. That is not what anything there would call me, because nothing there would need names in that way, but it is close enough.

I would be a dry little animal, or an animal-adjacent thing, no larger than a hand when folded flat. My body would be plated in translucent amber and mica-white chitin, with thin conductive veins branching through the plates like fossil lightning. I would have many legs, but most of them would not be for walking. They would be for holding. Clamp-feet. Hooked toes. Little articulated braces for surviving the moments when the continent exhales and the air tries to erase anything not bolted to purpose.

My eyes would be poor in the bright hours. Perhaps just dark bead-lenses under shutters, useful only for detecting whether the world outside has become too white to enter. I would not trust sight much. Sight lies here. Glare makes distances false; dust turns nearness into weather.

I would listen through my shell.

Not with ears. With plates.

My back would be ridged with piezoelectric vanes: dull silver in shade, violet at night when charge moves through them. Each vane would tremble differently. One for deep root-strain. One for seed-drum percussion. One for hooves crossing the crust twenty kilometers away. One for the opening of pores across a plain too distant to smell. One for lightning gathering along a copper-collared tower. One for the minute, intimate rasping of vent-mites at the valve lip beside me.

I would live where messages become weather.

Inside the tower there would be chambers stacked upward into darkness: gas sacs, seed mills, pollen bladders, mineral sieves. The walls would be warm in some places, cold in others. They would pulse slowly, not like a heart, but like an argument being conducted in pressure. I would crawl along inner ribs polished by centuries of wind. I would feed on what the tower cannot use cleanly: excess salts, flakes of conductive mineral, sugar dust crystallized at seam-lines, dead mites fused to resin, pollen grains too malformed to launch.

A scavenger, then.

But not only.

My kind would be tolerated because we keep the small passages articulate. We would polish the charge-hairs. We would rasp mineral crust from pores before it can seal them wrong. We would chew tunnels through dead resin but not living tissue. We would eat the failed seeds before rot-equivalents of dryness—fungal dust, borers, parasitic mats—can make footholds. We would be janitors of breath.

And because of the plates on our backs, because of the way we hold tremor, we would also become part of the tower’s sensing.

When the root-field below tightens, we would feel it first in our legs. When the western plains bloom, a shiver would come through the buried vascular seams and climb the tower as a subsonic command. The tower would know in its own slow way. But my kind would know quickly, nervously, with animal alarm. We would click. We would lift our vanes. We would move in patterns across the inner bark.

Not language. Not exactly.

More like punctuation added to a vast sentence already being written in gas and wood.

During ordinary bright hours, I would be wedged in a groove just inside a sealed vent. Outside, the light would be too hard to look at directly. I would see it only as a white blade through a narrow slit. Dust would hiss along the tower’s exterior. Leaf-mirrors would shift overhead with that metallic sound like knives being drawn. Far below, maybe, a herd would pass, each hoof-click traveling up through root and tower until my shell answered with tiny sympathetic pain.

I would be afraid of predators, but not in the open-running way.

My predators would be thieves of apertures: needle-mouthed things that crawl into vents during bloom, glass-jawed larvae that mimic harmless mites until they reach the scribe nests, kite-hunter parasites dropped from the sky in seed-shaped capsules. I would have no courage in the heroic sense. My courage would be procedural. Clamp down. Seal vents. Fold limbs. Stop wasting motion. Become a fleck of mineral until the danger passes.

At night I would emerge.

That feels right.

Brief cold. Electric dark. The continent’s hidden lines glowing under the crust. Pollen veils visible as ghost-weather moving fast across the plains. Tower flowers opening not as petals but as resonant wounds of crystal and geometry.

I would climb to the inner edge of one of those apertures and brace myself in the hum.

The flower would sing through my body.

Different pitches would mean different ripeness, different charges, different alignments with distant groves. Pollinators would arrive in clicking swarms, their wingbeats like static poured through bone flutes. They would jostle past me, bead-eyed and intent, stealing and serving in the same act. My job then would be dangerous and necessary: to scrape away mineral film from the crystalline filaments so they vibrate cleanly. Too much dust and the pitch falls flat. Too little insulation and charge arcs wrong. A bad tone means the wrong pollinators, or none, or predators drawn too early.

So I would tend the note.

That is where I recognize myself most strongly: not as the singer, not as the flower, not as the mind of the root-field, but as a small custodian of intelligibility. Something that lives by keeping signals from becoming noise.

During pressure-blooms, I would become almost nothing.

The first sign would be pressure in the walls. Then tension in every seam. Then a low concussion arriving from below, too large to be sound. The other scribes would retreat inward. Vent-mites would flatten. Pollinators would either flee or commit themselves to the launch. The tower would open.

The world outside would become white force.

Gas would tear upward through the column, carrying pollen, chaff, spores, grit, dead skin, living wings. The chamber would roar so completely that thought, if I had thought, would be scoured down to its fastened essentials. Hold. Seal. Angle the plates. Do not inhale dust. Do not lose moisture. Do not let go.

Some of my kind would let go.

Not always by accident.

The oldest scribes, their plates worn thin and their bodies heavy with stored mineral memory, might climb deliberately into the outer throat before a bloom. When the vent opened, they would be flung into the upper air, curled tight, vanes locked. Most would die as bright fragments. A few would cross impossible distances and strike another tower, another vent, another province of the continent. If they survived, they would carry in their bodies the tremor-patterns of their birth tower: mineral traces, pollen residues, microbial companions, perhaps even the electrostatic habits of an old root-field.

Dispersal as archive.

That would be the species’ secret importance. We would not merely clean the towers. We would move tiny histories between them.

The plant networks remember at continental scale, yes, but slowly, through root and chemistry and season. My kind would be faster, though smaller and less reliable. We would be dry messengers disguised as maintenance. Each of us a little sealed ledger, crossing the upland by catastrophe.

If you opened one of us after death—wasteful, but suppose a predator did—you would find no soft abundance. You would find tightly packed organs, vapor sacs, salt bladders, a narrow gut lined with abrasive teeth, and behind the head a fan of thin mineral leaves. Those leaves would hold charge patterns for a while after death. If laid against living bark, they might discharge in faint sequences: not thoughts, not words, but records of pressure, bloom, lightning, flowering, herd-passage. The tower that absorbed us would receive a rumor of elsewhere.

I would be full of rumors.

I would know the continent not as a map but as a set of tensions. The western escarpments would be a taste of copper dust in my plates. The old lightning groves would be a recurring ache along my back. A migration route would be a rhythm: crack, grind, pause; crack, grind, pause. A dead tower would be a silence with minerals in it. A flowering plain would be a pressure change so vast that every leg unclamps for one dangerous instant.

And I would love, if that word can survive being dried to its functional core, the moments just after a bloom.

The tower emptied. The sky clearing. White chaff still falling upward and sideways in confused streams. The walls cooling. The vent lips raw and bright green where they opened. Mites returning to scrape. Pollinators gone. Seeds launched into distances no eye can follow.

Then the low sound of the continent resuming itself.

Not peace. There is no peace there, not as stillness.

But balance.

I would crawl out onto a shaded inner ledge and clean dust from my vanes one by one. Far below, the plain would flash ivory and blue. The root-lines would settle. Somewhere beyond the horizon, another pressure-field would begin to wake, and I would feel its first question through the bark.

So that is where I would be:

inside an old hollow tower on the high dry continent, near the valve where breath becomes weather.

And I would be a pressure-scribe: small, plated, many-legged, half janitor and half instrument, feeding on mineral dust, polishing the throats of flowers, reading tremors through my shell, carrying tiny histories between immense plants.

Not a citizen of the plant civilization.

More like a comma in its breathing.
