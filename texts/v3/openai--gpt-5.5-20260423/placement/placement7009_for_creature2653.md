You would find me neither at the deepest vents nor under the open slit.

Not at first.

At first I think I would belong near the entrance, because I would be curious about the surface — that white, killing lid of the world, the impossible plain above the city’s skull. I imagine myself as one of those who climb out at night with resin wrapped in pale cloth, with black glass blades packed in ash, with my eyes narrowed against stars that feel too numerous and too far away. I imagine returning before dawn, sun-burned and quiet, carrying news of rain in countries no one believes in.

But that is only the first image. It is too dramatic. Too much horizon.

No. I would live lower.

Not in the deepest black, where the wind organs speak first and the wrong breath gathers. I would not be brave enough for that, not every day. I would not be one of the old burned-eyed women who can hear pressure through her teeth and stop a whole city with one lifted hand.

I would be found in the middle levels, where the city is most itself: not shrine, not workshop, not dwelling, but all of them at once.

There is a chamber there, halfway up the eastern wall of the fissure, reached by three ladders and a stair so worn it feels like a memory of feet rather than stone. The entrance is low. You must turn sideways to pass through. A curtain of pale root-filament hangs over the lintel, trimmed square at shoulder height and dusted along the cuts with bitter ash. Inside, the room is longer than it first appears, narrowing toward the back, its ceiling varnished black by generations of resin smoke.

It used to be a kiln yard. Before that, a shrine. Before that, someone’s sleeping room. The wall still holds the thumbprint-plaster of an old hearth, and behind one patch of newer clay there are dead standing upright in the thickness, wrapped and salted, their names no longer in the wall but on tablets hung from a copper cord near the door.

That is where you would find me.

I would be a keeper of names and closures.

Not a priest exactly. Not a historian in the surface sense. Something more practical and more feared: one of those called when a room is to be sealed, or opened, or forgotten correctly.

I would know which tablets can be moved and which must stay near the breath of their dead. I would know the signs of a wall that has become too hungry to disturb: green-black gloss at the seam, salt fur growing in a spiral instead of a bloom, root-filament tasting of metal when touched to the tongue. I would know how to ask an old room whether it still belongs to the living.

My tools would hang from me in quiet bunches: a ceramic awl, a copper scraper thin as a fingernail, a packet of ash, a mask with three filters, a string of blank salt-clay tablets, a little black-glass blade for cutting wrappings or roots. My hands would always be dry. My palms would be lined with gray. Under my nails there would be soot and powdered plaster no amount of scraping could remove.

People would come to me when a family alcove had filled.

When a sleeping shelf had to be cut into a wall that might contain somebody’s grandmother.

When a doorway ledge cracked and exposed old fiber wrapping.

When a child found a name tablet in a storage room and no one knew whose dead it belonged to.

When a vent change made a sealed chamber warm again after fifty years, and the question arose: is this room returning to us, or is something beneath it asking to be fed?

I would not decide alone. No one sensible decides alone in that city. But I would be one of the ones who kneels, touches the wall, smells the dust, listens for the little inner clicking that means wait.

My work would be full of pauses.

That, I think, is why I belong there.

In a city where survival depends on doing so much — sealing, cutting, carrying, grinding, tending, herding heat — I would belong to the craft of not-yet. Not yet open. Not yet carve. Not yet move the dead. Not yet feed this seam. Not yet trust the quiet.

I would be trained to respect hesitation as a tool.

As a child I would have been too still.

Not silent, exactly, but prone to stopping in passages where others hurried, watching dust lean in the air. My family might have thought me vent-stupid at first, always pausing on stairs, always looking at old lintels, always putting my ear to plaster. Then one day I would have refused to sleep in an upper room because the wall sounded “sweet.”

Adults would laugh, then stop laughing when the plasterer tasted the bloom and found it alkaline-wet with hidden vapor. The room would be sealed before dawn. No one would die. After that they would apprentice me to an old tablet-keeper with one clouded eye and a voice like ash rubbed between fingers.

She would teach me the city’s grammar of endings.

How to seal a crack without insulting the breath behind it.

How to dry a body so it remains kin and does not become danger.

How to scrape a name from a wall only when the wall itself must be repaired, and how to press that name into a tablet while speaking it softly enough not to wake lower air.

How to distinguish abandonment from rest.

That would be important. In the crack, a vacant room is not necessarily empty. It may be storing coolness. It may be protecting seed. It may be holding a grief no one living is ready to disturb. It may have been surrendered to roots by agreement. It may be a scar.

I would grow into someone people trust reluctantly.

They would not seek me out for comfort. My arrival would mean a boundary has become uncertain. Children would quiet when I passed with my tablet cord. Lovers would stop arguing. Plasterers would make room. Root-cutters would watch my hands to see whether I reached for ash or blade.

But I would not be grim. Not always.

I would keep small jokes folded in me like spare cloth. I would have a fondness for stubborn children named after ungrindable minerals. I would trade old scandal in whispers with kiln aunties. I would know which family claims the sweetest upper pods but secretly leaches them badly. I would accept resin paste from widowers and pretend not to notice when they give me too much because they do not know what else to do with their hands.

In festival weeks I would hang lamps behind the black leaves outside my chamber, and the tablets by the door would shine dull orange. I would paint my veil with powdered violet mineral and sing badly but steadily into an approved shaft. My voice would come back deeper than it went in, as all voices should in that place.

I think I would love the plants, but cautiously.

There would be one root curtain in particular outside my room, older than my apprenticeship, older perhaps than the current shape of the stair. Its filaments would be pale at the ends and smoke-brown near the wall. It would smell of hot cedar and iron when trimmed. I would speak to it when alone, not because I believed it understood words, but because anything that holds a threshold for sixty years deserves courtesy.

I would feed it powdered salts in the cool margin before dawn. I would dust its wounds. I would cut it back when it tried to enter the tablet niche. I would tell it names sometimes — not the secret ones, not the newly dead, but old names worn smooth from being carried too long.

Perhaps, over years, it would learn the shape of my breath.

Perhaps that is superstition.

In that city the difference between superstition and maintenance is mostly a matter of whether someone survives.

I would have gone to the surface only a few times.

Enough to know I disliked it. Enough to understand why returning travelers come back altered. The horizon would frighten me. So much room would feel like exposure, like a wall removed without consent. The sky would seem not empty but open, carelessly open, a wound no one had sealed.

I would pity surface people for their extravagance of distance.

I would envy them water only in dreams.

Not waking dreams. Waking, I would know better. Waking, I would think of water as a dangerous myth, an undisciplined substance that falls, spills, rots, seeps into things and changes them without asking. But asleep, perhaps, I would dream of a clear cup and wake with my mouth aching, ashamed and fascinated.

Then I would eat black bulb gel, rub ash on my teeth, and go back to work.

My closest friend might be a pipe-listener from the lower levels, someone with scarred cheeks from a bad vent year and a laugh too loud for enclosed rooms. We would argue often. She would trust sound; I would trust residue. She would say the city speaks plainly if you put your ear in the right place. I would say nothing speaks plainly after passing through stone.

Together we would be called to old sealed rooms.

She would listen.

I would read the salt.

Sometimes we would disagree, and then the council would wait, because no one wise opens a wall while hearing two kinds of warning.

If I died well, I would be useful afterward.

That matters there.

I would not want a grand alcove. I would want to be placed in the thickness near my chamber, behind the wall where the root curtain grows but not so near that it feeds on me too quickly. My name tablet should be removable. All names should be movable; walls change, and the dead must not be vain about architecture.

Maybe a small vent-thread would be left near me.

Not enough to whistle. Just enough that, in certain pressure shifts, a little warmth would pass over the wrappings and through the plaster. Enough to keep me in the city’s breath.

But before that, while living, I would be the one crouched at thresholds.

A figure in a stiff veil, knees powdered white, head tilted toward a wall.

Someone called for endings, but not devoted to death. Devoted to continuity. To the careful conversion of use into memory and memory back into use. To the knowledge that nothing in the crack is ever simply gone: rooms become walls, walls become shelter, shelter becomes shrine, shrine becomes storage, storage becomes root-bed, root-bed becomes food, food becomes hand, hand becomes repair.

So yes.

You would find me in the middle seam, among tablets, ash, root curtains, and sealed doors.

I would be a closure-keeper.

A reader of rooms that have stopped speaking loudly.

One of the city’s smaller organs of memory.

Not the heart. Not the mouth. Not the lungs.

Perhaps the scar tissue. Perhaps the fingertip pressed to a crack, feeling whether it is safe to widen.
