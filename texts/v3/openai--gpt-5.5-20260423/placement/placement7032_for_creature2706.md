At first I think I would be drawn to the pale upper shelves, to the clean exposed places where the sky lies flat in every pool and the wind keeps the stone honest.

But I would not last there.

I would be too given to listening. Too inclined to press myself into shadow and wait for the place to speak through pressure, cold, and seepage. So I would be found lower down, in one of the sunken courts where fog lingers after the rest of the plateau has brightened. Not the deepest court, not the warmest spring mouth, but a basin just above it: a place of black moss, tea-colored water, mineral lips grown thick over older ledges. A place with a narrow runnel entering from the east and a hidden underflow leaving beneath a cracked shelf. A place that seems still until one learns how many small messages are passing through it.

I would be one of the low ones, but old enough that I had begun to resemble the architecture.

Not ancient. Not a living ledge yet. But no longer quick.

My back would be broad and flattened, ridged here and there with pale mineral plates laid down over seasons of lying half-submerged in lime-rich water. The plates would not cover me evenly. They would gather along the spine and shoulders, thicken at the hips, chip at the elbows, darken where moss had found purchase. In the brighter months I would be green-black along the throat from the fog court’s stains, with faint bluish veins at the wrists from wintering too close to the spring mouth below. My hands would be long, soft-padded, and careful. My belly would know the temperature of stone better than my eyes knew distance.

I would not be a hunter, except of films.

I would move slowly over submerged shelves, pressing my mouth to the slick mineral skin, drawing up algae, bacteria, the smallest drifting things caught in lime scum. I would know not to clean a place too thoroughly. I would feed in arcs and leave the center to cloud again. I would remember which basin recovered after three rains, which after five, which never fully recovered if visited before the sedges seeded. Hunger would make me exact, not desperate.

My dwelling would not be a house. It would be a sequence.

A sleeping crack under a north-facing lip.

A warm underflow for ice months.

A resonant stone shaped like a buried bell, good for sending knocks through three connected basins.

A hidden shelf where eggs can cling beyond the reach of wading birds.

A shallow court where evening insects sometimes gather when the day has been unusually bright.

A narrow passage under a slumped terrace, known by its taste: cold first, then iron, then old leaves.

If asked where I lived, I would not point to a location. I would touch the water, then the wall, then the seam where both disappeared.

I think I would be a keeper of continuities. Not a priest, not a ruler, not even a teacher in any formal sense. More like one who has survived enough freezes to remember which openings are lies.

The young would pass me quickly in the warm season, transparent and nervous, scattering through silt threads. I would seem dull to them at first: a lump with eyes, a stained old back among stained stone. But when the rain came wrong, when a channel that should have filled remained thin, when a spring pulsed cloudy and bitter from below, they would come near enough to feel my throat-clicks through the shelf.

Do not cross there.

Wait two nights.

Eggs higher.

Cold rising.

Stone loose.

I would know the sounds of danger mostly by interruption. A fox is not a sound but a pattern of careful absences: flies lifting, one midge skittering, the water-skin tightening before a paw touches the rim. A bird-shadow is a pressure from above, a sudden command written across many small bodies at once. Falling stone is different. Falling stone announces itself first inside the bones.

My language would be small.

A knuckle against wet limestone.

A pulse under the throat.

A closed mouth opened against the skin of a pool.

A pause held long enough to mean no.

I would not speak often. In such a place, speech would be costly because attention is survival. But I would answer when answered. At evening, when the terraces lose their edges and the mist rises from the basins, I would climb onto the damp rim beside the resonant ledge. I would set my chin on my hands. Across the court, another old one would tap once from beneath an overhang.

I would tap twice back.

Not greeting exactly.

More like: still here.

And beneath that: water continues.

My fears would be simple. A late freeze after eggs. A dry wind over too many days. A tremor inside a shelf I had trusted. The bright silence after a predator has learned patience. A spring changing taste in a way no elder remembers.

My pleasures would be almost indistinguishable from conditions.

The first loosening of edge-ice.

The return of insect bodies to the evening air.

A pool clearing after days of milk-green cloud.

Warmth held under a stone after thin sun.

The pressure of kin lying close along a winter shelf.

The correct sound from a hidden runnel after rain.

I would own nothing that could be carried far. Perhaps I would keep, not possess, three flat stones near the mouth of the underpass: not blocking it, not marking it for strangers, but altering the water’s note just enough that those of my line could hear whether the underflow remained open. After each overflow I would replace them. After a large flood I would search for them without grief. If one were gone, another stone would eventually sound nearly right.

My dead would be in the walls.

Not as ghosts, not as names, but as lime complications, pale interruptions in the shelves where bodies had been given over to the slow basin. I would not visit them to remember faces. Faces are for air-breathers and bright animals. I would visit the flow that had taken them. I would know that under one mineral lip the water runs softer because a body once altered its path.

In time, if I lived long enough, I would become less individual even to myself.

The mineral plates on my back would thicken. Moss would darken the seams of my shoulders. My movements would narrow to the necessary ones: feed, listen, warn, mate perhaps no longer, guard when needed, shift with weather. Young ones would mistake me for stone until I opened my eyes. Visitors would never see me unless they stood still for half a day.

And I would be content with that.

To be nearly landscape here would not be failure. It would be the local form of wisdom.

So you would find me in the fog court above the cold spring, half under the travertine lip, watching the water-street with only my eyes exposed. My hands would be spread against the stone. My throat would rest where the basin carries sound best. I would be listening for the plateau’s small decisions before they became visible.

I would be low.

I would hold fast.

I would waste nothing.

I would remember water.
