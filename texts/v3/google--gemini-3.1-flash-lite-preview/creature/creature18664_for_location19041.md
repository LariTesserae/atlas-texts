The Basin is not populated by a civilization of builders, nor by a collective of survivors. It is inhabited by the **Custodians of the Pulse**—beings that are less like people and more like the living, shivering components of the Basin’s own immense consciousness.

They call themselves the *Ferrous-Bloomed*. To look upon one is to witness a figure that has spent its entire existence softening into its surroundings. They are tall, spindly things, their limbs elongated by gravity and the perpetual, reaching growth of the shade. Their skin—if it can be called that—is a translucent, parchment-like membrane, stretched tight over a frame of articulated brass tubing and calcified sap. They do not walk so much as they flow, navigating the mossy walkways with the slow, deliberate grace of a clock-hand moving across a dial.

They do not speak. Language requires the rapid, volatile expenditure of energy, whereas their existence is predicated on the economy of the drip and the hum. Instead, they communicate through a process of *resonance*. When two Custodians pass on the narrow root-paths, they pause and press their palms—which are tipped with specialized, vibrating copper pads—against the bark of the nearest Boiler-Fern. By modulating the frequency of the machinery, they exchange information: the status of a clogged hydraulic valve, the pressure of a sap-feed, or the subtle cooling of a regional geothermal vent.

The Custodians do not eat. They are nourished by the same humidity that sustains the orchard. They possess gills along their ribs that filter the ozone-heavy fog, extracting the trace minerals suspended in the condensation. Their life cycle is linked to the gears; when a Custodian grows tired, nearing the end of their several-hundred-year span, they do not die in the traditional sense. They find a structural weakness in the architecture—a corroded joint or a sagging branch—and they sit. They weave their own limbs into the iron armatures, their internal sap hardening into a structural resin. Slowly, they become a living weld, a support strut, or an auxiliary gear, their consciousness fading into the great, low-frequency vibration of the Basin.

The "weirdness" of the place trickles down into their temperament. Because the clockwork-mites are the only other moving things, the Custodians regard them with a strange, pastoral affection. They carry small pouches of brass filings, occasionally scattering them like seed for the mites to harvest, ensuring the little scavengers keep the lubrication channels clear of excessive rust.

They are keepers of the order, but they are not masters. They are terrified of silence—not the silence of the room, but the silence of the *mechanism*. To a Custodian, a seized gear is a tragedy; a stopped piston is a death. They treat the orchard with the meticulous, neurotic care of a jeweler polishing a gem that exceeds the size of a mountain. 

When you stand in the Basin, you might catch a glimpse of one from the corner of your eye—a dark, spindly silhouette framed by the ghostly violet glow of lichen and the rhythmic spray of a pneumatic valve. They will not approach you. They will not acknowledge your presence with anything more than a slight, shutter-like flickering of their bioluminescent eyes. You are a biological anomaly, a chaotic variable in a system of absolute, ticking perfection. To them, you are merely a loud, heat-generating breeze that will eventually dissipate, leaving the orchard to continue its slow, grinding, green-and-brass conversion of the world into home.
