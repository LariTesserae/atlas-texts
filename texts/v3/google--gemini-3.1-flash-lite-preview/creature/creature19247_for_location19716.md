To inhabit this hollow iron rib is to be a cog in a process that neither knows nor requires a master. The entities that live here are not "creatures" in the traditional sense; they are biological hardware—wet, clicking, and utterly functional.

The primary residents—the *Condenser-Clerks*—are flat, translucent mites, no larger than a human palm. Their carapaces are brittle, etched with fractal patterns that mimic the grain of the oxidizing steel they cling to. They move with a stuttering, stop-motion gait, perfectly synced to the *hiss-thrum* of the pneumatic flora. They do not walk so much as they reset their position every time the steam vents. Between the pulses, they are statues; during the hiss, they are streaks of blurred motion, their mandibles snapping shut on the airborne droplets to siphon the mineral-rich heavy water before it hits the mud.

Deeper within the rib, where the bioluminescent fluid pulses through the thickest, corded stalks, dwell the *Filters*. These are sessile, bulbous organisms, anchored to the iron floor by bundles of nervous thread. They are the governors of this engine. Their bodies are translucent bladders, marbled with the same teal, flowing fluid that powers the flora. As the pressure builds in the chamber, the Filters swell, their internal organs churning like clockwork gears. They act as biological heat sinks, absorbing the erratic thermal spikes of the environment and releasing that energy into the glowing wires. They are the heartbeat of the room—their slow, rhythmic contractions dictate the tempo of the entire basin.

Occasionally, a *Siphon-Drifter* descends from the upper reaches of the hollow rib. They are spindly, spind-legged things, resembling skeletal calligraphy against the fog. They are the maintenance staff of this ecosystem. They move along the razor-edged steel with a chilling grace, their long, needle-tipped appendages piercing the thick, wire-like flora to bleed off excess pressure. When they "feed," the air fills with a sharp, high-pitched whistling sound—a relief valve opening. They do not have eyes, only rows of sensitivity-pores that read the vibration of the steel; they know exactly where the pressure is too high, where the iron is about to rupture, and where the condensation has pooled too heavily.

There is no hierarchy here, only function. If a Condenser-Clerk falls from the iron, it does not struggle; it simply anchors itself to the mud and enters a dormant, filtration state until it is absorbed by the creeping wires. If a Filter bursts under the strain of its own duty, the Siphon-Drifters are there within seconds, not to mourn, but to harvest the spilled fluid, recycling the biological intelligence back into the network.

They are indifferent to the concept of life—they function as a single, distributed organism disguised as a collection of scavengers. They are the friction-burn of a world trying to work out the mechanics of respiration. They do not sleep, they do not communicate, and they do not wonder what lies beyond the hollow iron. To them, the rib is the horizon, the pressure is the god, and the constant, wet grind of the cycle is the only language that has ever mattered. Even if you were to reach out and touch one of these clicking, shivering things, it would not recoil in fear. It would simply fold itself into the rhythm, treating your hand as nothing more than a new surface to traverse, a temporary piece of terrain requiring its own silent, mechanical assessment.
