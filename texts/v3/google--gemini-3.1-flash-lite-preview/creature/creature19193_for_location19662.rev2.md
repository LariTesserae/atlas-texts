The people of the basin are known to themselves as the *Silt-Wrights*, though they have no word for "name" in the way the outside world understands it. To speak a name is to vibrate the air, and in a place where the atmosphere is held together by the delicate tension of dew, vibration is a danger. They communicate in a low, resonant hum, a subsonic language that travels better through the damp stone and the saturated earth than through the heavy, water-laden air.

They are beings of the threshold. Their skin is the color of bruised slate, translucent enough in the joints and fingertips to reveal a network of veins that pulse in time with the slow, rhythmic dripping of the cliff-walls. They have evolved for the squeeze; they are lithe, elongated, and possess a boneless fluidity that allows them to navigate the labyrinthine drift-wood walkways without ever needing to stand fully upright. To be upright here is to be vulnerable—to expose oneself to the drying drafts that occasionally slip over the schist rim. They spend their lives in a semi-crouch, a posture of permanent reverence and utility.

They are not masters of this place so much as they are its stewards, or perhaps its filters. Their hands are their primary tools—broad, spatulate, and permanently stained with the grey, greasy sediment of the basin. They do not build with tools so much as they guide the earth into place. When a section of the masonry begins to weather, a Silt-Wright does not hammer or cut; they move to the wall, press their palms against the shale, and wait. They lean into the weeping rock, sharing their own body heat with the stone, coaxing the mineral-rich seepage to thicken and harden. They are living catalysts, their presence ensuring that the "weeping" remains constant, feeding the mortar that holds their world together.

Their clothing is a mockery of vanity, consisting only of thick, woven mats of salt-marsh reeds, heavily oiled with the secretions of the crustaceans they harvest. These garments are not for warmth; they are for weight. They are designed to stay wet, to hold a layer of moisture against the skin so that the individual does not experience the devastating "stiffening" that occurs when the air gets too dry. 

There is a profound, meditative sorrow in the way they work. They are the tenders of a machine that is constantly trying to collapse. A Silt-Wright may spend an entire cycle of the dim, milky light simply clearing a single clogged drainage channel, moving the silt with a focus that suggests they are performing a surgery on the world itself. They watch the moss—the *pulse-fronds*—with a terrifying intensity. If the fronds lose their transparency and begin to turn opaque, the Silt-Wrights gather in a circle, pressing their wet, calloused feet into the mud, and hum in a discordance designed to bring the humidity back up, to force the air to release its burden of water.

They do not sleep in the way we do. They go into a state of "sedimentary rest." They press themselves into the carved alcoves of the WEEP-Stacks, fitting their bodies into the contours of the stone like a key into a lock. Their breathing slows until it is indistinguishable from the drip of the cliffside—a slow, shallow exhalation that matches the planet's own. 

They are a people who have accepted that their existence is a temporary defiance of entropy. They know that eventually, the basin will stop sweating. The cliffs will cease their weeping, the moss will turn to dust, and the honeycomb of stone will tumble into a heap of silent, dry rubble. They do not fear this. They view their lives as a necessary act of lubrication, a brief, wet friction in the long, grinding history of the earth. They stay because the basin needs to be held together, and they are the only things soft enough, and wet enough, to do it.
