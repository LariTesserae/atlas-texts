To live in the Basin is to cease being an individual and to instead become a component—a sentient gear in the slow, grinding clockwork of the deep earth.

The inhabitants here are the **Silt-Keepers**, though they have no name for themselves that does not involve the rhythmic hiss of a steam valve. They are not entirely biological, nor are they entirely machine. They have evolved in a landscape where stone is soft as putty and metal is the only permanence, and thus, they have become a synthesis of both.

Their bodies are lean, elongated, and heavily jointed, armored in plates of salvaged, oxidized copper and brass that they have "grown" into their own skin. They do not walk so much as they lurch, their feet broad and webbed to distribute their weight across the treacherous, embryonic muck. They are taller than a man, but they spend their lives stooped, constantly adjusting valves, tightening rivets, or clearing the dense, bioluminescent mats of moss that threaten to choke the intake pipes of the Low-Works.

Their eyes have long since atrophied, rendered useless by the gloom and the constant weeping fog. In their place, they have developed sensory ridges along their jawlines and collarbones—thick, sensitive clusters of cilia that map the world through vibration. To a Silt-Keeper, the "sight" of the basin is a complex symphony of resonance. They "see" the structural integrity of the canyon walls through the pitch of the dripping water; they "read" the health of the machinery through the cadence of its clanking. If a boiler begins to rattle with a hollow, high-pitched tick, it is a cry of pain to them, as visceral and urgent as a bleeding wound.

They live in the "interstices"—the hollow, rusted shells of retired pumping stations and the undersides of the great, low-slung iron gantries. Their homes are nests of woven wire and softened, mineral-rich clay that hardens into ceramic shells around their sleeping bodies. Above them, in the dark, the steam-saturated ceiling drips, and the Silt-Keepers catch this runoff in suspended canvas troughs, filtering it through mats of the very crustaceans they share the basin with.

They do not speak with vocal cords. Instead, they manipulate small, chest-mounted diaphragms—hollowed-out resonators salvaged from the wreckage of the works—that allow them to mimic the thrum of the pumps. A conversation between two Keepers is a series of modulated bellows and metallic clicks, a local dialect of the Basin’s own industrial pulse.

They are, in every sense, the basin’s immune system. They feel no ambition to climb the walls or escape the pressure of the earth above. To them, the sky is a myth—an impossible, terrifying emptiness. They believe that they are the architects of the planet’s base, that without their constant tending of the pistons and their rhythmic tapping against the rusted pipes, the heat beneath the crust would run wild, and the "wet-birth" process would cease, leaving the world a static, dead thing.

They are perpetually damp, perpetually coated in a fine sheen of mineral oil and clay grit. When they die, they do not decompose in the conventional sense. They simply settle, their metallic-fused bodies sinking into the hardening clay, becoming, in time, a part of the bedrock itself. A Silt-Keeper who is "retiring" will often wedge themselves into a crevice near a particularly loud steam vent, locking their joints into a permanent, bracing position to help support the growing weight of the ceiling. 

They are the patient ones. They find a holy comfort in the deafening noise. They are the dust-covered ghosts of a world that is not yet ready to be born, holding the pressure, keeping the rhythm, and waiting for the earth to finish its long, slow work.
