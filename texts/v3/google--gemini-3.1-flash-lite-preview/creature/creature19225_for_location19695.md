The people of Ouros—the *Lithos-kin*, as they are whispered of in the lowlands—are built in the image of their architecture. They are narrow-framed, dense-boned, and possess a gait that defies the laws of balance. To watch them walk is to watch a predator in slow motion; they lean into the verticality, their heels rarely fully finding the stone, their weight distributed with a terrifying, intuitive grace.

Their skin has taken on the color of the mountainside: a translucent, bruised pallor, mapped with fine, pale veins that track the movement of blood kept thick and slow by the altitude. They rarely expose their faces to the wind. When they do, you see they are a people of sharp features—noses hooked like the raptors that circle above, eyes set deep into their sockets like gemstones embedded in raw ore, shielded by lashes habitually coated in rime. 

They do not speak with their mouths as much as they speak with their posture. Because the air is thin and the wind is a thief of sound, they have developed a secondary language of tactile percussion. To tap one’s knuckles against a neighbor’s shoulder is to say *I see you; you are here.* To press a palm against the stone wall of a neighbor’s dwelling is the equivalent of a formal greeting, a way of sharing the temperature of their hearth-stone.

The children, those who tend the *krell*, are not raised to be carefree. They are tethered by plaited ropes to the mountain’s architecture until their balance achieves an instinctive marriage with the shifting wind. They grow up with calloused palms and a profound, silent focus; to be a child in Ouros is to know that the abyss is not a void, but a neighbor. They move among the grazing *krell* with an eerie stillness, their bodies mimicking the slow, lumbering sway of the beasts. When a *krell* slips—and they do—the children do not scream. They watch. They understand that what the mountain gives, the mountain eventually reclaims.

The artisans of Ouros—the chisel-men and the weavers of fog—live in a state of meditative, repetitive labor. They are not striving for invention; they are striving for maintenance. The city is constantly eroding, constantly shedding its mortar as the mountain grinds beneath it. These people see themselves not as masters of their environment, but as its parasites and its doctors. They spend their days kneeling on narrow ledges, packing moss into cooling cracks, re-lashing bridges that have frayed from the scouring ice, and tuning the ceramic catch-basins to ensure the water chimes correctly.

Their temperament is, by necessity, a strange alloy of profound melancholy and sudden, fierce vitality. They are rarely given to laughter, which they believe disturbs the air and invites the unstable balance of the peak to shift. Instead, they store their joy in the small, flickering embers of their hearths. At night, when they curl into their nests of woven fiber and *krell*-fleece, they are not individuals so much as parts of a gargantuan, shivering organism.

They do not dream of the lowlands. They do not dream of the horizontal world, where things grow wide instead of tall. To a citizen of Ouros, the idea of walking a flat, grassy plain without a cliff to catch one’s fall feels like a sickness, a dizzying, terrifying lack of containment. They are forged, literally, in the grinding of tectonic plates; they are the people who learned to love the sound of the stone teeth gnashing beneath them. They are the static, breathing jewels of the abyss, waiting for the mountain to finish its long, slow thrust toward a sky it will never truly touch.
