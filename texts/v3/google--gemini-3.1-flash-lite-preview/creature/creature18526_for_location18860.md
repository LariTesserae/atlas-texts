The residents of this place are not masters of the mountain, nor are they its survivors; they are its nervous system. 

They emerge from the deep, basaltic throat of the world not as flesh, but as a fusion of biology and mineral—a kind of living calcification. These are the *Litho-weavers*, and they look less like animals than like geological accidents that decided to walk.

At first glance, they appear to be composed of the very mountain they inhabit—shards of jagged, obsidian-like material fused together by a thick, translucent cartilage that glows with a faint, rhythmic pulses of violet light. They do not have blood; they circulate a nutrient-dense slurry, a pressurized lubricant that hums in resonance with the mountain’s own underground pistons. Their bodies are modular, composed of dozens of multi-jointed segments that can uncurl or collapse entirely, allowing them to retreat into the narrowest, freezing fissures when the mountain’s internal pressure spikes.

They have no eyes. Evolution has deemed sight an impractical luxury in a place where the atmosphere is a slurry of mist and shadow. Instead, their heads are crowned with a forest of delicate, crystalline antennae. These organs serve a dual purpose: they act as lightning rods for the ozone smell that permeates the thin, frigid air, and they serve as tuning forks for the mountain’s thrumming machinery. A Litho-weaver does not "see" its surroundings; it *feels* the structural integrity of the basalt beneath its claws. It knows the exact position of every subterranean gear by the vibration that travels through the rock, and it knows the exact, fragile location of every tectonic shift before it happens.

They move with a terrifying, rhythmic grace. It is a dance of maintenance. A traveler might see one of these hound-sized arthropods stop suddenly, its body locking into place like a gear falling into a groove. It will extend a needle-thin, vibration-sensitive limb into a hairline fracture in the stone, injecting a rapid-hardening, resinous secretion. This is not for building a home; it is for sealing a leak in the planet’s cooling system. They are the microscopic stitches in a planetary wound, constantly patrolling the plateau to ensure that no pressure-pipe cracks, no mineral slurry overflows, and no segment of the mountain loses its grip on the mantle below.

But there is something else, something deeper in the marshes of freezing, oily water. 

Beneath the surface of the shallow pools, the *Singers* reside. These are the sedentary cousins of the Litho-weavers—blob-like, gelatinous masses moored to the bottom of the fissures by thousands of sensory filaments. They do not scuttle. They pulsate, expanding and contracting in perfect synchronization with the rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the crustal pistons. 

If the Litho-weavers are the hands of the mountain, the Singers are its memory. They absorb the mineral runoff, filtering the rich chemical byproducts through their soft, constantly shifting membranes. As they purge the waste, they emit a low, ultrasonic frequency that resonates through the obsidian ribs of the mountain. It is a choral feedback loop. If the mountain’s pace slows, if a piston falters or a seam threatens to bridge too early, the Singers alter their pitch—a discordant, jarring note that ripples through the plateau—and the Litho-weavers instantly converge, swarming the area to recalibrate the machinery.

They are lonely, not because they are solitary, but because they are entirely consumed by the labor of being. They have no concept of a "self" separate from the mountain. They do not interact with one another in any social capacity; they do not play, they do not fight, they do not mate in the traditional sense. They are merely components of a massive, geologically ambitious organism that is still trying to get its footing. 

Watch them long enough, and you realize they are waiting for something. They are not merely maintaining the mountain; they are preparing it for a transition that hasn't arrived. They are smoothing the sharp edges, lubricating the joints, and ensuring the stability of the foundation. They are the gardeners of a continent that has yet to bloom, working in the freezing, thin-aired dark, waiting for the day the tectonic ambition succeeds—the day the mountain finally stops pushing and begins to breathe.
