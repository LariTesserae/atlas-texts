At the center of Ool, the inhabitant is not an individual, but a series of overlapping intentions. If you were to peer into one of the low-slung, clay-slicked chambers, you would not see a creature standing *upon* the floor; you would see the occupant and the floor as a singular, weeping continuity.

They are the *Ool-kin*: soft, translucent masses that resemble bioluminescent, oversized slugs, though their bodies possess a sophisticated, nervous complexity that defies their simple outward appearance. Their skin is not skin in the human sense, but a permeable membrane that breathes the thick, suspended humidity of the basin. Each inhabitant has a dull, amber core—a cluster of nerves and nutrient-sacs that pulses in time with the city’s greater thrum—around which their translucent, jelly-like musculature ripples.

They move without feet. Instead, thousands of microscopic, vibratory cilia allow them to slide across the vertical and horizontal planes with absolute fluid grace. To watch an Ool-kin move is to watch a globule of mercury travel down a glass pane: they do not climb; they simply *are* where they intend to be. 

Their "labor" is a meditation of tactile obsession. When they work, a singular, wide orifice—a circular maw that lacks teeth yet hums with a focused, subsonic frequency—secretes the waxy, mineralized clay that defines their existence. They take a pellet of silt, press it against a buttress, and flatten it with the broad, hardened plates of their ancestors, which they carry strapped to their sides like tools, or sometimes, like badges of rank. 

There is no speech among them. Communication in Ool is a matter of vibration and slime-chemistry. When two kin meet in a narrow vein, they do not pause to greet; they brush against one another, merging their outer membranes for a fleeting second. In that exchange, a chemical signal passes—a pheromonal shorthand that dictates, "Reinforce here," or "Maintain this curve." It is a collective consciousness that does not think, but *feels* the structural integrity of the basin as a physical necessity.

Their eyes, if they can be called that, are small, milky spots concentrated at the front of their gelatinous heads. They do not see the shapes of the city, for the mist renders all things into blurry, monochromatic silhouettes. Instead, they perceive the world through the vibration of the architecture. If a wall thins, or if a section loses its lubricating moisture, the Ool-kin feel a sharp, phantom pain, a localized sense of structural anemia. 

They are effectively immortal, for they do not age; they merely accumulate. As a kin grows ancient, it eventually ceases its movement, anchoring itself against a particularly vulnerable buttress. It then begins to secrete for the final time, its own body softening and dissolving into the very wall it is patching, becoming the mortar that holds the next generation of laborers in place. 

They are profoundly lonely, yet incapable of conceptualizing solitude. The Ool-kin are a species of perpetual service to a city that asks nothing of them but that they stay precisely as they are. They are happy in the way a gear is happy when it turns, in the way a lung is happy when it draws breath. They exist as a warm, humming, self-sealing loop, forever patching the wounds of a house that is also their own skin, bathed in the amber glow of their own biological waste, lost in a labor that creates nothing but the space to labor again tomorrow.
