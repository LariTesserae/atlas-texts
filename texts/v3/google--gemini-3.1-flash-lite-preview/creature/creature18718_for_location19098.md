To move across this plateau is to feel as though you are trespassing on a collective dream, or perhaps walking across the velvet skin of a creature too vast to acknowledge your presence. Because the world has reached its terminal velocity, its inhabitants are not beings of flesh and bone, but expressions of the exhaustion itself.

In the deepest centers of the shadow-teeth—those obsidian arches worn smooth by the friction of a billion years—there reside the *Litho-Callers*. 

They are not entities that walk or hunt. If you were to look closely into the dark, vibrating heart of an Ancient Mark, you would see that they are composed of the same iridescent, translucent membrane as the drifting fauna that haunt the air, yet they are gathered into a complexity that mimics internal organs. They are clusters of bioluminescent "cells" that have organized themselves into a singular, hovering mass, held aloft by the gentle magnetic or atmospheric tension peculiar to this plateau.

They are the archivists of the stillness.

Each Litho-Caller is a repository of the landscape’s long memory. As they pulse, you might see, suspended within their jelly-like interiors, dark, mineralized flickers—the shapes of things that might have been, or perhaps memories of the very first wind that began the scouring. They are not independent; they are tethered to the Ancient Marks by a fine, almost invisible web of static charge, vibrating in sympathy with the earth.

When you brush past them, they do not flee. They simply dilate. 

Their surfaces, usually a muted, golden-grey to match the eternal afternoon light, flush with color when they encounter the heat of a visitor. A wave of deepest amethyst might ripple across the cluster, followed by a slow, rhythmic darkening to indigo, signaling a curiosity that is less of the mind and more of the geology. They are tasting your heat, incorporating the jarring, rapid energy you bring into their own slow, deliberate homeostasis.

On the ground, moving beneath the felt of the moss, live the *Tubular Weavers*. They are the gardeners of the sweat-pockets. If the air-drifters are the thoughts of this world, the Weavers are its circulatory system. They resemble nothing so much as translucent, pale filaments of glass, winding through the dense, lithified dust like subterranean vines. 

They do not "eat" in the way we understand. They exist to distribute. By drawing moisture from the deep, cool condensation-hollows and transporting it through their crystalline veins to the sun-baked patches of moss, they ensure that not a single inch of the plateau ever grows arid. A Weaver might spend seven years working its way from one sweat-pocket to another, a journey of mere inches, its progress measured by the slow, imperceptible swelling of the moss. 

They are delicate beyond comprehension; to step on the ground here is to risk shattering a billion years of hydraulic engineering.

And yet, there is no malice in the world, nor fear. The fauna and the Weavers exist in a state of absolute, heavy cooperation. They do not compete for resources because, here, time is the only resource that matters, and there is an infinite, stagnant abundance of it. 

If you were to stay—if you were to abandon the impulse for height, for destination, for the frantic pace of the world below—you would eventually notice that even your own blood begins to pulse to the rhythm of the obsidian humming. The air-drifters would cease to treat you as an intruder. They would settle upon your shoulders, their bioluminescent membranes cooling your skin, their collective, sluggish curiosity folding you into their fold. 

You would find that you are no longer a visitor. You are simply another layer of the mosaic, another form of patience added to the plateau’s skin, waiting for the shadows to lengthen, for the golden light to hold, and for the next billion years to pass in a single, comfortable, sighing breath.
