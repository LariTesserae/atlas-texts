To inhabit this vertical throat is to relinquish the notion of the individual. Here, boundaries are liquid, defined only by the pressure of one neighbor against the flank of another.

The inhabitants—the *Litho-dermis*—are not insects in the way we conceive of them, nor are they truly mollusks, though they share the soft, hydrostatic vulnerability of both. They are flattened, translucent discs no larger than a thumbprint, their undersides a complex lattice of microscopic cilia that act as a thermal lung. They do not cling to the stone so much as they merge with it, their biology secreting a specialized enzyme that softens the limestone just enough to create a perfect, seamless pocket for their existence.

If you were to touch the wall, you would not feel a collection of creatures, but a single, undulating organ. The surface is warm, pulse-like and feverish. Under the pressure of your fingertip, the mass doesn't scatter; it yields, a slow, viscous displacement of bodies that flows around the obstruction as water flows around a stone in a stream.

They have no eyes; they have no need to see. Instead, the entire colony operates as a vast nervous system. Each disc is coupled to its neighbors by rhythmic, low-frequency vibrations—a constant, tactile Morse code that communicates the temperature of the rock, the flow of the mineral-rich dew, and the encroaching pressure of the layers shifting. When a ripple passes through them, it is not a flight response, but an adjustment of equilibrium. Like a localized tectonic shift, they redistribute their weight, passing the "memory" of movement through thousands of carapaces.

They possess neither mouthparts for biting nor appendages for predation. Their existence is one of pure absorption. Their translucent, armored backs act as solar collectors for the heat radiating from the mountain’s gut, while their undersides act as a membrane that wicks the mineral dew directly into their metabolic centers. They drink the mountain’s age, processing the stone’s runoff into the slow, steady energy that powers their grinding, collective existence.

They are the archivists of this silence. Because they stay in place for decades, they have become part of the geological topography. Over centuries, the empty exoskeletons of those that have reached the end of their cycle calcify, becoming a permanent part of the limestone substrate. New generations settle upon these ancient, stony records, building a layer of the future over a foundation of the past. 

There is no malice here, nor is there affection. There is only the immense, suffocating comfort of the collective. To be one of them is to be part of an unending, low-frequency song, a constant, microscopic friction that keeps the throat warm, keeping the cycle turning within the dark, mineral-heavy throat of the world. They are the stone’s own desire to be alive—a living skin wrapping the planet’s cooling heart, moving with a patience that makes the mountains themselves seem flighty and imprecise.
