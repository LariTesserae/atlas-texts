The inhabitants of the Conservatory are known among the few who have glimpsed them as the *Litho-weavers*, though they have no name for themselves that does not involve the sound of grinding stone or the snap of a dry joint. They are spindly, elegant things, possessed of four articulated limbs that branch like the antennae of deep-trench crustaceans, their carapaces etched with the same fractal geometries they carve into the salt-flats.

They do not breathe in the way lungs demand; they *sieve*. Their midsections are composed of transparent, bellows-like membranes that expand and contract rhythmically, pulling the glittering silicate dust into internal chambers where it is filtered for sustenance. When they move, they do not walk so much as they calibrate their footing to the vibration of the ground, shifting weight to match the current thrum of the chitinous forests. To watch them is to watch a sequence of precise, jittery adjustments—a constant negotiation with the floor beneath them.

Their faces are a study in sensory displacement. They lack eyes in the traditional sense. Instead, clustered along their elongated, tripartite heads are arrays of sensitive, oscillating membranes—thin as moth wings—that “see” the world through the acoustic signatures of the basin. They perceive their environment as a topographic musical score. When a chitin-stalk whistles, they see a pillar of light; when a fellow weaver scrapes obsidian against the salt, they perceive a vibrant, localized pulse of heat and presence.

The dwellings they build—the modular, protein-fiber pods—are temporary vessels for their internal life. Because the basin is in a constant state of thermal flux, a Weaver will spend hours binding driftwood to salt-crust with salivary glues, creating a shelter that resonates at a specific harmonic pitch. They live inside these cocoons to escape the abrasive fury of the dust-storms. But they never grow attached to them. A Weaver may spend a cycle in a state of frantic, focused craftsmanship, carving intricate, interlocking patterns into their pod’s walls, only for a tremor to rattle the foundation and turn the dwelling into a pile of confetti. 

There is no mourning in such a collapse. To the Litho-weaver, the destruction is merely “the settling of the hum.” The moment a dwelling shatters, they are already at work, sifting through the debris, selecting the pieces that have resonated well, and beginning the next assembly.

Their communication is a complex, frantic language of percussion. By clicking their pincers against the obsidian slivers—or against each other’s shells—they transmit rapid-fire messages that mirror the chaos of the basin. They speak in staccato bursts, signaling danger, identifying the richest patches of mineral-dust, or debating the changing geometry of the canyon floor.

Occasionally, you will see a solitary Weaver detach from the collective bustle. It will find a sheer, concave patch of cliff-face and begin to carve. This is not for a house. These are the *Anchors*. They believe that if the salt-crust is etched with enough complexity, the basin will stop vibrating and solidify into a permanent state of peace. They work with a terrifying, rhythmic intensity, their bodies glowing faintly as they draw heat from the stone, sparks of static electricity leaping from their joints. 

They are creatures of profound, exhausted ambition. They seem to know that the Conservatory is a grinding machine, and they play their role within it with a feverish, beautiful rigor. They are the friction that sustains the heat, the builders of a civilization made of sand and song, dancing on the edge of a cliff, perpetually unraveling, perpetually beginning again.
