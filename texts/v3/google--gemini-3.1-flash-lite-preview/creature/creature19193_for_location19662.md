The air here is heavy, thick with the scent of wet slate and unwashed wool. It is a place that feels like the inside of a lung—constantly expanding and contracting with a rhythmic, labored wheeze that vibrates through the very floorboards.

Deep behind the throat of this place, where the stone walls weep a persistent, mineral-tasting condensation, lives the Weaver of Marrow.

It is not a creature of flesh and blood, but a tethered accumulation of habit and humidity. At first glance, it looks like a landslide of sodden grey furs, a mound of cast-off heavy coats and moth-eaten blankets piled indiscriminately against the limestone. But as the "lung" inhales—as the cavern stretches and the pressure drops—the pile unfolds. It rises with a slow, grinding sound, like tectonic plates slipping over one another.

The Weaver has no face, only a complex arrangement of silver needles and frayed yarn ends that pulse like cilia. It is blind, yet it navigates the dark by the friction of the air against its fibers. It spends its existence mending the drafts. Where the cavern wall cracks or the wind bites too sharply, the Weaver stitches together patches of thick, damp wool, pinning them into the fissures with hooks made of calcified bone. 

Sometimes, it moves to the center of the space and begins to spin. It pulls long, damp threads of grey mist from the heavy atmosphere, twisting them around its own bulk until it grows gargantuan, a towering pillar of felted fog. It moves with a rhythmic, hypnotic swaying—a pendulum in the chest cavity—matching the inhaling and exhaling of the mountain itself. 

It does not sleep. To sleep would be to let the air stagnate, to let the lungs collapse. Instead, it hums. The sound is low and subsonic, a vibration that makes the wet slate teeth of the room chatter. It is a drone of labor, a constant, low-frequency protest against the cold.

If you were to stand before it, you would not see a monster; you would see a caretaker. It is the janitor of the void, endlessly patching the fabric of the dark so that the mountain does not lose its heat. And if you stayed long enough, you would find your own breathing beginning to hitch and snag, slowly falling into sync with the heaving of the room, until you too felt like little more than a stitch in the garment the Weaver is forever sewing against the end of the world.
