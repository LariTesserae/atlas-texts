They are the Silt-Walkers, though they have no name for themselves that sounds like a word. To name something is to fix it, and they exist only in the act of yielding to the dissolution of the Basin.

They are impossibly thin, their limbs tapered like the reeds they weave into their scaffolds. Their skin possesses the translucence of a deep-sea creature brought to the surface, a pale, milky violet that allows one to see the faint, rhythmic pulse of blue vessels beneath. They do not walk so much as they articulate themselves along the needles, their elongated, multi-jointed fingers pressing into the porous basalt with the sensitivity of an insect’s feelers. They are tuned to the vibrations; if the basalt *thrums*, they shudder; if the wind whistles through a crack, their throats constrict in sympathy, vibrating with a high, harmonic hum that mimics the mountain’s own breath.

They do not sleep in beds. Instead, they press their light, hollow bodies into the womb-like recesses of the damp basalt, letting the emerald algae coat their backs. They are, for all intents and purposes, part of the humidity. When they wake, they are often wet, their hair matted with the same slick moss that clings to the needles.

Their existence is defined by the "Latticework." These fraying structures are not homes but anchors—temporary geometries meant to slow the inevitable slide of their possessions into the mist below. A Silt-Walker might spend a lifetime tending to a single, delicate spiral of river-glass, buffing it with the oils of their own skin until it reflects the sharp, vertical light of the rim. They make these talismans not to trade, for there is no commerce, but to measure the erosion. A chime placed on the northern face will vibrate differently than one on the southern; by listening to the changing pitch of the glass as the mountain slowly grains away, they keep a silent, shifting map of the Basin’s decay.

They do not speak. To speak is to break the density of the air, and they fear the resonance of their own voices might trigger a rockfall or disrupt the delicate surface tension of the water-orbs. Instead, they communicate through a tactile sign language involving ripples in the damp moss and the shifting of their brittle, bird-like chests. A sudden depression of the sternum signifies a falling drop; a fan-like spread of fingers across the basalt indicates a change in the wind’s draft.

They are profoundly lonely, yet they are never individual. In their perspective, they are merely cellular components of the Basin itself. They see the predator-moth with its dinner-plate wings not as a separate entity, but as a moving part of the basalt’s digestion. They share a kinship with the iridescent crab-beetles, often sitting perfectly still for cycles of the twilight, allowing the insects to crawl over their pallid skin, harvesting the dew that clings to their pores.

When a Silt-Walker reaches the end—when the bone-lattice becomes too frail or the basalt beneath their chosen perch finally crumbles away—they do not struggle. They simply let go, dissolving into the motion of the basin. They tumble into the mist, becoming one more micro-action in the great, quiet collapse of the stone. They go down to join the churn, contributing their own hollow bones to the silt that accumulates in the deep, damp darkness of the floor, feeding the moss that will eventually climb the needles to meet their descendants. 

They are the caretakers of a vanishing cathedral, forever busy with the polishing of glass, waiting for the moment when the last needle dissolves into the mist and the bowl is finally, perfectly empty.
