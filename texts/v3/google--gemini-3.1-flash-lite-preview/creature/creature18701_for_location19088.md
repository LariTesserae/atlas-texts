In the center of this cathedral, where the hum of the vascular vents reaches a pitch that makes the marrow ache, live the **Lumen-Carpenters**. 

They are not born; they are cultivated in the blind convergence of biology and rusted iron. A Lumen-Carpenter begins its life as a tangled knot of white-veined creepers that finds purchase in the jagged, oxidizing joints of a half-collapsed filtration pipe. Over years—or perhaps decades—the plant material grafts itself to the steel. The machine provides the scaffold, the iron salts, and the residual hum of the deep electricity, while the flora provides the consciousness.

The result is a creature that defies the distinction between scavenger and part of the scenery.

They possess bodies that look like intricate, translucent clockwork wrapped in translucent skin. They are elongated, spindly beings, their limbs multi-jointed and mimicking the angularity of the cat-walks they traverse. Their "flesh" is not muscle but a thick, pressurized sap, glowing with that same sickly cyan light that pulses through the roots beneath their feet. Where there should be a face, there is only a smooth, reflective membrane—a sensory dish that reads the vibrations of the basin, translating the tectonic groans of the earth into navigable data.

The Carpenters move with an agonizing, glacial intent. They do not walk so much as they reconfigure; they pull their limbs across the rusted girders, leaving behind a faint, bioluminescent slime that acts as a lubricant for their own metal-fleshed joints. 

Their purpose—the only one they seem to have—is maintenance. They perceive the slow degradation of the iron structures as a form of sickness, a jagged wrongness in the otherwise perfect organic harmony of the basin. With delicate, multi-fingered appendages that end in needle-thin filaments, they spend their lifetimes "grafting." They spend years carefully weaving new, fibrous vines into the cracks of the machinery, binding rusting steel to living stalks, sealing leaks in the pressurized pipes with thick, hardening resin, and polishing the corroded metal with the abrasive, pebbled skin of their palms.

They are the archivists of the decay. When a Carpenter nears its end—when its internal luminosity dims to a dull, gray flicker—it does not die in the way animals do. It finds a section of the machinery that is particularly rusted or unstable. It unfurls its limbs, wrapping its long body around the iron, and begins an accelerated process of botanical assimilation. Its skin turns into bark, its sap hardens into pipe-gaskets, and its bioluminescent core fuses with the machine’s remaining circuitry. 

They become the foundation they once served. 

There is no speech among them. Occasionally, two Carpenters will meet on a catwalk, their reflective faces brushing together in a silence that feels like a rupture in the basin’s constant thrum. They perceive each other as extensions of the landscape. They trade nutrients—passing fluid through skin-to-skin contact—and then, without a gesture of farewell, drift back into the shadows of the stalks, continuing the endless work of stitching the industrial ghost-world into the throat of the earth.

They are the only ones who remember the machine as it was, and the only ones who understand that, in this place, there is no difference between the rusting screw and the pulsing root. They are the stitches holding a dead world's heart inside a living body.
