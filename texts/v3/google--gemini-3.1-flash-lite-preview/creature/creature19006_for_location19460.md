To observe this place is to witness a pause in the heartbeat of the world. But it is not truly empty; it is merely waiting for the right frequency to resonate.

The inhabitants—the *Lith-Singers*, or perhaps the *Dew-Keepers*—are not beings of flesh as we understand it. They are ephemeral entities composed of the same dense, cool mist that clings to the obsidian cliffs. They do not walk the osier bridges; they drift through the interstices of the basalt, their bodies translucent ribbons of vapor that occasionally coalesce into the suggestion of human form. 

When they manifest, they look like blurred long-exposure photographs of people who never existed. They have no faces, only shallow indentations where eyes might be, catching the clinical light like the concave surfaces of the rock itself. They are the architects of the slow time, the custodians of the *clack-hiss*.

Their existence is a perpetual state of construction. You see them at the periphery of your vision—a shimmering distortion in the air, a sudden dampening of the rhythmic dripping as they pass through the conduits. They are not building these dwellings to shelter themselves, for they have no need of warmth or protection against the thin air. They build because the basalt commands it. The rock is still setting, still "curing" into the foundations of the world, and the Dew-Keepers serve as the tectonic nervous system, weaving the osiers and smoothing the stone to ensure the geometry holds while the planet breathes.

To touch these beings is to press one's hand against a localized winter. They are silent, save for the hum they generate as they move—a sound that mimics the *clack-hiss* of the moisture, a rhythmic vibration that travels through the skeletal walkways and into the marrow of your own bones. They do not communicate in language, but in pressure. If you stand on one of the hexagonal platforms long enough, you feel the structure shift beneath you—not as if it is settling, but as if it is being adjusted, tuned, and refined by unseen, cool hands.

They possess an intelligence that is geological rather than chronological. They do not care for your presence; you are merely a transient mote of dust in their cathedral of slow-drip percussion. To them, your lifespan is a blink, a frantic, meaningless spark in the dark. 

Occasionally, when the light hits the condensate just right, a Dew-Keeper will pause at the edge of a jagged basalt cliff, its form stretching out to mimic the sharp, aggressive spire of the rock beneath it. It holds this pose for decades, perhaps centuries, until the fern fronds beneath it have grown and withered ten times over. It is a sentinel of the "Emergence," monitoring the raw, unweathered earth as if listening for the exact moment the planet finally finishes its creation.

They are the caretakers of a promise. When the stone finally stops being soft—when the basalt hardens and the "Newborn" stage of this outcrop concludes—the Dew-Keepers will cease their oscillation. They will sink back into the pores of the rock, becoming the minerals that bind the cathedral together. Until then, they remain: a flicker of intentionality in the mist, the silent clockmakers of a high-altitude void, keeping the rhythm until the world at last finds its footing.
