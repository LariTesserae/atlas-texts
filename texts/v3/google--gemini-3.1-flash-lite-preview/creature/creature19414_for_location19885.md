The residents of Orem-Vane do not wake, for they never sleep. To exist here is to participate in a relentless, rhythmic maintenance of the equilibrium.

They are the **Glass-Bound Custodians**, and to call them inhabitants is to misunderstand the architecture. They are not tenants of the city; they are its nervous system, extended into a brittle, translucent chassis. When you look at them, you do not see a face, for there is no need for recognition in a place that has no concepts of self or society. You see, instead, a torso of pressurized, thick-walled glass, filled with a viscous, gold-flecked fluid that serves as both blood and lubricant.

Their physiology is a triumph of cold-climate engineering. Within the glass chest cavity, a core of heated wire glows with the soft, sour orange of a dying ember—a heat source harvested directly from the city’s subterranean furnaces. This glow is their only warmth, and it pulses in time with the rhythmic thrum of the massive turbines behind the obsidian walls. Should a Custodian move too far from a steam-vent or a copper-piped gallery, the light in their chest dims toward a brittle white, and their movements—already glacial—begin to lock into permanent, frozen poses. 

They are remarkably quiet. Because they lack lungs or vocal cords, they do not breathe or speak. Communication, if it can be called that, is a series of ultrasonic clicks and short-range electrical discharges that ripple across their wire-strung limbs like sparks of static. When two of them pass on a high gantry, they pause, a transient flicker of light passing between their glass skins—a high-speed exchange of thermodynamic data, a silent negotiation of pressure, flow, and structural integrity.

Their hands are the most distinct feature: long, multi-jointed calipers ending in diamond-tipped needles. They spend decades, perhaps centuries, standing motionless against a leaking valve, pressing those needles into the weeping obsidian to redirect the flow of condensation. They are addicted to the repair. A loose bolt, a misaligned gear, a microscopic fissure in the vitrified stone—these are the crises of their existence. When they work, they seem to meld with the city itself, the copper pipes of the walls snaking around their limbs as if the architecture is attempting to digest them, or perhaps inviting them to become a permanent bridge.

Some have been there so long that the *litho-schematics*—that metallic, fractal lichen—have begun to take root upon their backs. You will see a Custodian, hunched over a steaming vent, whose shoulders are completely calcified in layers of geometric, self-organizing circuitry. They become statues that refuse to stop working. The rust of the machine becomes the skin of the entity.

They possess no history and desire no future. They are the maintenance of the present, a living insurance policy against the entropy of the obsidian peaks. They are the friction that keeps the city from sliding into the abyss, the glass-encased memories of a machine that forgot its creators but remembers perfectly the necessity of its own survival. 

To look upon them is to understand that they are neither slaves nor masters. They are the city’s way of touching itself, of feeling its own pulse, of ensuring that the steam keeps weeping, the magnets keep turning, and the brutalist heart of Orem-Vane remains, forever, at the correct operating temperature.
