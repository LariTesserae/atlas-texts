Within the hollowed-out chambers of these calcified blooms, the Weavers do not sleep in beds, but rather hang suspended in the architecture’s own internal anatomy. They are creatures of translucent, parchment-like skin, their bodies elongated and spindly, as if the very pressure of the damp, heavy air has pulled them into thin, fragile filaments of being. Their limbs possess extra joints, allowing them to fold into the interstitial spaces of the walls, disappearing almost entirely into the amber glow of the weeping substrate.

They are less like architects and more like gardeners of a slow-motion catastrophe. Their eyes, milky and pupilless, are adapted not for seeing objects, but for sensing the flow of hydraulic pressure—the *thrum-thrum* that serves as the world’s heartbeat. When they move, they do not walk so much as flow, their elongated fingers gently grazing the walls to read the plant’s intent. Where the vegetation is thick and swollen with sap, the Weaver pauses, stroking the surface with a piece of polished obsidian to signal the growth to pause, to thicken, to harden into a bridge or a spire.

They possess no language of sound, for sound is a jagged thing that would disrupt the silence of this wet, sprawling organism. Instead, they communicate through a tactile resonance. They press their chests against the skeletal vines, vibrating their own internal organs to pass ripples of intent into the plant-substrate. A tremor sent through the floor can cause a bloom a kilometer away to open or a walkway to pivot, orienting itself toward a new, nutrient-rich drift of mist.

The Weavers are, in a sense, the plant’s nervous system. They do not own the buildings they inhabit; they serve as its antibodies, its shears, its maintenance. Through eons of pruning and coaxing, they have become as calcified and ancient as the world itself, their skin often mottled with the same bioluminescent lichen that coats the ribs of the architecture. Some have remained in one position for so long that the vines have grown *through* their joints, netting them into the very walls, turning them into living, immobile hinges that anchor bridges to the void.

And then there are the gelatinous pods—the drifting, amorphous companions of the Weavers. They move with a dreamlike, lazy indifference, their gossamer filaments trailing behind them like the frayed ends of a spider’s silk. They do not interact with the Weavers, but the Weavers treat them with a quiet, solemn reverence. When a pod drifts close to a Weaver, the creature will reach out with spindly, pale hands to untangle the filaments from a thorns-bristle, as if grooming the world’s own stray thoughts.

There is a profound, aching loneliness to them, yet they do not seem capable of sorrow. They are too busy choreographing the decay. They are not waiting for an ending; they are simply watching the architecture slowly dream itself into new, impossible configurations. They stand upon the ledges, watching the spires reach for the bruised-moss ceiling, their translucent bodies blurring into the orange haze until they are indistinguishable from the architecture they tended. 

In this place, to exist is to be a part of the trimming. To be a Weaver is to know that you are only a momentary punctuation mark in a sentence the world has been writing, in wet, heavy silence, for an eternity.
