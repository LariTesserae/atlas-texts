They are the Olyran, and to look at them is to watch a movement that has forgotten its beginning.

They are elongated, their frames stretched thin by the thinness of the air and the necessity of balance. Their skin has taken on the hue of the high altitude—a pallor that mimics the underside of a stone, occasionally flushed with the faint, bruised violet of their own circulation. They are not built for heat or heartiness. They are built for the ledge.

When they move, they do not walk with the hurried franticness of those living in cities of change. They slide, their feet finding the serrated edges of the mountain with a prehensile grip that is more bird than man. They wear the rough-spun wool of the low-valley herds, but it is felted so tightly it has become armor, stiffened by the constant, cool dampness of the mountain breath. Their clothes are never bright; they reflect the basalt-blacks and cloud-greys of the city, rendering them nearly invisible against the mortarless walls.

Their faces are repositories of patience. Because the light in Olyra never shifts, their eyes have lost the twitchy, dilated quality of those who must adjust to day and night. They possess a heavy-lidded, steady gaze, like sculptures that have suddenly decided to observe the world back. They rarely speak. Speech is an expenditure of air, and here, air is a precious commodity to be measured by the breath. When they do communicate, it is in a series of rhythmic clicks and hums—a language that mimics the *tap-clink* of the water cisterns, a sonic twin to the city’s heartbeat.

They live in a state of suspended craft. A person may spend forty years polishing a single obsidian block until its surface is a mirror of the sky above, not because the stone is flawed, but because the act of polishing is a way of anchoring themselves to the static present. They are the caretakers of a gallery that is also their living room. 

In the shaded northern gardens, you will see the elders. They sit amidst the succulent mosses, their fingers tracing the veins of hanging ferns with the same delicate precision they use to carve the rock. Sometimes, they reach out to gather the silver beads of moisture from the stone, capturing life in a small, cupped palm before guiding the liquid to their lips. They carry no burdens; the city requires nothing to be transported, only maintained.

They feel no hunger for completion. They do not build for the future, nor do they mourn the past. To be Olyran is to be an inhabitant of the "Is Already." They are the living stone made flesh, the silent witnesses to the world that stopped. If you were to pass one on a narrow cat-walk, they would step aside with a grace so quiet it defies the gravity of the precipice, nodding once—a gesture of acknowledgment that you, too, have entered the stillness—before turning back to their chisel, their thread, or the slow, hypnotic task of watching the water fall.
