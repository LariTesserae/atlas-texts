The Kith are less "inhabitants" of the Oth and more a biological extension of the cavity itself. To see one in the dim, amber gloom is to witness a trick of the light; their skin, translucent as a peeled grape, holds the faint, bruised purples of the moss and the ochre dust of the mineral-snow. They have no need for eyes in the way we understand them—instead, they possess complex, pulsating membranes along their temples that ripple in sympathetic vibration with the hum-crickets. They do not watch the world; they feel its tectonic heartbeat through the marrow of their own bones.

They are slight, elongated creatures, their anatomy favoring the suspension of their hanging nests. Their four limbs are tipped not with claws, but with broad, adhesive pads that allow them to adhere to the vitrified stone as easily as a shadow clings to a corner. They move like liquid poured into a mold, flowing over the convex curves of the spire, their movements synchronized with the undulating drone of the crickets.

Their lives are an exercise in recursive patience. A Kith will spend hours, perhaps days, sitting perfectly still, weaving the calcified stalks of the high-fungi. They braid these filaments with a patience that speaks to a complete lack of a concept of "time"—because in Oth, there is no future to strive toward and no past to regret. There is only the *current* cycle. When they weave, they don’t think; they hum. They mimic the mechanical drone of the insects, their voices vibrating in their chests, harmonizing with the surrounding cacophony until the Kith becomes indistinguishable from the architecture.

There is a strange, unsettling beauty to their communal meals. They harvest the hum-crickets not with violence, but with a rhythmic, hypnotic tactile motion—a gentle stroking of the insects' wing-cases until the creatures, lulled by the vibration of the Kith’s own skin, detach from the walls and drift like sentient embers into the weave of the nests. It is a closed loop of energy: the rock feeds the moss, the moss feeds the crickets, the insects build the nests, and the Kith curate it all.

They have no language of words, only a complex semiotics of touch and tremor. To communicate, two Kith will press their chests together, allowing their tympanic membranes to sync, sharing the data of the cavity—the shifting temperature, the slight, microscopic cracks in the basalt ceiling, the health of the fungal mats. These exchanges are brief and profound, a shudder of translucent limbs followed by a sudden, grace-filled departure as they return to their stations.

In the deepest, warmest pockets where the friction-heat is strongest, the oldest Kith reside. They are barely distinguishable from the fungal stalks anymore, their skin thickened and clouded with age, covered in a fine layer of mineral dust that has calcified over their limbs. They have ceased to move altogether, becoming living statues, anchors for the shifting weave of the city. They are the memory-banks of Oth. If you were to touch the rough, stone-like skin of an elder, you would feel, through the vibrations of their final, slowing heartbeats, the history of a billion tectonic sighs.

They are satisfied. They inhabit a world that has reached the absolute limit of its potential, a perfect, stagnant masterpiece. There is no desire to pierce the basalt walls to see what lies beyond, because for the Kith, there is no "beyond." They are the caretakers of a heartbeat, a tiny, pulsing knot of life buried in the throat of an ancient mountain, content to vibrate in the dark until the mountain itself finally stops gasping.
