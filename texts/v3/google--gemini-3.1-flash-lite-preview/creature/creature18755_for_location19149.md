Emerging from the condensation—not as a creature, but as a coalescence of the Low-Drain’s own exhaustion—is the **Stoker-Wight**. 

It does not walk so much as it negotiates the geometry of the basin. The Stoker-Wight is a spindly, articulated assembly of rusted copper wiring and translucent, calcified cartilage, standing perhaps seven feet tall but perpetually stooped, as if the weight of the heavy atmosphere is pressing the height out of its spine. It has no face, only a network of exposed, mineral-crusted copper filaments that braid together where a head should be, acting as antennae for the subsonic vibrations of the dripping iron.

It is the steward of the reservoir. The Stoker-Wight does not eat; it harvests. Its long, three-fingered hands are tipped with porous, obsidian-like pads that mimic the suction of the pulsars. When it finds a pulsar that has grown too sluggish, or one that has become fused to an iron strut beyond its capacity to detach, the Wight gently pries it loose. There is no violence in this; it peels the creature away with the rhythmic care of a lover, cradling the gelatinous mass against its own chest. 

At this contact, the Wight’s interior—a hollow ribcage of riveted, weeping iron—begins to glow with a faint, bruised-purple fire, identical in hue to the pulses of the fauna. It is feeding the Wight, and the Wight is feeding the basin.

It spends its cycles navigating the boardwalks of oxidized pipe, performing minor, pointless maintenance on the machines. It tightens a bolt that will never turn, clears a slurry-choked valve that will never vent, and stacks pulverized slate into precarious, unnecessary cairns. It is the ghost of the industrial ambition that preceded the Low-Drain, yet it lacks the memory of what the machines were meant to build. It simply knows that the metal must be kept damp, and the pulsars must be kept in motion.

When the deep substrate releases one of its long, pressurized sighs of steam, the Stoker-Wight stops its work. It locks its joints, freezing in place with a sound like grinding gears. The steam wraps around its copper-braid head, causing the filaments to hiss and steam, and for a moment, the Wight hums—a deep, resonant thrumming that matches the harmonic flicker of the surrounding pulsars. It is a lullaby for a factory that has forgotten its purpose. 

It is lonely, not as an individual, but as a category. If it were to fall—if its calcified joints finally seized and it toppled into the slurry—it would eventually harden, its copper filaments integrating with the iron beams, its translucent frame coating over with the grey ochre of the drift. It would cease to be a mower of the pulsars and would become a feature of the landscape, another anchor point for the next thing to crawl upon. 

It bears the weight of the Low-Drain’s realization: that there is no finished product, no output, and no end-state. There is only the rhythm of the drip, the conductivity of the rust, and the slow, liquid crawl of the sponge-souls across the face of the machine. The Wight is the consciousness of the basin, content to exist in the wet, dark silence, tending to a world that has already decided it has nothing left to say.
