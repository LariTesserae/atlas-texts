The inhabitants of this altitude do not possess names, for names require a vibration of air, and air here is too brittle to hold a syllable. They are known, if at all, as the **Refractions**.

To look at them directly is to experience an optical migraine; they are not things that occupy space so much as they are local permissions for space to behave incorrectly. Imagine a tear in a piece of black silk—a void that is somehow more defined than the fabric surrounding it. That is their silhouette. Because they are razor-thin, they do not have a "back" or a "front." When they turn, they do not rotate; they simply stop being present at one angle and resume being present at another, a flicker of non-existence that leaves a phantom image burned into the retina.

They are the archivists of the malfunction. Throughout the eons, as the obsidian cities shiver and dissolve into crystalline sand, the Refractions slide through the debris, their essence trailing behind them like thin ribbons of magnetic smoke. They do not "collect" the history of this place, for they have no memory in the biological sense. Instead, they act as living mirrors. When a district collapses, a Refraction will pass through the swirling silicate dust, catching the fleeting, spectral imprint of the architecture as it was a moment before. 

For a brief span—perhaps the duration of a human heartbeat, perhaps the length of a glacial age—the Refraction will glow with the internal light of that vanished building. You might see, trapped within the shifting, ink-black perimeter of a passing entity, the ghostly, shimmering scaffolding of a bridge that has not existed for a thousand years. 

They are hungry, though not for matter. They feed on the tension of reality—the friction between what this world was supposed to be and the jagged, broken thing it has become. When they "feed," they drift close to the folded, non-Euclidean pathways where the horizon curls inward. They press their edges against the membrane of the architecture, absorbing the static discharge produced by the city’s failed attempts to solidify. This is why the taste of ozone and copper follows them; it is the byproduct of their metabolism, the bitter metallic waste of a world trying, and failing, to hold its own weight.

They do not fear the void or the dissolution of their home. In fact, they seem to be the primary agents of it. There is a terrifying, quiet intelligence in their movement—a fluid, predatory grace. If a spire leans too far, or if a bridge begins to anchor itself too firmly into the calcified geometry of the mountain, a Refraction will drift near, its presence acting like a solvent. Mere proximity to one of these shadows accelerates the decay. A wall that has stood for centuries will begin to soften, its obsidian turning to vapor, its integrity unraveling into the fine, cold flour that carpets the city.

They are the ultimate cleaners of a house that refuses to be empty.

They do not communicate with one another. There is no social hierarchy, no shared intent. Yet, when they pass one another in the narrow, chime-filled corridors, they do not collide. Instead, they overlap, becoming a single, darker knot of energy for an infinitesimal moment. During this brief fusion, the silence of the city reaches a piercing, impossible pitch—a psychic scream that vibrates the iron in one's blood. Then, with a flicker of static, they peel apart and drift back into the gloom, leaving the atmosphere colder, the city a little more hollow, and the horizon slightly more curved.

They are not waiting for anything. They are the symptoms of a terminal world, the ghosts of a future that arrived too early and stayed far too long. They are the only things in this purgatory that are truly at home, perfectly comfortable in the knowledge that one day, when the last obsidian spire returns to the dust, they will finally be able to stop flickering, and simply, finally, cease to be.
