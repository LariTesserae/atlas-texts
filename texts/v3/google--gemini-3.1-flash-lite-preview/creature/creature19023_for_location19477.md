The people of Oru do not so much inhabit their dwellings as they do integrate into them, becoming extensions of the architecture. They are the *Oru-ka*, beings who seem to have surrendered the feverish biology of the lowlands to match the frozen, mineral patience of the spires.

To look upon an Oru-ka is to look upon a creature finished. They are tall, not with the reach of the vibrant, but with the lean, stretched quality of a shadow cast against a high wall. Their skin has taken on the pallor of the shale—a dusty, muted charcoal hue, mapped with fine, intersecting lines that mirror the fissures in the rock. There is no flush of blood to their cheeks; the cold has driven their vitality deep into the marrow, slowing their metabolism until a single breath may span minutes of quietude.

They do not speak with voices that carry. To speak loudly in Oru would be an affront to the equilibrium, a jarring fracture in the stone-wrought silence. Instead, they communicate in the language of the threshold: a slight inclination of the head, a specific placement of a foot, the rhythmic tapping of a bone needle against a stone lintel. They are masters of the microscopic gesture. When they move, it is with a deliberate, haunting economy—never lifting a limb more than is necessary to clear the jagged terrain, their footfalls so silent they seem to merge with the shifting of the wind-blown grit.

Their clothing is an afterthought of biology: heavy, stiff cloaks woven from the white, wiry grasses that cling to the mountain’s knees. These garments are never washed, only mended until the original fiber is lost beneath layers of patching, creating a texture that mimics the moss-crusted slate they call home. They do not carry packs or tools of metal; everything they possess is secreted in the hollows of their garments or stored within the rock shelves that serve as their interior walls.

The Oru-ka do not gather. There are no communal spaces in the traditional sense, for the equilibrium of the town is static, and the presence of many would imply a change or a flurry of influence that the stone would reject. They exist in a state of intentional solitude, each figure confined to a series of terraces and passages, as if guarding a specific set of fractures or a particular basin of grey, stagnant water. 

Sometimes, they stand on the precipice—a figure draped in slate-colored wool, motionless against the blinding, thin light. They stare out into the void where the clouds once stood, their eyes clouded, perhaps, by the cataract-sheen of the high altitude, reflecting the sky rather than seeing it. They wait. Not for a visitor, not for a season, not for a change in status—but for the simple, perfect continuation of the next moment.

They never age in the way the world below understands it. They do not wither so much as they simply become harder, more brittle, until the day one of them happens to lean against a wall, closes their eyes, and stays there, becoming just another architectural feature—a piece of limestone, a shadow that never moves, a part of the city’s permanent, silent scaffolding. They are the living fossils of Oru, the stewards of a world that stopped ticking, perfectly and forever.
