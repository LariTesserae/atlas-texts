The beings who inhabit the Verdant-Hold are known to themselves as the *Xylo-Kin*, though to any outsider, they look less like a species and more like a deliberate extension of the canopy. 

They are gaunt, elongated figures, their limbs possessing a curious, knotty flexibility that mimics the very trees they dwell within. Their skin is not made of traditional flesh, but rather a tough, translucent membrane that appears faintly photosynthetic—shifting from a deep, bog-brown in the shadowed interiors to a vibrant, verdant olive when they step into the dappled light of the upper bridges. They do not possess lungs in the sense that a terrestrial mammal does; beneath the collarbone, a series of gill-like vents rhythmically expand and contract, filtering the oxygen-rich, moisture-heavy air.

Each Xylo-Kin carries a personal garden of sorts. Embedded into the surface of their skin, particularly along the spine, the shoulders, and the backs of their forearms, are symbiotic mosses and tiny, bioluminescent fungi. These growths act as a secondary nervous system. When a Xylo-Kin is anxious, the fungi dim to a pale, sickly ash-white; when they are healthy and busy at their craft, they glow with a warm, pulsing amber, illuminating the darker, wetter arteries of the city as they move.

They are rarely still because they are perpetually "tending." To a Xylo-Kin, occupancy is a heavy responsibility. They do not merely live in a home; they are the home’s immune system, its gardener, and its architect-surgeon. When you see one kneeling on a floor-mat of compressed root, they are not resting. They are likely whispering to the micro-fauna that keep the wood from rotting too quickly, humming vibrations into the marrow of the floor to encourage a structural branch to strengthen its weave in a high-traffic area. 

Their language is a stuttering, melodic clicking, modeled after the beetle-life that chitters through the walls. It is a tongue of urgency. They do not converse about abstract concepts; they communicate in immediate, practical imperatives: *tether this branch, harvest the sap-sealing gland, brace the node.*

Because the architecture is in constant flux, the Xylo-Kin have a distorted perception of time. They do not measure a year by the path of a sun they rarely see clearly, but by the "cycle of the boles"—the slow, century-long swelling and thickening of the foundation trees. A single individual might spend their entire lifespan specializing in the maintenance of a single, massive, twisting archway, molding it with their own body warmth and the help of specialized beetles. They are the gardeners of their own world, yet they are also the crop, their eventual death being the final act of fertilization that feeds the very wood that sheltered them.

When a Xylo-Kin reaches the end of their vitality, they do not leave the city to die. They seek out a "hollow-node"—a designated, deep-core chamber where the sap flow is most intense. They sit, they weave themselves into the architecture with the vines they have tended their whole lives, and they remain in position. Their body slowly dehydrates, their skin hardens into a bark-like casing, and their skeleton dissolves into the fibrous lattice, becoming the very structural support that will hold the city for another hundred years. 

Thus, the Verdant-Hold is not just a city—it is a vast, interconnected genealogical record. The walls contain the marrow of ancestors, and the floor you walk upon is, quite literally, the firmament of those who labored to keep the jungle from swallowing the light. Every Xylo-Kin moves with that rushed grace because they know they are walking upon their predecessors, and they are preparing the floor for their successors, all while the city breathes, sighs, and grows beneath their feet.
