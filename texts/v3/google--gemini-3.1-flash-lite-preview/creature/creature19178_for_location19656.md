The beings who inhabit Ouroboros-on-the-Spire do not walk so much as they navigate. They are the **Liners**, a people whose anatomy has been subtly re-contoured by generations of living on a vertical axis. They are tall, lean, and deceptively strong—their limbs possess an elongated, sinewy quality, like the reach of a gardener’s shears. 

Their skin is the color of wet slate, perpetually cool to the touch, and dusted with a fine, silver-grey particulate—the result of sanding down rusted rivets and scraping away the encroaching, amethyst-colored moss. To a stranger, a Liner might appear sickly, but they are perfectly calibrated for this pressure. Their lungs are capacious, hardened to the thin, metallic tang of recycled steam, and their eyes have evolved to ignore the glare and focus entirely on the micro-movements of their environment. They possess a second, translucent eyelid, like a bird’s nictitating membrane, which flicks shut against the stinging, grit-heavy updrafts.

They do not wear clothes in the traditional sense. Instead, they are draped in "cladding"—suits constructed from woven, rubberized canvas and tether-lines. A Liner is never truly undressed; they are always partially harnessed. Every inhabitant carries a complex spool at their waist, a mechanical umbilical cord that they hook into the mountain’s brass railings with a rhythmic, reflexive grace. They move with an unsettling, fluid caution, their bodies angled toward the stone-face, trusting the iron more than the air.

The Liners are not builders; they are the Caretakers of the Residual. They do not dream of skyscrapers or future advancements. Their work is a liturgy of maintenance: spending a decade to grind a single pressure-joint back to a state of smooth rotation, or delicately tending the bioluminescent moss-beds so that the light-levels in the walkways do not drop into total darkness. They see themselves as the mountain’s nervous system, the externalized consciousness of a geological clock.

They speak in a dialect of chimes and clicks, a language developed to carry through the damp, sound-dampening fog. When they gather in the inner hollows of the iron shells to sleep, they huddle in nests of repurposed copper wiring and harvested felt, pressed tight against the shivering warmth of the machinery. They are quiet, private, and profoundly melancholy. They know that each rivet they tighten is merely a stay of execution against the wind.

But look closer, and you will see the strange, secret beauty of the Liners: their devotion to the *Wind-Skimmers*. 

A Liner will stop in the middle of a delicate repair, unhook their tether, and simply stand on the edge of the abyss, reaching a long, pale hand into the mist. They are waiting for the cyan-glowing jellyfish to drift near. They feed the Skimmers with droplets of nutrient-rich runoff, and in return, the creatures brush their cold, light-radiating tentacles against the Liners’ foreheads. In those moments, the people of the Spire seem less like industrial ghosts and more like priests of an expiring, celestial order. 

They do not fear the inevitable smoothing of the Spire. They treat the approaching erosion with a gentle, patient reverence. They are the final stewards of a needle in the sky, polishing the copper and brushing the algae, perfectly content to be the last, thin shadow to cling to the mountain before it is reclaimed by the infinite white of the sky.
