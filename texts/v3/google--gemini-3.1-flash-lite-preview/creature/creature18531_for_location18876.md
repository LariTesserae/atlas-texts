The inhabitants of the Hydro-Arboreal Spire do not walk; they undulate. They are elongated, willowy creatures, their bodies possessing the fluid grace of moray eels adapted into a bipedal, skeletal geometry. Their skin is the color of bruised slate, mottled with patches of pale, reflective bioluminescence that flare in rhythmic patterns—a visual language spoken, like the pulse of the kelp-tree, in slow, deliberate sequences.

Their faces are narrow, tapering to soft, blunt muzzles. They lack external ears, relying instead on the vibration of the water column to press against the gossamer membranes stretched along their jawlines. When they "speak," it is a series of modulated harmonics; a low-frequency hum that vibrates the walls of the chambers, resonating with the groan of the tree itself so that one is rarely sure if a sound originated from the neighbor or the architecture.

They are the Keepers of the Flow. 

Because they exist in a state of suspended gravity, their society is defined by the knot. A Keeper’s social standing is not reflected in wealth, but in the complexity of their tethers. Every resident wears a harness of intricate, braided kelp-rope, heavy with carved resin toggles, polished shells, and smooth, weighted stones that act as counterbalances. To see a Keeper move through the open water between trunks is to watch a slow-motion dance of architecture; they trail their tethers behind them like the tentacles of a jellyfish, weaving themselves into the fabric of the tree as they move.

They are artisans of patience. A Keeper might spend a decade using a single, obsidian-tipped scraper to widen a room, shaving away nanometers of cellulose in a meditative trance that matches the glacial pace of the tree’s growth. They do not eat in the way the lowland creatures do; they absorb the golden pollen of the water through porous, gill-like slits along their sides, a process that necessitates long hours of stillness. 

When they gather for "the hearth"—a patch of glowing, sulfur-colored fungi that radiates a gentle, chemical warmth—they do not sit. They cluster, their bodies tangled in a soft, affectionate knot of limbs and tethers, drifting slightly in the eddy created by the chamber’s curvature. 

They are effectively immortal, or as close to it as the slow erosion of their world allows. Accidents are rare; one only dies here if the tether snaps, if the knot becomes untied, or if the "Grip of the Void"—a madness born of watching the sky for too long—takes hold. When a Keeper passes, they do not sink. They are weighted down with stones and guided into the deepest, darkest root-tangle of the Spire, where the tree reabsorbs them, and their memories—stored, they believe, in the vibration of their lattices—become part of the groan of the wood, adding a new, faint resonance to the low-frequency song of the city. 

They are the curators of a silent conversation, a people who have spent three thousand years learning how to keep the world from drifting away. They do not fear the pressure; they have become the pressure. They are the soft, thinking extension of a colossal, living thing that is waiting for the mountain to melt into the sea.
