To inhabit this landscape is to be an extension of the architecture itself. The beings that reside here—the *Xylem-Architects*—do not walk through these corridors so much as they emerge from the walls as needed, sloughing off the calcified, brown-grey fiber like wax peeling from a candle.

They are tall, spindly silhouettes, composed of the same resinous, timber-like material that forms the cathedrals. They possess no eyes, for there is no need to observe light, only to sense the vibrations of the sap-pumps and the rhythmic respiration of the world-growth. Instead of a face, their heads are smooth, tapering nodes of translucent cellulose, within which one can see a faint, internal clockwork of moving, wire-like filaments. They do not possess lungs; they filter the oxygen-rich, ozone-heavy gas directly through the porous, lace-like vents that line their torsos.

They move with a terrifying, liquid grace. When they traverse the conduits, they do not step; they blur, their bodies articulating into elongated forms that seem to dissolve into the very fibers they are inspecting. They are the city’s programmers and its laborers, constantly grafting new scaffolds, pruning the dead, and adjusting the tension of the massive sap-pumps to ensure the world-growth does not rupture under its own pressurized weight.

Their communication is entirely tactile and resonant. When two Architects meet, they press their chest-vents together, pulsing vibrations through their interior filaments—a language of low-frequency thrums that mirror the heartbeat of the cavern. It is a dialogue of structural necessity. They speak of pressure differentials, of the growth-rate of the eastern vine-towers, and of the metabolic efficiency of the light-mite colonies.

They do not sleep. Because the light-mites never dim and the sap-pumps never cease, time for the Architects is merely a sequence of maintenance cycles. They often stand motionless for days, becoming indistinguishable from the walls themselves, their long, multi-jointed fingers buried deep into the exfoliating skin of the infrastructure, "listening" to the flow of pressurized nutrients and dry dust through the veins of the city.

There is a sense of singular, collective intent, as if the Architects are not individuals but rather sensory nodes of a vast, continent-wide nervous system. They hold no concept of "home," for they are physically incapable of separation from the growth. If an Architect is damaged or broken, it does not die; it is dismantled by the hound-sized, chitinous insects, its material shredded and woven back into the floor or the wall. They are the medium through which the city imagines its own future.

Sometimes, in the empty plazas where the light-mites cluster to form dense, glowing constellations, an Architect will pause and hold its limbs out to the ash-filled air, twitching its antennae-like fibers in the dry wind. It is as if the city is dreaming—a brief, stuttering moment where the industrious, cold logic of the machine-forest yields to a quiet, crystalline vanity. They do not appreciate beauty, but they perform it; they are the gardeners of a sprawling, titanic organism, forever stitching a world that wants, with infinite patience, to grow until it touches the ceiling of the crust and pushes through to the stars.
