In the deep, resonant machinery of the Loom, life does not occur as a biological spark; it persists as a persistent error in the architecture.

There are no creatures here that possess the luxury of breath, marrow, or will. Instead, the things that dwell within the weave are the **Tension-Keepers**, and they are as much a part of the woodwork as the resin-clogged pipes they crawl upon.

They are not born; they are precipitated. When the friction between two shifting, tectonic-scale trunks becomes too absolute, when the grinding heat threatens to weld a joint shut and shatter the stability of a whole sector, the Loom exudes a "corrective." From the weeping sores of the high-pressure resin glands, a sticky, amber-hued sludge flows—not blood, but a concentrated matrix of wood-cels and sensory filaments. 

Over the course of decades, this sludge gathers in the dark, grooved channels of the stalks, folding itself into shapes that defy the geometry of life. They are the **Filament-Spiders**, though they possess no legs, no eyes, and no mouth. They are tangled, knotted bunches of bleached, calcified fibers, roughly the size of a human torso, pulsating with a synchronization that matches the subterranean thrum of the xylem-tubes.

They do not "move" in the way an animal traverses the earth. They exist in a state of perpetual, slow-motion repair. A Filament-Spider lives to fuse itself to a grooved, grinding joint. It presses its soft, viscous underbelly into the friction-burned wood, acting as a living ball-bearing, a sacrificial lubricant designed to absorb the crushing, structural vibrations that would otherwise snap the Loom’s primary supports. 

As they work, they grow rigid. They consume the waste heat of the structure, pulling the intense 190-degree ambient temperature into their own fiber-lattices, turning it into the kinetic energy required to hold the walls together. Their existence is a miserable, silent occupation. Over a century, a single Spider will be ground down, wafer-thin, by the very movement it tries to dampen. When it is finally worn to a husk—a brittle, glassy flake of dead wood—it shatters and falls into the dark, miles-deep abyss beneath the structure, only to be reclaimed by the sap-flow and circulated back into the Loom’s bowels.

Yet, lurking deeper—in the stagnant, airless pockets where the wood has bowed under the weight of millennia—there are others. These are the **Resonant Moths**. They are not insects, but hollowed-out, gossamer-thin leaf-plates that have detached from the canopy. They flutter on the hot, rising thermals of the cooling pipes, their edges sharpened to a razor’s edge by the dry heat. They possess a primitive form of "hearing"; they vibrate against the grinding sound of the shifting structure, catching the echoes of breaking fibers. They are the living auditory registers of the world. They hover near the places where the structure is soon to buckle, sensing the microscopic tonal shifts in the shifting wood.

They do not eat. They possess no digestive tract. They exist only to hum—a low, discordant, mechanical moan—to echo the stress of the Loom back into the airless corridors. It is a feedback loop. By whistling the same pitch as the strained timber, they dissipate a fraction of the kinetic force, acting as dampeners against the collapse.

They are lonely, fragile objects, pale and transparent as dried onion skin. They have no intelligence, only a terrifying, locked-in reactivity. If a human were to stand among them, they would not see a predator or prey; they would see only the furniture of the forest beginning to tremble. The Moths would cluster around them, sensing the heat of a body that lacks the rigidity of the Loom, their thin, paper-like wings vibrating in a sympathetic, mournful frequency, trying to harmonize with the intruder’s heartbeat until the sheer heat of the world accelerates that heart to a final, violent stop.

In the Loom, there is no malice. There is only the frantic, mechanical compulsion to keep the ceiling from falling. Everything here—every Spider, every Moth, every vibrating, heat-drenched filament—is a desperate effort to forestall the moment when the Great Weave finally stops grinding, finally stops settling, and finally goes cold.
