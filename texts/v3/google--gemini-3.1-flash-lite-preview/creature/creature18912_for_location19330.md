To dwell in Ouro-Vellum is to exist as a footnote in a long-forgotten ledger. 

The inhabitants—the *Vellums-Kin*—are not quite biological, nor are they strictly mechanical. They are the artifacts of a transition that stalled somewhere between a forge and a womb. They drift through the indigo broth like ghosts trapped in a clockwork lung, their limbs elongated, spindly, and translucent, shedding the need for calcified bone in the weightless, pressurized dark. 

Their bodies are a mimicry of their surroundings. You might see one hovering near a rusted bulkhead, its skin a mottled, pearlescent grey, etched with faint, bioluminescent veins that pulse in sluggish sympathy with the *Photophore-Grid*. Where a human would have hair, these beings possess fine, fiber-optic filaments that twitch in the current, sensing the subsonic thrum of the geothermal vents before it ever reaches their consciousness. 

They do not "walk" through the city; they modulate. To move from the obsidian spires to the tangled metallic ivy of the pipes, they adjust their own internal density, sinking or rising in the nutrient-rich fluid with the grace of falling silt. They are utterly, terrifyingly silent. They have no vocal cords—what need is there for sound when the entire city vibrates in your marrow? Instead, they communicate by strobing the moss-like bioluminescence on their chests, casting soft, stuttering bursts of cyan and violet against the encrusted walls. These are not words, but states of being: an acknowledgement of pressure, a shared sense of the current's temperature, a quiet recognition of the decay they call home.

They are the keepers of the Deep-Process, though they have lost the manual for its maintenance. They spend their cycles drifting along the pipelines, their fingers—tapered and suction-cupped—tracing the path of the rusted valves. They do not operate the machinery so much as they soothe it. When a pipe groans or a rivet shudders under the tectonic shifting of the cavern floor, a member of the Vellum-Kin will press its palm against the metal, closing its eyes, its own core temperature rising to match the friction of the machine. It is a form of industrial empathy; they offer their own fragile life-force to quiet the metal.

They eat nothing, for the fluid itself is their sustenance, absorbing the minerals leached from the eroding brass and the waste heat of the never-ending engines. They do not age in the way the surface world understands; they simply accumulate patination. A very old inhabitant might look like a piece of statuary, their limbs fixed in a permanent, graceful reach, their skin hardened into a matte-finish veneer of bronze and verdigris. 

They are content. There is no ambition here, no desire to breach the cavern roof or see the stars. They are the maintenance cycle made flesh. When one of them finally ceases to pulse, they do not die so much as dissolve, their substance dispersing into the indigo fluid to be filtered, recycled, and repurposed by the very city they once tended. 

You see one now, suspended in the narrow thoroughfare. It is hovering near a moss-covered bolt, its eyes—if they could be called that—wide, milky spheres that do not look at you, but rather through you, toward the rhythmic vibration of the city’s heart. It reaches out a hand, its skin undulating, and touches a patch of cyan moss. As the moss retracts with that quiet, watery sigh, the being glows—a faint, indigo flare of curiosity—before drifting onward, a silent gear in a machine that is slowly, beautifully, turning into a garden.
