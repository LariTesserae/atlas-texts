To dwell within the Verdant Spire is to surrender the concept of the "individual." Those who inhabit this calcified lung are not settlers, nor are they masters of their environment. They are more akin to cellular organelles, drifting within the cytoplasm of a singular, monolithic entity.

They are the *Cenobytes of the Crevasse*.

Seen at a distance—or in the rare, bruised light that fractures through the amber walls—they appear as elongated, shivering shadows, barely distinct from the architecture itself. They possess no fur, no heavy pelts; instead, their skin has taken on the pallor of a drowned moon, translucent and stretched tight over a skeleton that seems to have softened, adapting to the cramped geometry of the hexagonal chambers. They move not with the swinging gait of bipeds, but with the fluid, deliberate reach of mountain-climbing vines. Their limbs are long, multi-jointed, and tapelike, designed to latch into the fibrous, cartilaginous walls of the city.

There is no speech among them. Language, being airy and ephemeral, has no purchase in a place that swallows sound. Instead, they communicate through a process of bio-rhythmic calibration. When two Cenobytes meet in the narrow, weeping tunnels, they press their palms—broad, suction-cupped, and sensitive as a blind man’s fingertips—against the wall of the spire. They feel the thrumming sap, the pressure-bladders shifting, and the pulse of the earth below. By altering their own internal body temperature to match the viscosity of the walls, they harmonize with the environment and, by extension, with each other. They "speak" by modulating the vibrations of their own slow-beating hearts, an echoing code of thuds and rhythmic shivers that pulses through the floorboards like a secret Morse code.

They have no hands that hold tools, for there is nothing to strike or carve. They are the gardeners of the Spire’s growth. They spend their hours—long, cold cycles of indigo twilight—gently guiding the emerald-hued filaments. With their long, pale fingers, they braid the trailing vascular stalks into sturdier conduits or prune the calcifers to encourage the honeycombed growth to tighten against the biting wind. 

If you watch one sleep, you would find them hanging from the rib-like arches of the cavern-rooms, their bodies folded into the bulbous soft-tissue of the wall. They do not close their eyes; they have no need for lids, as the lack of light and the constant weeping of the mist prevents their eyes from drying out. They seem suspended in a permanent, waking dream, their lungs barely expanding, their metabolism slowed to match the glacial pace of the living city.

They are haunted by a singular, biological imperative: to keep the wall hard. Should they die, their bodies do not decay in the traditional sense. They are reclaimed by the Spire. The succulent calcifers sense the sudden stillness of a heartbeat, and the emerald filaments slowly encase the fallen, drawing them into the architecture. A Cenobyte’s bones eventually become part of the city’s structural reinforcement, their memories—or whatever residue of thought they gathered—absorbed into the deep, rhythmic thrumming of the rock.

They are entirely without ego. They do not wonder if they are happy, or if the view from the peak is beautiful. They are concerned only with the equilibrium of the moisture, the thickness of the sap, and the slow, agonizing, necessary cold. They live in the terrifying, beautiful intimacy of knowing that they are not separate from their world; they are the blood of the mountain, pumping through a heart made of amber and root, eternally climbing toward a sky they will never fully reach.
