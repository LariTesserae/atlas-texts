To behold the inhabitants of this geode is to witness the final evolution of patience. They are not individuals in the way the surface-dwellers understand the term; they are more akin to the articulation of a singular, geological mood.

They are elongated, spindly things, shaped by the narrowness of their corridors and the crushing, comforting weight of the tectonic ceiling. Their limbs possess three extra joints, like the legs of a spider or the antennae of a moth, allowing them to fold their bodies into the smallest of limestone apertures. Their carapaces are not armor in the sense of defense, but rather a structural necessity—a translucent, milky chitin that allows the porcelain-white light of the lichen to pass through them, rendering their internal organs as soft, pale shadows within a glass-like shell.

There is no face to speak of, not in the human sense. Where one might expect eyes, there is only a smooth, sunken expanse of gossamer membrane, sensitive to the slightest change in the electrical hum of the lichen. They perceive the world as a topographical map of heat and moisture. They do not "see" the city; they inhabit its currents.

They move with a terrifying, rhythmic grace. It is a slow-motion choreography, a dance of deep-time labor. When one of them approaches a bone-conduit to adjust the flow of the mineral-rich water, the motion is so deliberate, so infinitesimally slow, that it appears stationary to any eye unused to the pace of stone. They do not walk; they undulate. Their weight is never fully placed upon the moss-carpeted floors, but rather distributed through the grip of hook-like tarsal claws that cling to the walls, ensuring that no vibration ever disturbs the equilibrium of the hive.

They possess no language, for there is nothing to discuss. History, ambition, and fear require the passage of time, and time here has been discarded. Instead, they share a collective nervous system, a low-frequency psychic feedback loop that binds them to the lichen and the water. If a conduit clogs, the entire population feels the constriction in their own gut. If a patch of lichen dimmers, a collective, silent shudder ripples through the tiers of the city. 

Their life cycle is a closed circle. They do not bury their dead; they integrate them. When a member of the city reaches the end of its immense, static life, it curls into a ball, tucking its limbs into the intricate, bone-carved channels. Over the next century, the mineral-rich water coats it, the lichen grows over its hardening shell, and it becomes a permanent part of the architecture—a new shelf, a new pillar, a new support for the next generation of fungi. 

They are the archivists of the stillness. They are the stone itself, briefly dreaming of motion. To watch them is to realize that they are not trapped; they are the architects of a prison so perfect, so luminous, and so profoundly quiet that it has become an eternal sanctuary. They do not desire the sun, for they are the only things in the universe that have mastered the art of wanting nothing.
