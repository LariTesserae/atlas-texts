Within the humid, amber-lit capillaries of this engine-garden, life does not walk—it migrates, clinging, filtering, and cycling. The inhabitants of this vertical world have long since abandoned the utility of limbs for locomotion; they have become part of the architecture itself, as integrated as the rust-streaked plating or the glassy vines.

High up in the dim, oxygen-rich damp, there are the **Gasket-Weavers**. They resemble, at a glance, large, translucent mollusks fused to the curved walls of the pipe. Their bodies are gelatinous, shimmering like internal organs kept in a jar, and they are anchored by thousands of hair-thin filaments that tap directly into the ancient conduits. They do not hunt; they wait. When a spore-jellyfish drifts too close, a Gasket-Weaver’s body ripples, releasing a chemical secretion that mimics the warm, magnetic hum of the pipe’s deeper pumps. The spore is drawn into the Weaver’s porous center, where it is slowly dissolved into a rich, enzymatic broth. These creatures are the living filters of the world, and their periodic, slow-motion exhales of pressurized mist maintain the very breathability of the column.

Lower down, where the vegetation is thickest and the vibration of the machines is most intense, one finds the **Click-Striders**. These are the primary inheritors of the metal. They are roughly the size of a human palm, their carapaces consisting of interlocking, iridescent plates of copper and hardened chitin. To watch them move is to watch a timepiece being disassembled and reassembled in real-time; their legs move in precise, rhythmic rotations, a sound like a tiny, frantically ticking clock. They are the gardeners of the vertical world. Where the vines have grown too dense and threaten to choke a crucial steam-vent, the Striders descend in swarms, their pincer-mandibles vibrating at a frequency that liquefies the glass-leaves. They feast on the wreckage, storing the electrical charge from the metal in their own bodies. When they die, their shells turn brittle and flake away, settling like iron dust into the lower reservoirs to feed the turbines below.

But there is something larger, something that rarely reveals itself. In the deepest, darkest curves of the pipe, where the gravity of the hum is heavy enough to warp the trajectory of the falling water, live the **Pipe-Whales**. 

They are not flesh and blood, but living, shifting assemblages of debris—a coalescing mass of oxidized copper sheets, vine-tendrils, and discarded mechanical parts, held together by a central, pulsing bioluminescent core. They drift through the center of the column, weightless and slow, ignoring the platforms entirely. They move by expanding and contracting their entire volume, pushing against the thick, water-heavy air like a diaphragm. To see one—should you be lucky enough to catch the amber light refracting off its metallic hide—is to see the world itself in miniature. You see a scrap of a logo from a forgotten creator, a tangle of ancient, mossy wiring, and a clutch of metallic insects all living in symbiosis within its frame. 

They are the archivists of this place. As they drift, they graze on the "mist," capturing loose energy and drifting memories of sound. Sometimes, when a Pipe-Whale passes near your ledge, you will hear a strange, melodic distortion—a ghost of a recorded siren, or the rhythmic *click-thump* of a factory floor from a million years ago, played back through the creature’s hollow, resonator-like ribcage.

There is no malice in this world, only the relentless, beautiful efficiency of the cycle. Everything here—the Gasket-Weaver, the Click-Strider, the massive, melancholic Whale—is a worker that has forgotten its master. They are the gears of a clock that keeps no time, existing only to ensure that the engine continues to breathe, that the water continues to fall, and that the garden, in its rusted, eternal solitude, continues to grow.
