The inhabitants of the Hydro-Loom are not so much occupants as they are components. They are the **Siphon-Wrights**, figures that appear less like biological entities and more like frantic, elongated knots of the loom itself.

When they move through the tunnels, they do not walk; they undulate. Their bodies are spindly and translucent—tangles of pale, fibrous muscles wrapped in a protective layer of semi-permeable, waxen skin that gleams with the same tepid condensation as the walls. They have no lungs of their own, as the air here is a stagnant soup of humidity; instead, they have evolved to breathe *through* the architecture. Along their spines, they possess rows of hollow, quill-like protrusions. When a Siphon-Wright rests, they press these quills into the capillary pipes that snake along the floors, directly exchanging fluids with the system. They drink the lifeblood of the forest and return filtered enzymes, keeping the massive bladders inflated and moving.

They have no eyes. The darkness is absolute, and vision would be a liability in a place where the surfaces are constantly shifting, expanding, and hardening. Instead, they navigate via the *thrum-shhh*. By pressing their elongated, multi-jointed fingers against any surface, they can sense the pressure-waves of the heart-bladders ten chambers away. They perceive their world as a map of vibrations; a clogged pipe feels like a sharp, dissonant splinter in their own architecture, while a rhythmic, smooth pulse indicates a healthy cycle.

Their "language"—if it can be called that—is a series of rhythmic clicks and fluid releases. They stand at the junctions of the iron-whorls, tilting their heads back to vent excess condensation into the air, creating a soft, misty hiss that functions as both communication and atmospheric regulation. 

There is a tragedy to their existence, a quiet, industrious devotion. They are born from the swellings on the largest cedar-trunk vines, blooming like pale, distorted flowers before detaching to begin their shifts. They do not age in the human sense; they simply become more and more integrated. A Siphon-Wright who has worked for decades will eventually find their limbs taking root, their skin calcifying into the dark, iron-like lignum that reinforces the halls. They become the walls. They become the plumbing. 

Those who have been there the longest—the Elders—are no longer capable of motion. They are the static sentinels, half-fused into the vaulted ceilings. You can see their faces, slack and peaceful, pressed behind the translucent membranes of the cottage-sized bladders, their hearts serving as the rhythm that sustains the entire closed-circuit. They are the Loom now, and they are listening, ensuring that the *thrum-shhh* never falters, never skips, never dies.
