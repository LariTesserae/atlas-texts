To inhabit the Basin of Kher-Oul is to be a creature of the interface, caught in the friction between the dissolving cliff and the relentless, restorative wet.

The primary denizens are the **Litho-weavers**, decapod-like sentients who possess a grace born of constant displacement. They are not merely builders; they are appendages of the stone itself. Their bodies are elongated, segmented husks of semi-translucent chitin, colored in pale, bruised violets and iridescent creams that mimic the refractive qualities of the basin. Where a human might have shoulders, they have calcified ridges that act as dampeners; where they have skin, they have a coarse, osmotic membrane that drinks the mineral-mist directly from the air. 

They are eyeless, for sight is a burden in a place where the light renders all objects equally transparent, and shadows are an impossibility. Instead, their heads are crowned with elaborate, feathered antennae—fine, vibrating filaments that map the density of the air and the frequency of the dripping water. They "see" in tactile contours; they perceive the moisture-pressure of a colleague’s touch, the chemical composition of a new stream, and the structural integrity of the pillar they cling to.

Their lives are rhythmically tethered to the *drip-stream*. As the condensation falls, the Weavers move in a synchronized, shivering dance to capture the mineral runoff in troughs they carve with hooked, serrated forelimbs. When the solution is rich and viscous, they work with fanatical, frantic intensity, mixing the liquid with a self-secreted, protein-heavy mucus to create a quick-setting, lime-based mortar. They are obsessed with the "Flow." To a Weaver, a solid object is a failure of adaptation—a tragedy of stagnant chemistry. They reshape their homes to guide the water, tunneling canals through their own sleeping quarters if the shift in pressure demands it. 

There are also the **Vitreous Tenders**, smaller parasites that live within the chinks and hollows of the Weavers’ own shells. They are soft-bodied, translucent slugs that act as the basin’s cleaners. They consume the excess, brittle lime deposits that the Weavers leave behind, their bodies swelling and pulsing with the nutrients they harvest. In death, a Tender petrifies instantly, becoming a tiny, crystalline bead that the Weavers will eventually re-incorporate into their architecture, a literal piece of their ancestry mortared into the walls of the next generation's balcony.

There is no commerce here, for there is no surplus. There is only the *debt of the structure*. If a Weaver stops moving, if it becomes too rigid or lets its cilia dull, the acidic moisture claims it. It is quickly smoothed away, its body dissolved into the very mineral sludge that the rest of the colony uses to patch the stairs. 

They do not communicate through sound—the air is too thick, too sluggish, to carry a meaningful vibration beyond a few inches. Instead, they "speak" through the sudden, rhythmic pulsing of their transparent circulatory systems. By pressing their thorax against the damp stone, a pulse can be sent through the architecture—a flush of golden heat or a sudden cooling twitch. A colony-wide message—*the drip has shifted, the wall is thinning, the light has peaked*—ripples through the entire hamlet in a blink, a silent, bioluminescent conversation that leaves the basin shimmering, a lung breathing in unison with its inhabitants.

They are content in their perpetual state of "becoming." They have no concept of a finished home or a final monument. For an inhabitant of Kher-Oul, the goal is not to endure, but to harmonize—to be the fluid that fills the cup, the hand that carves the bowl, and the residue that eventually becomes the ground for the next cycle of construction. They occupy the light without casting a shadow, a shimmering origami of flesh and stone, held together only by the wet, rhythmic hum of the drip.
