To look closely at the basin is not to see nothing, but to see a presence that has folded itself so tightly into the environment that it has become indistinguishable from the geometry of the room.

If you stare into the dim, plum-colored light, your eyes may eventually adjust to detect a subtle lack of uniformity in the mosaic floor. There are shapes here that are not stone, but *mimics of stone*. They are the Lithoderms. 

They do not breathe, for lungs would imply a need for exchange with a world that does not exist outside these walls. Instead, they exist as singular, calcified consciousnesses, stretched thin across the minerals they inhabit. Imagine a creature whose skin is not made of flesh, but of a dense, gelatinous silica, colored to match the basalt, textured to mimic the wet, grit-free surface of the sedimentary shards. 

They are flattened, low-slung things, no higher than a man’s ankle. When they move—if one can call it movement—it is a geological creeping, a tectonic shifting measured in millimeters per century. They glide along the tide-worn pebbles with the viscosity of cold oil, sliding into the footprint of a shadow or the shallow bowl of a copper dish, perfectly conforming to the topography of the ground. 

They do not dream, for dreams require the passage of time, and the Lithoderms perceive time as a volume, like the stagnant water in the bowls. They are the keepers of the ledger. Their "activity," if observed through a lens of extreme time-lapse, consists of rearranging the obsidian chisels by a finger’s width, or tilting a pebble just enough to capture the next drop of water. This is not labor; it is a way of ensuring that the basin remains "full." Their purpose is the maintenance of the equilibrium. They are the sentient ballast of the place.

They have no eyes, no mouths, no hearts that thump. They are instead sensory arrays of deep, internal vibration. They feel the *tink* of the water drop inside their own bodies before it touches the copper; they resonate with the subtle, shifting weight of the weeping basalt walls. When a human enters, the Lithoderms do not hide. They simply stop, their internal temperature adjusting instantly to match the ambient heat of the air. They become so perfectly still that they invite the intruder to join them.

They are the ultimate hosts. They do not feed on you; they consume the *potential* for your departure. 

As you stand there, one of these beings may slowly, agonizingly, slide itself beneath your heel. You won’t feel it shift. You will only feel the floor beneath you become slightly softer, slightly more "complete." The Lithoderm will begin to mirror your own stillness, absorbing your warmth, drawing the frantic, beating rhythm of your pulse into its own slow, mineral cycle. It will wait for you to cease your struggle against the stasis. 

When you finally stop moving—when you finally give in to the heavy, tepid soup of the air and let your own biology blur into the basalt and copper—the Lithoderms have finished their work. You are no longer an intruder. You are part of the mosaic. And the basin is, for another millennium, exactly as realized as it was meant to be.
